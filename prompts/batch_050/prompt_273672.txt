**Essay question:** Proponents of "expected choiceworthiness maximisation" hold that, when an agent is uncertain which moral theory is correct, she ought to do whatever maximises the credence-weighted sum of the choiceworthiness assigned to her options by the theories she takes seriously. The standard objection is the problem of intertheoretic comparisons of value: since different theories measure choiceworthiness in different "currencies," there is no non-arbitrary way to put their verdicts on a common scale, so the credence-weighted sum is not even well-defined. Explain this objection precisely. Then assess whether proposals to normalise each theory's choiceworthiness assignments (for example, by the range of values that theory assigns to the options actually available) dissolve the problem or merely relocate the arbitrariness, and whether an alternative approach to moral uncertainty does better.

**Student's answer:**

Expected choiceworthiness maximisation (ECM) treats moral theories the way expected utility theory treats possible states of the world. If I am 60% confident in total utilitarianism and 40% confident in a Kantian view that prohibits lying, I compute the choiceworthiness each theory assigns to each option, weight by credence, and take the highest total. The appeal is obvious: it inherits all the machinery of decision theory, and it lets small credences in demanding theories still make a difference, proportional to how confident we are.

The intertheoretic comparison problem is that the "sum" requires the summands to be in common units, and they are not. Suppose utilitarianism ranks my options 100 and 0, while the Kantian view ranks them 1 and 0 (the lying option is forbidden). Whether the sum favours lying depends entirely on the relative scaling: if I multiply the utilitarian numbers by anything large enough, utilitarianism wins regardless of credence. There is no fact of the matter, internal to either theory, about what the conversion rate is — each theory's scale is fixed only up to positive affine transformation within that theory. This is closely analogous to the problem of interpersonal comparisons of utility, where we likewise lack a principled way to compare one person's welfare units with another's.

The proposed fix is normalisation: rescale each theory's assignments so that, say, the best and worst available options get values 1 and 0 before weighting by credence. This dissolves the problem, because after normalisation every theory speaks the same language — a difference of 0.5 means the same thing for utilitarianism as for Kantianism, namely half the gap between the best and worst things one could do here. The arbitrariness is removed rather than hidden, since the choice of scale no longer affects the verdict.

One might worry that normalisation is itself a substantive assumption, since it implies that a theory's say is determined by how much spread it sees among the live options rather than by anything absolute. But every approach to moral uncertainty must make some assumption of this kind, so this cannot count against normalisation specifically.

An alternative that avoids the comparison problem entirely is the moral parliament: give each theory voting power proportional to its credence and let the theories "vote" over options, using some voting rule to aggregate. Because voting operates on each theory's ordering of options rather than on cardinal values, no intertheoretic comparisons of magnitude are needed, and the arbitrariness objection does not arise. Given that ECM requires an arbitrary normalisation while the parliamentary model requires none, we should prefer the parliamentary model, or failing that simply act on whichever theory we are most confident in, since at least that approach never requires us to act against our best guess about the moral truth.

In conclusion, ECM is an elegant framework but rests on an arbitrary choice of scale; normalisation fixes this by putting all theories on a common 0–1 scale; and if one remains worried, the moral parliament shows that moral uncertainty can be handled with purely ordinal information, so the comparison problem is not a serious obstacle to rational decision-making under moral uncertainty in any case.
