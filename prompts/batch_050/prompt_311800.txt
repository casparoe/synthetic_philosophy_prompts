Question: Maxwell's demon appears to violate the second law of thermodynamics by sorting molecules without doing work. Using Szilard's one-molecule engine, explain the modern resolution of the paradox, and say where in the measure-store-erase cycle the entropy cost actually falls. Why does it matter that the demon has a memory?

Student's answer: Szilard simplified the demon to a box with a single molecule, a movable partition, and a piston. Once the demon learns which side the molecule is on, it can insert the partition and let the molecule push a piston as it expands isothermally, extracting kT ln 2 of work from heat drawn from a single reservoir -- an apparent second-law violation. The resolution is that the demon can't get the needed information for free. Measuring the molecule's position requires physical interaction (e.g. bouncing a photon off it), and this measurement process necessarily dissipates at least kT ln 2 of entropy into the environment, which cancels the work gained. So the second law is saved at the measurement step. The demon's memory matters because it must record the outcome, but since any recording device is physical, storing the bit is also subject to thermal fluctuations, which is why erasure and storage both end up being costly. Overall the demon is really just a heat engine whose efficiency is bounded by Carnot's limit once you count the informational overhead.
