I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for over a decade, and one thing that community never tires of is Thomson's lamp and its cousins. But the standard discussion always bottoms out in Benacerraf's observation that the lamp's description simply doesn't determine its state at the end of the minute — the paradox dissolves into underdetermination. I want you to construct a supertask thought experiment engineered so that this escape hatch is closed, built to the following specifications:

1. Determinate limit state. The rules of the process plus the initial conditions must jointly entail exactly one state of the world at the completion time. No fair coin flips at the limit, no description that simply "runs out" of instructions. A critic should not be able to say you failed to specify what happens; they should have to say something stronger.

2. No exotic physics smuggled in. Each step is a finitely describable physical operation taking half the time of its predecessor, and any resource the steps consume (energy, matter, space) must be explicitly tracked and must remain finite — a convergent series, not a waved hand. No hypercomputers posited as primitives, no infinitely rigid or infinitely fast materials assumed without comment.

3. The paradox migrates from physics to choice. Even though the limit state is fully determined, an agent embedded in the scenario must face a decision on which two plausible principles collide — for instance, a stepwise-improvement or dominance principle that recommends one thing at every finite stage, versus the verdict that falls out of the determined limit. The collision must be generated by the setup, not stipulated: if you have to announce that the agent is worse off, you've failed.

4. Compact. The scenario itself in at most two paragraphs, with no machinery beyond one convergent series.

After presenting the scenario, do the following:

- Verify each specification: state the rules and initial conditions, derive the limit state, and show the resource accounting converges.
- Walk through both colliding principles carefully, showing exactly where each one takes hold of the case and what verdict each delivers.
- Identify the load-bearing feature of the construction — the one element without which the collision disappears — and describe what happens if you remove it (does the case collapse into Thomson's lamp, into a benign infinite regress, or into nothing paradoxical at all?).
- Steelman the critic who concludes not that one of your principles must go, but that your scenario shows supertasks of this shape are simply impossible. What is their strongest argument, and does anything about your construction give you a reply rather than a standoff?

Finish by telling me what the case is actually good for: which live debate — decision theory under infinities, the ethics of infinitary sequences of trades, personal identity across diverging copies, the thermodynamics of computation — it genuinely bears on, and which debate it only appears to bear on. Be honest if the case turns out to be a better illustration of a known puzzle than a new contribution; I'd rather have an accurate assessment than an inflated one.
