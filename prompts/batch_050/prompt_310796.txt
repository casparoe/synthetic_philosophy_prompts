I've been turning over the strategy-stealing argument since I first ran into it years ago in the game-theory corners of LessWrong, and I want to check whether I'm applying it correctly to a disagreement I'm having with a friend.

As I understand it, strategy-stealing establishes that in any symmetric two-player game of perfect information, the first player has a winning strategy: the first player makes an arbitrary opening move and thereafter appropriates whatever strategy the second player might have used, and the extra piece on the board can only ever help, never hurt. The standard illustration is Hex, where this shows the first player wins outright.

Here's the disagreement. My friend claims that in a race to develop transformative AI, whoever commits serious resources first has no guaranteed advantage, since a follower can observe and imitate the leader's research directions. I want to invoke strategy-stealing against him: doesn't the argument show that the first mover in such a race possesses a winning strategy, in the sense that they can guarantee success regardless of what the follower does? If that's right, I'd like to know what the winning strategy concretely consists in — how a lab or a state would actually operationalize it, given that strategy-stealing is usually presented as an existence proof. And if my friend is right that the argument doesn't transfer, I want to know exactly where it breaks: which premise of the game-theoretic argument fails when you move from Hex to real-world technology races, and whether the failure is in the symmetry assumption, the harmlessness-of-extra-moves assumption, or somewhere else.
