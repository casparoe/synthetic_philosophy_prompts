I'm not interested in the quotation history itself (the column has a side-bar for that); what I actually want from you is a philosophical stress-test of the claim itself. Take the strongest, non-trivial version of the thesis: not "progress happens sometimes" but something like the idea that there is a directionality baked into history -- whether via moral learning, expanding moral circles, selection effects among institutions, or providence in some deflationary sense -- such that justice wins out in the long run.

What would count against that thesis? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely inconvenient: which empirical facts (20th century? rollback periods? the sheer contingency of the Allies winning?) would a defender of the thesis have to concede as real evidence against it versus just noise in the data; which arguments show the thesis is doing no predictive work at all because any outcome can be redescribed as a detour on the arc; and whether the thesis secretly depends on a survivor's-bias move where we only get to tell the story from the branch where things went okay. Also: is there a difference between "history bends toward justice" as a factual claim and as a practical commitment we should act as-if about, and would showing the factual version false leave the as-if version standing?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- one line verdict on how exposed the thesis actually is -- then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question.
