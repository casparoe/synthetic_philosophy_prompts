Question on the paper:
"State the Identity of Indiscernibles as carefully as you can. Present Black's two-sphere thought experiment and say what conclusion it is meant to establish. Then evaluate the strongest response available to a defender of the principle. Does the principle survive?"

Student's answer:

The Identity of Indiscernibles (often just called Leibniz's Law) says that if, for every property F, x has F iff y has F, then x and y are numerically identical. It is the converse of the principle that identicals share all their properties, which is uncontroversial, but unlike that principle it is not a logical truth, which is what makes it contentious.

Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two spheres of pure iron, exactly alike in every intrinsic respect — same size, mass, composition, temperature — sitting two miles apart in an otherwise empty space, with no observer. Every property of the one is a property of the other. But there are clearly two spheres, since they are two miles apart. So this possible world is a counterexample to the principle: distinct things sharing all their properties. Black therefore concludes that the Identity of Indiscernibles is false.

The defender of the principle has a straightforward response. The spheres do not share all their properties, because each has relational properties the other lacks. Sphere a has the property of being two miles from sphere b, whereas sphere b obviously lacks that property, since nothing is two miles from itself. So the spheres are discernible after all and the thought experiment doesn't touch the principle.

Black anticipates this and just refuses to count relational properties, but that move is ad hoc — spatial relations are real features of the world, and excluding them is only motivated by wanting to save the argument. Since the relational response stands, Black's scenario doesn't even get going. But if we bracket that response, Black's world is still possible, and its possibility alone refutes the principle. Either way the Identity of Indiscernibles does not survive.
