I work at a small animal advocacy org and we've had basically the same argument every quarter for two years now: should we shift money away from chicken welfare campaigns toward insect farming (mealworms, black soldier fly, crickets -- the farmed insect industry is already in the trillions of individuals per year and growing fast). The pro side does the multiplication: even if the probability an insect is sentient is only a few percent, expected suffering averted dwarfs anything we can do for vertebrates. The other side says multiplying a near-zero probability by a huge number is just a way of laundering a guess, and that there's a real difference between "probably not sentient" and "definitely a tiny bit sentient in expectation." Everyone has read the same behavioral studies -- bees doing motivational trade-offs, injured fruit flies and anesthetic effects, all that -- and nobody updates.

My working hypothesis is that the debate looks like it's about insect neuroscience but is actually about whether small credences are allowed to do moral work at all: the anti side doesn't disbelieve the bee studies, they just don't think "5% sentient" is a thing you can multiply by a trillion and act on, as if the uncertainty is a different *kind* of thing than a probability. But maybe I'm wrong and it's actually a substantive fight about what evidence could even count as detecting sentience in a creature whose nervous system has a million neurons -- like, is the disagreement that one side thinks "sentience" only makes sense given certain architectures, so behavioral markers are beside the point?

So my question: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A hidden empirical question about insect brains, a decision-theoretic one about tiny probabilities and huge stakes, or a conceptual one about what we're even ascribing when we ascribe sentience that far down the tree of life? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress -- some new experiment, a better framework for moral uncertainty, or just admitting the two camps are answering different questions under one budget line.
