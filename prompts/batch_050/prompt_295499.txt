I organize a monthly reading group at my local library — a mixed crowd of mostly engineers, two retired schoolteachers, and one very patient philosopher — and next month we are doing Wigner's 1960 essay on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics. I get the opening forty-five minutes to lay out the argument before discussion, and experience tells me that if I present the essay as a single mysterious observation, the evening collapses into people trading vague claims about whether math is invented or discovered. I want a teaching plan instead: the argument built so that its puzzling character is earned in stages rather than asserted. Please give me the order in which the pieces have to go on the table, with the dependency reasons — what has to be in place before the claim that mathematical concepts, developed for internal and largely aesthetic reasons with no physical target in view, keep turning out to be exactly the concepts nature requires, can even be heard as surprising rather than as a platitude about math being useful.

For the carrying example, I am torn between conic sections and planetary orbits, complex numbers and quantum mechanics, and group theory and particle physics. Tell me which one to build the session around, what the example must have to actually generate Wigner's puzzle (my sense is that the mathematics must predate the physics and must have been developed for reasons internal to mathematics, and that an example where the math was invented to solve a physical problem, like calculus, would quietly destroy the whole point), and what the example must lack — I suspect it must not be a case where any rough mathematical description would have done, since Wigner's wonder seems to depend on the precision of the fit, but I want that spelled out. Then the misconception map: I expect, in roughly this order, the deflationary response that math is just a language we designed to describe nature, so of course nature is describable in it; the selection-effect response that we only notice the successes and forget the vast pile of mathematics that describes nothing physical; the conflation of Wigner's puzzle with the mundane fact that statistics and arithmetic apply to everything; and, from one or two members, the leap to Platonism or something mystical as if Wigner had proven it. For each, tell me where in the sequence it will predictably surface, whether to defuse it preemptively or let it arise, and what to say — in particular, why the language response does not dissolve the puzzle for a concept like the square root of minus one appearing uninvited in the deepest law we have, and what the selection-effect response gets right without dissolving the whole problem.

Finally: give me a check I can run at the forty-minute mark that distinguishes someone who has understood the structure of Wigner's argument from someone who has merely absorbed a mood of awe — ideally a question I can pose whose answer reveals whether they see why the two-stage setup (mathematics developed independently, physics then requiring precisely that mathematics) does the argumentative work. And tell me what I should openly decline to get into at this level — I assume Tegmark-style claims that the universe literally is mathematics and the measurement problem in quantum mechanics both belong on the do-not-open list, but if one of them turns out to be unavoidable in discussion, advise me on how far to let it go.
