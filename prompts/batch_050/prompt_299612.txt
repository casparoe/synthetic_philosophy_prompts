The exam question was: "Distinguish decision under risk from decision under ignorance. Define the maximin and minimax regret rules. Explain why Rawls thinks maximin is the rational choice rule behind the veil of ignorance, why Harsanyi thinks the parties would instead maximize expected utility under an assumption of equal probabilities, and whether the dispute between them is fundamentally a disagreement about rationality."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim: "Decision under risk is when the agent knows the probabilities of the possible states of the world; decision under ignorance is when the agent has no probability information at all and knows only which outcomes each available act produces in each state. The maximin rule says: for each act, identify the worst outcome it could yield, and choose the act whose worst outcome is best. Minimax regret is closely related: the regret of an act in a given state is the difference between the act's payoff in that state and the best payoff any act achieves in that state, and the rule tells you to choose the act whose maximum regret across states is smallest. Both rules have the advantage of requiring only ordinal information, since all that matters is how the outcomes rank. Rawls argues that parties behind the veil of ignorance should use maximin. Deprived of all knowledge of their position in society, they have no basis for assigning probabilities, and Rawls holds that three features of the choice situation make maximin rational: there is no reliable ground for probability estimates, the parties care little for gains above the minimum that maximin secures, and the alternative principles have worst outcomes that would be intolerable. He adds that the parties must be able to honor whatever they choose under the strains of commitment, so they cannot gamble on principles they might later be unable to accept. Of course, this reasoning only goes through if we assume the parties are unusually risk-averse, as Rawls acknowledges; without that psychological premise, a gamble on better average outcomes would look tempting. Harsanyi disagrees with the whole setup. He argues that a rational agent behind the veil would assign equal probability to occupying each social position, by the principle of insufficient reason, and would then maximize expected utility, which leads to average utilitarianism. On his view, maximin is just irrational risk aversion, and Rawls's theory is a special case of expected utility reasoning with a pathologically shaped utility function. Since Harsanyi derives utilitarianism from the same original-position setup using only the standard axioms of rational choice, his argument shows that Rawls's conclusion rests on a technical error about probability, so the disagreement is, in the end, really about rationality."
