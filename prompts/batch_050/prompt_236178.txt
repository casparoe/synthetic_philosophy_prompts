I'm a high school senior, and our school's Philosophy and Economics Society runs an annual essay contest for which I'm preparing an entry. The topic I chose is the sunk cost fallacy — specifically, whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Below is my current draft (about 500 words), followed by the kind of feedback I'm hoping for. I'd rather receive honest, critical comments than encouragement, since I still have time to revise before submitting.

**In Defense of Sunk Costs**

Everyone learns the rule early: don't throw good money after bad. Money already spent is gone, and rational decisions should weigh only future costs against future benefits. The person who sits through a terrible film because the ticket cost fifteen dollars is the textbook fool. I want to argue, however, that the rule is overstated, and that there are at least three situations in which honoring sunk costs is rational.

First, self-trust. Every decision sets a precedent for my future self. If I abandon my novel at chapter ten because I've grown bored, quitting becomes easier the next time a project turns tedious. Finishing things partly *because* I started them builds a track record my future self can rely on, the way keeping a promise to a friend makes the next promise more credible. The sunk cost functions as a commitment device against my own weakness.

Second, reputation. Other people cannot see inside my head; they can only see whether I finish what I begin. Someone who drops every difficult course or leaves every team mid-season acquires a reputation that closes doors. Because the costs I've already paid make finishing credible, honoring them works as a public signal that I follow through.

Third, the judgment of my past self. The fact that I once judged this project worth investing in is itself evidence. My past self had reasons — some of which my present, discouraged self may have forgotten. Dismissing that earlier judgment entirely assumes that the person who wants to quit is automatically a better judge than the person who chose to begin, and that isn't obviously true.

I grant one large concession: genuinely new information changes everything. If, halfway through a degree, I discover the profession would make me miserable, staying merely because of tuition already paid is irrational. So the principle I defend is modest: sunk costs create a *presumption* in favor of finishing — rebuttable by new information — not a command to finish everything.

Perhaps the word "fallacy" survives my argument. But the simple rule, I think, needs a footnote.

A critic might say each one smuggles in future benefits (self-trust, reputation, information) and so never really defends weighing past costs at all.
