I teach an undergraduate seminar on rational choice theory -- about 16 students, one 75-minute meeting -- and we are about to spend a week on risk-weighted expected utility as a rival to standard expected utility theory. Design an in-class activity that makes students feel the pull of risk-weighting before I ever show them the formalism. It should be a sequence of real choice problems with point-based stakes, engineered so that three things happen in order: first, most students' own choices violate the independence axiom in the classic Allais pattern, without my prompting them; second, a calibration round reveals that if we tried to explain their small-stakes caution with a concave utility function, that same function would commit them to rejecting absurdly favorable large-stakes gambles; third, in a final round, some students strictly prefer to settle a choice between two prospects by flipping a coin rather than committing to either, which expected utility theory forbids but risk-weighting permits. Give me the full rules, the exact choice problems and payoff structures for each round, and a way to keep the stakes real without money. Then give me a debrief script that maps each round onto the difference between utility curvature and probability weighting, tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for instance, students reverse-engineering the point of a round and gaming their answers) and how to handle it, and states frankly which feature of the theory the activity cannot reproduce and I will simply have to assert. Close with a summary table with these columns: Round; Choice problem (brief); Predicted majority behavior; What expected utility says about it; What risk-weighted expected utility says about it; Likely failure mode when run live.
