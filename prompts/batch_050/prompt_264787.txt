PhD student in combinatorics here, lately reading up on constructive math because of a side project involving a proof assistant, and I keep tripping over something I cant find a clean name for. Both in my own proofs and in papers, the move "assume the negation and derive a contradiction" seems to come in two genuinely different flavors. Flavor one: I want to show that no graph in some family has property X, so I assume some graph has X, grind out a contradiction, and the deliverable really is just "that situation is impossible" -- nothing was ever claimed to exist. Flavor two: I want to show some object exists, so I assume that nothing satisfies the condition, derive a contradiction, and then announce the object exists -- but the proof never tells me how to find one, and the contradiction was about the hypothesis "nothing works", not about the object itself. My impression is that constructivists happily accept flavor one and reject flavor two, even though textbooks call both "proof by contradiction" and the two can look line-for-line identical in form. So: is there an established name for this distinction, or separate names for the two flavors? How exactly does flavor two relate to reductio ad absurdum and double negation elimination -- is the rejected step precisely the one intuitionistic logic refuses to validate, or is that a different cut? And if the standard terminology is genuinely a mess here (different authors seem to use "indirect proof" for different things), say so and propose clean labels with definitions, since I'd like to use them in my thesis. End your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...} giving the best name(s) for the distinction in a few words.
