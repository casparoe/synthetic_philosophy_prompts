I am taking a university seminar on medieval Jewish philosophy and would like comments on the following idea for a short essay on Maimonides and animal sacrifice.

My proposed thesis is that Maimonides treats biblical sacrifice as an institution designed for controlled obsolescence. On this reading, sacrifice was not simply an unfortunate concession to the habits of people formed by ancient paganism. The Torah redirected an apparently unavoidable religious impulse toward one God, one sanctuary, and carefully regulated procedures, while also limiting sacrifice in ways that made its eventual replacement imaginable. I plan to develop this interpretation through the discussion of sacrifice in the Guide for the Perplexed, especially Maimonides’ claim that many commandments had to begin from practices familiar to their original recipients. I also want to explore a tension in my thesis: if sacrifice was primarily pedagogical and historically conditioned, why did the law preserve its details so extensively; but if those details have enduring religious significance, is the concessionary interpretation too deflationary? A final section will consider whether Maimonides’ account implies anything about the moral status of the animals themselves, or whether their deaths matter to him only insofar as sacrifice educates human beings.

Please assess whether controlled obsolescence is a philosophically illuminating interpretation or whether it imposes an overly modern idea on Maimonides. I would especially value comments on conceptual accuracy, the strongest objection to the thesis, and how the essay could avoid treating the later replacement of sacrifice by prayer as something Maimonides himself explicitly predicted.
