Im TAing a philosophy of mind course this term and grading exam answers, and I want a second opinion on one before I commit to a mark. The question was:

"Using the Capgras delusion as your example, explain the two-factor theory of delusion formation. How does the theory bear on the debate over whether delusions are really beliefs?"

Here is the student's answer:

"Capgras delusion is the belief that someone close to you, usually a spouse or parent, has been replaced by an impostor who looks exactly like them. On the two-factor theory, associated with Davies and Coltheart, explaining a delusion takes two ingredients. Factor one is a neuropsychological anomaly that supplies the delusion's content: in Capgras, the patient's overt ability to recognize faces is intact, but the normal affective response to familiar faces is missing, so the person they see feels unfamiliar, like a stranger wearing their face. Factor two is a deficit in belief evaluation, often linked to right frontal damage, and it explains why the patient adopts the delusional hypothesis instead of a more conservative one. This contrasts with Maher's one-factor view, on which the anomalous experience alone does the explanatory work: Maher argues the delusion is a reasonable response to a deeply strange experience, and on his view this means delusions end up not really being beliefs at all. As for the doxastic question, the two-factor theory seems to support the view that delusions are beliefs, since it models delusion formation as hypothesis generation plus acceptance -- the same machinery as ordinary belief fixation. That said, because the second factor involves a breakdown in evidence responsiveness, and beliefs have to be revisable in light of evidence, the resulting states are defective beliefs at best."
