I have no formal training in history or archaeology, but over the past year I have been reading popular accounts of past societies that came apart, and I keep running into the same conceptual problem: when has a society genuinely collapsed, as opposed to merely changing? I have fairly strong gut verdicts on particular cases, but no principle I can state fits all of them. I would like help finding one — or an honest account of which verdicts I have to give up.

Here are my cases and my verdicts.

1. The eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BC: palaces burned, long-distance trade networks vanished, whole writing systems were lost, and some regions took centuries to recover. Verdict: a clear collapse.
2. The Classic Maya cities of the southern lowlands, gradually abandoned even though Maya people, language, and culture carried on elsewhere. Verdict: collapse.
3. The Western Roman Empire: political unity fragmented over several generations, and many ordinary people may barely have registered any single moment of failure. Verdict: collapse, though the slowness of it bothers me.
4. The Soviet Union: the state, the official ideology, and the economic system all ended within about two years; output and life expectancy fell sharply afterward. Verdict: transformation, not collapse — and I admit I cannot fully justify this, since by some material measures the decline was worse than in cases I do call collapse.
5. The French Revolution: a regime was violently replaced, but the society's administrative and economic capacities were, if anything, strengthened. Verdict: not a collapse.
6. Constantinople in 1453: the empire ended by conquest, and much of its machinery of government continued under new rulers. Verdict: not a collapse; a polity was defeated, but nothing fell apart from within.
7. Easter Island: deforestation, the end of the statue-building culture, serious population decline. Verdict: collapse.
8. The American South after the Civil War: its defining institution was abolished by force and its economy was wrecked for decades. Verdict: transformation, not collapse.

The tensions I cannot resolve are 4 against 3, and 8 against 2 and 7. In each pair, the case I call a mere transformation involved suffering and dislocation at least as severe as the case I call a collapse, yet the verdicts feel different and I do not think I am simply being inconsistent.

My tentative principle is this: a society collapses when it suffers a rapid, large, and lasting loss of collective capacity — of what its people can accomplish together that they could accomplish before — and the loss was not chosen by the population. I added the "unchosen" clause to protect my verdicts on the Revolution and the Soviet Union, but I suspect it wrecks my verdict on the South, and possibly on the Maya too. Please test it against all eight cases and repair it, or replace it.

What I want from your answer: a principle (or a small set of candidate principles), stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself; a note of which cases motivate each clause; your verdicts on three cases I have not considered — a society that deliberately gives up a major technology and simplifies, a society that hollows out over three centuries with no identifiable breaking point, and a community of two hundred people that simply disperses; and an honest statement of which of my eight verdicts your final principle cannot accommodate.

Please end your response with a final line of the form "Principle: ..." stating your settled principle in a single sentence.
