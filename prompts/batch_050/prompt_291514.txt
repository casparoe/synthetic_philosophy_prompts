I am currently enrolled in a course on practical rationality and the philosophy of action, and we have just spent a week on the toxin puzzle: an eccentric billionaire offers you an enormous sum if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon, where the toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but cause no lasting damage, and where payment depends entirely on whether the intention is formed, not on whether you ever drink. Working with a classmate, I tried to reconstruct the standard reasoning for why you cannot win the money, and our reconstruction appears to prove far more than it should. The argument runs as follows.

(1) You can rationally intend to do something tomorrow only if you now believe that you will in fact do it tomorrow.
(2) You now believe that you will do it tomorrow only if you believe that, when the time comes, doing it will still make sense from your point of view, that is, that you will have adequate reason, or at least sufficient motivation, to go through with it.
(3) In the toxin case, once the midnight intention has been formed and the money secured, drinking gains you nothing: payment turns on the past intention rather than the drinking, so at the time of action you face a day of pain for no benefit whatsoever.
(4) Therefore you cannot now believe that you will drink, and by (1) you cannot rationally intend to drink.
(5) The reasoning in (1) through (4) does not rest on anything specific to toxins; it applies whenever you know in advance that, by the time of action, the point of the act will already have been secured or lost by the intention itself, so that acting then serves no further purpose.
(6) Many perfectly ordinary intentions have exactly this structure. I promise a friend I will help him move next month, partly because making the promise now preserves the friendship; when moving day arrives, the promise has long since done its work, and carrying boxes gains me nothing the promise did not already secure. I buy a non-refundable ticket to a lecture so that I can stop deliberating tonight; when the evening comes, the money is spent either way and staying home is the more attractive option.
(7) So, by (5), I cannot rationally intend to help my friend move or to attend the lecture, and the same holds for an enormous class of everyday commitments, including promises and resolutions made precisely so that future temptation cannot undo them.
(8) But we form such intentions constantly, and a theory on which they are impossible has gone wrong somewhere.

I am not willing to accept that most of my ordinary commitments are ones I cannot genuinely intend, so I would like to know where the argument slips. Please identify the premise or step you would reject, explain why it looked innocent, particularly how it earns its keep in the original toxin case, and then state what rejecting it costs for the analysis of the toxin puzzle itself. In particular, does rescuing ordinary intentions mean conceding that I could win the billionaire's money after all, and if not, what makes the two cases come apart? If more than one step is contestable, I would rather give up whatever threatens the toxin conclusion than whatever threatens (6), but I want to know what I am paying either way. Please write your answer in American English.
