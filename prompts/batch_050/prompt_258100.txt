I sit on the research ethics committee of an academic hospital, and we are reviewing a multi-center trial that will randomize adults in septic shock between two fluid-resuscitation strategies, both of them standard of care and both represented in current guidelines. A colleague who opposes the trial has given me an argument that, if sound, condemns not just this trial but nearly every randomized comparison of standard treatments. Each step looks defensible to me in isolation, yet I cannot accept where it ends. I would like you to tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs.

His argument, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

1. A physician may enroll her own patient in a randomized trial only if enrollment is compatible with the duty of personal care: the duty to offer each patient whichever treatment the physician judges, on the evidence, to be best for that patient.

2. When the expert community is genuinely divided between two standard treatments, so that neither is known to be superior, randomizing between them is said to be compatible with (1): the physician who leaves the choice to a coin is not knowingly giving any patient inferior care. Call this the equipoise defense.

3. But a competent physician is almost never herself undecided. Anyone who has read the literature, discussed the cases, and treated such patients will have formed a lean -- a considered, evidence-based preference for one arm, however modest and however honestly hedged.

4. To assign at chance what one would otherwise assign on one's own judgment is to withhold one's best judgment from one's patient. So any physician with a lean who enrolls a patient breaches the duty stated in (1).

5. Hence the equipoise defense succeeds only for physicians who have no lean at all.

6. The only reliable way for a conscientious physician to remain without a lean is to refrain from engaging the evidence as it develops -- and deliberately not engaging the evidence is itself a failure of professional competence.

7. Therefore randomized comparisons of standard treatments can be conducted ethically only by physicians who are, by the standards of their own profession, practicing badly. The equipoise defense licenses trials on condition of incompetence.

Two things I would like to preserve if at all possible: the duty of personal care in (1), which seems to me the non-negotiable core of the physician-patient relationship, and the permissibility of at least some well-run randomized trials between standard treatments, ours included. If your diagnosis forces me to surrender one of these, say which and why.

Finally, apply your answer to a harder version of our case. Suppose that eighteen months in, the trial's monitoring board reports a trend favoring one strategy -- clinically suggestive, statistically non-significant, and visible only to the board. My colleague claims his argument now yields a further result: every enrolling physician who hears about the trend informally is barred from enrolling, while every physician kept ignorant of it is compromised by (6), so the trial must stop the moment the data begin to speak. Does the repair you propose in answer to the original argument also block this extension, or does the extension survive even if the original fails?
