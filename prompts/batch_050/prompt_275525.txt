Last weekend I got into a long argument with a friend, a committed utilitarian, about whether Kant's ethics of animals could ever be of any real use to people who make actual decisions. I sit on the animal care committee of a mid-sized research institute (mostly rodent work, some fish, occasional behavioral studies), and we review maybe thirty protocols a year -- surgeries, food restriction schedules, housing conditions, euthanasia methods, and the odd request that makes everyone at the table uneasy without anyone being able to say exactly why. My friend's position was that Kant is useless here, because on the standard reading Kant denies that we have any duties directly to animals: our obligations regarding them are indirect, owed to humanity, grounded mainly in the worry that cruelty to animals deadens our sympathy and brutalizes our character, along with some secondary points about gratitude and about animals as analogues of ourselves. I argued the opposite: that this framework, for all its oddity, tracks something our current welfare-scoring checklists miss, namely the way a protocol shapes the researchers who carry it out and the culture of the lab. Neither of us convinced the other, and I came away wanting to actually test my side rather than just assert it.

So here is what I would like from you. Take Kant's indirect-duty framework -- the character-habituation argument, the prohibition on wanton cruelty, the duty of gratitude toward animals that have served us, and the underlying claim that animals matter morally only insofar as our treatment of them bears on our duties to rational beings -- and turn it into a working decision procedure my committee could genuinely run on a submitted protocol, alongside (not instead of) our existing welfare review. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure must stop and hand things back to human judgment because the question is irreducibly interpretive rather than factual, and an honest statement of which distinctions from Kant's view had to survive the translation and which you dropped as unusable. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a protocol requiring repeated painful procedures on rats that are individually anaesthetized each time; a request to discard a colony of retired lab dogs rather than fund rehoming; and a graduate student's habit, discovered incidentally, of handling animals roughly and mockingly outside of any protocol. Crucially, my friend predicted that any honest operationalization will sometimes deliver verdicts no decent committee could accept -- permitting something grim because no human character is at stake, or condemning something harmless on fragile character grounds -- so please include at least one worked case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do at all.

Finally, I would like you to be candid about the enterprise itself rather than just executing it. At the end, tell me what would change your mind about the design you have offered: what argument, counterexample, or consideration would lead you to conclude that the indirect-duty framework resists proceduralization entirely, and where in your translation you think the reasoning is most likely to have failed -- whether in your reading of what Kant's view actually implies, or in the assumption that a framework of this kind can be made usable by reviewers who have never read a page of Kant.
