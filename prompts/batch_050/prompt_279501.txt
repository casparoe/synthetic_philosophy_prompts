A friend who reads the same blogs I do walked me through an argument last week, and I can't find the exit. It's about Good's theorem on the value of information — the result that, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer facing a decision, the expected utility of first receiving free decision-relevant information and then choosing is at least as high as choosing without the information, and strictly higher whenever the information might change the choice. Here's the argument as he gave it:

(1) Good's theorem is a theorem. For any decision problem, acquiring free decision-relevant information before acting never lowers expected utility, provided you update by Bayes' rule and then take the best available action.
(2) Therefore, free decision-relevant information can never make a rational agent worse off.
(3) Therefore, a rational agent should always accept free decision-relevant information when it's offered, and it is irrational to prefer ignorance in any such case.
(4) Some applications: if a genetic-testing company offers you a free panel for serious heritable risks, take it. If a reliable oracle offers to tell you the date of your death at no charge, listen. If your friends are planning a surprise party and you can find out for free, find out. Refusing any of these is leaving expected utility on the table.
(5) But at least one of these applications seems plainly wrong. Consider insurance. Risk pooling works under symmetric ignorance: I insure against genetic risks I haven't tested for. If everyone learns their risk status, those who learn they're high-risk become uninsurable at any price they'd pay, and even those who learn they're low-risk have lost something — they can no longer insure against having drawn the short straw, because they no longer face the uncertainty that made insurance worthwhile. Symmetric information can leave everyone worse off in expectation, not just psychologically but in dollars.
(6) So either Good's theorem is false, or something in the derivation from (1) to (4) fails.

I'm fairly sure the problem is not (1) — it's a proof, and I've checked the proof. But (5) also seems right; the insurance case is not a quirk of human psychology but a structural feature of the decision problem. So the culprit is somewhere in (2), (3), or (4), and I want to know exactly where.

A few requests. First, name the step you reject and state precisely which background condition of the theorem it violates — I have a suspicion there's an equivocation between two readings of "can never make you worse off" hiding in (2), and a separate smuggled assumption about what stays fixed when you learn something, but I can't pin either down cleanly. Second, explain why the bad step looked fine: it's not enough to say where it breaks, I want to know why it survived my first three readings. Third, price the alternatives: what do I lose if I insist that (2) is the problem versus if I insist that (3) overextends the theorem's scope? I'd like to keep both the theorem and the general lesson that information is usually worth having, so tell me which repair preserves the most.

Finally, please be careful to distinguish what's actually settled here — the mathematical content of the theorem and the conditions under which it holds — from what's genuinely contested among decision theorists and economists, such as whether cases like insurance, motivated reasoning, or a preference for not-knowing show that the theorem's scope is narrower than the folk version suggests, or whether they instead show something about the folk version's careless paraphrase. Don't present either resolution as the consensus if it isn't one.
