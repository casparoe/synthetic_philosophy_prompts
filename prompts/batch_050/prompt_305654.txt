Some years ago I came across a summary of a philosophical idea that has stayed with me: when you use a hammer skillfully, you do not experience the hammer at all. Your attention is on the nail and the work; the tool is transparent, almost an extension of your arm. Only when it breaks, goes missing, or proves too heavy for the job does it show up as a thing — an object with weight and properties, sitting there demanding to be inspected. I gather philosophers have technical terms for these two modes of being, but I will leave the terminology to you.

I would like you to write a satirical piece that takes this idea deadly seriously as a principle of product design. Specifically: an internal memo or press release from the design director of a fictional tool company, announcing that the firm has finally perfected its products by making them entirely unnoticeable. The humor should come from the actual logic of the idea rather than from generic corporate mockery. For instance: a tool the user can see or think about has already failed; customer complaints are the only true product reviews, since a complaint proves the tool existed; the returns department is the one place in the company where the product is ever encountered as a mere object; and the firm's crowning achievement, a hammer no user has ever perceived, cannot currently be located in the warehouse — whether this is the ultimate design triumph or a supply-chain catastrophe should remain genuinely ambiguous. The corporate voice must stay completely deadpan, and the satire should target design-industry talk of seamlessness and frictionlessness, not any particular thinker. A reader who knows the hammer example should come away feeling the writer understood it, not merely mocked it.

Please keep the piece to a hard maximum of 450 words.
