I'm TAing an undergrad environmental ethics seminar this semester and I want to build a decision-tree worksheet for the unit on wild animal welfare. The whole thing kicked off because a student in office hours argued, completely deadpan, that paving over a meadow might be *good* for animals: most wild creatures are r-selected, most offspring starve or get eaten within weeks of birth, and if those lives are on net not worth living, then habitat destruction mostly prevents the creation of miserable future animals rather than harming anyone. The dedicated conservationists in the class were horrified, but honestly none of them could say where the argument goes wrong, and I realized the students were talking past each other because they disagreed on like four separate questions at once while pretending it was one debate about whether nature is good.

So: write me a decision tree that starts from a single shared commitment -- "individual wild animals matter morally for their own sake, not just as parts of species or ecosystems" -- and sorts the answerer into a named position via roughly 6 to 8 yes/no (or three-way) questions. The leaves I need to end up at, in some form: (1) the conventional conservationist, who thinks protecting habitat is good *for the animals themselves*; (2) the hands-off welfarist, who agrees wild lives are often bad but thinks intervention is too unpredictable or hubristic, so we mostly owe animals non-interference; (3) the interventionist welfarist, who wants things like vaccination, supplemental feeding, or fertility control *within* existing ecosystems; (4) the reductionist, who concludes that shrinking habitat and wild populations is, on balance, the best available outcome for animals; and (5) the redesigner, who thinks the long-term goal should be re-engineering or replacing ecosystems wholesale (gene drives against parasites, phasing out predation, that sort of thing). Feel free to add a leaf or collapse one if the logical space demands it, but those five need to show up.

The hard requirements: every branch node has to be phrased as a concrete question someone can answer *without* knowing which position it funnels them toward -- so no "do you believe nature is sacred?" and preferably grounded in a specific case, like a drought year where most fawns starve before their first winter, or a parking lot that kills the meadow's current residents but also prevents decades of future litters being born into short brutal lives. At least one node has to turn on the killing-vs-preventing-births distinction, since I think that's secretly load-bearing for the whole debate, and at least one node should force the answerer to commit on whether average wild welfare is positive or negative. Build the tree from the answer-space upward rather than stapling the five labels onto a quiz: show which combinations of answers my five positions occupy, explicitly note any coherent-looking combination nobody holds (e.g. someone who thinks wild lives are net-positive *and* opposes habitat protection -- is that incoherent, or just unpopulated?), and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate with a clean question, rather than pretending it can. Finally, mark the exact node where the answerer's population ethics -- their views on whether creating net-negative lives is bad in the same way harming existing animals is -- first starts doing work, because I want to point at it in class and say "the fight was here the whole time."
