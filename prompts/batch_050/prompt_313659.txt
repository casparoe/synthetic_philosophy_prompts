Take the COVID vaccine mandates of 2021-22 -- the healthcare worker requirements, the vaccine passports for restaurants and travel, the whole mess that eventually set off the trucker convoy in Canada -- and run them through Rousseau's general will machinery. And I mean the full machinery, not just the slogan: the distinction between the general will and the mere will of all, the idea that obedience to a law you've prescribed to yourself is freedom so that someone who refuses can be "forced to be free," and Rousseau's strange claim in Book IV of the Social Contract that when my side loses the vote, that doesn't just mean I lost -- it proves I was mistaken about what the general will actually was.

What I want: first, a careful mapping. In the mandate case, who is the citizen with the particular will, what is the general will even supposed to be here (public health as such? the considered judgment of the majority after deliberation? whatever the epidemiologists think?), and on a strictly Rousseauian reading, does the unvaccinated nurse who gets fired get forced to be free, or just forced? Where exactly in his argument does the answer turn?

Second, where the model breaks. A lot of the real dissenters didn't disagree about ends -- they disagreed about facts. They thought the vaccines were dangerous, or that the risk to them personally was overblown. Does Rousseau's framework even have a category for that kind of dissent, or is it forced to treat every holdout as someone putting their private interest above the common good? And notice the trap here: the Book IV move (the loosing voter was mistaken about the general will) looks tailor-made for factual dissenters, but applying it to them seems to require that "the general will" and "what the experts currently believe" are the same thing. Are they, for Rousseau? Should they be?

Third, run it the other direction: what does the episode do to the theory? You could read the mandates as a vindication -- a community coercing its members toward their own real freedom, exactly as advertised -- or as a reductio: this is precisely what everyone finds sinister about "forced to be free," because in a big pluralistic country "the general will" turns out to be whoever won, wearing a nicer hat. Tell me which reading you think actually survives contact with the case, and whether the Book IV bit ends up helping Rousseau or making things worse.

Don't just give me the standard "Rousseau the proto-totalitarian vs Rousseau the freedom guy" debate, I've seen that. Work the case.
