I'm a graduate student in legal philosophy, and a recent seminar discussion left me stuck on what seems like a tension inside ordinary criminal practice. In most jurisdictions, the insanity defense turns on a cognitive test: a defendant is excused only if, at the time of the act, they did not understand what they were doing or that it was wrong. On that standard, psychopathic offenders almost never qualify. They typically know full well that the law prohibits assault, fraud, or murder, can state the relevant rules, and can even predict how others will judge their conduct. What they appear to lack is affective: no guilt, little or no empathy, no felt pull of the victim's suffering. The law treats this lack as irrelevant to responsibility -- and, worse, in many sentencing regimes psychopathic traits function as an aggravating factor, evidence of dangerousness, manipulativeness, and poor prospects for rehabilitation, so the very same deficit that a moral philosopher might cite as mitigating ends up counting against the defendant.

Here is what I'd like you to do. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of moral knowledge that this practice presupposes. When the law holds the psychopath fully responsible because he knew that his act was wrong, what conception of knowing that something is wrong is it relying on -- is it committed to the view that moral knowledge is a purely cognitive, rule-stating capacity, entirely separable from whatever motivates or moves us? Does it quietly rule out the rival view, defended by some philosophers, that genuine moral understanding requires certain emotional capacities, so that someone who cannot feel the badness of cruelty does not really grasp that it is cruel, however fluently he recites the rule? Second, say whether the practice strictly needs its presupposition or merely fits it well: could a legal system hold psychopaths responsible on purely consequentialist or incapacitative grounds while remaining officially agnostic about whether they possess moral knowledge, and if so, does the rhetoric of blame and desert that pervades sentencing survive that agnosticism? Third, address the apparent inconsistency: is it coherent for one and the same trait -- the affective deficit -- to be treated by the theory as irrelevant to blameworthiness and by the sentencing practice as making the defendant more deserving of harsh treatment? Or is there an asymmetry between what excuses and what aggravates that dissolves the puzzle?

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the law instead adopted the view that moral knowledge has an essential affective component. Presumably some psychopathic defendants would move toward the excuse category, but I'd like you to think about the downstream consequences: would the law need a new evidentiary test for who counts as genuinely morally understanding, what would such a test look like given that affective deficits are hard to verify and easy to fake, and would the resulting system -- in which the least empathetic offenders receive the mildest blame -- be one we could actually accept, or does our commitment to blame here rest on something other than a theory of moral knowledge altogether?
