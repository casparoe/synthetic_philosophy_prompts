His line, roughly: if we assign decent credence to being in a simulation ourselves, then our far future is hostage to whoever's running us and could get switched off long before we fill the galaxy; and if we're at the bottom level of reality, the trilemma suggests advanced civilisations either tend to go extinct or end up running lots of simulations, and on his reading both branches mess with the naive "astronomical waste" picture. She replies that simulated lives would still count morally, so the value doesn't vanish, it just relocates — and he says that's exactly the sort of move he wants pinned down properly rather than waved through.

Draft the joint statement the two of them could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I want: a numbered section of agreed claims (about the structure of the simulation argument, about what longtermism actually commits you to, and about what neither of them is saying — e.g. neither thinks "we might be simulated" licences apathy about real suffering); then a disagreements table where each residual disagreement is stated precisely, in wording that side would accept for its own position; then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I suspect one crux is whether simulated minds carry the same moral weight as non-simulated ones, and another is whether credence in astronomically large futures should be shrunk by shutdown risk and by resource limits on any simulation we'd live in — but do identify others (e.g. whether fanatical reasoning survives once the far future is bounded, or whether the trilemma itself is evidence about feasible futures). For each crux, give a concrete case, observation, or argument that both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.
