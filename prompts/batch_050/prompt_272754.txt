In preparation for my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, I asked a colleague to draft a one-paragraph gloss of eliminative materialism for the syllabus. He wrote: 'Eliminative materialists such as the Churchlands hold that beliefs and desires do not exist. Folk psychology is a theory, and a stagnant one: it has made no progress in millennia, so we should expect it to be discarded the way phlogiston and caloric were, with neuroscience supplying the replacement vocabulary. The eliminativist therefore denies that there is anything it is like to feel pain, since talk of inner experiences is just more folk-psychological theorizing, and maintains that mature cognitive science will let us drop belief-talk from everyday life as well as from the laboratory. The view's obvious problem is that it refutes itself: if there are no beliefs, the eliminativist cannot believe eliminative materialism, and cannot assert it either, since assertion requires belief. Most philosophers accordingly regard the position as a historical curiosity.' I suspect parts of this are fair and parts are not, but I want to get the record straight before the syllabus circulates. Please go through the gloss and render a verdict I can act on, structured as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: identify which claims an eliminative materialist would accept as stated, which involve conflations or overstatements (in particular, whether the thesis about propositional attitudes extends to phenomenal experience, and whether the self-refutation objection is decisive or merely rhetorically effective), and whether the closing sociological judgment about the view's standing is accurate.
