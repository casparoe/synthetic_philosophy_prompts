Working on a short essay for my blog (mostly LW/ACX-adjacent readers) and want comments before I post it. It's about the Kelly criterion and whether it deserves to be treated as its own theory of rational choice rather than a footnote to expected utility.

The hook is Kelly's original 1956 paper, A New Interpretation of Information Rate. Everyone remembers the colorful setup -- a gambler getting noisy tips about horse races over a channel, so the optimal betting scheme turns out to be governed by channel capacity. What I focus on instead is something subtler in the paper's logic: Kelly never says you should maximize expected log wealth because log is your utility function. His argument is that the Kelly bettor's wealth grows exponentially at the maximum achievable rate, so given enough repetitions he pulls ahead of any essentially different strategy with probability approaching one. It's an asymptotic, almost-sure dominance claim, not a claim about preferences.

My essay's notes, roughly:

1. The standard textbook move dissolves Kelly into expected utility: Kelly bettor = EU maximizer with u(w) = log(w). I argue this gets the justification backwards. On the EU reading, a Kelly bettor who claims not to care about log-utility is making a mistake; on Kelly's own reading, no utility function appears anywhere, just a theorem about long-run growth rates.
2. I call the resulting stance "geometric rationality": judge strategies by their time-average growth rate rather than by any expectation over outcomes. I claim this is a genuinely different normative criterion, since it can rank gambles in cases where EU with any concave utility would too, but the grounds are different (what will happen to you over time vs. what you prefer in expectation).
3. The soft underbelly: the whole justification leans on repeated play from the same distribution. For genuinely one-shot decisions there is no long run to dominate in, so geometric rationality seems to say nothing. I gesture at ergodicity economics (Peters et al.) as an attempt to rescue it by reinterpreting "expectation" as "time average" even for one-shot cases, but I'm not sure that move works or that I'm representing it correctly.

What I'd like comments on: (a) is my claim in (1) accurate -- do Kelly's actual results really avoid any utility assumption, or is the log smuggled in somewhere I haven't noticed? (b) Is (2) as novel as I think, or am I reinventing a distinction the literature settled decades ago? (c) For (3), is the one-shot objection fatal, and does the ergodicity-economics response actually adress it or just change the subject? Also general comments on whether this is interesting or tiresomely over-argued welcome.
