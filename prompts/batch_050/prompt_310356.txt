As parliamentarian to the governing council of a national learned society, I must circulate a short written opinion before our next meeting, and two pathological items of business have landed on my desk at once.

The first is a motion newly put forward by a member who is feuding with the executive board. Its full operative text reads: "Resolved: if this motion is adopted, then the executive board is dissolved and special elections are held within thirty days." Defending it on the floor, the mover argued as follows: suppose the motion passes; then, by its own terms, the board is dissolved; so the motion's content is true; so there is no reason not to pass it. This is Curry's paradox in parliamentary dress: because the antecedent is the motion's own adoption, the supposition appears to discharge itself, and an argument of the same form could "establish" any consequent whatsoever — a budget, a name change, the society's dissolution — without the consequent ever being defended on its merits.

The second surfaced when our secretary reviewed the standing rules. At the 2019 annual meeting the council adopted an open-ended schedule of precedence rules, one for each subsequent year, every one providing that all rules adopted under the schedule for all later years shall have no force. No rule mentions itself, and the schedule is not circular; yet no consistent account of which rules are in force seems available. If the 2021 rule is in force, every later rule is void — but then the 2022 rule's claim fails, which means some later rule is in force after all. And if the 2021 rule is void, its claim fails, so again some later rule is in force, and the same contradiction simply reappears one step further down, with no final year to stop it.

Please draft the opinion. It should (i) rule on whether the motion is in order, distinguishing it from harmless self-reference such as "this resolution takes effect upon adoption"; (ii) rule on the status of the schedule; (iii) propose the text of a standing rule that blocks both pathologies while preserving the council's undoubted power to dissolve its own board — or itself — by ordinary majority vote; and (iv) state fairly the strongest objection to your ruling, which the mover's allies are already pressing: that policing the logical form of motions is anti-democratic gatekeeping under another name.
