I have no training in philosophy, but some time ago I came across William Kingdon Clifford's essay "The Ethics of Belief" and it changed how I think. One passage in particular struck me, from his story of the shipowner who talks himself out of his doubts and sends an unseaworthy ship to sea:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

I have since adopted this as my own position: a belief is to be judged entirely by how it was formed, never by whether it happens to come out true, and believing without adequate grounds is a moral failing even when no one is harmed by it. What would count against this view? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, covering at least: cases where stifled doubts or wishful thinking seem harmless or even beneficial (the hopeful patient, the trusting spouse); the objection that we cannot control what we believe and so cannot be obligated to believe otherwise; the worry that "sufficient evidence" is either undefined or defined so loosely that the view risks no counterexample at all; and the charge that the position proves too much, condemning nearly everyone's religious, political, and personal convictions. For each, tell me whether someone holding Clifford's position would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it, and close with an honest verdict on how exposed the view actually is.
