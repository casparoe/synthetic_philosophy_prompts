I recently came across the following gloss in a colleague's teaching notes on Dennett's account of the self, and I would like to know how much of it I can rely on before citing it in a lecture:

"According to Dennett, the self is an illusion. There is no inner subject anywhere in the brain; there is only a brain that spins a story about a character it calls the self, in roughly the way a novelist deliberately invents a protagonist. Since this character is a fiction, Dennett concludes that selves do not exist, any more than Sherlock Holmes does: what exists is neural machinery plus a useful tale. The apparent unity of your experience is produced by a single master narrative that the brain is continuously writing and revising. This also explains, on his view, why patients with severe amnesia or multiple personality disorder have lost or multiplied their selves — the story breaks or forks, and the character dissolves or splits accordingly. Dennett's own label for the self, a center of narrative gravity, is meant to drive the point home: a center of gravity is not a real part of a physical object but a made-up reference point, and the self, he says, is made up in just the same way."

My own inclination is that this is broadly right in spirit but that something in it is uncharitable — I have a vague memory that Dennett resisted the inference from "fiction" to "nonexistent" — but I cannot reconstruct his actual position well enough to say where the gloss goes wrong. Please audit it. Separate what Dennett would accept without qualification from what is a common simplification and what reverses his point outright. In particular: Is the analogy to a center of gravity being used here in the direction Dennett intended, or the opposite? Is he committed to there being a single, continuously updated narrative? Would he describe the storytelling as something a deliberate author does? And does the concluding claim — that the self is "made up" in the way a point on a page is made up — follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or only from its errors?
