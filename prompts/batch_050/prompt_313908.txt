Fair warning: I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy background, so forgive the vocabulary. Years ago I read Gödel, Escher, Bach and later I Am a Strange Loop, and I've basically bought the core claim: a self isn't a thing you have, it's a symbol -- a pattern a system builds once it gets complex enough to model itself, and the feeling of being an "I" is what that self-referential loop is like from the inside. As someone who writes code that inspects and modifies itself for a living, this clicked for me hard. I know the difference between a system with a crude self-model and a rich one, and it seemed obvious that the self is the far end of that spectrum.

Now I want someone to talk me out of it, or at least show me what I'm risking. What would count against this view?

A few specific angles I'm stuck on. First: LLMs. A large language model will happily talk about itself, but the self-talk only exists when the context calls it up -- there's no persistent loop, just weights plus whatever's in the prompt window. Does Hofstadter's picture imply these things have flickering, momentary selves? Because that feels like either a reductio of the view or a weird vindication of it, and I can't tell which. Second: the circularity worry. Saying the self is what the loop looks like from the inside seems to smuggle in a looker, which is exactly the thing the view was supposed to explain away. Is that a real problem or am I missing something? Third, and this is the one I'd find most persuasive if anyone can deliver: does the view actually predict anything a rival doesn't -- like the self just being the organism, or a narrative the brain tells, or flat-out illusionism? If every possible observation is compatible with the strange-loop story, has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that even a problem for a view of this kind?

What I'm after is a map of the view's exposure: which arguments or findings a strange-loop defender would themselves concede as counting against the position, which ones they'd brush off, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. And if there's some result from neuroscience or AI that would settle this one way or the other in principle, I want to hear it, even if we can't run the experiment yet.
