I just got voluntold onto the "ethics review" committee at the consumer app company where I work, and I need help before this turns into a disaster. Every new feature that touches notifications, defaults, or engagement mechanics now has to get reviewed before launch — we're talking two or three specs a week, and I usually have about a day to turn each one around. The problem is the committee keeps deadlocking over the word "manipulative." Half the group uses it for anything designed to change user behavior, which would condemn every interface ever built, and the other half reserves it for flat-out lying, which means things like fake countdown timers and guilt-trippy confirm-shaming pop-ups sail right through. I know there's a real literature on the manipulation vs. legitimate persuasion line — stuff about deception, about pressure that short-circuits choice, about covertly bypassing someone's rational capacities vs. actually engaging their reasons — but none of it comes packaged as something a PM with a deadline can apply, and I can't hand a designer a journal article and call it a review process.

So: turn whatever the philosophy actually knows about this distinction into a procedure my committee can run in under 30 minutes per feature. I want ordered questions, what counts as a yes or no at each step, and clear verdict outputs (pass / pass-with-changes / fail), plus explicit markers for the points where the procedure has to stop and hand things to a human because the question is one of values rather than checkable facts. Be honest about which theoretical distinctions have to survive the simplification and which ones you're dropping, and where the procedure will predictably misfire. Then run it on three real-ish specs: (1) a fake scarcity countdown on a checkout page that resets if you refresh, (2) a streak mechanic with variable-reward push notifications in a fitness app aimed partly at teenagers, and (3) a cancellation flow that takes five screens while signup takes one. At least one of these should come back with a verdict the growth team will hate, and at least one should clear something that feels a little icky anyway — if your procedure never does either, it's probably just encoding vibes. Write the whole thing in American English.

At the very end, give me a single machine-readable line I can paste into our review log, in exactly this form: Verdicts: <feature 1> = <verdict>, <feature 2> = <verdict>, <feature 3> = <verdict>
