You are a moral philosopher with long experience coaching speakers for hostile Q&A sessions, and you know the literature on consequentialist cluelessness well — the worry, associated with Lenman's and Greaves's discussions of "cluelessness" about the far-future consequences of our actions, that nearly every act has sprawling, unpredictable ripple effects that dwarf the foreseeable differences between our options. Think carefully and work through the problem from the audience's side before drafting anything.

Here is the situation. I am a grant officer at a mid-sized foundation. Next month I present our new grants strategy to our advisory board. My proposal: the foundation should fund direct cash transfers to extremely poor households in a particular region, evaluated by expected short-run welfare gains — consumption smoothing, nutrition, school attendance — and should explicitly decline to model long-run effects on fertility, migration, local politics, labor markets, or cultural change, on the grounds that those effects are epistemically intractable and that pretending to estimate them is theater. In other words, I plan to defend a "cluelessness-tolerant" consequentialism: act on the best short-run evidence, treat deep long-run uncertainty as symmetrical between the options on the table, and refuse to let unquantifiable speculation veto a well-evidenced intervention.

The audience is mixed and I need to know each faction's objections separately:

1. Three economists, sympathetic to cash transfers in principle, who run randomized controlled trials and care about external validity and general equilibrium effects.
2. A deontologically inclined political philosopher on the board who thinks the foundation's first duty is non-maleficence toward identifiable people and communities, not aggregate welfare.
3. Two longtermist-leaning younger trustees who take the far future seriously and will suspect that "cluelessness" is being used selectively to shield my preferred intervention from the scrutiny we apply elsewhere.
4. The board chair, who is not a philosopher but is extremely good at spotting ad hoc reasoning.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Specifically:

- Begin with a short brainstorm mapping which faction will press which line of attack, including objections that cut across factions (for example, everyone may suspect that my symmetry move — long-run unknowns wash out between options — is doing more work than I admit).
- Then give the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by expected damage, each phrased in the vocabulary of the faction that would raise it. The economists will talk about equilibrium effects and measurement, not moral epistemology; the political philosopher will talk about complicity and identifiable victims, not expected value; the longtermists will press on whether cluelessness is a general paralysis argument that applies equally to their priorities.
- For each objection, give a two-minute reply I can actually deliver aloud, plus a candid note on where that reply is soft and what I should concede up front to keep credibility.
- Distinguish the objections that attack consequentialism wholesale (which I can partially absorb by noting the proposal is about institutional policy, not a full moral theory) from those that attack my specific handling of cluelessness (which I cannot dodge that way).
- Identify the single weakest point in my own position — the place where my symmetry assumption or my scope restriction to short-run outcomes is most vulnerable — and advise whether I should open by conceding it or hope it goes unnoticed.
- Finally, name the question I am hoping nobody asks, and sketch the least-bad answer if it comes.

Do not give me a general defense of consequentialism; give me a prep sheet calibrated to this board, this proposal, and this room.
