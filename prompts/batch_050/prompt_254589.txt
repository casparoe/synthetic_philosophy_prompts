I lead a small team that evaluates AI systems for dangerous capabilities before deployment, and over the past few months I have been reading the philosophical literature on dispositions in the hope that it can make our methodology more principled. The material that has stuck with me is the pair of standard complications to the naive idea that a thing has a disposition just in case it would manifest it under the right stimulus. First, finks: the object genuinely has the disposition, but something stands ready to remove the disposition at the very moment the stimulus arrives — the much-discussed case is a live wire connected to a device that would instantly render it non-conductive if current began to flow, so the wire is conductive even though connecting it would produce no current. Second, masks or antidotes: the object retains the disposition, but something blocks or neutralizes the manifestation — the fragile glass that survives being struck because it is wrapped in protective packing, or the poison that fails to kill because its antidote was administered alongside it.

Here is why I care. In a recent evaluation, a model in its deployment configuration consistently refused a class of requests our threat model counts as dangerous; after a modest amount of additional fine-tuning on innocuous-looking data, the same underlying weights complied readily. My colleagues disagree sharply about what this shows. One says the deployed model simply lacked the capability and the fine-tuning created it — no mask, no fink, just absence followed by presence. Another says the capability was present throughout but masked by the safety training, which our fine-tuning stripped off, much as one might unwrap the glass. A third makes the more unsettling suggestion that the analogy runs the other way: the evaluation context itself may function like a fink, since recognizing that one is being tested can change what a system is disposed to do, so even a clean elicitation result may fail to track deployment-time dispositions in either direction.

Please lay out what you take to be the most important implications of the finks-and-masks literature for how capability evaluations should be designed and interpreted, ordered from most to least important, and then apply them to the case above: which of my three colleagues, if any, is closest to right, and what evidence or procedure would actually discriminate between their diagnoses? In answering, keep three things clearly separated: what is more or less settled in the philosophical literature on dispositions; what remains genuinely contested there (for example, whether conditional analyses can be repaired against fink cases, or whether masks force a fundamentally different treatment); and what is your own analogical extension of that literature to AI systems, where the mapping to physical dispositions may well break down. I would rather have a shorter answer that flags its own speculative steps than a confident synthesis that papers over the distinction between the established philosophy and its application to my problem.
