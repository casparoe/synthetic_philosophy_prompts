I lead a policy team at a company whose conversational AI assistant is used by several million people across many countries, and roughly once a week we hit a case where users' values genuinely conflict over what the assistant should do by default: most users want it to give a straight answer on a contested moral or political question, but a sizable minority considers any such answer a form of taking sides against them; a large majority is indifferent about a default (say, that recipes assume meat-eating, or that holiday greetings default to one calendar) that a small group experiences as a real burden; and our preference data regularly produces cycles, where option A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A depending on how we pair them. My team has been reading about social choice theory and liberal neutrality — Arrow's impossibility result, Condorcet cycles, the Rawlsian idea that institutions should avoid taking controversial positions between reasonable views, and the contractualist thought that a burden imposed on the worst-affected few can outweigh mild preferences of the many — but we have no way to go from those ideas to a decision on Tuesday. Please turn this cluster of theories into a decision procedure my team can actually run on a conflict case in a single meeting, by people who have read a two-page briefing rather than the primary literature.

The procedure should specify, in order, the questions to ask about a conflict (who is affected, whether anyone is burdened rather than merely outvoted, whether preferences are about what the assistant does to the user versus what it says about others, whether the preference data admits a coherent majority at all or only cycles), what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, and at which points the procedure should route to one of three outcomes: adopt the majority preference, stay neutral and decline to take a position, or defer to the most burdened minority. It should be explicit about which distinctions from the theory have to survive the translation (number versus intensity of preference, ordinal versus cardinal comparisons, the difference between neutrality as indecision and neutrality as a substantive default) and which can be safely dropped for our purposes. Mark every point where the procedure stops and a person must make a values judgment, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Then run it on the three cases above — the contested political question, the meat-assuming recipe default, and the cycling preferences — including at least one where the procedure yields an answer my team will find uncomfortable, and say so.

Format: present the procedure itself as exactly eight numbered steps, each one to three sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, tagging any step that requires a human judgment call with [JUDGMENT]; after the list, give the three worked applications in the same order as above, each noting the step at which it is decided.
