My mother died last year, and since then several people have told me some version of the same thing: she lives on in me. My uncle put it in a way that stuck with me. He said a person is like a piece of music -- the sheet music isn't the performance, and when one orchestra stops playing, the piece isn't gone if another orchestra still knows it by heart. My mother "played herself" through me for thirty years, he said, so a real part of her is still being performed, just on different instruments. He wasn't offering this as a mere comfort. He argued from it: because my habits, turns of phrase, and ways of handling conflict were shaped by her, grief's finality is overstated, and I should feel her death as a relocation rather than an ending.

Around the same time I read an account by a writer whose wife had died suddenly, in which he argued -- seriously, not poetically -- that a coarse-grained copy of her patterns went on operating inside him, and that this was not a metaphor but a literal, if lower-resolution, continuation of her. That emboldened my uncle's analogy in my mind, but the more I lean on it the less sure I am of what it's carrying.

I'd like an audit. Please map the analogy explicitly -- a table of correspondences is fine -- covering at least: what plays the role of the sheet music, the performance, the orchestra, and "knowing the piece by heart" when the target is a person persisting in someone else's brain. Then separate the places where the mapping breaks. Some candidates I've been chewing on, though I don't trust my list: a score is fixed and a person isn't; an orchestra plays from the outside while my dispositions act from the inside; two orchestras can play the same piece simultaneously, but does anything like that make sense for a self; a performance that drifts from the score is still a performance, but is a degraded copy of my mother still my mother; and the piece presumably doesn't care who plays it, whereas it seems to matter to me whether she is in there. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion my uncle drew, and which are just pedantry? What does the analogy quietly import -- about what a self is, about identity over time, about whether copies count -- that it never argues for? And when the music-analogy is fully spelled out, does it support the relocation-not-ending conclusion, or does it only support something much weaker, like influence? If there's a better analogy for whatever part of his claim does survive, name it and say why it fits better.

Please end with a single line stating the bottom line, beginning "Verdict:".
