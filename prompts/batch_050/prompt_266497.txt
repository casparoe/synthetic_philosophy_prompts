So last night I got into this huge argument with my roommate (she's in law school, I'm doing a phil PhD) about how committees should reach verdicts, and it kinda spiraled. We were talking about a hiring committee case: three criteria -- research quality, teaching, and "fit" -- and the rule that a candidate gets an offer only if the committee judges them good on all three. I was defending the position that the committee should vote separately on each criterion and just let the chips fall, even though, as she gleefully pointed out, you can get a majority saying yes on research, a majority saying yes on teaching, a majority saying yes on fit, and yet a majority saying no to hiring, because the majorities are made up of different people. She says that outcome is obviously insane and proves premise-by-premise voting is broken. My view, which I've now committed to presenting at our department's work-in-progress seminar next Thursday, is stronger and probably dumber: the "insane" outcome isn't a bug, it's the procedure correctly revealing that the group as a group never actually endorsed the conjunction, and if the committee wants to hire the person anyway they should have to reopen deliberation and change some individual minds rather than just vote on the bottom line directly. I call this the "deliberation-or-nothing" position -- no quota rules, no supermajority patches, no weighing the conclusion vote against the premise votes.

The audience: it's a mixed crowd, which is the problem. There are two formal social choice people who know the impossibility results cold and will immediately want to know how my view handles agendas where the premises are interconnected rather than logically independent (like when "fit" partly supervenes on research and teaching), plus they always ask about strategic voting. There's a legal theorist who thinks in terms of the doctrinal paradox and cares about whether courts owe litigants reasoned, coherent verdicts -- I suspect he'll say my view is fine for hiring but catastrophic for actual judgments. And there are a few deliberative-democracy folks who will be suspicious that my "reopen deliberation" clause just smuggles in the conclusion-based outcome through the back door, since everyone in the room will know the point of reopening is to get the hire through.

Can you draft me a Q&A prep sheet? I want the objections roughly in the order they're likely to come up and flagged by who'll raise them, phrased the way these specific people would actually phrase them (the social choice folks talk in terms of consistency conditions and manipulability, the legal guy talks about reason-giving and legitimacy, etc.). For each one: a two-minute reply, what I should concede outright, and where my reply is softest. Also tell me which of my two escape hatches -- biting the bullet that the inconsistent verdict is fine, or leaning on the reopen-deliberation clause -- is the weaker one, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, because I genuinely don't have an answer to it yet.
