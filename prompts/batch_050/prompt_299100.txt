I work as a data analyst at a regional hospital, and last month I ran into Simpson's paradox in the wild: our new post-surgical protocol looked worse than the old one in the aggregate, yet better within every severity stratum. Next week I have thirty minutes to explain Simpson's paradox to our department's clinicians — people comfortable reading a table of percentages but with no formal statistics training — so that it stops being a curiosity and becomes something they actively check for. Plan the explanation rather than deliver it: what has to come first and why (should I open with the paradoxical table or with the idea of a confounder? does any of this require conditional probability as a prerequisite?), the single example that should carry the whole talk — should it be our actual surgical numbers or a constructed case with cleaner arithmetic, and what must that example have and lack for the reversal to be visible rather than asserted — and the misconceptions I should expect, such as the assumption that one of the two answers is simply the correct one, or that this is just small-sample noise, or that bigger datasets are immune, with the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. Finish with one question I can pose at the end that would show whether the idea has actually landed, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — causal diagrams, perhaps, or the mathematics of non-collapsibility — and how to say so without making the audience feel the interesting part was withheld.
