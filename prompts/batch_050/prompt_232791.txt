You are a senior philosopher of science with particular expertise in confirmation theory, the epistemology of explanatory inference, and the long-running dispute between explanationists and Bayesians. I host a long-form philosophy podcast recorded before a live audience of graduate students. Next week's guest is Dr. Miriam Adu, author of a new monograph defending inference to the best explanation (IBE) against its two classic detractors: the objection, associated with Bas van Fraassen, that IBE can at best select the best of a bad lot -- the true hypothesis may never enter the pool of candidates we consider -- and the objection that explanatory goodness (what Peter Lipton called loveliness) is no reliable guide to probable truth (likeliness). Adu's book rests on three distinctive commitments:

1. The regulated-pool reply. She concedes that IBE is only as reliable as the pool of hypotheses under consideration, but argues that in mature sciences the pool is not generated at random: background theory, the track record of failed predecessors, and anomaly-driven search systematically bias hypothesis generation toward containing the truth. The bad-lot objection, she claims, only bites where search is unconstrained.

2. Hard contrastivism. Going further than Lipton, she holds that there is no such inference as "p, because p best explains e." All legitimate explanatory inference is contrastive: "p rather than q, because p explains e better than q does." She further claims that comparative explanatory judgments just are, when made competently, implicit estimates of likelihood ratios -- which is her bridge between loveliness and likeliness.

3. Heuristic reconciliation with Bayesianism. She grants that nothing in her view outruns what an ideal Bayesian agent could compute, but insists IBE earns its keep as the heuristic by which finite agents set and revise likelihoods and priors. Explanation, on her picture, is epistemically indispensable to creatures like us even if it is formally dispensable in principle.

Draft ten interview questions, in the order you would actually ask them, so that the pressure on her position builds across the hour rather than arriving all at once. Requirements:

- Every question must turn on the actual content of the three commitments above or on the classic objections they answer. No generic or biographical questions.
- For each question, write the follow-up you would ask in response to the reply Adu is most likely to give, given her stated commitments. The follow-up matters more than the question; say explicitly what answer you are anticipating.
- Include at least one question pressing whether the regulated-pool reply proves too much: eighteenth-century chemists' hypothesis pools were regulated in exactly her sense, yet excluded oxygen for a generation. Does her view license their phlogiston inferences as rational at the time, and is that a verdict she can live with?
- Include at least one question pressing commitment 2 from the Bayesian side: if comparative loveliness is nothing over and above a likelihood-ratio estimate, in what sense is explanation doing any work rather than merely relabeling the Bayesian machinery?
- Include at least one question on unconceived alternatives: the historical record suggests scientists have repeatedly failed even to conceive the eventually victorious hypothesis. Contrastive inference among live options seems structurally blind to this. Where does that leave hard contrastivism?
- End the sequence with a question about what evidence or argument could count against the view -- what would she have to discover to conclude the book was wrong?
- Include one question she is unlikely to have faced on the book tour: is the book's own argument for IBE itself an instance of IBE? If yes, is that circular or virtuously self-supporting? If no, by what non-explanatory inference was her philosophy of inference established?
- Aim for questions a sharp, charitable host would ask -- pressing where the literature presses, not grandstanding.
