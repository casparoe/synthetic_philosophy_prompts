I'm in a graduate seminar on Scanlon's contractualism, and I've hit a wall on the aggregation problem that I'd like explained intuitively rather than formally. The basic machinery I think I understand: an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle no one could reasonably reject, and the assessment of complaints proceeds person by person, comparing each affected individual's grievance rather than pooling them. What I genuinely can't get a grip on is *why* this individualist restriction makes aggregation so difficult, and whether Scanlon's own way out actually works. The case that bothers me is the stylized one where a rescuer can either save one person from death or spare a vast number of strangers each a very minor harm. Scanlon famously holds that in at least some such cases the numbers shouldn't count — the many people's complaints are each too trivial to rival the one person's, so you save the one. That verdict seems defensible. But once you adjust the stakes — one death against many cases of permanent paralysis, or one death against many deaths — most people (including, apparently, Scanlon) want the numbers to matter, and it's unclear to me what in the contractualist framework licenses that shift without smuggling in the aggregative reasoning the framework was designed to avoid.

My specific confusions are these. First, what exactly is the principled barrier? Is the problem that interpersonal aggregation treats persons as vessels for value rather than as sources of claims, or something more structural about how reasonable rejection works? Second, Scanlon proposes a kind of tie-breaking maneuver: where the strongest individual complaints stand equal, look to who has the next-strongest complaint and so on down, letting the greater balance of complaints decide. Does this proposal actually resolve the hard cases, or does it only relabel the intuitive aggregation it can't justify — and what is the strongest objection to it? Third, is there a stable, non-arbitrary line between the cases where contractualism should ignore numbers and the cases where it shouldn't, or should a contractualist just bite the bullet somewhere?

Please lead with a one-sentence bottom line on whether the tie-break proposal succeeds, then explain the underlying issue in plain terms, using the one-versus-many rescue case throughout rather than multiplying examples.
