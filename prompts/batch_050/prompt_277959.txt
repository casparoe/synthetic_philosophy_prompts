I cover biology for a general-interest science magazine, and maybe once or twice a month I end up writing about a study where the upshot is that some trait evolved because it gave its bearers higher fitness. A philosopher friend recently ruined this beat for me by pointing out that if fitness just means reproductive success, then saying the trait spread because it increased fitness amounts to saying it spread because the organisms carrying it left more offspring, which is close to just restating what spreading is. And yeah, going back over my notes, a lot of the press releases I work from define fitness using the exact survival or offspring counts the study then reports. I gather the standard fix in philosophy of biology is to treat fitness as a propensity, a tendency to leave offspring that an organism can have even when it doesn't pan out, since the well-adapted antelope can still get hit by lightning. But that seems to bring its own trouble, because you can't measure a tendency directly, only its actual results, so the fitness claim can survive even when the prediction fails, which starts to sound unfalsifiable to me.

So here's what I want. Turn this into a procedure I can actually run on a paper or press release in about twenty minutes before deadline, to decide whether the fitness claim in the story is doing real explanatory work or is basically circular, and what that means for how I can write the article. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, what to do when the paper just doesn't contain the information (which will be often), and be honest about which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases. First, a two-year field study finding that deeper-beaked birds left more surviving offspring, with the authors concluding beak depth is an adaptation to drought. Second, a mesocosm experiment with replicate fish populations, predation pressure manipulated across tanks, coloration evolving in the predicted direction in most replicates, plus a transplant experiment showing the pattern is heritable. Third, a paleontology paper arguing that the sail on a fossil reptile's back was selected for thermoregulation, where obviously no reproductive data can exist. Make sure at least one of these comes out as spike it or hedge it hard in a case where I'd really rather run the splashy version, because the sail story is the fun one.

One formatting request: I want to forward this to my editor and a couple of colleagues, so write it as continuous prose, like an email. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just plain paragraphs, even for the procedure itself. And keep it short enough that I'll actually use it when I'm on deadline, which is the whole point.
