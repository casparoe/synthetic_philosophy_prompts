Write an essay on whether AI systems that give advice to humans (rather than acting autonomously) should reason as utilitarians or as deontologists. In your analysis, take seriously three complications that the standard debate often ignores. First, an advisory system's reasoning is, at least in principle, auditable by its users: a rule-based recommendation can be checked against the rule, whereas a welfare calculation can only be checked by redoing the calculation — consider what this asymmetry implies for which moral framework an advisor *ought* to use, as opposed to which framework is true. Second, an advisor's recommendations are inputs to a human decision-maker who may systematically over- or under-weight them; ask whether the right framework for the advisor depends on predictable features of its audience rather than on the moral facts alone. Third, consider whether a deontologically constrained advisor can be quietly more utilitarian in effect than an openly utilitarian one, given how people actually react to recommendations that trade off identified victims against statistical benefits. Weigh these considerations against the familiar arguments on both sides, but do not end on a both-sides note: reach and defend a verdict about which framework such systems should use, and state clearly what would have to be true for your verdict to be wrong.
