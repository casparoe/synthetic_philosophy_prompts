I'm a junior in high school, and for my civics capstone project I've spent the semester tracking the published decisions of our state's School Choice Review Board, the body that rules on disputes between families and districts over transfers, charters, and homeschooling arrangements. I'd like you to respond as a legal philosopher who specializes in education policy and the logic of precedent. Please think carefully and work through this step by step: before settling on an answer, brainstorm at least three general principles that could rationalize the Board's full track record, test each one against every prior decision, and discard any that fail even once. Only then move to the prediction.

Here are the Board's decisions so far, as I've summarized them from its opinions:

1. A family in a district whose schools failed state standards three years running sought a transfer to a higher-performing public school across the district line. Approved.
2. A family in an adequate, mid-performing district sought to homeschool with an annual portfolio review and standardized testing. Approved.
3. A family in the same failing district as case 1 sought to homeschool but refused any form of external assessment and submitted no curriculum plan, citing a philosophical objection to state evaluation. Denied.
4. A family in an adequate district sought to send their child, at full private expense, to an unaccredited academy that administers no standardized tests. Denied.
5. A nonprofit charter proposal in a struggling district, with lottery admissions and mandatory state testing. Approved.
6. A for-profit charter proposal in an affluent suburb, with selective admissions and only internal self-evaluation. Denied.

The new case, which the Board hears next month: a family in a failing district wants to homeschool through an accredited online private school whose coursework generates detailed internal assessments and progress reports, which the family will share with the district -- but they refuse state standardized testing and the district's portfolio review, again on principle.

My questions: What will the Board most likely decide, and which principle is it implicitly running? Which of the precedents is the closest analog, and why? If the track record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so, identify the two strongest competing principles and what each predicts, and then describe the single hypothetical future case that would most cleanly reveal which principle the Board is actually committed to. I'm less interested in what the right policy is than in what this particular Board, given its own history, is committed to.
