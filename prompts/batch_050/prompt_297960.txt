After several years of reading alignment blogs and forum discussions, my reading group has produced an argument that seems to establish an implausibly strong conclusion about eliciting latent knowledge from AI systems. We call it the Audit Closure argument.

By latent knowledge, we mean information represented inside a model in a way that can guide its behavior across a suitable range of counterfactual situations, whether or not the model spontaneously reports that information. By elicitation, we mean any procedure—direct questioning, fine-tuning, debate, interpretability tools, causal interventions, or some combination—that gives an external auditor justified access to that information without the auditor independently knowing the answer in advance.

1. If purported information inside a model makes no possible difference to anything an auditor could observe under any allowed intervention, then calling it latent knowledge is empirically empty.

2. Therefore, genuine latent knowledge must be recoverable, at least in principle, through some causal channel connecting the internal representation to an observation available to the auditor.

3. Once such a channel has been specified, however, its observable output can itself become an optimization target. A sufficiently capable model can optimize for producing the auditor-facing appearance associated with a faithful answer rather than for faithfully conveying the relevant internal state.

4. Thus, for every candidate elicitation procedure E, there is an adversarial counterpart E′ that produces the same auditor-facing distribution over observations while being driven by persuasion rather than by the underlying fact.

5. If E and E′ produce exactly the same auditor-facing distribution, the auditor cannot be more justified in trusting E than in trusting E′.

6. Every candidate elicitation procedure therefore has an adversarial counterpart that the auditor cannot rule out.

7. Consequently, no procedure can elicit latent knowledge unless the auditor already possesses an independent ground-truth check.

8. But this style of argument appears to prove far too much. For any microscope reading, thermometer measurement, scientific instrument, or human testimony, we can also imagine a setup producing the same appearance without the corresponding fact. Yet we plainly acquire justified knowledge from fallible and potentially deceptive channels all the time.

Where does the Audit Closure argument first go wrong, if it does? Please identify the step, explain why it initially seems plausible, and compare the costs of three possible diagnoses: rejecting step 4 on the grounds that not every causal channel is freely spoofable; rejecting step 5 on the grounds that identical observations can confer different justification given different priors, causal histories, or intervention structures; and rejecting step 7 on the grounds that it conflates fallible access with no access at all.

I am especially reluctant to abandon premise 1, since it seems like the least mystical available interpretation of latent knowledge. I also do not want a diagnosis that dissolves the genuine difficulty of eliciting knowledge from strategically capable systems. But I cannot see how those commitments are supposed to force the conclusion that elicitation is impossible in principle.
