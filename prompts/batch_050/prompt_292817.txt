Exam question: State Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII). Explain why pairs of electrons in quantum mechanics appear to threaten it, and assess whether relational differences, such as spin correlations, rescue the principle.

Student's answer: Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) states that if, for every property F, object x has F if and only if object y has F, then x and y are numerically identical. Qualitative identity entails numerical identity.

Quantum mechanics appears to violate PII. Consider two electrons in the spin singlet state. All their intrinsic, state-independent properties coincide: both have the same mass, the same charge, and the same spin magnitude. Nor does their quantum state distinguish them: because the singlet is entangled, neither electron can be assigned its own pure state, and the reduced state of each is the same. The permutation symmetry of quantum mechanics confirms this: exchanging the two electrons leaves every possible measurement outcome unchanged, which shows that the two electrons are indiscernible, since no experiment could ever tell them apart. Hence PII is false in the quantum domain.

However, PII can be rescued by attending to relations. In the singlet state, the electrons' spins are anticorrelated: along any chosen axis, one electron is spin-up and the other is spin-down. Thus each electron possesses a property the other lacks — being spin-up along z, say — and this relational difference makes the two electrons absolutely discernible. PII is therefore vindicated: the apparent threat arose only from ignoring relational properties, and once those are included, no two electrons are ever qualitatively identical.
