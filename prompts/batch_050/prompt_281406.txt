I spent my career as an experimental evolutionary biologist — population genetics, mostly Drosophila — and since retiring I have been working my way through the philosophy of biology literature. The units of selection debate has left me genuinely puzzled, and I would like a diagnosis rather than yet another restatement of the positions. Please answer as a professional philosopher of biology would: think carefully, begin by brainstorming the candidate diagnoses before committing to one, and be willing to tell me that the question as I have framed it is misconceived.

Here is what puzzles me. When I was a working scientist, the level-of-selection question looked empirical: you ran the numbers, you looked for group-level adaptations, and Williams's old arguments about parsimony told you whether you needed to invoke selection above the individual. But the literature I am now reading seems to have dissolved the empirical content. Everyone accepts the same formalisms — the Price equation, inclusive fitness, covariance partitioning — and I keep encountering the claim that kin selection and multilevel selection are mathematically intertranslatable, two ways of carving up the same data, with the choice between them a matter of convention or heuristic convenience. If that is right, then a disagreement I took to be about the causal structure of the world has turned out to be about bookkeeping. And yet the participants clearly do not behave as if they are disagreeing about bookkeeping: the gene's-eye people and the multilevel-selection people still write as though something real is at stake, and philosophers keep proposing criteria — causal rather than statistical notions of selection, questions about which level bears adaptations, about what counts as an evolutionary individual, about screening-off relations — that are supposed to restore substance to the dispute.

So my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the debate has become a disagreement about what a causal explanation in biology has to do — the genic side wanting a single privileged accounting scheme, the multilevel side wanting explanations that track actual population structure — and that this is why no experimental result has ever settled it. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a live empirical or formal question still hiding inside it? Please distinguish carefully between three possibilities: that the disagreement is verbal, sustained by different uses of words like 'selection' and 'for the good of'; that it is substantive but rests on rival intuitions about causation and explanation that neither side can argue for from neutral ground; and that there remains a real, resolvable question — about when group-level traits count as adaptations, or about when screening-off tests pick out a level — that the disputants have simply failed to isolate. In each case, tell me what the evidence for that reading would look like, drawing on what proponents on each side actually say and concede.

Finally, whatever diagnosis you settle on: what would count as progress? A formal result, a conceptual distinction, an agreed taxonomy of cases, an admission that several questions have been run together under one heading? Or is this a debate that should be expected to persist indefinitely, and if so, is that a defect or simply what happens when a science's organising concepts outrun its mathematics?
