I am assembling the assessment framework for a public body that reviews whether allegedly discriminatory technologies — facial-recognition deployments, benefit-scoring algorithms, hostile street furniture — can be challenged as political by design rather than merely by use. Before we look at any candidate theory of the politics of artifacts, I want the specification such a theory must meet.

Write the desiderata: what an adequate account of when and how artifacts can be said to embody, enact, or evade politics has to deliver. Specifically I want:

1. The cases it must get right, with the consideration each case presses. Among others: large-scale systems said to demand centralised or authoritarian organisation by their very technical logic; machines designed with decent intentions that reliably shift advantage between groups; deliberate exclusion achieved through design; the famous case of the low parkway overpasses on Long Island, which should be included even though historians and sociologists have challenged the standard telling on both factual and interpretive grounds — the theory must say what happens to the underlying idea if the anecdote fails; and infrastructure-level cases such as gauges, standards and protocols, where the politics is rarely located in any single thing.

2. The distinctions it must respect: political by design versus political by use; inherent versus contingent politics; intended versus unintended effects; the artifact as constraint versus the artifact as one causal factor among many. For each distinction, say whether drawing it sharply would drain the theory of content or rescue it from overreach.

3. The empirical commitments it must be consistent with: findings from the social study of technology concerning interpretive flexibility, user appropriation, stabilisation and closure, and the fact that the same design can be political in one setting and inert in another. State explicitly whether this context-sensitivity is compatible with the demand for a crisp criterion separating political from non-political artifacts, or whether the two desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable. That tension is the part I most want addressed.

4. Which desiderata are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are preferences of particular camps — the causal-priority claims of technological determinists, the symmetry between human and non-human agents demanded by actor-network approaches, the neutrality intuition behind the view that technologies are mere tools, and the class-analytic lens of critical theory — with the motivation behind each.

5. Which single desideratum does the most to exclude candidates, and which currently popular positions it excludes.

Then, briefly and without a full scorecard: how Langdon Winner's two-category account (inherent versus contingent technological politics), actor-network treatments of mundane devices such as speed bumps, social-construction-of-technology accounts of stabilisation, and the plain instrumentalist claim that technologies are neutral tools fare against the list, noting where each fails a non-negotiable requirement.

Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — which desiderata are non-negotiable and whether any subset appears jointly unsatisfiable — then give the specification. No preamble, and do not restate the brief.
