I'm doing a talk in two weeks at our department's works-in-progress series and I want to go in prepared, because this is not friendly territory. My paper tries to rescue a person-affecting intuition from the nonidentity problem in climate policy cases. Roughly the argument: when we say a carbon-intensive policy "harms future generations," the people who exist under that policy wouldn't have existed under the greener alternative, so they can't claim to be worse off than they'd otherwise have been — standard Parfit setup. But I argue we can still make sense of the harm claim by comparing each actual person to their closest counterpart under the alternative policy, i.e. whoever would have occupied roughly their slot (same region, same generation, similar family role) had the other policy been chosen. On my view, "the policy harms generation 2100" comes out true iff the actual 2100-people are worse off than their counterparts would have been. I borrow the counterpart machinery loosely from modal metaphysics but I insist you don't need to buy into Lewis's ontology for the move to work, just the matching relation.

The audience is about twelve people. Two population ethics grad students who know Reasons and Persons inside out and will pounce on any sloppiness about same-number versus different-number choices. A Kantian who thinks the whole aggregationist framing is rotten from the start. A climate economist visiting from the policy school who cares about discount rates and visibly thinks metaphysics is a distraction from cost-benefit analysis. Plus a few genuine fence-sitters who are actually persuadable. My advisor says the counterpart move "has been tried and failed somewhere" but couldn't remember where, wich is not exactly reassuring.

So give me a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this specific crowd — not generic nonidentity objections I could find in a textbook — in roughly the order and tone they'll come in, keeping in mind the grad students will be technical, the economist impatient, and the Kantian unimpressed. For each objection: how damaging it is, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a two-or-three-sentence reply I can deliver on the spot, and where that reply is softest. Also flag wich objections I can deflect by restricting my claim to same-number policy choices and wich ones follow me even there, since that's my main escape hatch. And finally, tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and whether I should preempt it in the talk itself or leave it buried and pray.
