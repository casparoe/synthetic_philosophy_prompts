"Grice's theory rests on the idea that conversation is a cooperative enterprise governed by a set of maxims -- be truthful, be informative, be relevant, be perspicuous -- which function as rules of good conversational conduct. When a speaker deliberately and openly violates one of these maxims, the hearer, assuming the speaker is still being cooperative, is entitled to work out an additional intended message, and this inferred message is a conversational implicature. Because implicatures arise from the interaction of context with general principles of rational behavior rather than from the conventional meanings of the words used, Grice held that they are never part of what is said, and he proposed cancelability without contradiction as the definitive test for distinguishing an implicature from genuine semantic content. Underlying all of this is a more general account on which a speaker's meaning something reduces to the speaker's intention to produce a belief or other effect in an audience; meaning is thus at bottom a psychological matter, with linguistic convention playing no essential role. Critics have pointed out that the maxims routinely conflict and that Grice supplied no algorithm for resolving such conflicts, from which it follows that the theory cannot predict hearers' interpretations and therefore fails as an explanation of communication."
