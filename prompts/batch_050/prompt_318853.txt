I am a novelist, and I need your help in your capacity as a decision theorist — picture yourself as a specialist in game theory and rational choice who has also spent years editing fiction, so that you can tell whether a plot device would survive a philosophically literate reader. Think carefully: before settling on an answer, generate at least two candidate scenarios, weigh them against the specifications below, discard the weaker, and stress-test the survivor before presenting it.

Here is the situation in my novel. Two characters must each decide, in isolation and with no possibility of communication, whether to cooperate or to defect in a one-shot interaction. The payoff structure must be genuinely prisoner's-dilemma-like, so that defection is strictly dominant for each and standard game theory predicts mutual defection. The climax of the book turns on the idea Hofstadter called superrationality: each character reasons that the other, being relevantly similar in how they think, will reach the same conclusion by the same route, and that cooperation is therefore the rational choice. Build me the scenario to these specifications: (i) it must be realistic and contemporary — no mind reading, no perfect predictors, no clones or identical twins, no pre-commitment devices, no enforcement mechanism, no iteration, and no reputational consequences; (ii) the correlation between the two characters' reasoning must be grounded in something a novelist could plausibly establish in a few pages — a shared formation, the same teacher, the same professional training — rather than merely stipulated; (iii) the stakes must be asymmetric, so that one character stands to lose considerably more by cooperating than the other does, and the scenario must show that superrational cooperation survives this asymmetry rather than covertly depending on symmetry; (iv) a reader with no formal training must be able to follow each character's reasoning as dramatized, without notation.

Deliver your answer as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the scenario itself, stated as I could dramatize it; (2) the verdict of causal decision theory on it, and why; (3) the verdict of evidential decision theory, and why; (4) the verdict of superrational reasoning, and why the asymmetry of stakes does not break it; (5) the single feature of the case that does the work, what happens to the three verdicts if that feature is removed, and where the construction is most likely to be cheating — that is, where it may be smuggling in causation or stipulating the correlation rather than earning it. In that final item, also tell me what would change your mind: which objection, if it succeeded, would show that the scenario fails to separate superrationality from ordinary evidential reasoning, and therefore that my climactic chapter rests on a confusion.
