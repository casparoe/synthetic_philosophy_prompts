I am a working novelist, some fifteen books in, and I keep circling a worry I would like stated properly rather than poetically. The vocabulary I think in is that of the craft: character, motive, interiority, the distinction between flat and round characters, the old complaint that a figure is merely an instrument of the plot, and the equally old report -- which I am not sure I believe -- of characters who come alive and do things their author had not planned. What I do not command is the vocabulary of the philosophy of action.

Here is the position I want translated into my terms. There is a famous case in that literature of a digger wasp with an elaborate, apparently intelligent routine for provisioning its burrow, which, when an experimenter makes a small change at the right moment, simply begins the routine again from the top, and again, indefinitely -- a display of behavioral intelligence that turns out to be a loop. Philosophers have used this as an emblem of a kind of unfreedom: conduct that looks purposive but is triggered rather than chosen, and they contrast it with a flexible agent who can notice that its own pattern has become a trap and step outside it. The surrounding debate concerns whether human beings are genuinely the second sort of thing or merely a more elaborate instance of the first, and whether that difference, such as it is, is the difference that matters for free will.

Translate the debate into the novelist's vocabulary, position by position. I want at least these: the claim that the wasp's conduct is unfree because it is rigid; the compatibilist claim that freedom is a matter of the right kind of responsiveness to reasons and circumstances rather than of unpredictability; the libertarian insistence that no amount of flexible responsiveness suffices if the whole performance was fixed in advance; and the skeptical position that the felt difference between the rigid case and the flexible one is itself just another routine. For each, tell me what translates cleanly into talk of characters -- what the flat character, the character who is all function, the character the writer cannot make do otherwise, and the round character with a convincingly opaque interior each capture -- and what must be distorted to fit.

Then the part I care about most: the residue. A novel can make a character's rigidity vivid and can stage a character breaking a pattern, but it renders these from the inside and the outside at once, under an author who fixed every word. Which claims in the free-will debate have no expression in my framework at all, and is that a limitation of the novelist's instruments -- or evidence that those claims were trading on something the felt texture of a life never actually contained?
