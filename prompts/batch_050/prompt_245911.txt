I'm a committed presentist — I hold that only present things and events exist, that the past and future are not realms of being but ways things were and will be — and I recently got into a dispute at our department's reading group over McTaggart's 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time." I don't want to debate his conclusion (that time is unreal) here; what caught me was an intermediate step, where he argues that change cannot be grounded in the B-series alone, on the grounds that:

"An event can never cease to be an event. It can never get out of any time series in which it once is."

My interlocutor's claim was that presentism is precisely the view that denies this sentence — that on my view events do come into existence and do cease to exist, simpliciter, and that this is what genuine change consists in. I'm inclined to agree, but it made me realize I've never systematically asked what would actually count against presentism, rather than for it.

So: take presentism as the position under examination, and use the quoted passage as your entry point — first, say whether my interlocutor is right that presentism contradicts McTaggart's claim here, or whether a presentist can accept the sentence under some reading. Then give me a map of presentism's exposure: which observations, physical results, formal arguments, or intuitions would count against it, ranked from potentially fatal to merely awkward. I'm especially interested in (a) whether the relativity of simultaneity is genuinely a defeater or only a defeater-for-some-versions, and what a spacetime structure would have to look like for presentism to be straightforwardly true of it; (b) the truthmaker problem for truths about the past, and whether the standard presentist replies are answers or evasions; and (c) whether presentism has been qualified into something that no empirical or formal result could touch, and if so whether that is a defect for a metaphysical view of this kind. Distinguish throughout between what a committed presentist would concede counts against the view and what only its critics would count.

Please structure your response as follows: "Reading the passage" (about 120 words, adjudicating the dispute above); "The exposure map" (about 400 words, defeaters ranked from fatal to awkward, with one line each on why); "What presentists themselves concede" (about 150 words); "The insulation question" (about 150 words, on whether the view risks anything at all); and "Bottom line" (two or three sentences on how exposed presentism actually is).
