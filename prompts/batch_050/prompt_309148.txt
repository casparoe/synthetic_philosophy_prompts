I have no training in philosophy, but I have been chewing on a thought experiment I found online for months and I want to get my thinking in order. The setup: would it be worse for one person to undergo fifty years of excruciating torture, or for some astronomically large number of people to each get a single dust speck in one eye, annoying for a second and then gone forever? My gut says the torture is worse no matter how many specks you pile up, but I can't defend that without seeming to claim that a speck's worth of suffering, multiplied by any finite number, never adds to anything -- and that seems crazy too, since surely enough barely-noticeable discomforts can pile into something that matters, or else nothing bad would ever result from millions of small decisions.

What I want from you is not your answer to the case. I want the specification. Write the list of requirements that any principle for adding up harms must satisfy before I should trust its verdict on cases like this one. For each requirement, give me the consideration or the case that motivates it. Some candidates I've been mulling, which you should assess and correct rather than just accept:

1. The principle must give a definite verdict on torture-versus-specks rather than punting.
2. It must respect the fact that harms come in a continuous spectrum of intensity -- you can construct a chain of cases from a stubbed toe down to a dust speck where each step is barely worse than the last -- without letting that chain smuggle in the conclusion that everything is comparable to everything.
3. It must not imply that some trivial harm, repeated enough times across enough people, outweighs the worst suffering imaginable, unless it can make that conclusion palatable rather than absurd.
4. It must give the same answer whether a harm is concentrated in one person or distributed, when the total is held fixed -- or else explain why distribution itself matters.
5. It must comport with ordinary practice, where we accept that traffic safety measures, mildly annoying regulations, and minor medical side effects are justified by benefits spread across millions, without that practice committing us to trading away a torture victim.
6. It must not secretly rely on the number of affected people being finite-but-unknown in a way that collapses when the numbers get truly enormous.

For each requirement, mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable for any adequate principle or merely the preference of one way of thinking about harm. And here is the part I most care about: I suspect some subset of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, because my gut verdict on the case seems to conflict with the smooth-spectrum intuition. If that is right, prove it as best you can -- show me exactly which requirements are in tension and what has to give. If I am wrong and a consistent package exists, sketch it. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves; I will judge candidate principles against the list myself afterward.
