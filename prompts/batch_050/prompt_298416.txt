I cover biology and medicine for a general-audience science magazine, and two or three times a month I file a story that turns on a claim about what some biological thing is *for*: a gene, an organ, a behavior, a stretch of non-coding DNA. My fact-checkers keep flagging my drafts for teleology, my editors keep proposing headlines of the form "Scientists discover the function of X," and I keep having the same argument from scratch. I want a standing procedure instead.

Here is the conceptual background I'm working with, which you should take as given. Philosophers of biology distinguish at least two things scientists mean by "function": what a part or trait currently does within a system (its causal role, as the heart's role is pumping blood), and what it was historically selected by evolution to do (its etiological or selected-effect function, the reason it exists at all). These can come apart in both directions: a stretch of DNA can be biochemically active without that activity being its evolved function, and a trait can be useless now yet carry a selection history. Most bad coverage of "junk DNA," genes "for" traits, and vestigial organs comes from running the two senses together.

Write a screening procedure I can run on a paper or press release in about thirty minutes, before deadline, with no philosophy training and no access to the authors beyond a slow email exchange. It must specify: the diagnostic questions to ask in order; which answers license which verbs in my copy ("does," "contributes to," "has the function of," "evolved to," "is for"); the default hedged phrasing when the evidence supports only the weaker claim; the points where the procedure stops and hands me a judgment call, marked honestly as judgment calls; and what the procedure cannot settle at all.

Then apply it to three cases: (1) a large genomics consortium claims most of the human genome is "functional," where the evidence is detectable biochemical activity; (2) a knockout study in which mice lacking a gene show a specific defect, which the authors describe as revealing the gene's function; (3) an evolutionary paper argues a long-mysterious human trait was selected for a specific benefit, on comparative evidence alone. At least one application should block a headline my editor will want; flag that one explicitly and give me the defensible alternative.

Format: numbered steps, then short worked analyses, then a closing paragraph on the procedure's limits. American English, around 900 words.
