I'm preparing a session for an interdisciplinary seminar on literature and philosophy, and I've paired Tolstoy's novella The Death of Ivan Ilyich with the aesthetic theory he set out in his treatise What Is Art?, written after his religious conversion. In that treatise, as my students and I understand it, Tolstoy defines art as a human activity in which one person consciously, by means of external signs, transmits feelings they have lived through so that others are infected by them; he ranks works by the individuality of the feeling conveyed, the clarity of its transmission, and above all the sincerity of the artist, and he reserves the highest praise for art flowing from the religious perception of its age, which for him means the Christian ideal of the brotherhood of all people, along with simpler universal art whose feelings are accessible to everyone without special training. On these grounds he notoriously condemned most of the canon, including Shakespeare, much of Beethoven, and nearly all of his own earlier fiction.

What I want from you is a reading of the novella strictly by the standards of the treatise: does The Death of Ivan Ilyich pass its own author's test, and where does the test itself start to buckle under the reading? Some tensions I'd like addressed. First, the novella is a triumph of construction and irony: the opening scene in which Ivan's colleagues respond to his death by calculating promotions and whether to attend the funeral, and the compressed, almost dismissive summary of his life, are highly wrought literary devices, while the treatise treats polished technique, artifice, and appeals to refined taste as marks of counterfeit art. Is the craft here a vehicle of sincerity, or exactly the borrowed, manufactured infection Tolstoy condemns? Second, the feeling the ending transmits, pity and the release from the fear of death, looks like precisely the religious feeling the treatise demands of the highest art, but the bulk of the novella transmits something negative: disgust at falseness, at the lie of a well-ordered bourgeois life. Can an infection theory of art account for satire and revulsion, or only for positive feelings passed heart to heart? Third, consider Gerasim, the peasant servant whose uncomplicated acceptance of nursing Ivan is the novella's one pocket of genuine human warmth: does he function as the conduit of that simple universal art Tolstoy prized, and does it matter that we see him only through the consciousness of a dying aristocrat? Fourth, I want an honest accounting of where the novella resists the theory altogether, because I suspect it does: for instance, whether the story's power depends on precisely the literary artistry the treatise cannot explain or condone, and whether infection is even a workable criterion, since a skilled manipulator infects too.

Please write the reading as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Don't reduce the novella to a mere illustration of the treatise, and don't soften the treatise to make the fit look neater than it is; I want faithfulness in both directions. If your verdict is that the novella passes Tolstoy's test only in part, say which part and why. And end by telling me what, if anything, the fiction shows about artistic value and sincerity that the treatise's argumentative prose could not show on its own, since that's the question I most want my students to leave with.
