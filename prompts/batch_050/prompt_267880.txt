Write the joint statement that could conclude a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two camps on the two-envelope paradox. Background both camps accept: two envelopes are prepared, one containing twice as much money as the other; you choose one at random and may switch to the other before opening either. The notorious switching argument runs: if your envelope contains x, the other contains x/2 or 2x with equal probability, so its expected value is 5x/4, and you should switch; yet the same argument is available to whoever holds the other envelope, so it proves too much. Camp S (the symmetry camp) holds that the argument fails for a purely probabilistic reason: for any proper distribution over the amounts, the probability that the other envelope contains 2x given that yours contains x is not 1/2 for every x, and averaging over x the expected gain from switching is exactly zero; nothing about infinity need ever be mentioned. Camp E (the expectations camp) holds that this diagnosis is superficial, because switching recommendations of the same paradoxical shape reappear for proper distributions whose expected value diverges, so the genuine problem lies in the comparison of infinite expectations, and the finite cases are merely where the trap happens not to spring.

The deliverable is the document both camps would sign at the end of the collaboration, containing:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover, at minimum: which variants are in play (the closed-envelope case, where no amount is observed, versus the open-envelope case, where you see your amount before deciding); what the symmetry between the two players establishes without any calculation; the agreed verdict on the naive 5x/4 computation in the finite case; the agreed status of the known constructions where, conditional on almost every observed amount, switching really does have positive expected gain, even though unconditional switching gains nothing; and what it would mean to dissolve the paradox as opposed to merely blocking the bad inference.

2. A disagreements table, each row phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position. It should include at least: whether the finite case is fully explained without any mention of infinite expectation; whether the divergent-expectation constructions exhibit the same paradox or a different one wearing the same clothes; and what a resolution is required to accomplish (a diagnosis of the fallacy, a decision procedure, or both).

3. A list of cruxes: sub-questions such that settling any one would move at least one camp. Candidates you should consider include whether a finite-expectation proper distribution exists for which the open-envelope recommendation to switch is correct for every observed amount, and whether Camp E's diagnosis has anything to say about the closed-envelope case, where no amount is ever observed and no infinite sum appears.

4. For each crux, a case, construction, or argument that both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction or verdict recorded in advance of the analysis.

If some part of the disagreement turns out to be verbal — for instance, over what counts as *the* two-envelope paradox, or over what a resolution must deliver — identify that part explicitly and draft the sentence in which both sides concede it. Do not adjudicate which camp is right overall; the statement should close with the residual disagreement stated as precisely as the collaboration can manage.
