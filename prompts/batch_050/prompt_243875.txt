I'm putting together a graduate seminar unit on norms of assertion, and I want a decision tree that takes a student from "I agree that asserting differs from guessing, conjecturing, and merely speculating aloud" to a named position on what the norm (or norms) of assertion is. The usual problem with taxonomies of this debate is that they sort by slogan -- "knowledge norm," "truth norm," "justification norm" -- so students just pick whichever label sounds strongest. I want an instrument that sorts them by their verdicts on cases instead.

Please build the tree to these specifications:

1. Between six and nine branch nodes, each phrased as a question about a concrete case or a claim a student can evaluate without knowing which position the answer commits them to. Cases I want used somewhere on the tree: a Gettiered speaker whose belief is true and fully justified; a "selfless" asserter who states what she takes to be the best-supported view while privately disbelieving it (e.g., the teacher presenting material she thinks is false but pedagogically required); a modest speaker who asserts "p, though I don't know that p"; a speaker passing on an expert's testimony that she cannot herself vouch for; and an assertion made collectively, by a committee or an automated system, where no single individual knows the proposition asserted. Do not use all of them at one node -- distribute them so each does real classificatory work.

2. Leaves should include: the knowledge norm; a truth norm; a justification or reasonable-belief norm of the sort developed against knowledge norms; a sincerity or belief norm; a multi-norm or context-sensitive view on which the operative standard shifts with stakes or purpose; and a norm-skeptical leaf for the view that assertion is not constituted by any norm at all. Add further leaves if your branching questions generate them.

3. Build from the logical space, not from the list of names: derive the splits from what the diagnostic questions actually distinguish, note any combination of answers that yields an unoccupied position, and say briefly whether that slot is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unpopular, or genuinely unexplored.

4. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what further case -- real or hypothetical -- would separate them.

5. At the end, add a short note that separates what is common ground among participants in this debate (e.g., that some cases are desiderata any account must handle) from what is genuinely contested (e.g., whether the counterexamples offered against the knowledge norm succeed, or whether the debate is even about a constitutive rule rather than a defeasible epistemic standard). Do not present any one position as the consensus, and where my description of a case or view above is itself a contested reading, say so rather than silently adopting it.
