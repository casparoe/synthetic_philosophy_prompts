For a graduate seminar on the ethics of informal sanction, I want the adequacy conditions fixed before we assess any further accounts of so-called cancel culture. Write the desiderata that a theory of social punishment — sanction imposed by communities, audiences, employers, and platforms rather than by the state — must meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The list should address at least the following.

First, which cases it must classify correctly: a congregation expelling a member, a university disinviting a speaker, a mass online shaming campaign, an employer declining to renew a contract, a community shunning an offender, consumers boycotting a firm. Any account will have to say which of these are punishment and which are something else — self-protection, refusal of association, protest, ordinary market behavior — and a theory that lumps them all together or all apart has failed before it begins.

Second, which distinctions it cannot collapse: punishment versus mere avoidance or exclusion, punishment versus criticism, having standing to censure versus having reason to withdraw cooperation, the aim of a sanction versus its foreseeable effects.

Third, which features of the actual phenomenon it must explain or accommodate rather than idealize away: the absence of proportionality mechanisms, the lack of anything resembling due process, the tendency of sanctions to cascade beyond their intended target, and the expressive dimension — the fact that participants typically understand themselves to be doing justice, not merely defending their interests.

Fourth, which normative questions the taxonomy must leave open rather than settle by definition — in particular, whether the observation that no state is involved answers the demand for fair procedure or instead sharpens it.

Mark which desiderata you take to be non-negotiable and which reflect the commitments of a particular camp. If any pair of requirements looks jointly unsatisfiable, say so and explain the tension. Then, briefly, indicate how three familiar orientations fare against the list: the view that punishment is essentially a function of political authority, so that 'social punishment' is a category error; the functionalist view that any cost imposed for a norm violation is punishment whatever its source; and an expressive account on which social sanction counts as punishment when and because it stigmatizes a violation. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I will run the scoring exercise myself in class.
