A colleague of mine, who teaches political theory but not ethics, gave me this summary of the philosophical debate over why promises bind, and I'd like to know whether I can trust it:

"Philosophers have two main accounts of why promises generate obligations. The first is the practice view: promises bind because we have a social convention of promising, and conventions bind because they are mutually beneficial. So a promise made to someone outside your community, or made in a society without the convention, creates no obligation at all. The second, newer account -- usually associated with T.M. Scanlon -- grounds the obligation in the expectations you create: when you promise, you invite another person to rely on you, and disappointing reliance you deliberately invited is wrong. On this view, the moral work is done entirely by the other party's expectation of performance, so if the promisee never comes to expect performance -- say, they cynically assume you'll break your word -- no obligation arises. The standard objection to the practice view is circularity: the convention of promising only has force because people already think promises ought to be kept, so the convention can't be the source of the obligation. This objection is generally taken to have refuted the practice view, which is why most philosophers now hold the expectation account."

Two things make me unsure. First, the claim that expectations do *all* the moral work seems too strong -- surely something could bind me even when the promisee is skeptical. Second, I'm not convinced the circularity objection is as decisive as he makes it sound. But I can't articulate where the summary goes wrong, if it does.

Please audit the summary: which parts are accurate characterizations of the two views, which are common simplifications a specialist would qualify, and which are misrepresentations or conflations? In particular, address whether the expectation view really makes obligation evaporate when the promisee doesn't expect performance, whether the practice view really has the implausible implications about outsiders that my colleague attributes to it, and whether the circularity objection is rightly described as a refutation. Finally, given a corrected version of the two views, does his conclusion that the expectation account has won the debate follow?
