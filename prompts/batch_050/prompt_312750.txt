In my behavioral econ reading group someone keeps deploying the line that "cooperation emerges from the shadow of the future" like it's a theorem. Most recently he used it to close down a discussion about whether it could ever make sense to cooperate with a stranger you'll never meet again -- his move was basically: cooperation only pays when future interactions are at stake, so one-shot cooperation is just irrational, full stop. I suspect the slogan is doing way more work than it's entitled to, but I can't cleanly articulate where it breaks.

Can you unpack what precise claims that slogan could actually be making? I want a menu, not one reading. Off the top of my head I can gestue at a few candidates: an empirical platitude about how people behave toward repeat customers versus tourists; a game-theoretic claim about equilibria of repeated games (but then: infinite horizon or finite, discounted payoffs or not, and which equilibrium concept -- because I vaguely remember something about cooperation unraveling by backward induction in finitely repeated games, which seems to flatly contradict the slogan under at least one reading); an evolutionary claim about strategies like tit-for-tat actually winning out (but won against what, in what population structure?); and the strong normative reading my reading group guy wants, that absent future interactions cooperation is simply never rational.

For each reading: is it trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false? And what conditions would each need -- how long does the "shadow" literally have to be, in terms of discounting or continuation probability? Then the important part: which readings, if any, actually license the conversational move he made, i.e. ruling out one-shot cooperation as irrational? My guess is the readings that are true don't get him his conclusion and the reading that gets him his conclusion isn't true, but I want it shown rather than asserted.
