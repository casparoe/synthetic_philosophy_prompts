I host a small philosophy podcast and have been reading way too many LW-style arguments about ontology, map/territory confusion, and whether debates are secretly semantic. Next week’s guest is an unnamed metaphysician who defends four-dimensionalist worm theory as the right solution to the statue-and-the-lump-of-clay puzzle. I definately don’t want another episode where the guest just explains temporal parts and then declares victory.

Act as an expert philosophy-of-metaphysics interviewer and a careful adversarial collaborator. Think through the strongest pressure points before drafting: first give me a short brainstorm of 5 places where worm theory might be doing less work than it appears to, then write 10 interview questions.

For each question, include:
- the exact question I should ask, phrased naturally enough to say out loud;
- the reply the guest will probably give;
- a sharper follow-up that anticipates that reply;
- whether the exchange is mainly exposing a semantic disagreement, a metaphysical disagreement, or a question about explanatory payoff.

The set of questions should collectively press on:
1. what exactly the lump of clay is supposed to be, and whether the puzzle depends on smuggling in different sortals;
2. how two numerically distinct spacetime worms can coincide so completely without bringing back an awkward coincidence objection;
3. why the statue and lump can differ modally despite sharing all their present matter;
4. whether appeal to temporal parts genuinely explains persistence or just redescribes change in fancier language;
5. the apparent arbitrariness of picking out one worm as the statue given all the nearby candidate worms and vague boundaries;
6. why worm theory should be preferred to stage theory rather than merely grouped with it as four-dimensionalism;
7. what the view says about our ordinary practice of reidentifying objects over time, especially if that practice is based on a mistaken endurantist picture;
8. whether the theory multiplies objects too freely, including overlapping statues or lumps that differ only by one molecule or one brief temporal segment;
9. at least one new toy case that isn’t just the statue example with the labels changed;
10. what argument, distinction, or consequence would actually make the guest abandon worm theory.

Make the sequence cumulative: early questions can force the guest to state the view precisely, middle questions should distinguish the real metaphysical claim from semantic reframing, and the final questions should identify the guest’s true crux. No warmup questions, no career biography, no citations, and no generic asking about open problems. Please steel-man the position, but don’t let the guest escape by saying our ordinary talk is loose unless they can explain what the correct underlying ontology is and why it matters.
