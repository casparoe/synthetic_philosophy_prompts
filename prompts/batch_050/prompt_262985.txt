For the revision of my upper-division ethics syllabus, I asked a colleague to draft a short sidebar on Bernard Williams's critique of consequentialism, to be distributed alongside the week's readings. He sent me the paragraph below. My own impression is that it is broadly reliable but that something goes wrong in its final sentences; I would like an independent audit before I adopt it for the course.

"Bernard Williams argued that consequentialist moral theories cannot accommodate the place of personal commitment in a human life. In his best-known example, a traveler named Jim happens upon twenty captives about to be executed and is told that if he personally shoots one of them, the other nineteen will be released in honor of his arrival as a guest. Williams held that the utilitarian answer — kill the one — is obviously correct, and that what is telling is how easily the utilitarian arrives at it, without registering the moral cost of its being Jim's own act. Williams's deeper objection, which he called the problem of integrity, is that consequentialism assigns each agent responsibility for what he fails to prevent no less than for what he does, and so requires him to hold his own deepest projects — the ground projects that give his life meaning and make him the person he is — as perpetually liable to sacrifice whenever abandoning them would produce more good overall. He illustrated the same point with the case of a man who can rescue only one of two people, one of them his wife, remarking that a husband who first stops to ask whether morality permits him to prefer her has had one thought too many. The objection therefore shows consequentialism to be self-defeating: by demanding unlimited impartiality it erodes the very attachments, dispositions, and stable character that make people effective promoters of good outcomes, so that no one could live as a consequentialist without thereby frustrating consequentialist ends."

Please audit the paragraph claim by claim. For each substantive assertion, indicate whether a careful reader of Williams would accept it as stated, accept it with qualification, or reject it, and explain the ground for the verdict. In particular: Does Williams in fact endorse a verdict in the Jim case, and if not, what is the example meant to show? Is the negative responsibility attributed to consequentialism here described accurately, or overgeneralized? Are the integrity objection and the "one thought too many" remark properly presented as one point, or do they belong to different strands of Williams's critique, aimed at different targets? Finally, assess the concluding judgment: is self-defeat the conclusion Williams draws from the integrity objection, does it follow from the accurate parts of the paragraph, and how would Williams himself respond to the suggestion that a suitably indirect consequentialism absorbs the objection? Since the sidebar will be handed to students, close by indicating which of your corrections are pedagogically essential and which are refinements an introductory treatment could responsibly omit.
