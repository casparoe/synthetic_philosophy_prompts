I'm moderating a public colloquium Q&A with a historian-turned-philosopher whose new book defends what she calls the incomparability thesis: the claim that ranking historical atrocities -- the Holocaust, the transatlantic slave trade, the Holodomor, the Rwandan genocide, the Armenian genocide -- against one another on any scale of evil is not merely methodologically dubious but morally obscene, a kind of category error that disrespects the dead. Her standard move when pressed is to say that each atrocity is singular and that any comparison relativizes suffering. I want a sequence of eight to ten questions that makes that move progressively harder to repeat, and for each question I'd like a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give.

The questions should turn on the actual pressure points of the view, not on biography or generalities. In particular, I'd like the sequence to cover the following ground, in whatever order you think builds best:

First, the scope problem: does the thesis forbid all comparative judgments about atrocities, or only ordinal rankings? She will likely say only rankings, so the follow-up should force her to confront that her own rhetoric of singularity and unprecedentedness is itself a comparative judgment, and to state her thesis without smuggling one in.

Second, the epistemic versus moral ambiguity: is the objection to ranking that we lack a common measure of suffering, or that ranking would be wrong even if we had one? If she takes the epistemic horn, ask what better historiography or victim testimony would have to look like to license comparison; if the moral horn, press on why acknowledging that one event killed more people, or destroyed more generations of a culture, amounts to disrespect rather than description.

Third, the counting regress: demographers count victims, and her book cites those numbers. Ask her to state a principled line between permissible aggregation (ten million dead) and impermissible comparison (ten million is more than six), since her view seems to permit the first and condemn the second while the second is just the first done twice.

Fourth, the triage objection: commemoration budgets, museum space, school curricula, and reparations programs all require prioritization among atrocities in practice. Ask whether all such prioritization is obscene or only some, and demand the criterion. Her likely reply is that practical triage differs from moral ranking; the follow-up should ask what makes an allocation decision non-moral when it determines which victims' descendants get acknowledged.

Fifth, the political deployment worry: incomparability talk has historically been used to foreclose comparisons some audiences wanted blocked, as in the German Historikerstreit, where insisting on the Holocaust's uniqueness functioned to quarantine it from colonial genocide. Ask whether her thesis does ideological work regardless of her intentions, and how she distinguishes her position from the uses to which it has been put.

Finally, close with a question about what would change her mind: what would a permissible, non-obscene comparison between atrocities have to look like, and if she cannot describe one, ask whether the thesis is a conclusion she argued for or a commitment no evidence could touch.

Two further requests. Order the questions so that each one forecloses an escape route the previous answer opened, and tell me your reasoning for the ordering. And since I'll only get limited time: commit to the single question you'd ask if you could ask only one, and say why you expect it to be the hardest for her to deflect, rather than presenting me a menu of equally good options.
