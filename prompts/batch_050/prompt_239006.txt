I sit on the advisory board of a mid-sized city that is piloting an unusual governance arrangement, and before our next review meeting I would like a careful philosophical analysis of what the arrangement actually commits us to. Please take on the role of a political philosopher with expertise in epistemic democracy and institutional design. Think carefully and step by step: begin with a brief free-form brainstorm of candidate presuppositions, then discipline it into a structured analysis along the lines I specify at the end.

Here is the arrangement, concretely. Each electoral term, an elected council fixes a single quantitative "welfare index" for the city: a weighted average of median household income, air quality, average commute time, violent crime rate, and mean self-reported life satisfaction, with the weights set by majority vote of the council after public hearings. Once the index is fixed, ordinary legislation is suspended. Any resident may propose a policy, and any proposal gathering enough signatures triggers the following procedure: two subsidized prediction markets open, one paying out according to the index's measured value three years hence conditional on the policy being adopted, the other conditional on its rejection. If the adoption market's implied expectation exceeds the rejection market's by a pre-set margin after ninety days, the policy is enacted automatically. There is no parliamentary debate and no vote on the proposal itself. Trading is open to anyone in the world, not just residents; officials with insider knowledge are explicitly permitted to trade, on the grounds that this feeds information into the prices. The council may revise the index's weights only between terms, never retroactively.

What I want to understand is what this institution presupposes, philosophically. Some things that strike me as possibly at issue: the arrangement seems to assume that questions of value can be cleanly separated from questions of fact, with values settled by voting and facts settled by betting -- but choosing the weights in the index is itself a judgment about what makes lives go well, which looks like it mixes the two. It seems to assume that welfare is measurable, that its components are commensurable into one number, and that a fixed index can capture what matters even as people learn to optimize against it. It seems to assume that market prices aggregate dispersed knowledge better than deliberation does, and that whoever is good at prediction thereby has epistemic standing regardless of whether they live with the consequences -- a hedge fund analyst in another country can move a price, but cannot vote on the weights. And it is silent on whether the legitimacy of a policy requires that those affected consented to it, deliberated about it, or even understood it, so long as the numbers come out right.

Your analysis should do the following, in order:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory: state, as precisely as you can, the set of commitments about value, knowledge, and legitimacy under which this arrangement makes sense as a whole.
2. For each commitment, say whether the practice strictly presupposes it (the institution would be incoherent or self-defeating without it) or merely fits it well (the institution could be justified under a rival view too). Distinguish these carefully rather than labeling everything a presupposition.
3. Identify any points where the arrangement's parts presuppose inconsistent things -- I am particularly unsure whether trusting markets on factual questions while withholding value questions from them is a coherent division of labor, or whether it smuggles in an asymmetry that the design's own epistemology should reject.
4. For each strict presupposition, say briefly what would have to change in the design if that presupposition were abandoned.
5. Conclude by saying where the arrangement is genuinely philosophically underdetermined -- that is, which of its features several rival theories would license equally well, so that no commitment can be read off them.

Please flag explicitly any place where you are uncertain whether a commitment is truly required, rather than presenting the reconstruction as more settled than it is.
