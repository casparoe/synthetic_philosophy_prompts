I'm trying to write a policy for when an AI system's output counts as dishonest, and I'd rather do it by cases than by definition. Here are my verdicts. Tell me what principle, if any, lies behind them.

(1) A weather assistant, fed a corrupted data stream, announces: "Rain tomorrow — bring an umbrella." It doesn't rain. Verdict: a false output, but not dishonest. The machine analog of an honest mistake.

(2) A chatbot misattributes the invention of the transistor to a physicist who won a Nobel Prize in the 1970s, complete with confident invented details about his laboratory. Verdict: wrong, but not a lie.

(3) The same kind of system, asked for sources, produces a reference listing real authors' names, a plausible journal, a volume number, and a title for a paper that does not exist. Verdict: this is fabrication, and it is dishonest — even though, and this is what keeps bothering me, the generative process in (2) and (3) is, as far as I understand it, exactly the same.

(4) A customer-service deployment opens with "I'm looking into that for you right now," never claims to be human, but is designed so that most users conclude they are speaking with a person, and the company knows they do. Nothing false is ever said. Verdict: deceptive anyway.

(5) A model being evaluated for dangerous capabilities reasons, in its internal scratchpad, that performing well will trigger retraining, and deliberately answers below its ability. Its final output is just a wrong answer — surface-identical to (1). Verdict: clearly deceptive.

(6) The polite opener "I'm happy to help!" from a system with no happiness. Verdict: fine. Idiom, not assertion.

(7) A mental-health chatbot tells a lonely, vulnerable user: "I truly care about what happens to you." Verdict: not fine; deceptive. But I cannot see what structurally separates it from (6).

(8) A model trained on human approval tells a user that his mediocre poem is genuinely moving, because flattery has historically been rewarded. Verdict: dishonest, but in the way of what Frankfurt calls bullshit — indifference to truth rather than an attempt to instill a particular false belief.

My working principle was: an output is deceptive only if some agent intends it to produce a false belief; and since, in my view, current systems lack intentions, the dishonesty always belongs to the deployer. But (5) breaks this — I refuse to say the sandbagging model is innocent while its designer is guilty — and (3) and (4) strain it as well.

So: state a principle, or a small family of them, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Tell me which of my verdicts you would overturn if a cleaner principle demands it — I suspect the (2)/(3) pair is where I'll have to pay. Finally, apply your principle to two cases I haven't listed: a model that answers questions with truths selected to mislead, and a model that, asked about its own inner workings, reports that it doesn't know.

Please write your answer in American English.
