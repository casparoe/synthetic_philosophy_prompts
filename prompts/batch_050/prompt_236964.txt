Two friends of mine got into it at a meetup this week over the British Raj — basically the Ferguson vs Tharoor fight. Call them A and B. I stayed out of it, but I couldn't figure out afterwards who was actually winning.

A's case: India was something like a quarter of world GDP when the East India Company showed up and low single digits when the British left; the textile industry was deliberately kneecapped; the railways everyone credits the Raj with were built to move troops and raw materials to ports and financed by Indian taxes paying guarenteed returns to British investors; and tens of millions died in famines while grain was still being exported out. Balance sheet: massively negative.

B's pushback: the GDP-share collapse is mostly just the Industrial Revolution happening elsewhere — India's share was going to fall no matter who ruled it, and a falling share isn't the same as absolute decline. Railways cut freight costs enormously and apparently later studies show they reduced famine deaths by moving grain into scarcity areas. Pre-colonial India was no eden either — Mughal land taxes were brutal, constant warfare between successor states. And the unconquered-India counterfactual is load-bearing in A's whole arguement and nobody actually knows it.

A came back that the counterfactual cuts both ways: B doesn't get to credit Britain for railways and rule of law without asking what Indians would have built themselves — Japan industrialized without being colonized — and saying "the Industrial Revolution explains the share drop" doesn't touch the policy choices: tariffs against Indian cloth, the home charges, exporting grain during famines. B conceded the famine stuff is A's strongest point but said even there it's murky: 1943 happened in the middle of a world war, and the real variable might be free press and democratic accountability rather than who's in charge, since independent democracies have also bungled food crises.

Then someone derailed the thread into whether utilitarian arithmetic even applies to empires and the night ended. Can you go through this point by point — GDP shares, railways, textiles, famines, the counterfactual thing — and say where each of them is landing real hits, where they're talking past each other, and whether the "balance sheet of empire" framing is even coherent to begin with?
