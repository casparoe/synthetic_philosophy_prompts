So here's the thing that's been bugging me. My old university, like a lot of US colleges, hands out "merit scholarships" -- money off tuition for students with high GPAs and test scores, completely regardless of whether the student's family needs the money. A kid from a wealthy suburb with a 1550 SAT gets $20k off per year, explicitly framed as a reward for academic excellence. Meanwhile the very same university runs outreach programs and contextual admissions on the stated rationale that talent is evenly distributed but opportunity isn't, publishes reports about how SAT scores track family income, and its president gives speeches about education not being a prize you earn but a right or at least a public good.

So the university officially believes that test scores substantially reflect luck (rich parents, good schools, tutors), and it officially calls high scorers "deserving" of thousands of dollars. These two seem to be in tension.

My question: what theory of desert, if any, does the merit scholarship practice actually presuppose? A few options I can see: maybe it presuposes that achievement grounds desert even when the achievement traces back to lucky antecedents (so the causal history doesn't undermine the desert claim); maybe it's a Rawls-ish view where nobody really deserves their talents but institutions can still legitimately reward their exercise; or maybe it doesn't need a desert theory at all -- maybe "merit aid" is just price discrimination and marketing, a way to buy students who raise the rankings, and the desert language is decoration. If the last one is right, is the practice then dishonest in a way that matters, or is the decoration doing real work?

Structure your answer like this:

**The tension, resolved or not** (~150 words): is the university being inconsistent, or only apparently?
