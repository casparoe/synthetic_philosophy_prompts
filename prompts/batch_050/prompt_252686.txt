I'm running one session of an interdisciplinary reading group -- mostly economists, a couple of CS people, all fine with probability, none with much philosophy -- on why anyone should maximize expected utility in the first place. They all know the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms as a representation theorem (satisfy the conditions, get a utility function unique up to positive affine transformation, whatever) but they treat it as a formal curiosity, and one of them has already told me the money pump argument is a con because real people have diminishing marginal utility of money and would just stop trading. So my job is to teach the coherence case for EU maximization as an actual argument, in ninety minutes.

Plan the session rather than giving me the lecture. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to come in. I'm pretty sure you can't open with the money pump, because it presupposes a bunch of things -- what preferences even are, what makes a series of pairwise choices count as a single pattern that can be indicted, the idea that preferences over sure things generate obligations about lotteries -- and those have to be in place first or the pump just looks like a story about a gullible person. Work out the actual dependency chain for me: what has to be believed before the pump can look like an argument, and what's the earliest point each piece can be introduced.

- The carrying example. I want to run the whole transitivity case on one concrete scenario and I don't think it should be money lotteries -- partly because of the diminishing-utility dodge, partly because they'll pattern-match to finance and stop thinking. What should the example be instead, what features must it have, which tempting features must it NOT have, and at what point (if ever) do I let actual money back in?

- The misconception map. I'm expecting at least these, possibly more: (a) the pump shows intransitive people lose money, not that they're irrational; (b) it exploits the sequential framing and a resolute chooser who commits to a plan in advance escapes it; (c) it smuggles in that you must evaluate each trade on its own rather than the whole sequence; (d) 'irrational' here just means 'exploitable', which is a practical defect, not an epistemic one, so who cares. For each one tell me whether it's a genuine gap in the argument or a misunderstanding, and exactly where in the sequence to head it off -- I suspect some of them have to be defused before the pump is even on the table.

- The honest limits. Where should I openly concede that coherence arguments alone don't deliver full EU maximization -- my understanding is they get you transitivity and maybe independence, and the step from there to expected utility is where the real fighting is -- and how do I make that concession without the group concluding the whole enterprise is a bluff?

- One check at the end: a question or a variant I can pose that would show they've grasped the argument's structure rather than just remembered that intransitivity is bad. Something like asking what changes if the trades are offered in a different order or all at once, but you can probably design a better probe than that.

No readings, just the plan.
