bit of context: i work in ops at a mid-sized company and moonlight in a classical chinese philosophy reading group, and the two worlds have collided in an annoying way. one of the consultants we use keeps saying that han feizi's legalism — running a state on clear laws and incentives rather than on the virtue of rulers — is "just running a company on KPIs", and that han feizi basically invented OKRs around 240 BC. his broader point is that all the modern guff about culture, mission and leading by example was pre-refuted by the legalists two millennia ago, so we should stop worrying about it. when i repeated this in the reading group someone shoved chapter 7 of the han feizi (the "two handles" chapter) at me and told me to read it properly.

i did, and the bit that broke the analogy for me (or maybe proves it?? that's what i want sorted) is this, in the translation on the chinese text project:

"Ministers present their proposals in words, and the ruler assigns them tasks based on those words, then holds them solely accountable for achieving results according to those tasks. If achievements match the task, and the task matches the words spoken, then rewards are given; if achievements do not match the task, or if the task does not match what was said, then punishment is imposed. Therefore, when ministers make grand claims but achieve little, they are punished—not because their achievements are small, but because their accomplishments do not match their words. When ministers make modest claims but achieve great results, they are also punished—not because the ruler is unpleased by great achievements, but because their accomplishments still do not match what was claimed."

so: audit the KPI analogy for me, with this passage as the pivot. specifically:

- the correspondence table first. what plays the role of the KPI, of the bonus and the sacking, of the appraisal process, of the org chart, of the CEO, and of the board. be careful with the last two — i suspect there's no clean candidate and that this matters.
- then the thing that genuinely puzzles me: no modern incentive scheme punishes over-delivery. people sandbag their targets, sure, but nobody gets sacked for beating them. han feizi thinks over-delivery is worse than failure. what is his system optimising for that a KPI system isn't? does this show the analogy maps the surface mechanics but inverts the point — that the "two handles" are for the ruler keeping his grip on the ministers rather than for extracting more work from them? or is there a reading where he's simply a more ruthless incentive designer than any modern firm dares to be?
- where else the mapping breaks: exit options (my analysts can resign; his ministers couldn't), who polices the person at the top (a firm has a board and a market; his ruler has the tiger's-claws problem from the same chapter), and the small detail that a company can fold without anybody being executed.
- and finally the conclusion the consultant was using the analogy to carry. does "legalism = KPI management" actually support "culture-and-virtue talk is soft-headed", or does the passage cut the other way — since a system that punishes people for doing better than promised looks uncomfortably like what happens when you run a real firm on metrics alone and watch everyone optimise the metric instead of the work? if the properly audited analogy predicts gaming, sandbagging and information-hoarding, then maybe the reading group wins and it's the consultant who's been carried by it.

tell me who's right. and if there's a better analogy than the KPI one for what han feizi is actually doing — someone in the group suggested anti-takeover defences, someone else suggested dog training, which felt a bit unkind — say which one you'd defend and why.
