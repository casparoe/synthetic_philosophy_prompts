I retired three years ago after a career as a research chemist, and one of the pleasures of retirement has been a philosophy reading group I joined at our local library. We recently finished a unit on philosophy of language, and I volunteered to run a hands-on session on presupposition and conversational dynamics before we move on. I would like you to design that session for me.

The setting: roughly ten participants, all adults, most without formal training in philosophy, meeting for ninety minutes in a library conference room. What I want them to come away seeing is something I, as a scientist, found genuinely startling when I first grasped it: that a conversation maintains a running record of what is mutually taken for granted, that speakers can add to this record without ever asserting anything, and that content smuggled in this way is oddly insulated from challenge. More specifically, the activity should let participants feel three things in their own behavior rather than merely hear them described. First, the difference between what a sentence asserts and what it presupposes: when someone says something that takes for granted a fact the listener does not know, the listener typically just updates quietly rather than objecting, whereas the same content presented as an assertion invites scrutiny. Second, that this silent uptake has a breaking point -- some presupposed content is too surprising or too loaded to absorb smoothly, and listeners balk -- and that the difference between smooth accommodation and presupposition failure is something speakers exploit. Third, that presupposed content is sticky in a way asserted content is not: denying or questioning the main claim of a sentence tends to leave the backgrounded content standing, so a speaker who plants a presupposition can survive an outright rebuttal of what was actually said. Finally, I would like some scorekeeping element in the activity itself: the group's shared record should visibly constrain what can felicitously be said later, so participants notice that the state of the conversation, not just the words, does real work.

Please give me a runnable design: concrete rules, roles, and a turn structure (ideally with small stakes or a scoring scheme that rewards keeping the exchange flowing, since that is precisely the pressure that makes accommodation happen), a minute-by-minute timeline, and example material I can adapt -- though I would rather participants generate their own presupposition-laden sentences during play than recite famous textbook examples. Include a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the activity onto the concepts above, anticipates what will predictably go wrong (for instance, participants who cannot stop themselves from challenging everything, which breaks the game but is itself instructive), and explains how to handle it. I would also ask for an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that any game with explicit rules makes accommodation deliberate and visible, whereas the real phenomenon is largely unconscious and automatic -- if you agree, tell me how the facilitator should name that gap without deflating the session.
