At a reading group last week, a friend walked me through an argument that has been bothering me since, because each step looks reasonable on its own and the conclusion seems unhinged. His target was any moral theory that evaluates actions by the total welfare they can be expected to produce. (1) You ought to perform whichever available action has the highest expected total welfare. (2) Expected total welfare is probability multiplied by welfare, and welfare has no upper bound: a world with ten trillion flourishing lives is better than one with a trillion, and so on without any ceiling. (3) For any fixed probability p, however small, there is some quantity of welfare large enough that p times that quantity exceeds the expected welfare of any ordinary alternative, whether that is keeping your five dollars or giving it to an effective charity. (4) A stranger who promises to create 10^100 flourishing lives tomorrow if you hand him five dollars tonight is almost certainly lying, but his claim has some nonzero probability of being true. (5) Therefore handing over the five dollars has higher expected total welfare than refusing, and you ought to pay. (6) Since nothing in steps (1) through (5) depended on who made the promise or how it was phrased, the same goes for any promise of sufficiently vast welfare, however outlandish the story; total welfarism is therefore fanatical, fanaticism is absurd, and so (1) has to go.

My friend took this to show that expected-value reasoning about welfare is bankrupt in general, not just in contrived street encounters. I would very much like to keep both expected-value reasoning for ordinary decisions and the thought that more welfare really is better with no ceiling, so I want to resist the argument somewhere — but I cannot locate the misstep, and I worry that the first thing I reach for ("no one would or could really deliver on such a promise") is just a flat denial of (4) rather than a diagnosis of anything. So: which step actually fails, why does it look so harmless when stated in the abstract, and if the argument is in fact valid, which premise is the cheapest to abandon and what exactly does abandoning it cost you in cases that aren't contrived? Please take a definite position rather than surveying the options, and give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
