I'm on the animal welfare committee of a mid-sized grocery chain, and we have to decide whether to certify a new shrimp supplier. On environmental stuff they're great, but two of their practices have split the commitee. First, their hatchery does "eyestalk ablation" on breeding females: they snip or cauterize one eyestalk, which for hormonal reasons makes the shrimp mature and spawn much faster. This is standard practice across the industry, and the shrimp survive it and go on breeding. Second, they kill the shrimp by dumping them into an ice slurry, which takes a while, rather than using an electrical stunner that knocks them out in about a second before death. The stunner costs money but not that much.

Half the committee thinks shrimp almost certainly don't suffer in any way that matters, or at least that the evidence is far too shaky to hang a contract on, and that if we start regulating this we'll end up policing fruit flies in the warehouse. The other half thinks the evidence we do have -- avoidance learning, shrimp tending injured body parts, trade-offs where they'll endure a shock to get at food -- is enough that cutting off a sensory organ without pain relief and slowly freezing them to death is hard to defend when cheap alternatives exist.

Write the memo for the committee recommending whether to certify them (and under what conditions, if any). I want a clear bottom line up front, the reasoning laid out so a non-philosopher trustee can follow it, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on the side you recommend against -- no strawmanning. One more thing: whatever principle you use to deal with uncertain sentience, apply it consistently. Tell me what it implies about the live mussels we already sell, which just get steamed, and about the farmed salmon, where everyone agrees they're sentient but nobody agrees on what a good death looks like. If your principle gives a weird verdict on either of those, say so and explain whether that's a problem for the principle or a bullet we should bite.
