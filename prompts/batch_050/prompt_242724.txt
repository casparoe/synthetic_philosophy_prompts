In Chapter XI ("The Calculus of Probabilities") of his Science and Hypothesis, Henri Poincaré writes: "How can we know that two possible cases are equally probable? Will it be by a convention? If we insert at the beginning of every problem an explicit convention, well and good! We then have nothing to do but to apply the rules of arithmetic and algebra, and we complete our calculation, when our result cannot be called in question. But if we wish to make the slightest application of this result, we must prove that our convention is legitimate, and we shall find ourselves in the presence of the very difficulty we thought we had avoided."

Taking this passage as your point of departure, please explain what it implies for the question of whether a finite sequence -- say, a particular string of two hundred coin-toss outcomes written down on a page -- can itself be random, as opposed to the process that generated it. Specifically, I would like you to explain whether Poincaré's regress about equally probable cases generalizes: just as he takes the probability of an event to be well-defined only relative to a convention for counting cases, perhaps the randomness of a finite string is well-defined only relative to some further choice (a candidate distribution, a reference class of sequences, or a description language in which we test whether the string can be compressed). Address whether this relativity should be understood as a merely technical nuisance that a sufficiently careful definition can eliminate, or as a deep conceptual point showing that randomness is never an intrinsic property of a finite object. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
