Next month I am delivering the keynote at the naming ceremony for the Zera Yacob Seminar Room in our university's new ethics center, and I would like you to write the speech. Some background, since it shapes everything: I am the director who proposed the name, and the selection committee pushed back — they wanted a saint or an emperor, someone with a feast day or a crown, and I had to argue for a seventeenth-century Ethiopian philosopher who fled religious persecution, spent two years in a cave near Emfraz, and there composed the Hatata, his Inquiry, in which he refuses to accept any doctrine — Orthodox, Catholic, Muslim, or Jewish — on authority alone, and instead tests each claim against reason, which he understood as God's own gift to humanity rather than a rival to God. The audience will be genuinely split: roughly half will be philosophers and historians of African thought who know the Hatata well and will bristle at any anachronism (he was emphatically not a secular modern liberal — prayer frames his entire method), while the other half will be donors, trustees, and families who have never heard his name spoken aloud.

The speech should run about seven minutes, and I want the philosophical content to do real structural work rather than serve as decoration. My hope is that the speech enacts his method instead of merely describing it: open with a question rather than a claim, take up the committee's assumption that a namesake must be a ruler or a martyr, and subject it to the same treatment Zera Yacob gave inherited doctrine — asking what reason, as opposed to tradition and prestige, actually supports on the matter. From there, the speech should draw on the parts of his ethics that make him a fitting patron for a room devoted to inquiry: his refusal of asceticism (he could not accept that God would create food, wine, and marriage only to forbid their enjoyment), his treatment of his wife Hirut and his servant as fellow reasoners rather than subordinates, and his famous observation that the rival religions condemn one another by the identical method of inherited assertion, so that no inherited tradition can adjudicate the dispute — only inquiry can. The speech should close by naming what a seminar room full of students actually owes him: not reverence, which he would have distrusted as another form of inherited authority, but the cultivated willingness to be unconvinced.

Two constraints. First, please do not put any direct quotations from the Hatata in quotation marks — paraphrase his ideas instead, since I cannot verify exact wording and do not want to read a fabricated line aloud in front of the historians. Second, please write the speech in American English.
