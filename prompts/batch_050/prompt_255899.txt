I've been reading the rationalist blogs for going on fifteen years now, and there's a tension in the community's charitable practice that I keep turning over without resolving. On the one hand, the effective altruism ecosystem built its reputation on near-term, rigorously evaluated philanthropy: randomized controlled trials, cost-per-life-saved estimates, GiveWell-style spreadsheets that deliberately confine themselves to effects that can be measured within a few years of the intervention. On the other hand, the same intellectual community produced the longtermist argument that the expected value of the far future swamps anything we can do for people alive today, which rests on the claim that our actions have enormous, unpredictable consequences propagating indefinitely into the future -- the problem that gets discussed under the heading of cluelessness. Both commitments coexist, sometimes in the same organizations and the same donors, and I've never seen anyone state plainly what has to be true about acting under uncertainty for that coexistence to be principled rather than a failure of nerve.

So: what does the near-term practice actually presuppose? I can see a few candidates. Perhaps it presupposes that the unknown long-run consequences of, say, distributing bednets are symmetric in expectation -- that for every story where malaria eradication slightly shifts economic growth and political development for the worse, there's an equally probable story where it shifts them for the better, so the unmeasurable effects cancel out and the measurable near-term effects dominate the calculation. Perhaps it presupposes something epistemic rather than symmetric: that cluelessness isn't total, that we have more reliable grip on proximate effects than distant ones, and that rational action should be driven by the part of the outcome space we can actually model. Perhaps it presupposes that tractability is doing the real work and the expected-value framing is a rhetorical shell. And on the longtermist side, I'd like to know whether averting extinction risk is itself vulnerable to the same cluelessness objection -- whether the claim that we can identify actions that robustly improve the far future smuggles in exactly the kind of long-horizon predictability that the near-term practice implicitly denies. Does holding both practices require a coherent two-tier epistemology of consequences, and if so, can you state it precisely?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) as charitably as you can, then give me your verdict on whether the two practices can be jointly justified or whether intellectual honesty forces the community to abandon one of them. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with -- I'd expect several rival views could license the same spreadsheet. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or argument would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where in your reconstruction you think your reasoning is most likely to fail -- whether that's in how you've characterized cluelessness, in the symmetry assumption, or somewhere else.
