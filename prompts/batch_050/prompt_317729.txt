I run a small nonprofit that funds pilot programs in public health -- we usually have 8 or 10 running at once, each a year or two in, and every quarter my board makes me recommend which ones to continue, scale, or kill. Last month I pushed hard to keep funding a text-message appointment reminder program we've sunk about $300k and two years into, honestly mostly because shutting it down felt like admitting the whole thing was a waste, and afterwards one of my board members (an economist, annoyingly) told me I'd just commited the sunk cost fallacy in front of everyone. But I'm not fully convinced, because some of the reasons I gave out loud weren't obviously crazy: the team would be gutted, our local clinic partners have built their intake workflows around it, and the pilot is six months away from producing adherence data nobody else has. So when is "we've invested so much" doing legitimate work in a decision, and when is it just the fallacy wearing a costume? I've read the basic pop-econ version of this a dozen times and it never helps with the actual hard cases.

What I'd really like is a procedure my program officers can actually run -- like an ordered set of questions for any continue/kill decision that takes maybe an hour per program and doesn't require them to have read decision theory. It should pull apart the genuinely forward-looking considerations that are easy to confuse with sunk-cost reasoning (option value of waiting for more data, switching costs, commitments made to partners, what killing a project signals to future funders, morale effects on staff who'll work on the next thing) from the real fallacy, and say which of those survive scrutiny and which are usually just sunk cost in disguise. For each question tell me what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure should just output kill or continue on its own, and where it has to hand things off to a human judgment call because the question is really about values or unknowables -- be upfront about those handoff points instead of pretending the checklist covers everything.

Then run the procedure on two of my actual cases as worked examples: (1) the text-message program above, where the reminders perform slightly worse than a cheaper robocall alternative but switching would burn real goodwill with the clinics, and (2) a nutrition education pilot where the mid-term evaluation shows basically no measurable effect but the team lead swears we're about to turn a corner and just need one more year of funding. Ideally at least one of these should come out in a way I won't enjoy, otherwise I won't trust it. And finish by telling me what the procedure can't do -- the kinds of situations where it'll systematically misfire or where following it mechanically would be worse than just thinking.
