I am preparing to teach an advanced undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and before the students read any of the standard positions I want them to have a specification in hand: a written list of the desiderata a theory of free will must satisfy in order to count as an adequate account of when an agent is morally responsible for what they do. Write that specification for me.

The requirements list is the deliverable, so put your effort there rather than in surveying positions. For each desideratum, state the requirement precisely, give the case or argument that motivates it, and classify it as either non-negotiable (any theory failing it is thereby inadequate) or as a preference of a particular camp (in which case name the camp and say what it would cost to give the requirement up). The list should cover at least the following terrain, though you should reorganize or extend it as you see fit: the theory must say something about Frankfurt-style cases, where an agent acts on their own but a counterfactual intervener would have forced the same action had the agent shown signs of choosing otherwise; it must respond to manipulation arguments, in which an agent satisfies all the conditions a given compatibilist theory demands but acquired those very psychological features through covert external manipulation; it must address the Consequence Argument's challenge that no one has a choice about the past and the laws; it must take a position on whether responsibility requires alternate possibilities, ultimate sourcehood, both, or neither; and it must reckon with the luck objection pressed against libertarian accounts, namely that an undetermined decision at the moment of choice looks less like agency and more like randomness.

In addition, the specification must handle two cases I have constructed for the seminar, which the students will not have seen in the literature. First, the phased-overwrite case: over five years, an employer reshapes an employee's dispositions through a sequence of small, individually unremarkable incentives, performance reviews, and social pressures, none of which the employee could reasonably have resisted or even noticed as character-forming, until the employee's priorities substantially match what the employer wanted; at the end, the employee acts from the acquired dispositions. Say what an adequate theory must be able to distinguish here, and whether the requirement it suggests duplicates the manipulation desideratum or adds something to it. Second, the shared-authorship case: two people jointly plan an action over months, each repeatedly deferring to and revising against the other, such that neither can identify a moment of individual decision that fully explains the outcome. State what a theory must say about the location of responsibility in genuinely deliberative joint action, and whether that requirement is independent of the leeway-versus-sourcehood dispute.

Two further demands on the list itself. First, identify any subset of the desiderata that appears to be jointly unsatisfiable, and prove or carefully argue the tension rather than merely asserting it; if the full list is satisfiable only by a theory that gives up something each camp considers obvious, say so explicitly, since that is itself the result I care most about. Second, indicate which single requirement excludes the largest number of prominent positions, and which requirement does the most work in separating compatibilists from incompatibilists as opposed to separating camps within compatibilism.

Close with a brief scorecard running semicompatibilism, reasons-responsive compatibilism, event-causal libertarianism, and hard incompatibilism against the list, but keep this to a few sentences per position; the specification is the point, and I will run the detailed scoring in class. Where a requirement turns on an empirical question the seminar cannot settle, such as whether human deliberation is deterministic, mark that explicitly rather than hiding it inside a definition.
