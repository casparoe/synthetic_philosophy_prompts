Two friends of mine (both longtime LessWrong readers, like me) got into it over drinks last week about commitment races -- the argument got kicked off by some news segment about debt-ceiling brinkmanship -- and I keep flip-flopping on who's right, so I want an outside adjudication. By "commitment race" I mean a game where whoever makes a binding commitment first gets to shape the outcome, so both sides have an incentive to lock themselves in as early as possible. Reproducing their back-and-forth as best I remember it:

(1) Dan: in any chicken-type conflict, whoever commits credibly first wins. Throw your steering wheel out the window before the other driver throws his and he has to swerve. So commitment races are races you should try to win: commit early, commit publicly, and make it genuinely impossible for yourself to back down.

(2) Priya: that strategy can't be universalized. If both sides follow it, both commit first and you get the head-on collision -- mutually assured destruction nearly ran this experiment for real during the cold war. So the actual policy-level takeaway is: never enter commitment games, and if the other side commits first, back down.

(3) Dan: a known policy of always backing down when the other side commits is maximally exploitable. It hands a first-mover advantage to literally everyone you'll ever deal with and guarentees you eat the cost of every standoff forever, which is plausibly worse in expectation than occasionally risking the collision. A credible commitment to stand firm is exactly how you avoid that fate -- that's the entire logic of deterrence.

(4) Priya: deterrence commitments are commitments to do something you'll have no reason to do when the moment actually arrives. Retaliating after you've already been hit buys you nothing, and refusing to swerve once the other guy's steering wheel is gone just gets you killed. Such commitments only work if the other side believes you'll act against your own interest later, and a strategy built on cultivating a reputation for future irrationality is both fragile and borderline incoherent for an agent who updates.

(5) Dan: then the fix isn't fewer commitments, it's better ones. The problem is that existing commitment technology only lets you commit unilaterally and destructively. If both parties could verifiably precommit to a fair split -- joint, conditional commitments that bind everyone at once -- the race structure disappears entirely. Work on the commitment tech instead of unilaterally disarming your own ability to commit.

Adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating. And at the end, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
