I cover cosmology and fundamental physics for a general-interest magazine, and over the past few years a growing share of the preprints, press releases, and expert pitches that cross my desk lean on what I have learned to call anthropic reasoning: arguments that draw conclusions from the bare fact that we exist, or from where we find ourselves as observers. The doomsday argument that humanity's extinction is sooner than we think because we appear surprisingly early in our species' history; the claim that the fine-tuning of physical constants, combined with our existence, is strong evidence for a multiverse; arguments that certain cosmological models should be heavily favored simply because they contain vastly more observers than their rivals. I was burned early on by writing up one of these uncritically and receiving a long, patient, devastating letter from a philosopher, and since then I have tried to educate myself. What I have gathered is that these arguments hinge on a subtle choice about self-location: roughly, whether you reason as if you were sampled at random from the observers that actually exist, or whether you instead treat your own existence as evidence favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist. And I have gathered that the literature contains a notorious cautionary case, often called the presumptuous philosopher, in which a scientist uses the second style of reasoning to conclude from an armchair, with overwhelming confidence, that the universe is a trillion times larger than it looks, and on that basis declines to fund a telescope that could settle the question empirically.

What I want is a vetting procedure I can run, alone, in under an hour, on any article or pitch that invokes anthropic reasoning, before the magazine's name goes on it. It should be a sequence of questions in order: what to ask first, what a satisfactory answer to each question looks like, where the procedure terminates with a verdict of do not publish this framing, where it terminates with publishable but only with specified caveats that I must write into the piece, and where it deliberately hands the decision back to me because the remaining question is a judgment call no checklist can settle. Concretely, I would want it to force me to identify the reference class the argument is sampling from and whether that choice is doing unacknowledged work; to identify which of the two sampling assumptions above the argument is implicitly using; to test whether the conclusion survives, weakens, or flips if the other assumption is substituted; and to detect the presumptuous philosopher pattern specifically, meaning any case where observer-count reasoning does the work that observation was supposed to do, or where a theory is credited or penalized purely for how many observers it contains. It needs to work with back-of-envelope probability at most, and wherever a step cannot be made mechanical, it should say so plainly rather than dress up intuition as procedure.

Please then run the finished procedure on three cases of the kind I actually face: a paper arguing that existential risk is higher than standard estimates suggest, on doomsday-style grounds; a senior cosmologist arguing in an interview that fine-tuning plus our existence is strong evidence for a multiverse; and a preprint arguing that cosmological models predicting vastly more observers deserve such a large boost in credence that our existence alone nearly settles a live empirical question in cosmology, telescope data pending. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure rejects something flashy and eminently quotable that my editors would love, and I want to see that rejection worked through honestly rather than fudged. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which disputes in this territory are genuine open problems that no editorial checklist can adjudicate, and what I should write in a piece when the argument I am covering turns on one of them.
