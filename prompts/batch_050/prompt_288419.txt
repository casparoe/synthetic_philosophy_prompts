And here's the answer I have to grade (name removed):

"Kant's point in this passage is that there are two different ways a maxim can fail the categorical imperative test. In the first kind of case, the maxim cannot even be conceived as a universal law of nature. His stock example is the lying promise: if everyone in difficulty made promises they had no intention of keeping, then nobody would believe promises anymore, and the practice of promising would destroy itself. So the maxim of falsely promising repayment when I need money cannot be thought as a universal law, because universalized it eliminates the very institution the maxim depends on. This is a contradiction in conception, and the duties that correspond to this kind of failure are perfect duties, duties that admit of no exceptions, like the duty never to make a false promise.

The second kind of failure is subtler. Some maxims can be conceived as universal laws but cannot be willed as universal laws. A world in which nobody ever helps anyone is perfectly conceivable, since nothing about it is self-undermining the way universal false promising is. But Kant argues that no rational being could will such a world, because every rational being necessarily wills his own happiness, and in a world of universal non-assistance there would be times when his happiness requires help he would not get. Willing the maxim as universal law would frustrate an end the agent cannot give up, which is why no rational agent could choose to live in that world. Duties that stem from this kind of failure are imperfect, like beneficence: imperfect in the sense that our finite capacities mean we cannot possibly help everyone, so we inevitably have to pick and choose who to help and when.

So the two tests operate on different levels: the first asks whether the maxim still makes sense once everyone acts on it, the second asks whether a rational agent would want to live in the world the universalized maxim would produce. Kant's conclusion is that maxims sort cleanly into two classes: those that can be universalized, which morality requires us to act on, and those that cannot, which are forbidden."
