My sister got really into a wellness thing a few months ago -- supplements, manifestation journaling, the works -- and when I pushed back she said "it works for me, so it's true for me," and the awful part is I caught myself nodding. That sent me down a rabbit hole and I've been reading James's Pragmatism (the 1907 lectures), Lecture VI on truth. There's a bit near the start where he's mocking the rationalist picture:

"But the great assumption of the intellectualists is that truth means essentially an inert static relation. When you've got your true idea of anything, there's an end of the matter. You're in possession; you know; you have fulfilled your thinking destiny. You are where you ought to be mentally; you have obeyed your categorical imperative; and nothing more need follow on that climax of your rational destiny. Epistemologically you are in stable equilibrium."

I love this passage and I want to be on James's side of it. But I've now written down my actual commitments and I'm worried they don't hang together, and I want an audit before I embarrass myself in an argument with my sister or with my philosopher friend. Here they are:

1. Truth is what inquiry would settle on at the ideal limit -- if investigators kept going forever, what they'd converge to is the truth. (I got this from Peirce secondhand, haven't read him directly.)
2. James is right in that passage: truth isn't an inert static possession, it's something that happens to beliefs as they get verified and lead us around experience successfully -- and for moral and religious questions especially, whatever proves itself expedient in the way of believing is what's true.
3. For boring empirical claims I'm a correspondence person at gut level: "the water main broke last Tuesday" is true because of what happened last Tuesday, full stop, and it would be true even if nobody ever checked and believing it never led anyone anywhere.
4. Fallibilism: any belief I currently hold, including my best verified ones, might turn out to be false.
5. There are truths nobody will ever verify, even at the limit of inquiry, because nobody will ever have the means or the reason -- like whether the number of breaths my grandmother took on her fortieth birthday was even or odd. Still true or false, one or the other.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? I'm especially unsure whether my Peirce-flavored (1) and my James-flavored (2) can coexist, or whether converging on a final settled opinion is just the "stable equilibrium" James is making fun of, relocated to the end of time. But don't assume that's the only problem -- check all the pairs. Rank the real tensions by severity, say for each one what would have to give, tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection (and why they only seemed bad), and then name the single cheapest revision that gets me to a consistent package. Write it in American English, casual register is fine, maybe 700 words or so, not counting the passage.
