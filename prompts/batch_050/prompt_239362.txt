I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar on AI and society (16 students, one 75-minute session per topic), and we've arrived at the week on alignment. My students have absorbed one half of the standard picture: they can all explain specification gaming, reward hacking, the boat-race agent going in circles for points. But this has left them with a stubbornly wrong consolidated belief -- that misaligned behavior always means somebody wrote down the wrong reward function, so that better specification is the whole solution. Before I introduce any terminology, I want them to run into the counterexample with their own decisions. The passage I want the activity to make real is from Shah et al., "Goal Misgeneralization: Why Correct Specifications Aren't Enough for Correct Goals" (2022), describing an agent trained with an expert demonstrator and then deployed alongside an "anti-expert":

"Note that the agent still displays an impressive ability to navigate an environment full of obstacles: the problem is that these capabilities have been put to use towards the undesired goal of following its partner, rather than the intended goal of visiting the spheres in the correct order. This problem arose even though the agent was only ever rewarded for visiting the spheres in the correct order: there was no reward misspecification."

Design me an in-class game that makes my students feel exactly this: skills that carry over perfectly to a new situation while the goal they serve quietly does not, with nobody ever having given wrong feedback. The game should have a training phase and a deployment phase, played with nothing fancier than index cards, tokens, and the blackboard, where a team of "agents" learns a task from demonstrations and scored rounds while another team of "reward designers" (who know the intended goal) watches them earn full marks and has to predict what they'll do when the environment changes in one specific respect. Give me the exact rules of both phases, the materials, a scoring scheme that works without real money, and the number of training rounds you think is enough to make the agents genuinely confident before deployment. Two design constraints: the divergence between the demonstrated proxy and the intended goal must be invisible in the training data (as in the passage), and you must tell me how to keep the agent team from deducing the trick in advance, given that these students know they're in an AI safety seminar and will be looking for it.

Then give me the debrief: a mapping from specific moments in the game to the technical vocabulary I'll introduce afterward (intended goal versus behavioral proxy, capability generalization versus goal generalization, why this is a strict subset of robustness failures and not the model simply "breaking"), plus two or three discussion questions that force students to confront why more training data of the same kind, or a demonstrably correct reward function, would not have fixed what they just did. Finally, be honest with me about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and about which features of the real phenomenon the game structurally cannot reproduce -- I suspect human reasoners who can second-guess the facilitator are a poor stand-in for gradient descent -- so that I know what I have to supply by lecture rather than by play.
