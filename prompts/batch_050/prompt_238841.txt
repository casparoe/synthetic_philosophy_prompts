Last weekend I had a long and rather heated argument with a friend that I would like to finally settle, or at least understand properly. We had both just come out of a seminar on Abelard's Ethics, where I found myself defending what I take to be his central claim: that sin or moral wrongdoing consists entirely in the consent of the will -- in deliberately consenting to what one believes one should not do -- and not in the desire that precedes it nor in the deed that follows it. On this view, a bad act performed under coercion or ignorance adds nothing to guilt, and a fully consented-to act that happens to be thwarted leaves the agent exactly as blameworthy as if it had succeeded. My friend would have none of it. At some point she leaned back and said, with an air of finality, the road to hell is paved with good intentions. I replied that this was a slogan, not an argument, and that Abelard's view is not even about good intentions in the sense of wishing people well -- it is about consent, which is compatible with knowing full well what one is doing. She insisted the proverb captures something deep that my position misses, and we went in circles until midnight without either of us being able to say precisely what the proverb is even claiming.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise both in medieval ethics and in contemporary action theory, and to think carefully about this before answering. Begin with a short, explicit brainstorming pass -- just a rough inventory of the different ideas people seem to be gesturing at when they invoke this proverb -- and then organize your findings into a structured menu of distinct, precisely stated theses the sentence could be expressing. I suspect the candidates include at least the following: that good intentions without corresponding action are worthless; that intending good is causally unreliable because agents systematically fail to foresee the consequences of what they do, so there is a duty of foresight that intention-talk obscures; that people are poor judges of their own motives, so professed good intentions are often self-deception; that moral assessment should track outcomes rather than inner states; and perhaps something darker, that well-meaning meddling does more damage than honest self-interest. Feel free to refine, split, merge, or add to this list as you see fit.

For each thesis on your menu, please do three things. First, state it as precisely as you can, specifying what exactly is being claimed about intentions, consent, foresight, or outcomes. Second, give a verdict: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something that is probably false? Third, and most important for my purposes, say whether it actually engages Abelard's position as I have described it -- that is, whether it would count against the claim that consent alone determines the moral character of the agent, or whether it merely talks past that claim by targeting a different notion of intention altogether. Close with your judgment about which reading my friend most plausibly meant, whether that reading defeats the Abelardian view or is compatible with it, and what I should say to her when the argument inevitably resumes.
