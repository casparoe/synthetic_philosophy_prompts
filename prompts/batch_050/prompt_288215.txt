I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the evolution of cognition, and I'm starting to plan an honors thesis for next year. Our anchor text has been Daniel Dennett's Darwin's Dangerous Idea, in particular his Tower of Generate-and-Test: the claim that intelligence comes in grades corresponding to increasingly cheap ways of trying out candidate behaviors, from Darwinian creatures (whose only testing ground is life and death across generations), through Skinnerian creatures (who can learn from reinforcement within a lifetime), to Popperian creatures (who can let candidate actions be tested in an inner simulated environment instead of in the world), and finally Gregorian creatures (who import thinking tools from their culture -- words, diagrams, notation, algorithms -- so that the inner environment itself is redesigned by memes rather than built from scratch by each brain). I find the schema compelling, but the more I sit with it the more it seems to gesture at open questions rather than settle them, and I'd like your help figuring out which of those questions a thesis could actually make progress on.

Here are the candidates I've been circling. First, the architecture question: is the Tower really a ladder, where each floor presupposes the ones below, or could there be a creature that is Gregorian without being robustly Popperian -- one that absorbs cultural tools and executes them without an inner rehearsal loop of its own? (Recent arguments that large language models are something like culture-shaped pattern completers rather than hypothesis-testers seem to make this a live issue rather than a verbal one, and I keep wondering what would even count as evidence either way.) Second, the transmission question: Dennett treats Gregorian minds as receivers of mind-tools, but what makes a mind a good host -- is there a specific cognitive prerequisite for being able to *use* a tool like decimal notation rather than merely parrot it, and does the schema say anything about that? Third, the explanatory-payoff question: does the taxonomy do real work, in the sense of generating predictions about animal cognition, child development, or AI, that finer-grained frameworks (Bayesian learning, predictive processing, classical dual-process theory) don't already make? And fourth, the normative question: if Gregorian minds are made rather than born, does that change how we should think about education or about what we owe people whose inner environments were stocked with bad tools?

What I'd like from you is a defended agenda, not a survey: which two of these (or one I've missed) most deserve a year of an undergraduate's time, and in what order? Please make your criteria explicit -- I care about which question is upstream of the others, which is tractable for someone with no lab access and a philosophy-and-cog-sci background, and which is neglected relative to its importance -- and tell me what would change your ranking, i.e., what finding or argument, if it appeared in the next year, should make me reorder the list. If you think one of my four candidates is actually a pseudo-question resting on a confusion about the Tower itself, say so plainly; I'd rather hear that now than in my thesis defense.
