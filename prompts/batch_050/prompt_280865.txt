I've been going back and forth with the other people in my housing policy reading group (we meet at a bar, which probably explains the quality of the arguments) and I want to nail down what I actually think about who gets a say in what gets built. For the group I finally read Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., 272 U.S. 365 (1926), the Supreme Court case that blessed zoning in the US, and one bit of Justice Sutherland's opinion stuck with me that isn't the part people usually quote. Describing the village's hierarchy of districts, he writes that "U-1 is restricted to single family dwellings, public parks, water towers and reservoirs, suburban and interurban electric railway passenger stations and rights of way, and farming, non-commercial greenhouse nurseries, and truck gardening" -- and then everything else, starting with mere two-family houses in U-2, is treated as one more step down a ladder that ends in junkyards and crematoria. What strikes me is the implicit grouping: a farm, a water tower, and a detached house belong together, while a duplex is somehow on the escalator down to the asphalt plant. I want to know whether my own gut verdicts secretly run on the same principle as the Court's, or a different one.

So here are my verdicts, and I'd like the principle behind them if there is one. (1) A homeowner who wants to split their big house into a duplex on a street of single-family homes: allowed by right, the neighbors shouldn't get a veto. (2) A 25-story tower in a low-rise historic district with a famous skyline: blocking it seems completely fine to me, and I don't feel bad about it. (3) Neighbors organizing to keep a shelter or a group home for people in addiction recovery off their block: not a legitimate objection, shouldn't count at all, even though honestly "we don't want those people here" is structurally a lot like "we don't want that building here." (4) The same neighbors blocking a concrete batching plant or a nightclub that closes at 4am: totally legitimate, veto away. (5) A six-story apartment that will put the next-door neighbor's garden in permanent shade: her complaint deserves real weight -- maybe the developer owes her setbacks or money -- but not a veto, which bugs me because the shade harms her more than the batching plant's noise harms any single one of its neighbors. (6) Someone who bought a house next to land that was already zoned industrial and is now outraged that industry arrived: basically no sympathy, you knew what you were buying next to.

What principle, if any, captures all six? The pair I can't reconcile is (1) against (2) -- both objections are essentially "this changes the character of what I live next to," and I can't tell if I'm smuggling in a size threshold, a settled-expectations baseline, a distinction between tangible harms and mere preferences, or just class prejudice with extra steps. Please state the principle (or two candidate principles) precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and be honest about which of my verdicts I'd have to abandon to get something coherent. Two more things: tell me what the principle predicts for a case I keep flip-flopping on -- a homeowner who converts their place into a full-time short-term rental, so the street slowly becomes a de facto hotel district, which seems like a "use" question like the batching plant but also like a property-rights question like the duplex -- and tell me whether the Euclid Court's own implicit principle (detached houses get a protected zone, everything else is a contaminant to be sorted by noxiousness) is actually my principle too, just stated more honestly than I'd like.
