At a dinner last week I told the old story about Death in Damascus: a man meets Death in the marketplace there and sees Death start at the sight of him. Taking this as a warning, he rides through the night to Aleppo — where Death duly keeps their appointment, explaining that she had only been startled because she had not expected to see him in Damascus, their meeting being set for Aleppo the next day. Two friends of mine then argued about the story for an hour, and I'd like a referee, because I genuinely can't place where the disagreement went wrong — or whether it did.

Daniel's opening position was that the man made a plain, identifiable mistake. Death's visible surprise was information: it told him she had not expected him in Damascus, hence that their appointment was elsewhere, hence that the rational move was to stay exactly where he was. The man read his clue correctly and then fled straight to the place the clue pointed away from. On Daniel's view the story is a cautionary tale about panicking, nothing deeper.

Sofia replied that Daniel's rule destroys itself the moment anyone follows it. If "Death looks surprised, therefore stay put" is what a rational person does, then a Death who genuinely foresees the future would foresee him doing it and would not have been surprised in the first place. The surprise can serve as evidence only if he cannot use it as evidence. Any conclusion his reasoning is capable of reaching is already contained in what Death foresaw, so there is no stable answer to the question of what he should have done — not because the question is hard, but because every candidate answer cancels itself.

Daniel took this to prove his side, in a way: a story with no coherent answer is simply an impossible story, like the barber who shaves all and only those who do not shave themselves. A being that unfailingly predicts a person's choices cannot visibly react to that person without contradiction, since the reaction becomes an input to the choice. So the right verdict is that the premise is broken, not the servant — and impossible setups teach us nothing about rationality, because rationality is only owed in worlds that could exist.

Sofia's response was that the setup is not impossible; it just shows that foresight and deliberation cannot coexist once the deliberator knows about the foresight, though each is perfectly coherent on its own. And she claimed there is a rational response after all: stop reasoning and flip a coin. Heads, stay in Damascus; tails, ride to Aleppo. Whatever Death foresaw, she foresaw a man reasoning; she cannot have foreseen the outcome of genuine chance, so the coin puts his whereabouts somewhere no appointment book could have recorded in advance.

Daniel's final move was to say the coin changes nothing, and worse for Sofia, it concedes his point. Either Death foresaw the coin's landing too — in which case the man is still doomed whatever he does and the coin was theater — or Death cannot foresee coin tosses, in which case the man never had his actions foreseen once he resolved to let a coin decide them, and the perfect-foresight premise is gone again. Every way out of the story, he said, works by quietly deleting one of its assumptions, which is exactly what you'd expect if the assumptions were never jointly coherent.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than declaring an overall winner: (1) Was the man's flight an identifiable error, given what Death's surprise indicated? (2) Can Death's surprise function as evidence for him, or is Sofia right that it is unusable in principle? (3) Is the scenario genuinely incoherent, as Daniel insists, or a coherent situation in which deliberation is merely self-defeating — and is there even a difference between those two verdicts? (4) Does the coin flip solve anything, or does it only relocate the problem? Finally, if both of them are missing something — some assumption they share that does all the damage — I'd like to know what it is.
