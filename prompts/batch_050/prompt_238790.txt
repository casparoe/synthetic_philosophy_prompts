I'm a second-year economics PhD student and next month I'm presenting a working paper to a Hayek society colloquium, and I want to walk in prepared rather than ambushed. My thesis, roughly: firms like Amazon and Walmart are internally planned economies on a continental scale -- no internal price system, no market exchange between warehouses, just allocation by algorithm and managerial fiat -- and they're gigantic. If planning that big works fine inside a firm, I argue, Mises' argument that rational economic calculation is impossible without market prices is empirically refuted, or at least badly wounded, and the real question about economic planning becomes a political/engineering one rather than a logical one. I've read Mises and Hayek and Lange, obviosly, but my training is in industrial organization, so I suspect I pattern-match their objections badly.

The audience: maybe forty people, mostly Austrian-school economists and political economists sympathetic to them, a couple of them senior people who've written on the calculation debate, plus some grad students and a few libertarian-leaning policy types. They will know the tacit knowledge literature and the standard replies to Lange cold, and they'll have heard versions of my Amazon argument before and already have a dismissal rehearsed -- probably something about islands of planning surrounded by a sea of prices. It's a twenty-minute talk with about forty minutes of Q&A, so I need to survive the questions, not win the seminar.

Prep me. I want the eight to ten objections I'm actually going to get from this specific crowd -- not the objections a neoclassical seminar would raise -- ordered by how likely they are to come up and how much damage they'd do if I fumble them. Phrase each one the way an Austrian economist would actually put it, with their vocabulary (dispersed knowledge, tacit knowledge, the regression theorem if it comes up, the difference between the knowledge problem and the incentive problem, etc). For each objection give me: what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply I can deliver from the podium, and a note on where my reply is weakest so I don't overreach. Also flag whether each objection is one my thesis can absorb with a modest retreat or one that threatens the core claim. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- the one where I'd have to bluff -- so I can at least decide in advance what my bluff is. Please write the whole prep sheet in British English, since the colloquium is in London.
