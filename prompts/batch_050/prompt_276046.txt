Next month I'm giving a colloquium talk at a philosophy department defending roughly this: the now-standard argument that large language models can't genuinely refer to anything — because nothing in their training data pins down whether their word "rabbit" picks out rabbits, rabbit-stages, undetached rabbit parts, or mere manifestations of rabbithood — is just Quine's gavagai argument dressed up in new clothes, and if it succeeds against LLMs it succeeds against human speakers too. So either reference is indeterminate for everyone, or the argument proves too much and we owe an account of what fixes reference in BOTH cases. My actual spin is that LLMs make the indeterminacy thesis newly vivid rather than showing anything special about machines, and I came to this after a friend in NLP insisted that model interpretability work had basically dissolved the whole problem, which struck me as exactly backwards.

The audience: mostly philosophers of language, including one or two genuine Quine naturalists who know Word and Object cold and will be protective of the actual argument (and annoyed if I flatten it), a Putnam-style externalist who thinks causal chains do all the work I'm denying, a Chomskyan linguist who regards LLMs as a category error about what language even is, and one ML researcher from the CS department who's coming because of the title and thinks probing/sparse-autoencoder results settle questions about what model tokens "mean." Nobody is hostile exactly, but the room has no shared premises about what would count as settling whether a system refers.
