In the General Introduction to his History of Civilization in England (1857), Henry Thomas Buckle argues, in effect, that the regularities disclosed by moral statistics -- the near-constancy, year after year, of marriages, crimes, and even suicides within a given society -- show that human affairs are governed by laws as steady as those of the natural sciences, and that what looks like the free choice of individuals is, in aggregate, so predictable that the historian's proper business is with these large social laws rather than with the doings of particular statesmen and generals. I have paraphrased rather than quoted him; take this as your statement of the position, and feel free to correct my paraphrase where you think I have got Buckle wrong.

Now take the French Revolution of 1789 and run it through Peter Turchin's structural-demographic model of secular cycles: the long integrative phases of growth and stability punctuated by disintegrative crises driven by state fiscal distress, popular immiseration, and elite overproduction -- more aspirants to elite positions than the society has positions to give, turning elite competition into intra-elite warfare. The familiar facts of the case are, on the surface, a gift to the model: a monarchy bankrupted by war debt and unable to tax its privileged orders, bread prices at generational highs in 1788-89, a bloated and resentful class of provincial lawyers, venal officeholders, and educated men shut out of advancement, and a crisis that indeed ends in state breakdown, civil war, and terror.

Do the analysis carefully in both directions. First, the mapping: identify exactly which observable quantities in pre-revolutionary France correspond to each variable in the model, which actors count as the overproduced elite, and whether the model's causal ordering (fiscal stress and immiseration first, elite defection and counter-elite mobilization second, breakdown third) matches the actual chronology of 1786 to 1794 rather than just the headline events. Second, the stress test: use Buckle's claim to press on Turchin, and Turchin on Buckle. Buckle thinks the interesting regularities are intellectual and moral and that the Revolution's content -- its ideas about rights, citizenship, and the state -- is where the law-governed part of the story lies; the structural-demographic model is largely silent about content and claims to predict only the timing and fact of breakdown, not what the revolutionaries believed. Does the French case show that a cycle model can predict a collapse it cannot describe, and if so is that a limitation or actually the strongest form of the claim? Conversely, does the sheer contingency Buckle wants to dissolve -- the tennis court, the October Days, the flight to Varennes, the survival of the republic at Valmy -- sit comfortably inside a model whose predictions are probabilistic and decadal, or does it show that the model and Buckle's statisticism share a common defect: they retrodict necessity from outcomes they could not have predicted in advance?

End with a verdict: if the French Revolution is the best case for quantified macrohistory, what exactly has been quantified and predicted, and what would have to be true of the next two centuries of revolutions -- 1830, 1848, 1917 -- for the model to count as confirmed rather than merely illustrated?
