A colleague in my reading group has put together the following argument, and although each step looks defensible on its own, the conclusion strikes us as a reductio of something, and we cannot agree on what. The context is the familiar debate about whether it is ever correct -- not merely convenient shorthand, but correct -- to say that a large language model believes something, for instance that Paris is the capital of France. Her argument runs:

(1) To have beliefs just is to be a system whose behavior is reliably and fruitfully predictable by treating it as a rational agent with those beliefs; there is no further fact of the matter beyond the predictive success of that interpretive strategy.

(2) The behavior of a good chatbot is reliably and fruitfully predicted by attributing beliefs to it: saying that it believes Paris is the capital of France, that it believes its interlocutor wants a concise answer, and so on, generates accurate predictions about what it will say next.

(3) Therefore the chatbot has beliefs in the only sense of "belief" there is.

(4) But the behavior of an ordinary thermostat is also predicted by attributing beliefs to it: say that it believes the room has grown too cold, and you correctly predict that it will turn the heat on.

(5) So, by parity of reasoning, the thermostat has beliefs in the only sense of "belief" there is.

(6) Any notion of belief on which thermostats have beliefs marks no distinction between systems with minds and systems without; it has been deflated to the point of vacuity.

(7) When we ask whether an AI system really believes something, the notion of belief at issue is precisely one that does mark such a distinction -- otherwise the question would not be worth asking.

(8) Therefore either (1) is false, or the question whether AI systems have beliefs is trivial and uninteresting.

We would like to resist (8): our working hope is that the question about AI belief is both substantive and answerable. I am also, I should admit, temperamentally sympathetic to (1), so if something has to go, I would rather it not be that -- though I recognize that preferences are not arguments. Please tell us which premise or inferential step you think should be rejected, and explain three things: first, why that step looks compelling at first glance, so that we can see how two competent readers were taken in by it; second, what rejecting it actually costs, in terms of commitments one must then carry elsewhere in one's philosophy of mind; and third, how the leading alternative diagnoses -- simply accepting thermostat beliefs as beliefs of a minimal grade, restricting (1) so that the interpretive strategy must be the best available one for the system in question rather than merely an available one, and abandoning (1) outright in favor of a more demanding, architecture-sensitive criterion for belief -- each distribute those costs differently. If you think the argument is in fact sound and (8) should simply be accepted, say so and defend that verdict against our reluctance.
