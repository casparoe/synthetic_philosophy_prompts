Since my dad died last year people keep telling me he lives on in us — in our memories, in the turns of phrase we picked up, in the way I catch myself sounding exactly like him everytime I tell a story. Hofstadter takes this kind of talk literally in I Am a Strange Loop: a self is a pattern rather than a thing, and coarse-grained copies of the pattern keep running in other people's brains, so part of his wife Carol genuinely persisted in him after she died, not just as a metaphor. I've been leaning hard on this thought to get through the grief, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. Lay out the correspondence table for me — what plays the role of the pattern, the substrate, the copy's fidelity, the "running" — and then the places it breaks: the copy is assembled out of my memories rather than continuing his first-person stream, there's nobody in my head having his experiences, and if the pattern is the self then the lower-resolution copies in my cousins' heads are also him, to lesser degrees, which seems to dissolve "him" entirely. Does the consoling conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was "lives on in us" doing all the work as a metaphor? And if it only works at coarse grain, tell me honestly whether that's a defect in the analogy or just what survival of a pattern would have to look like.
