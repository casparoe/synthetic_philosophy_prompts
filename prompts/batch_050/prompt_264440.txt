I am a PhD student in political theory, and I keep running into the line that nations are "imagined communities." Most recently, a senior colleague at a workshop on secession used it to shut down a session: since nations are imagined, he said, any argument that rests on the special moral standing of nations -- self-determination, claims on territory, duties to co-nationals -- builds on sand. The room moved on, but I suspect the slogan cannot do that work as stated, and I cannot say precisely why.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct theses the slogan might be expressing? I have in mind at least the following candidates, but feel free to split or add: (a) a near-trivial psychological claim that members of a nation cannot all know one another and must therefore conceive of each other abstractly; (b) a social-ontology claim that nations exist only in virtue of being collectively represented, like money or borders; (c) a historical claim that nations are recent constructions, bound up with print, standardised vernaculars, and the state, rather than ancient communities; (d) the stronger verdict that what is imagined is thereby imaginary, i.e., that nations are fictions in the way unicorns are; and (e) a normative deflationary claim that because nations are constructed, they cannot ground special obligations. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and then tell me which of the readings would in fact support my colleague's use of the slogan -- and whether any defensible one does. If "constructed" and "not morally load-bearing" come apart, I want that distinction stated carefully, since invented institutions (marriage, money, universities) plainly do carry obligations.

Finally, please flag which components of the slogan are broadly accepted among historians and social theorists and which remain genuinely contested -- for instance, how modern nations really are, and how much causal weight print and mass literacy actually bear in the standard story -- so that I do not present a contested reading of the slogan as settled consensus when I raise this in my workshop.
