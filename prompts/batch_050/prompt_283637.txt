I teach a mixed seminar of about 24 undergraduates -- half political science, half philosophy -- and I have one 75-minute session to make the Condorcet jury theorem land as a live result rather than a formula on a slide. Design an in-class simulation that lets them experience both halves of the theorem: the pull (a majority of individually mediocre but independent judges can be nearly infallible in the aggregate) and the fragility (the conclusion collapses once the independence assumption fails, and inverts if average competence drops below chance).

Concretely, I want a staged estimation-and-voting exercise with at least two contrasting rounds. In the first round, each student should receive a genuinely private, noisy signal about some binary question you devise (it must be something with a definite answer the facilitator knows, not a matter of taste), cast an individual vote, and watch the majority outperform almost everyone in the room. In the second round, the rules should be identical except that signals are now correlated -- I'd like you to engineer this through a shared information source the class doesn't realize is distorting everyone in the same direction, so that the majority confidently converges on the wrong answer. Give me the full apparatus: the exact tasks and how to construct the private signals from materials I can prepare in an evening, the scoring scheme (small stakes, no real money), the sequencing, the precise instructions to read aloud, and the moment at which the correlation mechanism is revealed. The theorem should not be named until the debrief.

For the debrief: map each round onto the theorem's formal conditions -- individual competence above one-half, statistical independence, sincere voting -- and indicate which moment in the activity dramatizes which condition. Include a short segment on the uncomfortable corollary the class just witnessed: if competence sits below one-half, the same aggregation logic makes the majority worse than a coin flip, so the theorem is a double-edged sword for any epistemic defense of democracy. If you can, add an optional third round where voting is unanimous-rule rather than majority-rule, so that students stumble into the strategic-voting problem (jurors voting against their private signal to avoid being a pointless dissenter) before I tell them this is a published result.

Finally, be frank about the pedagogy: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this (e.g., students sharing signals, the reveal falling flat, arithmetic errors in the tally), how to handle each, and which feature of the actual theorem the simulation cannot reproduce -- I suspect the asymptotic nature of the result is the main casualty with 24 voters, but tell me if there are others and what I should say about them out loud.
