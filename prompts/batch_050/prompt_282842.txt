Last week I rewatched some of the coverage of the "Miracle on the Hudson" -- Sullenberger ditching US Airways 1549 in the river in 2009 -- and ended up in a long argument with a friend about whether Sully genuinely deserved the hero treatment or whether he mostly got lucky that the river happened to be where it was. I'm taking a philosophy elective where we've just done moral luck (the Nagel/Williams setup, with the standard taxonomy of resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal luck), and I want to build my term paper around this episode, so I need the analysis to be tighter than my kitchen-table version of it.

Analyze the Hudson landing as a case study in moral luck. Map the actual features of the episode onto the taxonomy: which kinds of luck did Sullenberger's "heroism" depend on -- the bird strike happening at an altitude where the river was reachable at all, ferries being nearby, the crew performing, even his glider background? Then run the comparison with Tenerife in 1977, where the KLM captain -- by reputation one of the airline's most senior and safety-obsessed pilots -- made a time-pressured takeoff decision that produced the deadliest crash in aviation history. Same profession, arguably comparable skill, opposite outcomes, opposite reputations. What does moral luck theory say we should do with our judgments in each case: rescind some of the praise for Sully, soften the blame for the KLM captain, or is the whole "rescind/soften" framing the wrong response to the theory? And where does the theory strain against the reality -- for instance, is the accident-investigation apparatus (the NTSB hearings, the simulator recreations of whether he could have made it back to LaGuardia) an institutionalized denial of moral luck, or a way of allocating accountability that was never about moral desert in the first place?

To keep it concrete, run one counterfactual for me: a hypothetical third pilot who makes exactly Sullenberger's decisions, minute for minute, but the first ferry arrives five minutes later and two passengers die of hypothermia. Does your analysis of his competence and praiseworthiness change at all, and if not, explain why our real-world judgments would anyway. Finally, the part I actually need for the paper: what does the episode do to the theory -- does aviation's mix of skill and luck confirm Nagel's pessimism about moral judgment, or does it suggest the control principle was never meant to govern how we hand out professional praise and blame? Say how confident you are in each part of the analysis, and name the single claim you're least sure of.
