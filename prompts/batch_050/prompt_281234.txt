In a journal club on the foundations of statistical mechanics, I presented what I thought was a clean version of Maxwell's demon: a small being that sorts fast and slow molecules between two chambers, building a temperature gradient without doing work, apparently violating the second law. A colleague closed the discussion by saying, 'information is physical — the demon has to acquire and store information about the molecules, and that costs more entropy than the sorting saves.' Everyone nodded, but afterward I realized I couldn't tell what precise claim had just done all that work. When I pressed him later, he slid between three different things: that information must always be stored in some physical substrate, that erasing information necessarily dissipates heat (he called this Landauer's principle), and that entropy itself is just missing information. These do not seem to be the same thesis.

Here is what I would like. Take the slogan 'information is physical' and lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims it could be making in this vicinity — ranging from near-platitudes about physical instantiation, through quantitative claims like a minimum entropy cost per bit erased, up to strong identifications of thermodynamic entropy with information-theoretic entropy or claims that the laws of thermodynamics are really laws about observers' knowledge. For each reading: state it precisely enough that I could test an argument against it; classify it as trivial, substantive and defensible, or likely false; and note who or what the 'cost' is supposed to fall on (the demon, the memory register, the environment).

Then, and this is the part I care most about, tell me which of those readings actually suffice to rescue the second law from the demon. In particular, address the classic tension: early resolutions located the cost in measurement, while later arguments locate it in the erasure of the demon's memory when it is reset for the next cycle — and these rely on different readings of the slogan. Does the platitudinous reading get my colleague anything at all? Does the strongest reading prove too much, or dissolve the second law's objectivity? Finally, if the erasure-cost resolution is the correct one, does that vindicate the slogan in a form worth repeating, or would the discussion have gone better if he had just stated the specific thesis instead of the one-liner?
