My roommate gave me this argument after we both read Jane English's piece on what grown children owe their parents, and I can't find a clean way out of it. (1) English is right that parents' sacrifices don't create debts, since you can't incur a debt for something you never asked for and the sacrifices were made out of love, not as an investment. (2) What grown children actually owe parents is whatever friendship requires -- ongoing duties between people who care about each other, not repayment. (3) But duties of friendship are conditional on there still being a friendship; when a friendship genuinely dies, you don't owe your ex-friend anything beyond basic decency. (4) So if an adult child's relationship with a parent has genuinely died -- no affection, no shared life, no desire for contact -- the child owes the parent nothing beyond basic decency. (5) My roommate then pointed out that on this view, once a parent has advanced dementia and can no longer recognize the child, hold a conversation, or reciprocate anything, there is no friendship left, so the child owes nothing -- weekly visits to someone who doesn't know you're there are supererogatory at best. That last step seems clearly wrong to me, but I can't see what premise to reject that doesn't also bring back the debt model in (1), which I want to keep. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
