I'm putting together a study sheet for a poli sci course and want to mess with my students a bit. In The Prince Machiavelli lines up a bunch of founders and rulers and sorts them, roughly, by whether they owed their success to virtù or to fortuna. I want to argue the clean sorting falls apart on closer inspection, and I need the raw material.

So: rank these seven by how much their careers depended on fortune rather than their own virtù, from most fortune-dependent to least: Moses, Romulus, Theseus, Cesare Borgia, Agathocles of Syracuse, Francesco Sforza, and Hiero of Syracuse. But two complications:

1. Do it twice. Once for fortune-dependence in *acquiring* power, and once for fortune-dependence in *losing* power or nearly losing it. Cesare presumably flips position between the two lists; who else does, and is the flip ever big enough that the single overall ranking becomes misleading?
2. For the acquisition ranking, give each figure a rough split like 70% fortune / 30% virtù, with one sentence tying it to what Machiavelli actually says about them in the chapters where they show up. Then flag the one figure whose percentage you think is genuinely undecidable from the text, and say why.

Finally, one last question: if you can reconstruct how Machiavelli *himself* would rank them, does it match the ranking that's actually true of these guys historically, or is he stacking the deck to make his point about armed prophets and opportunity? If he's cheating somewhere, tell me where.
