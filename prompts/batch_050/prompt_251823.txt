I volunteer on the grant committee of a small regional health foundation (long story, my aunt got me into it) and we've hit a real disagreement that I keep turning over in my head. We have about $900k left in this cycle and it came down to two finalists. Proposal A would pay for the full treatment of one specific woman, mid-thirties, with a rare blood disorder — without it she very likely dies within a couple years, and no insurer or other fund will cover it. Proposal B would fund a mobile clinic that treats a chronic joint condition in poor rural counties: nobody dies from it, but it's genuinely miserable — trouble sleeping, can't work some jobs, constant low-level pain — and the program would reach maybe 7,000 people for years. There's no money for both.

Here's where the committee split. Half of us say: one life vs. manageable pain for many, obviously save the life, it's not even close. The other half, including our treasurer who thinks in spreadsheets, say that's just refusing to count: if 7,000 isn't enough, what about 70,000, or seven million? At some point the sheer number has to outweigh one life, they say, otherwise we're being sentimental. And someone raised a harder version: if proposal B instead saved 7,000 people from losing a hand, would it still be so obvious we should save the one life? If not, where's the cutoff, and if there's no cutoff, aren't we just saying numbers never matter, which seems crazy too?

So my question isn't really "which proposal," though I'd take a view on that. It's how to even think about it. Is there something confused about adding up lots of smaller harms across different people and weighing the total against one person's huge loss — like it's a category mistake, since nobody experiences "the total"? Or is refusing to aggregate just an excuse for arbitrary intuition, and any honest decision procedure has to let numbers count eventually? If the anti-adding view is right, I'd love to know how it handles the losing-a-hand case, and if the adding view is right, I'd love to know what stops it from saying we should let someone die to cure a billion headaches. My brother-in-law, who's a doctor, just says "save the life in front of you," which I find emotionally satisfying but I'm not sure it's an argument. What do you think?
