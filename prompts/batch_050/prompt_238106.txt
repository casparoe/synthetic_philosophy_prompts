I am a senior in high school and co-run our school's philosophy club, which meets weekly and has about fifteen members, most with no background in decision theory. Last month we argued about Newcomb's problem across two meetings, and I noticed something I want to turn into an activity: several members called themselves one-boxers after the first session but reversed themselves when I described the transparent-box version, and one member one-boxed against the predictor yet saw no problem with smoking in the case where a lesion causes both the desire to smoke and the illness, with smoking itself harmless. My conclusion is that most of us do not know which position we actually hold, and I want a short diagnostic instrument that finds out.

Please design a "which decision theory do your verdicts commit you to?" quiz for the club, subject to these constraints.

Format and audience. Eight questions. Each should be a concrete case answerable in ordinary English, with no occurrence of the words "evidential" or "causal" and no theory named anywhere in the questions. Each question offers two or three options. Stipulate every detail that could change a verdict — the predictor's track record and how it is known, what the agent knows about the mechanism, whether the boxes are transparent — since those details are exactly what made our members switch.

Cases that must appear, in some form: (1) the original Newcomb setup with an extremely accurate predictor and an opaque box; (2) a version with both boxes transparent; (3) a smoking-lesion-style case, where an action is correlated with a bad outcome without causing it and no predictor is involved; (4) the desert-driver case, where a rescuer saves you only because he correctly predicted you would pay him afterward; (5) the perfect-copy dilemma, where you and an exact replica of yourself each choose whether to cooperate, in isolation; (6) the coin-flip extortion case, where a reliable being rewards you in the actual world only because it predicts you would have paid in the counterfactual one. You may add up to two further cases of your own devising if they do diagnostic work these six do not.

Deliverables. (a) The eight questions with their options. (b) A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions; the leaves must include at least the causalist who two-boxes, the evidentialist who one-boxes on grounds of evidence, and the position that one-boxes, pays the driver, cooperates with the copy, and pays in the coin-flip case on grounds of logical or subjunctive dependence. (c) Since eight questions generate far more answer patterns than there are named positions, a table of the unoccupied patterns, diagnosing each as incoherent, merely unstable, or simply unexplored; I am especially curious about the member who two-boxes Newcomb yet pays in the coin-flip case, and the member who one-boxes Newcomb yet smokes and stiffs the driver — could anyone hold those combinations on principle? (d) A candid note on which positions the quiz cannot tell apart; I understand several refined variants of the one-boxing-and-paying position agree on every case listed, and the quiz should say so rather than pretend to separate them, stating what kind of question, if any, could. (e) A "consistency index" for the newsletter: a way of flagging members whose pattern matches no leaf, so a result reads "closest to X, but note the tension between your answers to questions 2 and 6" instead of forcing everyone into a box.

The aim is to sort members by their verdicts on cases rather than by the labels they favor, so please do not smuggle the labels into the questions; a member who has never heard of decision theory should be able to complete the whole quiz.
