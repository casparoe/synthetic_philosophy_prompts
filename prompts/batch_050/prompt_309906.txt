I am a novelist, and each summer I lead a week-long residential workshop for eight advanced fiction writers — people with published stories, well beyond basics, but with no philosophical training. This year I want to build the central craft session, ninety minutes, around an idea I have adapted from philosophy: the denial of what Wilfrid Sellars called the myth of the given. As I use it, the idea is that there is no layer of experience that simply presents itself to us uninterpreted — that even our most basic takings-in of how things are arrive already shaped by the concepts, expectations, and purposes we bring. My conviction is that this is the deepest thing a prose writer can learn about description: that there is no neutral rendering of a room, a face, or a street, and that the illusion of neutrality is itself a stylistic choice rather than an achievement of accuracy.

Plan the session for me; do not deliver it. I want the architecture. Specify the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and what depends on what — I suspect the writers must first catch themselves believing that their own early drafts contain neutral description before any abstract principle can take hold, but tell me whether that suspicion is right and why. Design the single carrying example around which the whole session turns: my instinct is two descriptions of the same kitchen, neither marked as anyone's perception, which turn out to be irreconcilable in what they find salient, and I want to know what such an example must contain and — equally — what it must not contain if it is not to collapse into the banal point that narrators have opinions. Map the misconceptions my students will predictably form, in the order they will form them, and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. I anticipate at least these: that this is merely show-don't-tell in grander dress; that it amounts to the claim that nothing is real or that all observation is arbitrary; that the practical remedy is simply more sensory detail; and that it is only a point about unreliable narrators. Finally, give me the closing exercise or question whose results would show the idea has actually landed rather than been politely absorbed, and tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level — the epistemological machinery, the history of the doctrine — and how to mark that refusal so it reads as restraint rather than evasion.
