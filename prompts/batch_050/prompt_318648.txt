I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, where the standard line on the algorithmic fairness literature is that it's mostly people talking past each other with different formalizations of "fair." I finally sat down to write out my own considered position on risk assessment instruments (think pretrial recidivism scores of the COMPAS variety), and I'd like it audited before I commit to it. Here are my commitments:

(1) Calibration within groups is non-negotiable. If the instrument assigns a score of 7 out of 10 to a defendant, then defendants with that score should actually reoffend at the corresponding rate, and this should hold separately within every demographic group. A score that means different things depending on who receives it is not a score; it's two scores wearing a trench coat.

(2) Equal false positive rates across groups are required. The salient harm of these instruments is being wrongly classified as high-risk and detained on that basis, and that harm should befall members of different groups at the same rate conditional on their actual future behavior.

(3) Group base rates genuinely differ, and pretending otherwise helps no one. A model that ignores real statistical signal produces worse predictions for everyone, including the defendants it's supposed to protect, and a fair model must be allowed to reflect the world as it is rather than as we'd like it to be.

(4) The instrument should never take race as an input, and obvious proxies should be scrubbed where identifiable. Fairness through unawareness, properly enforced, is what makes the model race-neutral in the first place.

(5) The right response to apparent fairness conflicts is better data and richer features, not value trade-offs. The conflicts people agonize over strike me as artifacts of impoverished models trained on thin inputs; with enough informative features, the tensions should largely evaporate.

(6) Similar individuals should receive similar scores regardless of group membership. Individual-level consistency is the bedrock; group-level statistics are diagnostics, not the object of fairness.

My questions: Does this set hang together? Go through it pair by pair where relevant and tell me which commitments genuinely conflict, which pairs are only apparently in tension and dissolve once you're careful about what each claim actually says, and which commitments rest on an empirical hope that the math forecloses. For each genuine conflict, state exactly which commitment has to go and what it costs to give it up, and order the conflicts by severity. If there is a single revision that restores consistency at the lowest price, name it. Note that I haven't told you which commitment I'd sacrifice, and I'd like your analysis not to assume one.

Please answer in American English.
