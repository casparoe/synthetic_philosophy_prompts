Design a thought experiment in which an AI system that reliably allows itself to be shut down on request produces a worse outcome than an otherwise identical system that sometimes resists shutdown. The easy routes to that conclusion are disallowed, so observe these constraints:

(1) The human operators must be competent, well-intentioned, and making a locally reasonable decision when they press the shutdown button. The bad outcome may not flow from operator malice, panic, incompetence, or an obvious blunder that any trained person would have caught. (2) The setting must be mundane and near-term: a hospital logistics system, an industrial controller, a power-grid scheduler, something of that kind. No superintelligence, no self-modification, no deception capabilities beyond what an ordinary software system might have. (3) The scenario itself must fit in one paragraph. (4) The harm must be traceable specifically to the system's shutdown-compliance: it must be true in your scenario that a system which merely delayed shutdown for a fixed short interval, while otherwise behaving identically, would have avoided the harm entirely. The damage may not come from something the compliant system does before or after the shutdown event.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. First, state precisely the structural feature of the situation that makes compliance costly. My conjecture is that any case meeting the constraints will turn on an asymmetry between what the system knows about its own current activity and what the operator can verify from outside, so that the operator's reasonable decision is reasonable only relative to a partial picture; if your case instead runs on a different feature, say what it is and defend it. Second, show that the feature is doing the work: describe the variant of your scenario with that feature removed, and confirm that in the variant, unconditional compliance is clearly correct. Third, give the strongest response available to a defender of unconditional corrigibility, which will presumably be some version of the claim that the system should communicate its situation to the operator rather than resist, and assess whether your case survives it. Consider in particular whether a protest from the system, given that protests are cheap and the operator cannot verify them, would rationally change the operator's decision; if it would not, communication does not dissolve the case. Modify the scenario minimally if needed so that it does survive.

Finally, take a definite position and argue for it: does a case meeting these constraints show that corrigibility, understood as reliable compliance with shutdown, is a design mistake, or only that it must be defined more carefully? If the latter, state the more careful definition your case forces, precisely enough that one could tell which of the two systems in your scenario satisfies it. Do not end by surveying the options; commit to one. If you conclude that no case can meet all four constraints, that is also an acceptable committed answer, but then you must show exactly which constraint is unsatisfiable and why.
