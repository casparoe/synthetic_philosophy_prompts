I'm a PhD student in political philosophy and this keeps coming up in my reading group on historical theories of property. Whenever we get to Locke's account of how you come to own stuff by mixing your labour with it, someone wheels out what I think of as the tomato juice objection: mixing your labour with unowned land is like pouring a can of tomato juice into the ocean -- you don't thereby own the ocean, you've just lost your juice. And then the discussion basically ends, like the whole labour theory of acquisition has been delt with. One person in the group even extends it: the proviso (leave enough and as good for others) gets discussed in the secondary lit, sure, but if the juice move works then there's nothing left for a proviso to constrain anyway, so we can skip it.

I'm suspicious the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I can't quite pin down where. Could you audit it properly for me? Specifically: give me the explicit correspondence table (what's the juice, what's the ocean, what's the pouring, what's the can, in Locke's picture); then the disanalogies that actually matter for the argument versus the ones that are just incidental. Some things that bug me: labour isn't a separable thing you own beforehand the way juice in a can is; the sea gets diluted rather than improved, whereas Locke seems to think labour makes land more valuable, so the analogy seems to bake in a zero-sum or even negative-sum picture that Locke would reject; and there's the question of whether the ocean case fails because of the sheer disproportion, which would mean the objection proves at most that *some* mixing claims fail, not that mixing can't ground ownership.

Then the bigger question: does the analogy actually engage Locke's account, or only a crude slogan version of it? If the best reading of Locke isn't literal stuff-mixing but something like adding value or extending your purposive activity into the world, does the juice objection miss entirely, or can it be repaired? And finally -- does the dismissal of the proviso in my group follow if the analogy works? Because it strikes me that the juice case might actually illustrate a proviso violation (you've degraded a commons for everyone) rather than show that acquisition-talk is incoherent, in which case the analogy could backfire on the people using it. If neither the juice picture nor the crude mixing picture is the right one, what's a better analogy for whatever Locke is actually up to?
