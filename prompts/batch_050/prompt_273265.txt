So I'm a retired physicist and lately I've been reading about the history of science, and I've landed on a position that I gather is a bit out of fashion: I think the so-called Whiggish way of writing history -- treating the past as, to a large extent, a story of progress toward what we now know -- is basically defensible when it comes to science specifically. Not for political or moral history, where "progress" is murkier, but science really did get somewhere (germs really do cause disease, phlogiston really doesn't exist), so judging earlier figures by how close they got seems fine to me. What would count against this view? I don't want a survey of what historians grumble about -- I want the actual defeaters: which specific historical cases does my view handle badly or can't describe, which commitments does it force on me that I might not want (e.g., about what "truth" or "knowledge" even means historically), and where does it collapse into something weaker than I thought I was defending. Please rank these from fatal to merely annoying, and say which ones a smart Whig would actually concede as damaging versus brush off. And I'd rather you take a definite stand at the end -- is my position salvageable in some restricted form, and if so which form, rather than just laying out the menu.
