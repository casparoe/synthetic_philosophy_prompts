I am a retired population geneticist who has spent the last two years making up for a career spent mostly ignoring the philosophy of probability and decision theory. A reading group I belong to has lately been working through material on ergodicity economics — in particular the distinction between the ensemble average (the expected value across parallel possible outcomes) and the time average (the growth rate a single individual actually experiences as a gamble is repeated over time), along with the well-known coin-toss game in which each round multiplies your wealth by 1.5 on heads or 0.6 on tails, so that the expected value is positive while the time-average growth rate is negative. One member of the group, a former actuary named Margaret, has firm views on which risks are worth taking, and I have been keeping a private tally of her verdicts, because I suspect she is running a consistent rule but cannot work out which one. Her record so far: she refused to play the 1.5/0.6 coin-toss game for repeated rounds at her full bankroll, despite conceding its positive expected value; she happily accepted a one-off bet of £1,000 at even odds to receive £2,500, calling it good value since she could afford the loss; she pays her house insurance premiums without complaint, although she knows they exceed the actuarial expected loss; and she refused a repeated double-or-nothing bet with a 55 per cent chance of winning when the condition was that 60 per cent of her current wealth be staked afresh on every round, again despite its positive expected value per round.

Here is the case I intend to put to her at our next meeting. A wealthy acquaintance offers her a strictly one-off opportunity, contractually guaranteed never to be repeated: she may stake 90 per cent of her total savings on a single wager with a 55 per cent chance of doubling the stake and a 45 per cent chance of losing it entirely. I would like you to predict her verdict, but I am more interested in your reasoning than in the prediction itself. Please identify the candidate decision rules that are each consistent with all four of her earlier verdicts — I can think of at least three, including maximising the time-average growth rate of wealth, ordinary expected-utility maximisation with diminishing marginal utility of some curvature other than the logarithmic one, and a simpler heuristic of refusing any risk of ruin while otherwise maximising expected value — and state what each candidate predicts for the new wager. Where a candidate rule does not obviously apply to a strictly non-repeatable bet (since there is no long-run growth rate to maximise), please say so, and explain what that reveals about whether the rule was really about repetition all along or is better understood as a fixed attitude to risk. Then give your best guess as to which rule Margaret is actually running, with reasons. Finally, describe one further wager, different from all of the above, whose outcome would most cleanly discriminate between the two leading candidate rules, and explain why it would settle the matter. Please write your answer in British English.
