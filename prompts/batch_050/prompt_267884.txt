At a logic reading group, two graduate students were discussing Carroll’s regress argument concerning deduction. Their exchange ran as follows:

1. Mara said that the regress is not merely about an unusually stubborn reasoner. Imagine someone who accepts \(P\) and also accepts “if \(P\), then \(Q\)” but does not infer \(Q\). Adding “if \(P\), and if \(P\), then \(Q\), then \(Q\)” as another premise gives the person one more thing to believe, but still does not produce the transition to \(Q\). Modus ponens must therefore be something a reasoner does, not merely another proposition the reasoner accepts.

2. Julian replied that this separates belief and action too cleanly. Anyone who genuinely accepts \(P\) and “if \(P\), then \(Q\)” is already committed to \(Q\). If the person refuses to draw the conclusion, the likely explanation is either failure to understand the conditional or an inconsistent set of commitments. Validity is a relation among propositions; no psychological act of inferring is required to make \(Q\) follow from the premises.

3. Mara answered that Julian was conflating entailment with inference. The premises may objectively entail \(Q\), while the person still lacks an epistemically legitimate route to \(Q\). A proof is not just a list of true propositions; it is a sequence of transitions. Unless some rule licenses each transition, a formal derivation has no epistemic force for the person following it.

4. Julian objected that the language of “licensing” recreates the dilemma. If the license is itself a proposition that must be accepted, the regress begins again. If it is instead a policy, disposition, or practice, it may causally explain why the reasoner moves to \(Q\), but it is unclear how it could justify that move. In his view, Carroll’s argument shows that the demand for a wholly noncircular justification of deduction is misconceived, not that deduction contains a missing ingredient.

5. Mara then offered a metalogical justification: any interpretation that makes \(P\) and “if \(P\), then \(Q\)” true also makes \(Q\) true. Modus ponens is therefore truth-preserving, and showing that it is truth-preserving justifies its use. She argued that circularity is fatal only if one is trying to convince someone who refuses every inferential rule whatsoever; ordinary justification may legitimately employ the very pattern whose reliability is being established.

6. Julian’s final response was that Mara had confused two different achievements. Her semantic argument may show that modus ponens preserves truth, but it already relies on conditional reasoning and therefore cannot supply the basic entitlement to use it. Moreover, proving that a rule is reliable does not by itself explain what makes a particular movement from premises to conclusion an inference rather than a mere association or habitual response.

They agreed that the premises entail the conclusion, but continued to disagree about what further thing, if any, is required for a reasoner to be justified in inferring the conclusion.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly correct, partly correct, or mistaken, and explain why. In doing so, address at least the following issues: whether modus ponens must be a premise, a rule, or something else; whether accepting premises is sufficient for being committed to their conclusion; the distinction between entailment and inference; whether Mara’s semantic justification is viciously circular; and whether Julian’s distinction between causal explanation and justification successfully answers her. Finally, identify the most important distinction or question that both speakers have left insufficiently examined.
