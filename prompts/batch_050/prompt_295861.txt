I'm a novelist working on my fourth book, and I keep writing characters who do terrible things without any apparent guilt, and my critique partners keep telling me these characters read like cartoons -- either they're cackling villains or they're basically just autistic-coded people who get punished for missing social cues. I went down a rabbit hole about whether real psychopaths actually know that what they're doing is wrong, and it turns out this is genuinely contested: they pass the standard tests (they can say murder is wrong, they can say why society condemns it), but there's research suggesting they draw the moral/conventional distinction differently -- like, they don't reliably treat "hitting someone" as wrong independent of what an authority figure says, the way they treat "talking in class without raising your hand." So there's this whole question of whether reciting the rules counts as knowing right from wrong, or whether moral knowledge requires some kind of felt grasp or internalization that they lack. Fascinating philosophically, but I need it to be *usable*.

So here's what I want, and I'd like you to act as a moral psychologist turned fiction consultant: think carefully and turn the psychopathy-and-moral-knowledge debate into a working procedure I can run each time I draft or revise a scene with one of these characters. Give me a checklist or sequence of tests I apply to a character's dialogue, interiority, and choices: questions like "does this character distinguish rules-that-are-rules-because-someone-said-so from rules-that-would-be-wrong-anyway, and if not, how does that show up in what they say rather than in an info-dump?" It needs to handle the fact that I'm doing this maybe fifteen times per manuscript, per scene, in half-hour revision passes, and I'm not a psychologist. Specify which distinctions from the literature have to survive translation into fiction (my instinct: the moral/conventional one, and the reciting-rules vs. actually-being-governed-by-them one) and which I can safely drop without writing something false. Be explicit about where the checklist stops and taste has to take over.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- something like "a convincing low-empathy character is built by doing X, not Y" -- then give the procedure, no preamble. Then run it on three quick cases so I can see it work: (1) a hedge fund guy who ruins people legally and genuinely can't see what the fuss is about, (2) a character I'm scared of, a charismatic con artist who quotes scripture and *seems* to know right from wrong perfectly, and (3) a protagonist I want readers to keep liking even though the procedure will probably tell me she's written as more monstrous than I intended -- I want at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like, so be honest about it. Also flag what the procedure can't settle, e.g. whether the character is ultimately responsible for what they do, which I suspect I have to decide as an authorial choice rather than derive.
