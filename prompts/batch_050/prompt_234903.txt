I am convening a workshop on the ethics and epistemology of generalizing about peoples, and I want the two invited participants to produce a joint statement in the style of an adversarial collaboration rather than another exchange of position papers. Draft that statement for them. The first participant is a cross-cultural psychologist who holds that "national character" is a defensible statistical construct: on her view, aggregate differences in personality, values, and dispositions between national populations are real (if typically small in magnitude), measurable, and occasionally relevant to explanation and policy, and she regards a generalization about a people as epistemically on a par with any other actuarial generalization about a group one did not choose to join. The second participant is a critic who holds that national character talk is a conceptual error dressed up as an empirical finding: he grants that individuals differ psychologically, that cultures shape behavior, and that survey instruments detect cross-national variation, but he denies that these admissions license the notion of a people's character, and he thinks the concept survives only by oscillating between an essentialist reading that is false and a probabilistic reading too weak to do the work its users expect of it. Both, I stipulate, reject crude biological essentialism; both accept that within-population variance on the relevant traits exceeds between-population variance; and neither thinks the other's view is held in bad faith.

The document should contain, in this order: a numbered list of claims both parties can sign, concerning what the empirical record does and does not establish and what a charitable version of each position must concede; a set of precisely formulated residual disagreements, each stated in wording the party whose view it describes would itself accept, with particular care that the psychologist's formulation not build in the legitimacy of the construct and the critic's not build in its incoherence; the cruxes, where a crux is a sub-question such that its resolution would force at least one side to revise -- I expect candidates to include whether measurement artifacts such as reference-group effects and acquiescence bias undermine the data at the level of between-country comparisons, whether a small but reliable mean difference can ever justify a person-level inference, whether a probabilistic generalization about a nationality differs in any principled way from actuarial generalizations used in insurance or hiring, and whether the foreseeable misuse of such generalizations bears on their epistemic status or only on the ethics of publishing them -- and for each crux, a concrete study design, case, or argument both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance of the outcome; and finally a diagnosis of which portions of the original dispute are, on joint inspection, verbal, with the reason given in each case. Do not adjudicate between the positions, and do not let the agreed-claims section collapse into banalities that a third party with no view on the matter would also sign: each agreed claim should be one that at least one of the two initially found it cost something to accept.
