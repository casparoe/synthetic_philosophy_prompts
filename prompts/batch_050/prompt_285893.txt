Ok so here's where I'm at on forgiveness, going purely by gut on cases, and I want to know what principle (if any) is behind my verdicts, because I suspect there isn't one. Case one: a stranger rear-ends me, gets out genuinely mortified, apologizes, pays for everything. I think I'd basically be doing something wrong if I *didn't* forgive him -- like the apology settles it and staying cold would be a character flaw in me. Case two: my mother pressured me for years to forgive my grandfather for what he did to her, and I think that pressure itself was wrong -- nobody gets to demand forgiveness on someone else's behalf, and she didn't owe him anything. Case three: a friend forgives the guy who screwed *me* over in a business deal, on my behalf, and it made me furious -- I don't think he had the standing to do that, forgiveness isn't transferable. Case four: my uncle forgave his business partner who never apologized, never admitted anything, and would do it again tomorrow, and everyone calls him a saint for it -- but honestly it reads to me less like virtue and more like he's just letting himself be walked on, condoning it almost. Case five: same uncle, but with the guy who keeps borrowing money and never pays it back -- there the "forgiveness" seems totally fine and even good, just declining to hold the debt over him. So forgiving an unrepentant repeat offender of a debt feels noble, forgiving an unrepentant repeat betrayer feels like doormat behavior, and I can't articulate why those differ. Case six: forgiving someone after they're dead -- I think this is genuinely possible and can matter, my cousin did it and it changed her, even though there's obviously no transaction happening. Case seven: self-forgiveness -- I keep saying people "should forgive themselves" but when I think hard about it I'm not sure it's the same thing at all, it feels metaphorical next to the real cases.

So: is there one account that gets me all of these -- that forgiveness can be obligatory after a real apology for a small wrong, that it can't be owed to third parties or demanded by them, that standing matters, that repetition-without-repentance poisons it for wrongs but not for debts, and that it survives the death of the wrongdoer? If not, which of my verdicts is the weak one and has to go? I have a tentative principle myself -- forgiveness is obligatory exactly when the wrongdoer has done everything reparation requires, and supererogatory otherwise -- but I think case two and the dead-offender case break it, so tell me how to repair it or what to replace it with. And say what your account predicts for a case I haven't listed, like whether a country can forgive another country's historical wrongs. Please write your answer as continuous prose, like a letter -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
