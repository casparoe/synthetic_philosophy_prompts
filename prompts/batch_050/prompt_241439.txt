A friend of mine has just published a big nonfiction book (popular science, ~400 pages, loads of factual claims) and in the preface she thanks her fact-checkers and then writes something like: despite everyone's efforts, a book of this size is bound to contain some errors, and those are mine alone. Standard humble preface stuff. Anyway, at her launch event some guy in the audience pounced on it: "So you admit the book contains falsehoods. Then you don't actually stand by it. Either you stand by a claim or you don't — and you've just told us you don't." She mumbled something about it being a manner of speaking and we all moved on, but it's been bugging me for days, because it feels related to that thing where you'd rationally believe of any particular lottery ticket that it will lose, yet you know one of them will win.

So here's what I'd like: take this guy's slogan — you either stand by a claim or you don't — and spell out the different precise things "standing by" a claim could amount to. E.g. it could mean being certain of it, or having a credence above some threshold, or being willing to assert it, or being willing to bet on it at some odds, or staking your reputation on it, or refusing to qualify it, and probably others I haven't thought of. For each reading, say whether it's a plausible norm for belief or assertion at all, and crucially whether it actually gets him his conclusion that my friend's preface is irrational or that she's being somehow dishonest. I'm fairly convinced his move is a trick, but I want to know exactly which reading he's tacitly relying on and why that reading is one nobody could actually live by (it seems to me it would forbid believing essentially anything empirical, but maybe that's too quick).

Please don't just give me a menu and shrug — commit to an answer: which precisification is the right one for ordinary belief and assertion, what's the correct way to describe my friend's epistemic state (does she believe a contradiction? believe each claim while believing their conjunction is false? or is the right description in terms of credences all the way down?), and where exactly the lottery case and the preface case come apart, if they do. If you think the audience guy is actually onto something, say so, but give reasons. And write it in British English please.
