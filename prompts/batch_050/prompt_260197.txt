I am a PhD student in bioethics working on the structure of justificatory criteria in health law, and I am trying to pin down something that puzzles me about assisted dying statutes, without yet committing to a view on whether assisted dying is permissible at all.

The practice I have in mind is this. Jurisdictions that permit some form of physician-assisted dying tend to fall into one of two families. In the first family (the US states that allow it, for instance), eligibility requires a terminal diagnosis with a prognosis of roughly six months or less to live; unbearable suffering, however great, is neither necessary nor sufficient. In the second family (the Netherlands, Belgium, and one track of Canada's regime), eligibility turns on unbearable and irremediable suffering, with no requirement that death be imminent; a person with decades of life expectancy can qualify if their suffering meets the standard. Both families insist on capacity, voluntariness, and repeated requests, so those are shared. What interests me is the divergence in the substantive gateway.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of what each gateway presupposes. Specifically: the six-month prognosis model seems to treat assisted death as permissible only when the person is, in some sense, already dying, as though what is being authorized is the timing and manner of an imminent death rather than a choice to end a life. Does that commit it to the view that death's badness is exhausted by what it deprives us of, so that where little good life remains there is little harm to consent to, or to some quite different picture, for instance that medicine may not hasten death but may stop resisting it? And the suffering model seems to presuppose that remaining life can be worth less than nothing to the person living it, and that the patient is the authoritative judge of that. But I suspect each model also quietly rules out rival views it never argues against, and I suspect each is internally unstable: the prognosis model rests on predictions physicians are known to make badly, and the suffering model typically draws lines (mental suffering alone, minors, advance directives) that sit awkwardly with its own logic. Please identify, for each model, what it strictly presupposes versus what merely fits it comfortably, where it is philosophically underdetermined (could several distinct theories of death's badness license the same statute?), and where its internal boundaries betray a compromise between incompatible commitments. Finally, tell me what each regime would have to look like if we replaced its implicit theory with a straightforward autonomy-based view on which competent adults may end their lives for any adequately considered reason.

Throughout, I would like you to flag how confident you are in each part of the reconstruction — distinguishing claims that follow from the legal texts themselves from claims that are your best interpretation — and to single out explicitly the one point in your answer you are least sure of and why.
