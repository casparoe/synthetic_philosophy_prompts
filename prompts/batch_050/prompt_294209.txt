I teach epistemology and the philosophy of law, and for the past three years I have served on my university's academic integrity panel. We hear about forty cases a term, each in a single one-hour hearing, and we must decide, under the university's clear-and-convincing-evidence standard, whether to impose sanctions ranging from a failing grade to expulsion. Our rulings can be appealed, and the panel includes scientists, an economist, and student members with no philosophical training. The cases that consistently divide us are those resting on what I take the evidence literature to call naked statistical evidence. Typical examples: a multiple-choice exam on which two students gave identical answers to thirteen of fifteen questions, including the same four wrong answers, with the instructor's calculation that the probability of such agreement arising independently is less than one in ten thousand; a plagiarism-detection service reporting 68 percent textual overlap between a submitted essay and a paper sold online; and a department's proposal that students fitting a certain statistical profile should be presumptively treated as cheats because, historically, nine in ten students with that profile were found to have cheated. My economist colleague's position is simple: if the posterior probability of guilt exceeds the threshold the standard requires, we must find against the student, and any reluctance is innumeracy. I keep wanting to resist by appeal to the familiar puzzles — the bus case, in which a plaintiff can show only that the defendant's company operates most of the buses on the route where she was run off the road, and the gatecrasher hypothetical, in which an organizer can show that most attendees entered without paying yet intuitively cannot recover against any particular attendee — but I cannot get my colleagues to see why these intuitions should govern an actual tribunal rather than being dismissed as squeamishness. What I want from you is a translation of whatever the philosophical literature on this problem genuinely establishes into a procedure my panel can actually run in a hearing. Please identify which distinctions must survive the translation for intelligent non-philosophers — for instance, whatever turns out to be defensible in the distinction between a bare base rate and evidence that is individualized or case-specific, the reference class problem and who bears the burden of resolving it, the difference between the probability that the evidence would exist given innocence and the probability of innocence given the evidence, and whatever explains why an extremely strong statistical case such as a DNA match feels different in kind from the gatecrasher — and which refinements can safely be dropped. The deliverable should be an ordered set of questions to ask about any piece of statistical evidence, with a statement of what counts as a satisfactory answer to each; the point at which the procedure halts with a finding of insufficient evidence no matter how high the reported number is; the points where the procedure must stop and hand the question to the panel as a matter of values rather than evidence (I suspect the legitimacy of convicting someone on a bare statistic is one of these, but I want your view); and an honest account of where the procedure will misfire. Then apply it to the three cases above — the matching exam answers, the 68 percent overlap report, and the statistical profile presumption — and make sure at least one application delivers a verdict the panel will find genuinely unsatisfying, explaining why that outcome is a consequence of the principle rather than a defect in it.
