Here's a position I find myself holding, and I want it stress-tested rather than defended. The position: when a gamble has an undefined expected value — as in the Pasadena-style construction where the payoff series is only conditionally convergent, so that rearranging the terms lets you make the sum come out to anything — rationality simply falls silent about whether to take the gamble. On my view, there is no fact of the matter about whether accepting is better than refusing; any verdict a decision theory delivers here is an artifact of how it happens to order the terms, not a discovery about the gamble. Since I can't derive a unique expectation, I treat the choice as rationally optional in the same way I treat choices between incommensurable goods.

What would count against this? I'm looking for the strongest defeaters, ordered by how much they should move me. Some candidate pressure points I've half-considered: arguments that dominance or stochastic-dominance reasoning can constrain the choice even where expectations don't exist; invariance principles that pin down a verdict regardless of how the series is arranged; claims that the same conditionally-convergent structure shows up in more mundane settings (infinite-horizon plans, unbounded utility functions, infinitary population ethics), so my "rational silence" verdict would generalize further than I'm comfortable with; and the charge that I owe a positive account of what *does* decide the question, since "anything goes" is itself a substantive decision-theoretic commitment that needs defending rather than a neutral default.

For each defeater, say whether a fair-minded defender of my position would concede it as a genuine threat or bat it away as question-begging, and distinguish what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward. Finally: if my position survives, tell me honestly whether it survives because it's well-founded or because it's been insulated — i.e., whether "rationality is silent here" is the kind of claim that could ever be forced to pay rent in a concrete case.
