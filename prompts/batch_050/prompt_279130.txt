I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on the epistemology of legal proof, and I've been asked to lead our jurisprudence reading group for a session on Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957). I want to frame the film as an intervention in the naked statistical evidence debate -- the blue bus case, the gatecrasher paradox, and the question of what proof beyond a reasonable doubt requires beyond high probability. Read the film in those terms for me.

Here is the tension I want pressed in both directions. On one side, the film appears to dramatize exactly the orthodox philosophical position: the prosecution's evidence is individuated rather than statistical (an old man's testimony about hearing and seeing the defendant flee, a neighbor's identification through the windows of a passing elevated train, a supposedly distinctive knife), and the deliberation proceeds by testing each piece against the particulars of the case. Juror 10's overt appeal to base rates about what defendants from slum backgrounds are like is treated by the film as a moral failure rather than a rational one, which fits the intuition that naked statistics cannot legitimate a verdict. On the other side, the jury's own case for doubt is riddled with statistical reasoning: generalizations about how reliably frightened eyewitnesses perceive, about the motivations of elderly witnesses who feel overlooked, about what a roaring train does to the conditions of observation. Even the reenactment of the old man's walk to his front door is a crude frequency argument. So the film seems to condemn base-rate reasoning in one register while relying on it in another. Does that distinction survive scrutiny, and does it map onto any of the principled lines the literature has tried to draw -- individuating versus statistical evidence, sensitivity or safety conditions on the knowledge that verdicts are supposed to express, the difference between the weight and the strength of a body of evidence?

I would also like the reverse reading. The gatecrasher case generates its paradox by stipulating that purely statistical proof feels illegitimate even when it exceeds the relevant standard; but 12 Angry Men arguably shows that individuated evidence can be epistemically worse than the statistics it displaces, since everything the prosecution offers belongs to categories of testimony we have excellent aggregate reasons to distrust. Does the film, taken seriously, undermine the very intuition the gatecrasher paradox trades on?

Be honest about where the film resists this treatment: it is a drama about prejudice, civic duty, and group dynamics rather than an essay in evidence law, and some of its famous beats may be doing moral or political work that my epistemological frame simply flattens. Finally, tell me what a film like this contributes to the debate that a journal article cannot -- is there anything about how statistical and testimonial reasons actually move deliberators that only becomes visible when you watch the deliberation happen?

Please write the response in American English, and don't worry about citing specific articles; I'm after the reading itself.
