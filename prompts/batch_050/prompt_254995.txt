So I'm a philosophy PhD student who's been embedded in an AI research lab for a fellowship year, and I've somehow ended up on the lab's model welfare review board (yes we have one, it's new, nobody knows what they're doing). I need to write our board's decision memo on an actual proposal, and I want to draft it properly rather than just going with vibes.

The case: a postdoc wants to train RL agents in a simulated environment with "predator" entities. To get robust avoidance behavior, they want to use very large negative rewards (−1000 per timestep of predator contact, vs. +1 for task completion), across roughly 200,000 training episodes. The agent architecture has a persistent internal "threat" variable that (a) globally shifts the policy toward caution even in contexts where no predator is present, (b) carries over between episodes, and (c) is legible to the agent itself via a readout head it can condition plans on.

Our chair drafted a criterion for when a reward signal counts as welfare-relevant valence, i.e. when a negative signal might be something like suffering rather than just a number in a training loop: (1) the signal updates a persistent internal state that modulates behavior across contexts, (2) the agent would act to avoid the signal even at a cost to its task performance, and (3) the state is integrated into a self-model the agent uses for planning. Under this criterion, the proposed agents plausibly pass all three, which would make this, by our own lights, an experiment that mass-produces something in the vicinity of distress.

Draft the memo for me. It needs to recommend approve / approve with conditions / reject, and actually land somewhere. A few things it has to deal with:

- One senior board member thinks the whole framing is confused: the reward is a scalar in the update rule, the policy never observes the reward as an input, the agent just gets shaped, so there's nothing it is like to be shaped. Engage the strongest version of this, not the dumb one.
- Condition (2) of the chair's criterion looks untestable here, since predator avoidance IS the rewarded behavior -- avoiding the negative signal and succeeding at the task are the same trajectory, so we can't seperate "wanting to avoid the signal" from "doing the task". Does that break the criterion or just its application?
- Whether the magnitude of the penalty matters at all, morally speaking, or whether it's the architecture and training regime that matter.
- If you go with approve-with-conditions, the conditions have to be things the postdoc could actually implement and we could actually verify, not "ensure the agents aren't suffering".

The memo should be honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side of whatever you recommend -- the chair will not circulate anything that strawmans the opposition.
