Between 1961 and 1989, well over a hundred people were killed attempting to cross the Berlin Wall, and several hundred more along the inner-German border as a whole. The legal situation at the time was roughly this: East Germany's Border Law of 1982 expressly authorized border guards to use firearms, as a last resort when milder means had failed, to prevent crossings that the state classified as serious crimes, and unlawful departure from the country had itself been made a criminal offence under the East German penal code. After reunification, German courts prosecuted former guards and, later, the officials who had directed border policy. Because the reunification arrangements largely required acts to be judged under the law in force where and when they were committed, with the milder of the two German states' laws to be applied, the courts could not simply impose West German homicide law retroactively. They convicted on two grounds: first, that the shootings were unlawful even under East German law properly interpreted, since East Germany had ratified the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, with its protections of life and of the freedom to leave any country, and its own constitution purported to guarantee similar rights; and second, invoking the Radbruch formula, that positive law which conflicts with justice to an intolerable degree must yield to justice and loses its character as law. The European Court of Human Rights later held that these convictions did not violate the prohibition on retroactive punishment.

Analyze this episode through the Hart–Fuller debate.

Begin with Hart. On his legal positivism, was the Border Law valid law, and were the guards' actions lawful at the time they were committed? Trace the analysis through the rule of recognition rather than skipping to the moral verdict. Hart took the view, in connection with the postwar German cases, that where punishment is morally demanded despite the lawfulness of the act when performed, the honest course is an openly retroactive statute, not the pretense that the act was criminal all along. Was that course actually available to the German courts here, given the reunification settlement? If it was not, work out precisely what Hart's position commits him to saying should have happened, and whether that conclusion is one the positivist can comfortably own.

Turn to Fuller. Test the East German border regime against his eight desiderata of legality: generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, practicability, stability, and congruence between official action and declared rule. The uncomfortable result appears to be that the Border Law passes most of these tests rather well — it was published, clear, stable, and official practice conformed to it closely. Does Fuller's theory therefore lack the resources to condemn this law as non-law? Must he reach for something like Radbruch's substantive formula, and if he must, does that concession show that a purely procedural natural law was never sufficient on its own — or is there a Fullerian line of resistance, perhaps through the desiderata of generality or congruence, that you can construct and then assess?

Next, press Hart's warning. He argued that appeals to supra-statutory law obscure the real dilemma and invite judges to disregard law whenever they find it wicked. Apply that warning to the courts' first ground of conviction: the claim that the shootings were unlawful under East German law itself. Is this an instance of exactly the evasion Hart criticized, a legitimate exercise in interpretation (the Covenant was, after all, ratified East German law), or something in between? Be disciplined here about what would distinguish interpretation from disguised retroactivity.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction and say what the episode shows about each theory: whether the courts' evident need to deny that the acts were lawful at the time vindicates Hart's charge that natural-law reasoning encourages evasion over candour; whether the case sits beyond the reach of Fuller's eight desiderata, and what that implies about the scope of his theory; and whether the positivist's honest alternatives — acquittal, or punishment by a legislature willing to say plainly what it was doing — were defensible options in a case where men had shot fleeing civilians under colour of valid law.
