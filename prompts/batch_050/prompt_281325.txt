So my book club (four people and a dog, we meet at a bar) just did Rousseau's Social Contract and we got completely stuck on Book I, the bit where he says that whoever refuses to obey the general will can be compelled by the whole community, which he infamously cashes out as being forced to be free. Half of us think this is the most sinister sentence in political philosophy, the other half think it's obviously right once you get what he means, and I honestly can't tell if they're even disagreeing about the same thing. I want to see this fight staged properly, so write me a dialogue.

The two characters: Priya, who defends Rousseau, and Marcus, who thinks the whole general-will apparatus is a con. They're neighbors in a housing co-op, and the dialogue happens the night after a co-op meeting where a majority (with turnout of only about 60% of members) voted to require every unit to pay into a retrofit fund for insulation and heat pumps, whether or not the individual owner wants it. One holdout, an older guy named Leon who abstained and now refuses to pay, is threatening to just not comply. Priya thinks making Leon pay is exactly Rousseau's case and is fine. Marcus thinks this example shows precisely what's rotten in the idea. Some things they should actually grapple with, not just gesture at: whether the general will is the same thing as whatever wins a vote, and what happens to that identification when turnout is low or someone abstains; whether Leon, who may simply not share the collective's priorities and never will, can coherently be described as free when he's made to pay, or whether that only works for citizens who already see the law as their own; and what work the word free is doing in the phrase, i.e. whether it's the ordinary everyday freedom or some redefined rational-autonomy freedom, and whether swapping in the redefined one is a legit move or a bait-and-switch. Marcus should be smart about this and not a cartoon libertarian, and Priya shouldn't hide behind jargon; both of them respond to what the other one actually said, and each should land at least one hit the other struggles to answer. Nobody quotes page numbers at each other, they talk like real people who read the book last week.

Format-wise: start with a section called The Setup, 3 or 4 sentences of scene-setting. Then The Dialogue, the main event, somewhere around 800-1000 words. Then a short section called Scorecard, a few bullet points on each character's best moment and where they dodged or changed the subject. Finish with Unresolved, two or three sentences on the one question you think they genuinely failed to settle.
