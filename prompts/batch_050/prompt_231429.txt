I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and this year I got roped into judging our annual undergraduate essay contest. The question we set was: "Physicists often say things like 'energy is conserved because the laws of physics don't change over time.' Does a symmetry like that actually explain the conservation law, or is it just a fancy way of restating it?"

Below is one of the entries we recieved. Give it a grade (we use A through F with +/-) and, more importantly, write the comments I'd hand back to the student: point-by-point, saying what's right, what's wrong or sloppy, and why. I don't want a general impression — I want enough specific justification that I could defend the grade if the student emails to complain. For context, about half our entrants are physics majors and half are philosophy majors, and we told everyone we expected Noether's theorem to come up but weren't requiring any formalism.

---

The connection between symmetry and conservation is one of the crown jewels of physics. Whenever a physical law has a symmetry, there is a quantity that stays constant — this is the content of Noether's theorem. Because the laws of physics are the same at every time, energy is conserved; because they are the same at every place, momentum is conserved; and because they are the same in every direction, angular momentum is conserved. So symmetry is not merely a mathematical convenience. It is arguably the deepest kind of explanation physics offers, since symmetries pick out what the world must be like regardless of its contingent details.

Take parity, the symmetry between left and right. Since the laws of physics are symmetric under mirror reflection (setting aside the weak interaction), Noether's theorem tells us there must be a conserved quantity corresponding to parity, and indeed physicists do track whether parity is conserved in particle reactions. The theorem is what licenses that practice.

Or consider a planet orbiting a star in a spherically symmetric gravitational field. Because the field is symmetric, the orbit shares that symmetry: the planet moves in a circle. If orbits were ellipses, as the textbooks oddly insist, the symmetry would be broken, and we would expect a conserved quantity to go missing. The fact that nothing goes missing shows the symmetry is doing real explanatory work.

Finally, does the symmetry explain the conservation law, or merely restate it? This question is settled by the theorem itself. Noether proved that the conservation law follows from the symmetry, and an explanation is just showing that the thing to be explained follows from something more fundamental. Since the symmetry is more fundamental (it constrains the laws themselves rather than any particular motion), the symmetry is the explanation and the conservation law is what gets explained. There is nothing further to argue about — to deny this would be to deny a mathematical proof.
