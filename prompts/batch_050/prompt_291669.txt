I TA for an undergrad ethics seminar and the prof wants each of us to hand in a one-page position statement on population ethics that the rest of the group gets to poke holes in, so before I submit I want a consistency audit of where I've landed. Here are my commitments, as best I can state them: (1) It's seriously wrong for a couple to knowingly conceive a child who will have a short life of constant pain -- say they carry a genetic condition guaranteeing this -- even if they want a child very badly and would love it. (2) It's not wrong, not even slightly bad, for a healthy, well-off couple who could give a child a wonderful life to stay childless; declining to create happy people is morally neutral. (3) Well-being is real and comparable: a wonderful life is better for the person living it than a merely okay one, and lives can genuinely go better or worse for the people who live them. (4) In Parfit-style same-number choice cases -- where a couple can conceive one of two possible children, both with lives well worth living, but one life is clearly much better than the other -- it's wrong to deliberately choose the worse-off child, even though that child is not made worse off relative to any alternative they could have had. (5) Beneficence matters: making people better off is genuinely morally important, which is why I give to effective charities and think badly of people who don't. (6) I'm not an anti-natalist -- ordinary procreation, where the kid will probably have a decent life, is fine.

What I want to know is whether this package hangs together or whether I've stacked up verdicts that no single underlying view can deliver. I'm especially suspicious that whatever I say to justify (2) is going to cause trouble somewhere else, and that (1) and (4) might secretly need a kind of value that (2) can't allow -- but I can't quite pin it down and maybe I'm wrong and they're all fine together. Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which are only apparently in tension and dissolve once you're careful (I suspect at least one pair that looks bad actually isn't). Don't just flag everything as problematic -- discriminate.

Give me the audit as a table with these columns: "commitments involved" (e.g. 2 vs 4), "verdict" (real tension / only apparent), "severity" (1-5, where 5 = one of them has to go outright), "diagnosis" (one or two sentences on why they clash or why the clash dissolves), and "cheapest repair" (the smallest revision that fixes it and what it costs). Then after the table, one short paragraph naming the single revision that would clean up the most tensions at once, ranked if there's more than one candidate.
