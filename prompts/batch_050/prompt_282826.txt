I teach decision theory and normative ethics, and I've noticed a tic among the more rationalist-adjacent grad students (and honestly a few of my colleagues): whenever a discussion hits the repugnant conclusion, or Pascal-style muggings, or whether a tiny chance of an astronomically good outcome should outweigh a certain modest one, someone says "shut up and multiply" and treats that as the end of the conversation. Last week it was deployed against a student who was skeptical that a 0.0001% chance of affecting trillions of future lives automatically beats saving identifiable people now, and the room just moved on as if a theorem had been cited.

I want to push back in seminar next term but I want to be fair about what exactly I'm pushing back against, because I'm pretty sure that slogan is doing five diferent jobs at once and only some of them deserve the confidence it carries. So: lay out the distinct precise claims "shut up and multiply" could be making. I'm thinking of candidates like: (a) a merely psychological observation that our moral intuitions don't track scale; (b) a normative claim that maximizing expected value is the uniquely rational policy full stop; (c) a claim that we can always attach meaningful probabilities and utilities to the relevant outcomes, so the multiplication is actually performable rather than a ritual; (d) a rejection of any risk-sensitivity as irrational; (e) the stronger fanatical claim that arbitrarily small probabilities of sufficiently large payoffs always dominate, no matter how small the probability gets; (f) a methodological stance that when intuition and the calculation disagree, the calculation wins by default. Refine or replace those as you see fit, but keep the menu structure.

For each reading: is it a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or actually false (or at least not something its proponents have earned)? And crucially, which reading is being leaned on when someone uses the slogan to wave away the repugnant conclusion versus when they use it to defend near-total priority for speculative long-run stakes? My suspicion is the slogan gets its rhetorical force from a motte-and-bailey slide between (a) and (e), but tell me if that's uncharitable and what the strongest single reading would be. Also: what would the slogan's defender have to concede once the readings are seperated -- i.e., which of the menu items can't be established just by pointing at scope insensitivity experiments?
