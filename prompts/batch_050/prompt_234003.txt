So I got into a dumb argument with my brother over the weekend about the Bronston case -- the 1973 Supreme Court one where a guy under oath was asked if he'd ever had a Swiss bank account and answered that his company had had one in Zurich for about six months, which was true but completely dodged the question, since he'd had a personal account too. The Court said that's not perjury: literally true but misleading answers are the examiner's problem, not a crime. My brother's view was basically that the witness lied, full stop, and the Court got played. What struck me afterward is that the legal system seems weirdly split on this. In perjury doctrine, only the literal falsehood counts -- you can be as misleading as you want with technically true statements and you're untouchable. But walk over to advertising law and it's the opposite: a literally true ad can still be illegal deception if it misleads consumers (though then regulators have to prove actual people were misled, not just point at the sentence). And the oath itself -- swearing to tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth -- sure seems aimed at exactly the kind of strategic half-answering that perjury law then declines to punish.

So here's what I want to know: what does perjury law, taken on its own, presuppose about the moral and conceptual difference between lying and misleading? Does it only make sense if lying -- asserting something you believe is false -- is a distinct and specially grave wrong, maybe because the liar commits themself to the falsehood in a way the misleader doesn't, keeping plausible deniability or "letting the listener draw the inference"? Or is the whole thing better explained without any deep moral distinction at all -- say, as a pragmatic line-drawing move, since what's literally said is publicly settleable while what's merely implicated is fuzzy and would make trials unmanageable? And then, across the legal system as a whole: do perjury doctrine and advertising law presuppose *incompatible* theories of deception, or is there one coherent picture under which courts should police only what's said while regulators can police what's conveyed? If the implicit theory really is that misleading is less wrong than lying, I'd like that reconstructed properly -- what would have to be true about assertion, intention, and hearer uptake for it to hold -- and whether the same legal system is actually commited to it or just happens to fit it. If instead the law punishes the liar harder for purely institutional reasons, tell me what would have to change in courtroom practice if we dropped the moral distinction entirely and treated a deliberately misleading true answer as exactly as bad as a false one.
