At a meetup of a reading group I belong to (we mostly work through rationalist-blog canon and adjacent history), two members got into a disagreement that ran long past the scheduled discussion, and neither would concede. I stayed out of it and would like it adjudicated properly. The question on the table was a counterfactual: would there have been a Nazi Germany — or something close enough to deserve the name — without Hitler?

(1) Dan opened with the structuralist case. Take the reference class of interwar Eastern and Central European democracies: nearly all of them collapsed into authoritarianism of one flavor or another — Poland, Hungary, Romania, the Baltic states. Weimar Germany had the Depression, the Versailles grievance, mass anti-Semitism, a destroyed political center, and no democratic tradition to speak of. On base rates alone, some form of right-wing authoritarian takeover looks like the basin of attraction, and Hitler is the name we happen to have for whoever fell into it. Great Man stories, he said, are what you get when you do inside-view history instead of reference-class forecasting.

(2) Ruth replied that his reference class refutes his conclusion. Piłsudski's Poland and Horthy's Hungary became authoritarian without industrializing genocide or launching a war of annihilation in the East. So she granted him the weak thesis — some authoritarian Germany was likely — and denied the strong one: the specific character of the regime, the exterminatory program, and the timing and recklessness of the war depended on one man's particular obsessions and his personal authority to override institutional brakes. Great Man theory, she said, doesn't claim individuals always matter; it claims they matter at high-leverage junctures, and 1933–41 was one.

(3) Dan's counter had two parts. First, she was committing a selection error: the alternate leaders in her comparison class never held power in Germany in 1939, so "they wouldn't have done it" is untested, and German conditions — wartime radicalization, bureaucratic competition between agencies outbidding each other in ruthlessness — would have pushed whoever ruled in the same direction. Second, Hitler himself nearly failed repeatedly (the failed putsch, the Nazi vote declining in late 1932, the backroom maneuvering that could easily have gone differently), and the forces pressing toward dictatorship survived every near-miss; meanwhile his obsessions were hardly unique to him — Himmler, Heydrich, and Goebbels shared them — so deleting Hitler at birth may just promote the next fanatic in the queue.

(4) Ruth answered that "the next fanatic in the queue" is an unfalsifiable promissory note unless he can specify the mechanism that surfaces and empowers a comparably fanatical successor, since the plausible 1932 alternatives — a Strasser-led party or a conservative military junta — point away from exterminationism, not toward it. She conceded she couldn't prove Hitler's necessity from her reference class, but said he couldn't prove structural sufficiency either, and the burden belongs to whoever asserts the more surprising claim — which, given how reliably history rhymes, is the claim that one man redirected it.

Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the four, say whether the argument succeeds, fails, or partly succeeds, and why. Then say what both of them are missing — I suspect they're treating one question as at least three separate counterfactuals (authoritarian takeover, aggressive war, genocide) with different answers, and conflating "necessary" with "sufficient" with "probability-raising" throughout — and tell me whether that's right. Finally: who is more correct overall, and what evidence, if any, could actually settle a counterfactual like this rather than just make each side feel vindicated?
