I'm a backend engineer who somehow got voluntold onto my company's product policy committee, and we keep hitting the same shape of decision over and over: policy A imposes a small cost on a huge number of users, policy B imposes a big cost on a small number, and we have to pick one. Think: an aggressive spam filter that occasionally kills a legit small business's whole account vs. a laxer one that makes everyone see a bit more spam every day. Standard cost-benefit math just multiplies and picks, which feels wrong in some cases and right in others, and I can't articulate when.

I read a couple of summaries of Scanlon's contractualism — the rough idea that an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by principles nobody could reasonably reject — and it seems like the right frame for this, but summaries never tell you how to actually *use* it. Especially the part about whether you're allowed to stack up millions of tiny complaints against one person's big one.

So: write me a decision procedure the committee can actually run in a 90-minute meeting, three engineers and a lawyer in the room, no philosophy background, decisions that affect real users and sometimes get escalated to legal. It should say what questions to ask in what order, how to state each affected group's strongest complaint in the right form (I gather it matters that you reason from standpoints rather than tallying up named individuals?), what makes a complaint count as "reasonable" vs. just intense, where adding up small harms is or isn't allowed and what the procedure does when it isn't, and where it has to stop and admit this is a judgment call. Then run it on two real-ish cases: the spam filter tradeoff above, and a proposal to make the site slightly faster for everyone by dropping support for old hardware, which would definately lock out some users entirely. Be upfront about which steps are dressed-up intuition. Answer in American English please.
