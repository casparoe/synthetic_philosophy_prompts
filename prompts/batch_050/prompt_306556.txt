My national transportation licensing commission votes next month on whether to certify the first commercial teletransporter, and I have been asked to draft the written opinion we will circulate beforehand. The device scans the traveler at the departure booth, destroying the body cell by cell as it does so, transmits the complete structural information, and assembles at the destination a person physically and psychologically continuous with the traveler in every testable respect. Six thousand trial trips have been completed without incident: each traveler steps out remembering stepping in and resumes work, marriage, and debts without disruption. Our counsel holds that certification first requires a ruling on a prior question — whether the person who steps out is the same legal person as the one who stepped in, for purposes of property, contracts, marriage, and criminal liability. One event hangs over the vote: in a late trial the destruction stage failed silently, and the original was found alive in the departure booth eleven hours after the arrival-booth copy had gone home to the traveler's family; the estate, the marriage, and custody of the children now need rulings for both claimants. The opinion must recommend certify, refuse, or certify with conditions; take a definite position on the personhood question and on the status of the two claimants produced by the malfunction; give the strongest argument on the rejected side its due; and confront the awkward fact that everything we can observe is exactly what we would observe whether the machine transports people or destroys and replaces them — so the ruling cannot pretend to rest on empirical evidence about who, if anyone, survives the trip.
