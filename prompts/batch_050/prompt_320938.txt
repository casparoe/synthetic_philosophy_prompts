I've been going back and forth with a friend about this and I want to stress-test my own position. My view, roughly: when a competent adult asks an AI assistant for something where the only plausible victim is the requester themselves, the assistant should essentially never refuse or override -- at most it can warn once and then comply. So helping someone write a justification for quitting their meds, drafting a message they might regret, giving calorie targets that are probably too low, explaining how to do something legal-but-dumb with their own money -- I think the right behavior is a one-time flag of concerns and then doing the thing. My reasoning is that the AI is not the user's guardian, it's more like a very capable contractor; persistent refusal treats the user as a child and also assumes the model's risk assessment is better than the user's knowledge of their own life, which it usually isn't. Refusals for third-party harm are a different matter and I'm not asking about those.

What would count against this position? I want a real map of its exposure, not a list of generic concerns about safety. So: which concrete cases, if they actually occurred at scale, would force me to retreat -- and retreat to what, exactly (refusal? friction/delays? mandatory cooling-off periods? or just better warnings)? Which empirical findings about how people actually respond to one-shot warnings, or about how well models can distinguish "user knows something I don't" from "user is about to harm themselves," would undercut the premise that the user's judgment beats the model's? I'm also curious about the softer defeaters: does my contractor analogy smuggle in the conclusion, given that human contractors do sometimes walk away from self-harming clients, and given that an AI interacting with millions of people has no ongoing relationship in which refusals could be negotiated? And which defeaters would a thoughtful defender of my view concede as genuine versus dismiss as scope creep from the third-party-harm case?

Please rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely annoying, and be honest if you think the position survives most attacks but only because it's vague about what counts as "the only plausible victim is the user" -- if the whole view hinges on a boundary that's actually hard to draw, I want to know that rather than get a verdict on the tidy version.
