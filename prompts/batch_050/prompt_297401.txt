In my upper-division epistemology seminar — sixteen students, one seventy-five-minute meeting — we are approaching the unit on whether practical stakes bear on knowledge. The students will have read nothing on the topic, and I want no theoretical vocabulary on the board until the final half hour; they will meet the literature's bank-style cases next week, and I want the phenomenon to have happened to them first.

Design a two-phase in-class activity to do this.

In the first phase, individual students should occupy the subject position. Each holds a belief on good but checkable evidence, and the rules — not any announcement from me — escalate what rides on acting on that belief without checking, round by round, from nothing to a real but trivial stake (points toward some token prize). The design should let me record, for each student, the round at which they first stop to verify, and how their self-ascriptions shift as they do so — from "I know it" to "I'm fairly sure" to silence. The escalation must be load-bearing: remove it, and the phase should collapse.

In the second phase, hold the subject's evidence and situation fixed and vary instead the position of those assessing the belief: different observers, for whom different things ride on the subject's being right, must each say whether the subject knows. The two manipulations should be capable of coming apart, because the two views we are about to read diverge precisely here. On one, it is the subject's practical situation that determines whether a true, well-grounded belief amounts to knowledge. On the other, it is the attributor's context that fixes how much "knows" demands, so the subject's situation matters only insofar as the attributor occupies or mentions it.

Deliverables: complete rules and materials for both phases; the payoff scheme; facilitator instructions that keep me from telegraphing the point; a debrief that maps particular moments in the room onto purist invariantism, attributor contextualism, and interest-relative invariantism, and that uses the group's own tallies from the two phases to ask which view their judgments actually conformed to; an honest forecast of how this goes wrong on a first run — students gaming the escalation, "I know" collapsing into mere confidence-talk, observers quietly importing the subject's stakes into their own verdicts — with a remedy for each; and a frank statement of what the activity cannot reproduce, in particular whether a rising urge to double-check is evidence about knowledge at all, as opposed to justified confidence or conversational propriety, and how much of that gap the debrief must close by argument rather than by experience. Close the debrief by identifying the single control condition that would move the exercise from a demonstration toward an experiment. No reading beforehand; everything, debrief included, inside the seventy-five minutes.
