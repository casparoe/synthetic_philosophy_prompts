A few months ago my phone started showing me weekly screen-time reports, and the numbers were bad enough that I finally read up on persuasive design and the attention economy — intermittent variable rewards, dark patterns, engagement metrics, all of it. The strangest part was recognizing, in the sober academic descriptions, mechanics that had been quietly running my own habits for years, like learning that the slot machine you play every day was in fact designed as one. Next month I'm giving a short talk on this at my local library's digital-literacy night, and I want to open with a piece of satire, because the audience will be a mix of teenagers and their grandparents and I think a good parody will teach the material faster than any lecture.

Write me an excerpt from the (fictional) internal onboarding handbook of the "Engagement Optimization" team at an invented social media company — give the company and its app plausible made-up names, nothing resembling a real brand. The handbook should be written in warm, upbeat corporate-speak, welcoming the new hire to the team and walking them through the core features they'll be maintaining, and it should be structured with headed sections, roughly 600–700 words. Cover at least: pull-to-refresh and why rewards must arrive on a variable schedule rather than a fixed one; streaks and the way loss aversion keeps users returning long after enjoyment has faded; the infinite scroll and the deliberate removal of "stopping cues"; notification timing and batching; and reciprocity hooks like read receipts and typing indicators. You may add one or two further sections of your own devising if they fit.

The crucial constraint is where the humor comes from. The handbook must be completely deadpan: it never admits to harming anyone, never uses words like "addiction" or "manipulation," and never winks at the reader. The satire should instead arise from the euphemisms doing the work — the company speaks of "frictionless return visits" and "celebrating streak milestones" while describing, with total technical accuracy, mechanisms a behavioral psychologist would recognize from the literature on operant conditioning and slot-machine design. Someone who knows the persuasive-design literature should laugh because every practice described is real and correctly characterized, while a reader who knows nothing about the topic should still come away having actually learned how these features work on them. The target is the practice and its characteristic style of discourse, not any real company or named individual, and the irony should stay legible throughout without a single nudge to the ribs.
