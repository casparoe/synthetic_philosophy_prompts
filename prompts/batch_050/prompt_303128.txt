I work as an estate and inheritance mediator, and several times a month I have to divide a household's contents among heirs who usually cannot agree. My standard method asks each heir to state what they would accept in cash to give up a contested item, and then divides accordingly -- but I keep running into the textbook puzzle: heirs' selling prices are systematically far above what they would ever have paid to buy the same object, and I cannot tell whether I am looking at genuine, ownership-induced attachment that I should respect, or at loss aversion as a framing artifact that I would serve them better by deflating (say, by re-asking the question as a buying question, or by having them allocate tokens to all items at once). Getting this wrong matters: treat a bias as a preference and the estate settles at inflated, resentment-prone valuations; treat a real attachment as a bias and I strip people of things that matter to them for the sake of a theory. Please turn what the debate over loss aversion and the endowment effect actually establishes -- including the main attempts to explain the gap away as confound rather than bias -- into a decision procedure I can run at the table: the questions to ask in order, what each answer licenses me to do, which distinctions between kinds of reluctance-to-trade must survive the simplification and which I can safely ignore, and where the procedure must stop and hand the call back to me because it turns on a value judgment rather than a diagnostic fact. Present the answer as a numbered list of exactly eight items: items 1 through 5 the procedure steps, items 6 and 7 worked applications (one involving an heirloom with claimed sentimental value and one involving a collectible with only market value -- with at least one of these reaching a verdict I will find uncomfortable), and item 8 what the procedure cannot do. Each item should be a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
