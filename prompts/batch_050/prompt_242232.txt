I'm prepping for a philosophy of perception seminar and we're stuck on Kendall Walton's transparency thesis about photography -- the idea that when you look at a photograph of your grandmother you literally see her, indirectly but genuinely, the way you see things through a telescope, whereas a painting of her only lets you see a depiction. The pushback we got in class was roughly that seeing something requires getting some information about where it is in relation to you right now, and a photograph (especially an old one of a dead person or a demolished building) can't deliver that, so at best we're seeing an image that carries information *about* her. Half the room thinks that's decisive, the other half thinks it's an ad hoc fix to avoid the weird conclusion that we see dead people.

What I want is a thought experiment built to order that isolates exactly which feature is doing the work in this dispute. Constraints: (a) the case has to involve two viewing setups that are matched for counterfactual dependence -- that is, in both setups, what the viewer's visual experience is like would have been different if the scene in front of the recording device had been different, and this dependence is belief-independent in Walton's sense (the experience tracks the scene regardless of what anyone thinks or intends); (b) the setups must differ only in whether the viewer can extract egocentric spatial information -- roughly, information locating the seen thing relative to themselves, even vaguely; (c) no sci-fi, no perfect prediction machines, just cameras, screens, mirrors, telescopes, live feeds, that kind of thing; (d) realistic enough that it could plausibly come up in an actual museum, hospital, courtroom, or living room, and describable in one paragraph.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping for me: state what the transparency thesis says about each of the two setups (see the thing itself vs. merely see a representation of it) and why, then what the egocentric-information view says about each and why, and show that the two theories give the same verdicts in one setup and come apart in the other. Then identify the single variable that flips the verdicts, say whether removing that variable collapses the case back into a boring example both sides already agree on, and finish with how a Walton defender would try to block the case -- e.g. by denying that egocentric information is really required for seeing, or by arguing my two setups aren't actually matched on counterfactual dependence after all. I want to use this in my presentation to force people to stop talking past eachother, so the case only counts if the verdicts genuinely fall out of the theories rather than being stipulated into the scenario.
