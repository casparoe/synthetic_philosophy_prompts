I've been persuaded, for some years now, of a view in the economics-and-politics of development that I'd like stress-tested. The view, roughly: the large differences in prosperity between nations today are explained primarily by differences in their economic and political institutions — secure property rights, constraints on the executive, rule of law applied broadly rather than to a connected few, the ability of ordinary people to invest and to keep the returns — rather than by geography (climate, disease environment, natural resources, access to coasts) or by culture (work ethic, trust, religious heritage). On this view, geography and culture matter mostly by shaping which institutions got built, and where they matter directly the effects are modest in comparison. The evidence I find most compelling is of the usual sort: border cases where a homogeneous population with a shared geography and culture is split by an institutional line (the two Koreas being the dramatic example, but also the historical division of Germany, and various city- and region-level discontinuities); the so-called reversal of fortune, whereby the regions richest in 1500 within the colonised world tended to become relatively poor, which sits awkwardly with pure geography stories; and the statistical work instrumenting present institutions with historical settler mortality, which I know is contested.

What I want from you is a serious account of what would count against this view — not a list of stock objections I can bat away, but a map of its genuine exposure. Concretely:

1. Which observations or findings, if they held up, would be decisive against the institutions-first view, and which would merely be awkward? I'd like these sorted from fatal to irritating. Candidate categories to consider (but don't limit yourself to them): cases of sustained high growth under apparently extractive institutions; cases where institutional reform preceded nothing much; evidence that the timing is wrong (growth episodes that begin before, or long after, the relevant institutional changes); evidence that the cross-border discontinuities are smaller or less clean than advertised; and results suggesting the instruments are invalid or the historical record mismeasured.

2. The endogeneity problem: institutions and prosperity plainly co-evolve. What would it even take, in principle, to establish the causal direction from history, given that we cannot rerun it? Is there a class of evidence that could in principle settle this, or is the view permanently dependent on judgement calls about a handful of natural experiments? If the latter, how much should that lower my confidence, and in what does the view's confidence actually consist?

3. What do the serious defenders of this position themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to what its critics say? I'm more interested in internal concessions than in external polemics.

4. Finally: has the view been qualified into near-unfalsifiability? "Institutions" is an elastic term, and I've noticed a pattern where any success story gets redescribed as institutional (there was always some reform, somewhere) and any failure as institutional too. If the view has become one where no possible development trajectory would disconfirm it, say so plainly — and tell me whether you think that's a fatal flaw for a historical-causal claim of this kind, or an acceptable feature of theories that operate at this scale of abstraction.

I'd rather have an honest verdict that the question is underdetermined than a tidy resolution. Where the institutions, geography, and culture hypotheses genuinely make different predictions, please identify the divergences; where they don't, say that too.
