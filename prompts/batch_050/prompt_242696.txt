I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the philosophical writings of the early quantum physicists, and we have just finished Schrödinger's What is Life? and Mind and Matter, together with the Vedanta-influenced essays in which he develops his views on selfhood. For my final paper I want to examine how his monism about consciousness would have withstood empirical and theoretical developments that arose only after his death in 1961. Before I commit to a thesis, I would like a rigorous rehearsal of the counterfactual itself.

My understanding of his position, which you should correct if I have it wrong: Schrödinger held that consciousness is fundamentally one — that the plurality of minds is an appearance generated by the spatiotemporal individuation of bodies — and he endorsed, in a guarded way, the Upanishadic identification of the individual self with a universal ground. Importantly, he presented this not as mysticism hostile to science but as compatible with it, and he treated the felt unity of each person's experience as the basic datum any theory must respect. His own acknowledged difficulty was what he called an arithmetical puzzle: how the one appears as the many.

With that in place, please reconstruct, as carefully as the evidence permits, what Schrödinger would most plausibly have made of each of the following three developments:

1. Human split-brain research. Beginning in the 1960s, patients whose corpus callosum had been severed appeared in certain experimental conditions to host two dissociated streams of experience within a single skull. The asymmetry here interests me: Schrödinger's puzzle ran from one mind to many bodies, but split-brain findings seem to run from one body to two minds. Would he have regarded this as a refutation of his monism, or — perversely — as indirect support for it, since it suggests that the unity of consciousness does not simply track the physical unity of the brain? Or is there a third response available to him, for instance treating the divided streams as not yet amounting to two full subjects?

2. Integrated information theory. IIT identifies consciousness with integrated information, treats it as graded rather than all-or-nothing, implies that structures with vast numbers of neurons but the wrong internal organization (such as the cerebellum) contribute little to experience, and predicts that a split brain literally contains two subjects. Would Schrödinger have welcomed IIT as a mathematically disciplined cousin of his monism, or rejected it precisely for making consciousness a measurable, divisible, locally instantiated quantity — arguably the opposite of what he meant? Consider also whether its willingness to attribute degrees of experience widely across nature would have struck him as a vindication or a vulgarization of the Vedantic picture.

3. Contemporary debates about machine consciousness. If the plurality of minds is an appearance tied to the individuation of biological bodies, does the standard question "could a machine be conscious?" get reframed or dissolved from his vantage point? Would he have expected artifacts to participate in the one consciousness, or would he have denied that the question makes sense absent the kind of embodied unity he took experience to presuppose?

For each of the three, structure your answer as follows: first give the reconstruction you judge most plausible; then grade your material into three tiers — (i) claims anchored in things Schrödinger actually wrote, (ii) reasoned extrapolations from his general commitments, and (iii) speculation — and say explicitly which tier does most of the work. State how confident you are in each reconstruction and why. Finally, end by singling out the single prediction across all three topics in which you are least confident, and explain what would settle it. Please avoid biographical padding; what I need is the argumentative structure, since my paper will be graded on whether the counterfactual is disciplined rather than on how much Schrödinger trivia it contains.
