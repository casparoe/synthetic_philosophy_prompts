So I've been reading up on decision theory for a seminar and I've gotten pretty attached to the view that standard expected utility theory can't capture rational risk aversion, and that something like risk-weighted expected utility -- the Buchak-style approach where your attitude toward risk is a separate parameter from the shape of your utility function over outcomes -- is the right fix. Roughly: the EU theorist has to explain the person who refuses the Allais gambles by saying they just have a very curved utility function over money, and that feels like it's explaining away the phenomenon rather than capturing it, because the same person with the same wealth attitudes will still rank the gambles the "risk averse" way. The risk-weighting theorist says two people can value outcomes identically and still rationally differ because one cares more about how the worst-case scenario shapes the gamble. I find this really persuasive and I basically want to know what I'd have to give up if I'm wrong.

So: what would actually count against the risk-weighted approach? I don't want generic "all theories have problems" hedging -- I want a map of where it's exposed. Like, I know there's a worry that REU agents get into trouble with dynamic choice (that they have to be resolute rather than sophisticated in sequential decisions, or they violate something like dynamic consistency), and I know some people argue the risk function is doing work that should either be reduced to utility over richer outcomes or rejected as irrational. But I can't tell which of these are near-fatal and which are just awkward. So tell me: what results, arguments, or cases would be genuinely decisive against the view, what would merely be uncomfortable, and what would its defenders themselves (Buchak, or the people who've worked on axiomatizing it) concede as real costs versus what only critics press? Also: is there any evidence that could bear on this at all -- like, could actual data about how people handle risk distinguish "curved utility function" from "global risk attitude," or are the two views empirically indistinguishable and it's pure theory choice? If it turns out they're observationally equivalent in every possible experiment, does that count for or against the REU picture, or is it neutral? And finally, be honest about whether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable -- it lets basically any risk attitude be rational, so does it risk explaining nothing by permitting everything, and if so is that actually a problem for a normative (as opposed to descriptive) theory?
