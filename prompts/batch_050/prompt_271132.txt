I run a reading group for second-year philosophy undergraduates, and in a fortnight I have one ninety-minute session to teach fanaticism about expected value: roughly, the thesis that for any prospect, however improbable, there is some sufficiently valuable outcome such that a chance of that outcome is to be preferred to some modest certain gain -- so that no probability is so small that stakes cannot in principle compensate for it. Please plan the session rather than deliver it. Specifically: (1) work out the dependency order -- which claims about expected value and about ranking prospects have to be in place before the thesis can even be stated clearly, and which familiar cases (St. Petersburg-type payoffs, Pascal's mugging) belong early as motivation and which belong late as complications; (2) select one carrying example for the session's center -- I am torn between a mugging-style case (a stranger offers a one-in-a-septillion chance of a septillion happy lives for your wallet) and a philanthropic case (a certain small donation versus a lottery ticket funding a speculative existential-risk project) -- and explain which isolates the thesis cleanly and what each contaminates it with (credibility of tiny probabilities, intuitions about charity, risk aversion); (3) map the misconceptions I should expect -- my guesses: that fanaticism is a command to actually chase long shots rather than a constraint on how prospects are ordered; that the mugging refutes the thesis rather than illustrating its cost; that rejecting fanaticism commits you to ignoring small probabilities entirely; that the thesis stands or falls with total utilitarianism -- and mark the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and (4) give me one closing question or case variant that would reveal whether the group has grasped the thesis itself rather than merely its counterintuitive reputation, plus a note on what to leave out at this level and say is being left out.
