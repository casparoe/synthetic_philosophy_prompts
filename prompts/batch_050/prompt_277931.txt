So I'm prepping for my comps in phil of science and I want to nail down my "official positions" on reduction and emergence before my committee gets to poke at them. My whole view is sort of anchored in a bit of Mill's System of Logic (Book III, the chapter on the composition of causes). Paraphrasing, since I don't have the book in front of me: Mill says that when causes act together the way mechanical forces do, the joint effect can be worked out from the laws of the separate causes, but in cases like chemical combination the laws of what results can't be derived from the laws of the ingredients taken separately — he coins "heteropathic" for laws like that. I read him as making a metaphysical claim there, not just a point about what 19th century chemists happened to be unable to compute: those laws are underivable in principle.

Here are my commitments:

(a) Everything is physical through and through; the chemical, biological, and psychological facts all supervene on the microphysical facts with metaphysical necessity. Two worlds identical in all thier microphysical detail are identical, period.

(b) Mill is right, on my metaphysical reading: there are heteropathic laws — laws of chemistry (and probably biology) that could not be derived from the laws of the constituents even by an ideal reasoner with unlimited compute.

(c) The physical is causally closed: whenever a physical event has a cause at all, it has a fully sufficient physical cause.

(d) Downward causation is real and not just a façon de parler. When a cell metabolizes or a person decides something, the higher-level state makes a causal difference in virtue of being that higher-level state — it's not merely that its microphysical realizer does all the work and the higher-level description is a useful bookkeeping device.

(e) The special sciences are autonomous because of multiple realizability: kinds like "catalyst" or "gene" or "monetary exchange" can't be identified with any single physical kind, which is why special-science generalizations resist reduction — but this doesn't make them second-rate or merely heuristic.

(f) The reduction of thermodynamics to statistical mechanics is the gold standard for what successful intertheoretic reduction looks like, and where reductions like that succeed, they give us real explanatory unification, not just pragmatic convenience.

Two questions. First: is my metaphysical reading of Mill defensible, or is the composition-of-causes chapter better read as an epistemic point about derivational intractability? If the epistemic reading is right, I'd rather know, since (b) leans on the stronger reading. Second and mainly: does the package (a)-(f) hang together? Audit it — tell me which commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only look like they conflict but actually dissolve on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than flagging everything as equally bad. For each real tension, say what the cheapest revision is and what it costs. I definately don't want a lecture on emergence in general; I want to know where my specific set breaks and what the minimal repair is.
