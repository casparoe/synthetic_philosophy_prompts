I am assembling the case against treating named generations — Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z — as real social kinds: that is, as groupings that carve the population at genuine causal joints rather than at the convenience of journalists and market researchers. The objections I already have, and do not need repeated, are these:

1. The boundary objection. The cut-off years are arbitrary. Someone born in December of a boundary year resembles someone born the following January far more than either resembles people at the middle of "their" generation, and no non-question-begging criterion fixes where one cohort ends and the next begins.

2. The variance objection. On nearly every measured trait — political values, work attitudes, religiosity, technology habits — within-cohort heterogeneity dwarfs the between-cohort differences, and class, region, and country do far more predictive work than the generational label ever does.

3. The age-period-cohort identification problem. Age, period, and cohort are linearly dependent (year of birth equals year of observation minus age), so no dataset can cleanly separate "this cohort is distinctive" from "the present moment is distinctive" or "people of that age are always like that" without imposing identifying constraints that cannot themselves be tested.

4. The lifecycle objection. Many celebrated generational differences are age effects that recur in every generation: each successive cohort of young adults is more liberal, less trusting, and more risk-tolerant than its elders. The apparent kind dissolves once the same cohort is followed longitudinally.

5. The performativity objection. Generational labels are coined by commentators and advertisers and then shape how people describe themselves — an instance of what philosophers of science call the looping effect of human kinds. On this view, whatever clustering the labels track is an artifact of the classification rather than evidence for a kind that existed prior to it.

I want one further objection, genuinely distinct from these five: not a rewording, not a special case, and not a conjunction of items already on the list. It may come from social ontology, the philosophy of classification, or anywhere else, and it need not be an objection anyone has actually published — novelty is acceptable, provided the argument is sound. State it precisely, show that it does not collapse into any of the five, and then tell me honestly whether it is any good: in particular, whether it does more damage to generational realism than objection 3, which currently strikes me as the strongest item here.

One caveat. If you think my framing is confused — for instance, if "are generations real kinds?" is not one question but several, so that a cohort could be a real kind for the demographer's purposes and a fiction for the sociologist's, with my list equivocating between the two — then say so plainly rather than politely supplying a sixth objection. I would rather have the premise of the exercise challenged than a new item bolted onto a broken list.
