I'm putting together a handout for my seminar on backward induction (we're doing the usual Rosenthal-style centipede and the debate about whether the paradox lives in the payoff structure or in the rationality/common-knowledge assumptions) and I want a puzzle built to order. Either construct the following or show it can't be done.

The target: a two-player, finite-horizon, perfect-information game in the centipede family with all of these features at once:

- Players alternate. On your turn you either Take (game ends, payoffs collected) or Pass (move goes to the other player). If everyone keeps passing, the game ends anyway at a fixed final node.
- Payoffs are small whole-dollar amounts. At most six decision nodes. No ties anywhere a player actually has to choose.
- The unique subgame-perfect equilibrium has Player 1 Taking at the very first node.
- And here's the demand that I suspect breaks everything: that equilibrium outcome is strictly Pareto-dominated by *every other terminal outcome* of the game. Not just by the far-off cooperative ending -- by literally every terminal node, including the ones where Player 2 takes early and Player 1 gets stiffed.

Constraints: no stipulated irrationality, no trembles, no chance moves, no incomplete information, no infinite horizon. The payoffs have to do all the work, not the players' psychology.

If it's impossible (I have my suspicions but haven't sat down with teh pencil and paper), prove the impossibility directly from the assumptions above, stated precisely, short enough to fit on one slide. Then find the *minimal* relaxation that makes the construction go through: weaken exactly one assumption, as little as you can manage, and build the game. And be honest about whether your relaxation is cheating -- if you need ties that kill uniqueness, or risk-averse utilities over the dollar payoffs, or a sneaky vacuous-quantification trick, say plainly what that costs the paradox. (For what it's worth, I'd also like to know whether your impossibility proof survives adding a third player or letting players mix; my guess is yes, which would be the interesting part.)

Either way, finish with the interpretive upshot for my students: the textbook story (Aumann, Binmore, that whole literature -- no need to litigate citations) blames the epistemic assumptions, i.e. common knowledge of rationality. Does your impossibility result, or your relaxed construction, push the other way -- toward the payoff structure being whats really load-bearing?

Also flag explicitly which single feature of the setup the impossibility proof hinges on, and what happens to the proof if that feature is removed.

End with a machine-readable line: \answer{Possible} if you built the game under the original assumptions, \answer{Impossible} if you proved it can't be done; if you had to relax something, append the relaxed assumption in a few words, e.g. \answer{Impossible; relaxed via ...}.
