My roommate just finished a big history degree and has started ending arguments with "history is written by the victors." Latest example: I mentioned something I'd read about a colonial war -- a case where, awkwardly for him, the losing side's account basically became the official one -- and he just shrugged and said "well, history is written by the victors, so who knows what actually happened." Which seemed to prove too much, since the book I was citing was also history. But I can tell the phrase isn't obviously nonsense either, it's doing something.

So: what are the different precise claims that sentence could be making? I want them laid out as a menu, roughly from most boring to most radical. Things I suspect are hiding in there: a causal claim about who controls archives and school curricula after a war; a stronger claim that what gets written down is propaganda and so false; a skeptical claim that we just can't know the past at all; maybe a claim about which events get commemorated rather than which get recorded; possibly something about who's cast as the villain. For each reading: is it a platitude, genuinely contested, or just false? The false-ness question matters to me because of cases where the losers pretty clearly did dominate the later narrative, which seems like a problem for at least some readings.

Also: one thing my methods-course friends keep hammering is that "history" (what historians do, with sources and peer review and so on) isn't the same as a community's collective memory of an event, and the two often come apart. Which readings of the slogan are actually about memory rather than history, and does the slogan sneakily rely on conflating the two? And does any plausible reading justify the *skeptical* use -- "so who knows what happened" -- as opposed to the weaker "be suspicious of official narratives" use? Because I suspect the sentence only sounds like it licenses the shrug if you equivocate between two of the readings, and I'd like that spelled out so I can bring it up next time he does this.
