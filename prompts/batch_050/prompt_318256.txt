At a philosophy discussion group, someone dismissed the grandfather paradox with the sentence “you can’t change the past.” The case under discussion was ordinary by time-travel standards: a traveler enters a machine in 2080, arrives in 1960, and tries to prevent his grandfather from meeting his grandmother. The speaker nevertheless accepted that the traveler might genuinely go to 1960 and might even have been part of that year’s history all along. That combination left me unsure what work the slogan was doing.

Please distinguish the precise claims that “you can’t change the past” might express. At minimum, consider differences among:

- influencing a past event and replacing one past event with another;
- an event’s occurring and a later proposition about that event’s becoming true;
- changing what the traveler remembers or expects and changing what happened;
- making it causally impossible to perform an action and merely ensuring that the action will not succeed;
- a prohibition built into time travel, a general constraint on coherent histories, and a psychological claim about how agents experience events;
- revising a single timeline, adding another timeline or branch, and discovering that the old timeline always contained the intervention.

Present the possible readings as a numbered menu. For each reading, state the thesis as precisely as possible, explain whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, equivocal, or simply false, and indicate whether it would justify dismissing the grandfather paradox. Please also identify which reading is most likely needed if the slogan is to permit backward causation while ruling out “changing” 1960, and say whether that reading solves the paradox or merely redescribes its consistency condition.
