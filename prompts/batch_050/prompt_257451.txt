My roommate's grandfather died last year and she trained a little chatbot on his old texts and emails so her mom can "talk" to him. We got into a long argument about whether that's okay, and she hit me with a line of reasoning that I can't escape but whose implications I hate. Roughly: (1) An action is wrong only if it wrongs somebody -- sets back their interests, violates a claim they hold, or deceives them about something they have a stake in. Ethics has to bottom out in someone being wronged. (2) The dead can't be wronged: they have no experiences to set back, they hold no claims anymore, and they certainly can't be deceived. (3) The living users know exactly what the bot is and choose to engage with it, so they're not deceived or coerced either. (4) People like me who just find the whole thing creepy don't count, since being grossed out isn't a claim anyone has to respect. (5) So building and using a griefbot wrongs no one, and is permissible, full stop.

Here's where it blew up. I pointed out that nothing in (1)-(4) seems special to dead people. (6) By the same reasoning, if I secretly built a bot of my living ex from her old messages and kept it entirely private so she never found out and nobody who knows her ever saw it, that would also wrong no one and be permissible. She bit the bullet and said fine, it's permissible. I think that's obviously wrong -- secretly simulating a living person is a violation of them even if they never learn about it, and I'd say the same about, like, someone reading her diary she never discovers is missing. (7) So (1) has to be false: things can be wrong without anyone being wronged. But then I've lost my whole framework, and honestly (1) is the premise I most want to keep, along with (5) -- I do want her griefbot to be okay.

So: is there a way to keep (1), (5), and the wrongness of the secret-ex-bot case all at once, or is that combination unavailable and something has to go? If the move is that interests can be set back without being experienced (which is what makes the diary and ex-bot cases wrong), doesn't that same move reopen the possibility that the dead have surviving interests the griefbot could violate, sinking (5)? And if instead the dead really can't be wronged because their claims expire at death, what principled line stops the living person's claims from also expiring the moment the violation is kept secret from them? Tell me which step you'd reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it actually costs me.
