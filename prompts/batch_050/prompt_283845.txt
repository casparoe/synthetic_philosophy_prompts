I chair my university's internal grants panel and we're deadlocked over a renewal, so I need the decision memo drafted. Here's the case. A cognitive-science lab has had panel funding for eight years for their "trace-competition theory" of memory recall. Year two was their moment of glory: the theory predicted a very specific error pattern in recall tasks that nobody had gone looking for, they ran the experiment, and there it was. Since then, though, the record is five published results that went against the theory, and each time the lab has responded by tweaking the theory -- adding an auxiliary assumption, adjusting a parameter -- in a way that absorbs the awkward finding but, as the skeptics on the panel keep pointing out, doesn't seem to predict anything new that wasn't already in the data. None of the tweaked versions has since generated a confirmed novel prediction. Two complicating wrinkles: (1) the most recent modification, a decay-rate assumption, actually is independently testable, and the lab has a registered study on it running right now with results due early next year; (2) there's a rival lab whose programme has predicted and confirmed two brand-new effects in the last three years, but that rival programme currently can't explain the original error-pattern result, which remains our lab's trophy. The lab's director also argues that a well-known, decades-old anomaly in the recall literature counts in their favour, because their theory explains it even though it wasnt built to -- the way people say the perihelion of Mercury supported relativity. The skeptics say a fact everyone already knew can't count as a win, full stop.

Draft the panel's memo: recommend renewal, refusal, or renewal with conditions, using the framework of research programmes -- progressive vs. degenerating problemshifts, novel predictions, protective belt, the lot -- to structure the reasoning. If you go with conditions, make them checkable, i.e. say what would have to be true by next review for the programme to count as back on a progressive track. Give the strongest version of the losing side's case a fair shake too, since the memo has to survive scrutiny from the members who vote against it.

Lead with the recommendation in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question back at me.
