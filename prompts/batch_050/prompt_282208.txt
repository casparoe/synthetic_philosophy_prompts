I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background who got pulled into the free will literature a few months ago after a long argument with a coworker about whether criminal justice is essentially a bug-triage system for human behavior. Since then I've worked through some of Dennett's compatibilist writing and, more recently, Gregg Caruso's case against retributivism, and I've been drafting a short position piece for our reading group at work. Before I circulate it, I'd like a consistency audit of the commitments I find myself holding, because I suspect at least one of them is doing less work than I think, but I can't see where.

Here is what I currently believe. (a) Dennett is broadly right that the kind of control that matters for responsibility — being responsive to reasons, capable of self-monitoring and self-correction, governable by moral considerations — is fully compatible with determinism, so a deterministic agent can possess everything worth wanting under the heading of free will. (b) State punishment is justified only by its forward-looking effects: incapacitation, deterrence, rehabilitation, and restitution. The state is never justified in inflicting suffering on an offender merely on the grounds that the suffering is deserved. (c) Even so, there is a real difference between blaming someone and merely managing them: a remorseless, calculating murderer merits resentment and moral condemnation in a way that a malfunctioning machine does not, and a justice system that treated offenders purely as malfunctioning systems to be debugged or quarantined would be missing something morally essential rather than just being unfashionable. (d) Caruso is right that retributive just deserts is indefensible; I accept his skepticism about basic desert, including his point that the luck problem undermines desert regardless of how the free will question comes out. (e) Our everyday practices of praise, blame, and holding each other accountable should be preserved in something close to their current form, not replaced wholesale by a public-health-style model of behavior management, which strikes me as creepily paternalistic even where it's effective.

What I'd like from you: an audit of this set, not a lecture on free will. Tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only appear to. In particular, I keep going back and forth on whether (a) and (d) sit comfortably together or whether accepting Caruso's reasoning quietly undermines my reasons for bothering with Dennett's compatibilism at all; I have a similar unease about whether (c) is smuggling in through the back door exactly what (b) and (d) throw out the front. Please rank whatever tensions you find by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for the worst one, identify the least costly revision — which commitment to weaken or give up, and what that surrender actually costs me in terms of the overall picture I'm trying to defend.
