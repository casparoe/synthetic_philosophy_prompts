I've been jotting down my gut reactions to various fish and shrimp farming practices and I'm stuck on what principle, if any, unifies them. Verdicts:

- Snipping off a female shrimp's eyestalk to make her spawn more reliably: clearly not on, I'd ban it outright.
- Letting prawns suffocate slowly in air at harvest when an ice slurry is cheap and available: not on either, no excuse.
- Electrical stunning of trout before slaughter, even though the fish visibly hate being crowded into the stunner: fine by me.
- Briefly lifting salmon out of water to vaccinate them, under anaesthetic: fine.
- Very high stocking densities where the fish eat and grow well but show ragged fins and low-level aggression: honestly feels fine, though I can't defend it.
- Thousands of cleaner fish (the wrasse used to eat lice off salmon) dying as a routine byproduct: this one really bothers me, more than the vaccination or the crowded stunner, and I can't say why.
- Killing prawns by gradual chilling, which is probably about as unpleasant as air asphyxiation, just slower and quieter: feels fine, which I realise flatly contradicts my second verdict.

So what principle captures these? Is it something like 'avoidable suffering is worse than unavoidable suffering', or a capacity-based view, or am I just punishing practices that look violent and forgiving ones that don't? Tell me which of my verdicts a coherent principle would force me to drop, and what it says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: trucking live fish for hours to slaughter. British English please.
