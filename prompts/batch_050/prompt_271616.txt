Write a deadpan formal letter, roughly 500-600 words, from a claims adjuster at a personal-injury insurance firm, approving a policyholder's claim for "catastrophic cognitive injury" after his smartphone was destroyed in a household accident. The grounds: under the extended mind thesis, which the company's legal department now accepts as settled, the phone was a functioning part of the claimant's mind, and its destruction is therefore brain damage. The letter should reason carefully and in good faith through the standard marks of extended cognition -- constant reliance, direct and effortless availability, automatic endorsement of the information retrieved, fluency of access comparable to biological memory -- and should preempt the obvious objection that the phone merely caused thoughts rather than constituted them, in terms a defender of the view would recognize as fair. Having granted the phone, however, the adjuster must find himself unable to stop: the same criteria, applied with the same scrupulousness, pull the cloud backup, the filing cabinet, the spouse who remembers the children's birthdays, and finally the open internet into the claimant's cognitive system and hence under the policy. The satire should land the cognitive-bloat objection without ever naming it, and the adjuster's reasoning should be sound enough that an extended-mind proponent cannot simply dismiss it -- the comedy and the discomfort should come from the view working as advertised, not from the adjuster being careless or stupid. Close with an actuarial note recalculating the premium.
