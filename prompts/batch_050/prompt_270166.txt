I teach philosophy at a mid-sized German university, and I've been asked — somewhat to my horor — to give the main address (about 20 minutes) at our town's annual memorial ceremony for the Jewish residents deported during the war. The audience is a minefield: a handful of survivors' relatives who travel in from abroad, local high school students who did archival projects on the deportees, town officials who want something dignified and inoffensive, and a few of my colleagues, including a historian who will catch any sloppiness. So I need actual help, not a generic commemoration speech.

Here's the complication I keep circling. Two years ago I found out from an archive that my grandfather, a mid-level railway clerk, almost certainly processed paperwork connected to the transports. My mother still can't say his name without crying. So I can't stand up there as an innocent lecturing the guilty, but I also can't stand up there *as* one of the guilty — I was born in 1981, I have done nothing, and performing inherited guilt would be its own kind of vanity. The only frame I've found that respects both facts is Jaspers's fourfold distinction from his postwar lectures on German guilt: criminal guilt (individuals, adjudicated by courts), political guilt (which falls on citizens as members of a polity and cashes out as liability — reparations, living under consequences), moral guilt (which only ever exists before one's own conscience, so no one can charge another person with it), and metaphysical guilt (the guilt of surviving, of solidarity with the dead, of not having done the impossible). I want that taxonomy to do the structural work of the speech, not just decorate it: the through-line should be that some of these die with their bearers (criminal and moral guilt), one of them gets inherited by the polity whether anyone likes it or not (political liability — and this is why the town still owes things), and one of them is the only thing the sixteen-year-olds in the audience can actually take up — not as inherited sin, which is incoherent, but as a task they can choose.

Act as a speechwriter who's genuinely expert in postwar German memory culture and in Jaspers, not someone generating greeting-card solemnity. Brainstorm the arc first — where the emotional peaks go, where the taxonomy gets explained versus merely enacted — and then write the speech. Hard constraints: my grandfather has to appear in it somehow, because I'd be lying by omission to an audience that includes victims' families, but it must not turn into a confession that makes the evening about me; nothing that could be heard as me forgiving anyone or asking anyone's forgiveness, since I'm authorized to do neither; and no kitsch — if the phrase "never forget" appears, it has to be problematized, not just deployed. Also, keep the Jaspers accessible enough that the high schoolers follow it without me ever sounding like I'm giving a lecture in a church hall.
