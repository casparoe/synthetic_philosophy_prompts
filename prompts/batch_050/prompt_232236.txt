I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a long piece on what egalitarians actually owe people. My editors keep asking me to commit to a metric — welfare, resources, capabilities — but I want to work backward from my own case judgments instead of picking a label first. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

A wheelchair user receives state-funded mobility equipment beyond what others get: clearly justified, not a violation of equality. An opera lover who can only be made as happy as the rest of us by expensive seats gets a subsidy: clearly not justified. A devout person whose religion requires costly dietary and ritual observance, raising her cost of living well above average, asks for compensation: I find myself thinking she has a legitimate claim, though I can't explain why she differs from the opera lover. Someone given exactly the same resource bundle as everyone else becomes severely depressed through no one's fault and is miserable: I think equality still demands something further for him. A healthy adult who chose a low-paying, leisurely life over a lucrative career and now envies others' wealth: no claim. A person born with a cheerful disposition who needs nothing extra: fine, no subsidy, but I balk at taxing her good spirits away.

Is there a single metric of advantage — resourcist, welfarist, capability-based, or some hybrid — that delivers all six verdicts, or must one of them go? Please state the leading candidate precisely, identify which of my cases each clause is doing the work for, tell me honestly which verdict it cannot save, and say what it implies for a case I haven't listed: a skilled worker whose chronic pain makes each hour of work cost her far more effort than it costs her colleagues.
