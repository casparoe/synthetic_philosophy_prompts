Okay so here's the setup. Dennett had this term, free-floating rationales, for the idea that natural selection lands on designs that exist for real reasons — the cuckoo's egg mimicry, the peacock's tail, whatever — even though nothing anywhere in the process ever thought or represented those reasons. The rationale is genuinely explanatory, it just floats free of any reasoner. Competence without comprehension, as he put it.

I've been chewing on whether LLMs are the first really big case of this in the artifact world. Gradient descent is blind search like selection is, and it converges on stuff that works for identifiable reasons (circuits tracking syntax, heuristics for arithmetic, persona behaviors) without anyone — not the engineers, not the model itself — ever having represented those reasons. And the model can also talk fluently about its reasons, wich is a whole extra mess, since the confabulated story it gives you and the actual selection-history reasons can come apart completely.

So the actual question. I'm a philosophy PhD student with about a year and a half, comfortable reading ML papers but no lab and no GPUs, and I want to work on free-floating rationales in AI rather than just rehashing Dennett. Questions I've been kicking around: do the real reasons for model behavior live in the weights at all, or only in the selection history, and what does the answer do to interpretability as a project? Are the model's post-hoc chain-of-thought explanations themselves free-floating rationales, or a different kind of thing? Could there be rationales in these systems that are in principle unreconstructable by any reasoner, and would that matter? Do the evolution-of-deception cases in biology actually tell us anything about reward hacking, or is the analogy just vibes? But dont just grade my list: tell me which two or three open questions in this space (mine or better ones) you'd prioritize, and defend the ordering. I care about what's upstream of what, what's actually tractable for a philosopher without experiments, and what empirical results over the next couple years would move a question up or down the list. And tell me what you'd deprioritize too — not because its unanswerable, but because answering it wouldn't change anything downstream.
