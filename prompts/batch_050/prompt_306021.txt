I lead research at a small AI lab, and our design reviews keep stalling on disputes that seem to be about words: whether a model genuinely understands a prompt or only pattern-matches, whether a dataset is biased or merely unrepresentative, whether an agent pursuing a goal can be said to want anything. My reading of Peirce suggests his pragmatism was built for exactly this: the idea that a concept's entire content lies in the conceivable differences in experience or practice that its object could make, joined to his account of inquiry as beginning from genuine doubt rather than paper doubt and of every settled belief as permanently revisable. Turn this into a procedure I can actually run in a ninety-minute review when such a dispute surfaces: the ordered steps for making each side state what observable or operational difference their reading would entail, the criterion for declaring the dispute empty rather than deep, and what to do when the only difference anyone can name is how they would feel describing the result. Mark the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated, and say how the procedure should treat a dispute like whether our models are conscious, where neither side can currently name a detectable difference. Give the answer as a numbered list of exactly six items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: items one through four the procedure in order, item five its verdict on the consciousness dispute, item six the kinds of disagreement the procedure will wrongly dissolve.
