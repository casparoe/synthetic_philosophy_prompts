"The quest for the historical Jesus has traditionally relied on criteria of authenticity to sift the Gospel traditions. In my view these criteria can authenticate individual sayings and events with reasonable confidence, and in doing so they can settle the broad outlines of who Jesus was, even if they cannot deliver every detail.

Consider first the criterion of multiple attestation: a saying or event that appears in more than one independent source is more likely historical than one appearing in only one. Jesus' proclamation of the kingdom of God, for instance, is attested in Mark, Matthew, Luke, and John -- four independent witnesses -- which makes it about as secure as anything in the tradition.

Second, the criterion of embarrassment: the early church would not have invented material that caused it difficulty. Jesus' baptism by John -- which implies John was the greater figure and that Jesus submitted to a baptism associated with the forgiveness of sins -- and above all the crucifixion of a supposed Messiah meet this criterion, and they give us two firmly anchored points in Jesus' life.

Third, the criterion of dissimilarity isolates material explainable neither by Jesus' Jewish environment nor by the later church, and these double-dissimilar sayings therefore form the most reliable core of the tradition. Once this core is fixed, the criterion of coherence can then authenticate further sayings that fit with it, so the core radiates outward and a substantial portrait emerges.

Admittedly, the criteria have limits. They presuppose judgments about the direction of influence and about what would have embarrassed or pleased which community, and different scholars apply them with different results. Recent work on social memory also warns that all tradition is already interpreted. So the criteria cannot settle every contested question -- the eschatological versus the non-eschatological Jesus, for instance. But because multiple attestation and embarrassment converge on a Jewish preacher of the kingdom who was baptized by John and crucified under Pilate, they do settle the essential shape of his life, which is what the question of who Jesus was fundamentally means."
