I help run a small nonfiction reading group, and at our last meeting a disagreement broke out that turned out to be more philosophical than anyone expected. One member had passed along a claim about a mutual acquaintance's job situation -- something she sincerely believed on decent but secondhand evidence -- and another member insisted she simply shouldn't have said it, full stop, while a third argued that the only thing that matters is whether it was true, which it was. I realized mid-argument that we were each invoking a different rule about when it's permissible to flat-out say something, and I'd like to turn that confusion into something useful: a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on the norm of assertion based on what they actually think, not on jargon they've picked up.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of named views. Start with the diagnostic questions, each phrased as a concrete case someone can react to without knowing any labels -- for instance, a lottery case (you believe your ticket will lose on purely statistical grounds; may you assert that it will?), a case of a true belief held on a lucky hunch, a case of a justified belief that happens to be false, and a case where the stakes of being wrong are unusually high. Each branch of the tree should be labeled with the claim or case-verdict that splits it, and each leaf should carry the name of the position it lands on -- knowledge norm, truth norm, reasonable-belief or justification norm, simple belief norm, some context-sensitive or stakes-sensitive variant, and any others the space generates -- plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment that leaf is stuck with. Aim for seven to ten questions and no more than about ten leaves; the audience is intelligent adults with no philosophy background.

Two further requests. First, wherever the tree generates a combination of answers that no actual position occupies, mark that leaf as unoccupied and say briefly whether the combination looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Second, if two positions turn out to be genuinely hard to separate with the questions I've suggested (I suspect, for instance, that some justification-based norms only differ in how strong the warrant must be), flag that pair explicitly and propose the single sharpest question that could split them, even if the question is subtle. I'd rather have an honest tree with a flagged gap than one that pretends every named view is cleanly distinguishable.
