I'm putting together my notes on the aggregation problem for Scanlon-style contractualism and I think I've got the standard menu of moves down. Tell me what I'm missing. Here's what I have so far:

(1) Scanlon's own line from What We Owe to Each Other: only individual complaints count, compared pairwise. When the complaints are of equal strength -- one death vs. five deaths -- the numbers decide, because each extra person on the larger side is another complaint just as strong as the first, with nothing on the other side to answer it. But weaker complaints never pile up into a stronger one: in the transmitter case you have to save the worker being electrocuted even though millions of viewers will miss the rest of the football match, since no single viewer has a complaint remotely comparable to his.

(2) The Taurek-style denial that the numbers should count at all, even between equal claims: the extra four losses aren't losses to anyone who would also suffer the first, so five deaths isn't worse *for anyone* than one death is for the one. Might as well flip a coin.

(3) The ex ante fix: the contractualist shouldn't compare certain outcomes at all but should justify policies to each person in terms of their prospects before it is known who ends up harmed. Tiny risks imposed on many people then come out as equal expected burdens, which smuggles the numbers back in through probability rather than through summing actual harms.

Is there a fourth option? I'm not after a variant -- "aggregate only complaints that are similar enough" is just (1) loosened, and slapping a threshold on top of (3) is still (3). I want something structurally different in how it makes headcount morally visible to a pairwise test. And then tell me whether it's actually available to a contractualist, given that the whole point of the view is justifiability to each person taken one at a time. If you think the menu is closed and there is no fourth option, make that case instead -- but it has to be an argument, not just an assertion that these are thier only choices.
