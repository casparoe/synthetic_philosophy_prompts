I got into a long argument with my brother about drunk driving last month and it kind of spiraled, so I sat down and wrote out what I actually believe about putting other people at risk without asking them first. Here's my list:

1. How wrong it is to impose a risk on someone is settled at the moment you do it. The drunk driver who makes it home fine did exactly the same wrong as the one who hits a kid. What happens afterwards is just luck and shouldn't change the verdict.

2. But what people are *owed* afterwards does track what actually happened. If your risky behavior never hurt anyone, you owe nobody anything; if it did hurt someone, you owe them compensation proportional to the actual harm.

3. Normal everyday stuff like driving to work or running a power plant is perfectly fine even though it imposes real risks on people who never signed up for it, because the risks are small and the benefits are big. Basically an expected value calculation is the right tool for deciding which impositions are okay.

4. Pulling the trigger of a revolver with one bullet in ten thousand chambers pointed at a sleeping stranger is monstrous, and no amount of benefit to anyone could make it acceptable — even though the expected harm is tiny, probably less than a year of normal driving imposes on strangers.

5. If someone genuinely consents to a risk (boxing matches, surgery), then way higher probabilities are totally fine than anything in #3.

Do these five hang together or am I contradicting myself somewhere? Go through them pairwise, tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only look like it on the surface, and for each genuine tension tell me how bad it is and what the cheapest fix would be. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'd rather hear that my list is a mess than get a pat on the head.
