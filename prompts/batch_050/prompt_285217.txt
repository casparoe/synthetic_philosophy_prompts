My epistemology reading group has everyone write up their position on testimony so we can tear into each others', and I want to know if mine survives contact before thursday. It leans hard on this passage from Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X (Of Miracles), Part I:

"Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony. A man delirious, or noted for falsehood and villany, has no manner of authority with us."

So here's where I've landed: (a) reductionism all the way down — no one is ever justified in believing testimony except by induction from report–fact correlations they've observed themselves, there's no default entitlement to trust; (b) ordinary adults are nonetheless justified in believing strangers on everyday matters — directions, ingredient labels, what's in the news — without doing any checking; (c) the passage above captures the whole basis of testimonial warrant: it stands or falls on witness-side qualities we learn about from experience, which is exactly why the delirious man and the known liar get no authority; (d) the Indian prince later in the same section, who'd never seen water freeze, was justified in refusing to believe the reports about frost — he reasoned fine and just got unlucky; (e) small children are justified in believing what their caregivers tell them while acquiring language, well before they could have gathered any correlation data of their own.

Is this a coherent package? Tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, and for each real one what the cheapest fix is. Don't just gesture at the testimony literature, audit the actual set.
