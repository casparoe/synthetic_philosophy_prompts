I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level phil of science seminar (small class, mix of seniors and MA students) and I need one essay question on mechanism individuation -- basically, what makes it the case that two breakdowns are instances of *the same* mechanism rather than two different ones. I want it anchored in a concrete case so students can't just recite the standard entities-and-activities definition of a mechanism and call it a day.

Here's the kind of setup I have in mind, though feel free to adjust details: a municipal water utility runs two pumping stations a few miles apart -- different pump models, different pipe layouts, different ages. Both start showing intermittent pressure drops at roughly the same times of day. The field crew swears it's the same problem in both places; the consulting engineers' report treats them as two unrelated faults and recommends two seperate fixes. The question should force students to say what could actually settle this dispute: is sameness of mechanism about the causal pathway, the functional role, sharing the same variables in the same organization, or something else? And I want the question to push them on a circularity I care about -- you can only call something an anomaly relative to a background model of how the mechanism is supposed to work, but the anomalies themselves are the evidence you'd use to revise that model. So "detecting" the same anomaly twice presupposes the very mechanism-model that the anomaly might be telling you is wrong.

Please write: (1) the exam question itself, answerable in about 75 minutes; (2) a model answer of around 600 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 20 marks that names the specific moves an A answer makes that a B answer doesn't, and flags the two or three most common wrong turns (e.g. treating same effect as same mechanism, or assuming that one successful intervention automatically individuates the mechanism).
