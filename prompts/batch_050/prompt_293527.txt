My spouse and I have spent the last six months trying to decide whether to have a child, and the conversation has turned philosophical in a way neither of us expected. I have become convinced of a view along the following lines: what matters morally is entirely negative -- the absence of craving, suffering, and frustrated desire. Pleasure beyond a state of untroubled contentment adds nothing; a person in a state of serene, desireless peace is not missing anything by lacking ecstatic experiences, and no amount of joy can compensate anyone for even one instance of genuine suffering, because joy is only ever the satisfaction or relief of a need, and a being with no unmet needs is already perfectly well off. My spouse holds the more conventional view that positive experiences have value in their own right, so that a sufficiently happy life can contain enough joy to outweigh its suffering, and adding a flourishing person to the world makes the world better rather than merely neutral. We keep going in circles, so we have agreed to try an adversarial collaboration and produce a single document we can both sign.

Draft that document for us. It should have four parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: things we both accept about the structure of well-being, the phenomenology of pleasure and pain, and what follows for cases like ours -- be careful to include only claims a defender of each view could actually endorse, and flag any case where what looked like agreement was really the two of us using a word (like "well-being" or "worth living") in different senses. Second, a short table of our genuine remaining disagreements, each stated in one sentence that I would accept as a fair statement of my position and one sentence my spouse would accept for theirs, phrased neutrally enough that neither of us feels caricatured. Third, a list of the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one of us. I suspect one crux concerns the introspective evidence -- when meditators and people in states of deep equanimity report their experience, do they report lacking anything, and would my spouse's view predict they should? Another may concern whether ordinary people, in reflective moments, actually treat peak experiences as things that can offset bad ones, or whether that is a philosopher's construction. You are welcome to add cruxes we have missed. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete observation, thought experiment, or real-world test we both agree bears on it, with each of our predictions recorded in advance -- e.g., what my view predicts people would say in the relevant case, what my spouse's view predicts, and what outcome would count as evidence against each.

Two constraints. Do not adjudicate the dispute or hint at which of us you think is right; the document must read as co-authored, not as a verdict. And keep everything tied to our actual situation rather than abstract population ethics: the question we ultimately need the document to illuminate is whether creating a new person whose life will contain ordinary joys and ordinary hardships is a way of adding value to the world, a way of adding risk, or neither.
