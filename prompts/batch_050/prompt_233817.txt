I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of logic course, and next week I'm presenting a short seminar paper on Carroll's tortoise puzzle to the class. My thesis is that the regress the tortoise generates shows something stronger than commentators usually claim: not merely that an inference rule cannot function as an extra premise, but that accepting a rule and accepting a premise are categorically different mental states, and that any attempted justification of deduction that supplies the reasoner with further premises is therefore changing the subject. I argue that this is what forces a non-inferential, dispositional story about what it is to follow modus ponens.

The audience is my seminar group plus the instructor, who leans toward proof-theoretic accounts of logical consequence and tends to press students on whether anything like a justification of deduction is even needed. Two of the stronger students in the group are broadly Quinean about logic, and one keeps raising the point that the tortoise only regresses because we demand a *verbalized* rule, which a creature can perfectly well reason without.

Please prepare me for the question period. What objections will actually come, phrased the way this audience would put them? Order them by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes and flag where the reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front — I suspect the claim that accepting a rule is a *different kind* of state from accepting a premise is where I'm most exposed, since dispositions and beliefs aren't obviously exclusive. And tell me which objection I should be hoping nobody raises, given that I haven't worked out how my view handles someone who sincerely utters modus ponens but never in fact draws the conclusion.
