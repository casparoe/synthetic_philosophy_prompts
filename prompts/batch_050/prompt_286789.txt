I have recently joined a policy reform commission in a middle-income country, and our mandate is to assess whether elements of the Nordic model — high taxes funding universal social insurance, comprehensive public services, coordinated wage bargaining — could be transplanted here. The commission has split into two factions that have been talking past each other for months, and I have been asked to turn their stalemate into a usable document.

The first faction, which I will call the inheritance camp, holds that the Nordic outcomes rest on endowments no legislature can create: exceptionally high levels of generalized social trust (the belief that most strangers can be trusted, as distinct from trust confined to one's family, clan, or region), a long tradition of impartial administration, and historically homogeneous populations. On their view the visible institutions are downstream of these endowments — high taxes are collectible because citizens already trust one another and the state, not the reverse — so copying the institutions into a low-trust society would purchase the costs of the Nordic model without its benefits.

The second faction, the engineering camp, holds that the causation runs substantially the other way: impartial, universalist institutions manufacture generalized trust over time by treating citizens identically and making it credible that everyone is bound by the same rules, so trust is a producible output rather than a fixed input. They point to the Nordic countries' own nineteenth-century poverty and emigration, to the difference between universal programs (which they say build solidarity) and means-tested ones (which they say breed stigma and resentment), and to evidence that tax compliance in high-tax states is sustained largely by employer withholding and third-party reporting rather than by citizens' eagerness to pay — which, if true, would make the revenue side of the model far more exportable than the inheritance camp assumes.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps accept — about what the Nordic countries actually do, about the bare correlation between measured trust and state capacity, about the limits of cross-national comparisons — formulated so that neither side is conceding its position by signing.
2. A disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in neutral wording, with a separate column giving each camp's own preferred formulation of its view, phrased so that camp would accept it as accurate.
3. A cruxes section: identify the three to five sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one camp (candidates include the direction of causation between impartial administration and trust, whether trust behaves as a slow-moving stock or a fast-adjusting flow, whether homogeneity is doing causal work or merely correlating with it, and how much of tax morale survives once withholding and reporting technology is held constant). For each crux, name a concrete case, natural experiment, or body of evidence that both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that evidence shows.
4. A short final section flagging any part of the dispute that the collaboration concluded was verbal rather than substantive — for instance, whether the camps mean the same thing by "trust" or by "the Nordic model" — with the basis for that conclusion.

End the document with a single sentence on its own line, beginning "Bottom line:", stating which one crux the commission should spend its remaining research budget on and why.
