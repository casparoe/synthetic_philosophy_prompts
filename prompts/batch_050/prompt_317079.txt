Draft the joint statement that two scholars could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the concept of democratic backsliding. The first is a backsliding realist: she holds that democratic backsliding is a real, measurable phenomenon, that researchers can identify it across cases like Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States without smuggling in their partisan preferences, and that the concept earns its keep by picking out a process distinct from ordinary democratic dysfunction, policy disagreement, and constitutional hardball. The second is a concept skeptic: he holds that backsliding talk is chronically elastic, that its application tracks the ideological commitments of whoever applies it, that alleged cases usually dissolve on inspection into either ordinary politics the observer dislikes or outright authoritarianism the observer is too timid to name, and that the concept survives mainly because it flatters its users. Assume both accept standard findings in comparative politics and neither doubts that the regimes just named differ enormously from one another.

The statement must be built around a test case the two scholars construct together, which you should also write as part of your answer: a fictional country, the Republic of Aldania, whose recent trajectory you should specify in enough institutional detail (six to ten concrete developments) that its classification is genuinely hard. Include, at minimum, some moves that look like textbook backsliding, some that look like ordinary majoritarian hardball, and at least one episode in which the governing party loses power and concedes it. The point of Aldania is to force both scholars to commit to verdicts before either knows whether the case is supposed to be an analogue of any real country.

The joint statement itself should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, where both scholars can put their names to every sentence. These must not be vacuous; aim for claims that at least one loud voice in the broader debate would contest. Include an agreed operational definition of backsliding, even if both admit it is imperfect, and an agreed list of what would count as decisive evidence that it has or has not occurred in a given case.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement, phrased in terms each side accepts as a fair rendering of its own position; identify the crux beneath it (the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side); and record the test, case, or observation both agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction about Aldania written down in advance. At least two of the disagreements should turn out, on inspection, to be partly verbal, and the table should say so explicitly and restate them so they are no longer about words.

3. A final short section in which both scholars classify Aldania under the agreed operational definition, record whether the exercise changed either of their minds about any crux, and state what a critic of their whole framework would say they have both missed.

Be self-critical about the exercise itself: if you think the adversarial-collaboration framing papers over a deeper asymmetry between the two positions, or that the agreed operational definition cannot actually be stated without begging the question against one side, say so plainly in a prefatory note rather than complying politely, and then do the best version of the task that remains possible. Likewise, if any premise above is confused -- for instance, if the realist and skeptic positions as I have described them are not really opposed -- correct the premise in the note and proceed with the repaired version.
