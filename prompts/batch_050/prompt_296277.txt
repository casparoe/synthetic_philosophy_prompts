I'm a junior in high school taking both AP Physics and an introductory economics elective, and something from my economics class last week has been nagging at me. We spent a lesson on Milton Friedman's famous essay on the methodology of positive economics, and my teacher summarized his position — which she called the F-twist — as the claim that the realism of a model's assumptions simply doesn't matter, and that a theory should be judged entirely by whether its predictions come true. She presented this as a settled, almost liberating result: economists are free to assume whatever is convenient, because prediction is the only tribunal. I found this exciting at first, but the more I've thought about it, the more it seems to collide with everything else I'm being taught.

In physics, we spend enormous effort on exactly the question Friedman seems to dismiss. When we treat a plane as frictionless or a gas as ideal, we're constantly drilled on when those idealizations are legitimate and when they'll lead us astray — my teacher even deducts points if we apply the ideal gas law in a regime where it breaks down, which seems like precisely the kind of assumption-scrutiny Friedman told us to skip. Meanwhile, a friend of mine has started wielding the F-twist as a general-purpose argument: he says climate models, epidemiological models, and basically everything else should be evaluated purely on predictive track record, and that anyone who probes whether their assumptions are realistic is just committing a methodological error that Friedman refuted seventy years ago. He even claims that, on Friedman's view, more unrealistic assumptions are actually preferable, since wildly false assumptions force you to build simpler models, and simpler models are easier to test.

I suspect something has gone wrong in my friend's reasoning, and maybe in my teacher's summary too, but I can't pin down exactly where. Is scrutinizing assumptions really just a confusion, as Friedman supposedly showed? If so, why does every practicing scientist I read about seem to care about it? And if not, what exactly was Friedman's point — was he saying something narrower than the version I was taught, and what would a fair criticism of even the narrow version look like?
