I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character — a philosopher of unnerving clear-sightedness — accepts Kavka's toxin offer and, to the disbelief of everyone around her, actually collects the prize. My early readers insist the psychology is impossible: someone who sees the situation perfectly clearly cannot genuinely intend at midnight to drink at noon, knowing she will have no reason to drink when noon arrives. Construct for me a thought experiment, set in the ordinary world with no drugs, hypnotists, or devices that implant intentions directly, in which a fully rational agent who understands the puzzle nevertheless forms the intention and wins. The hard constraint: your mechanism must not simply alter tomorrow's incentives — no signed contracts, hired enforcers, or side bets that make drinking the sensible option when the time comes, since that dissolves the puzzle rather than solving it. At noon the agent must still have every reason not to drink, and yet the midnight intention must have been both genuine and rationally formed. After the scenario, walk through her reasoning at midnight and at noon, identify which feature of the arrangement is doing the work, and say whether removing it collapses the case back into the original puzzle. Close with the strongest objection a skeptic could raise against the character as I have written her.
