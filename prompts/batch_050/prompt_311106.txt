I'm a backend engineer and I recently fell down a rabbit hole reading about this thought-experiment-y idea for modeling good deliberation: you take one person, and when they face a hard question they don't answer it directly — instead they break it into sub-questions, hand each one off to a fresh copy of themselves who only has a little time and a small scratchpad, and each copy does the same thing recursively until the questions are small enough to just answer, and then the answers get passed back up and combined. The copies apparently don't share memory, they only see the question they were handed and the answers they got back from below. I think it comes from the AI alignment world, the idea being that if you could actually run this, even a superhumanly hard question could be decomposed until each leaf is something an ordinary human can reliably judge.

I obviously don't have copies of myself, but I do have hard decisions, and it strikes me that a solo version of this might still be a legit technique — like structured deliberation you actually run on paper over a couple evenings. So: can you write me a concrete procedure I could follow? I want the actual steps in order — how to decompose a question, how to decide if a sub-question is small enough to just answer versus needs further splitting, what the "small scratchpad" constraint should mean in practice (how much context am I allowed to pass down, and why might passing down too much defeat the point?), how to recombine the answers at each level, and a stopping rule that isn't just "when I run out of weekend." Also flag where in the procedure I inevitably have to smuggle in my own gut judgment rather than follow a rule, because I'd rather know where those points are than pretend they don't exist.

And be honest about failure modes. The one that worries me as an engineer is that this looks a lot like recursive function calls with no shared state, and in code that has known pathologies: error compounding up the tree, decompositions that subtly change the question being answered, subproblems that aren't actually independent (so combining their answers double-counts something), and the root call framing things in a way that predetermines everything below. Tell me which of these the procedure can mitigate and which it just has to live with.

Then run it twice for me: (1) whether I should accept a staff engineer offer at a big company vs staying tech lead at my current startup, and (2) whether to greenlight rewriting our payments service as an event-sourced system. If the procedure spits out an answer on one of these that feels wrong to a normal person, don't sand it down — say so, because knowing where the method misfires is half of what I'm asking for. Decisions like the first come up maybe twice a year for me so spending real time is fine; stuff like the second comes up monthly and I can't spend more than ~3 hours on the process. I'm hoping this is the kind of thing where the structure survives being done by one tired guy with a notebook instead of an infinite tree of clones, but maybe not, in which case tell me that too.
