A popular science book I have been reading summarises the units of selection debate like this: 'The selfish gene theory settled the question of what natural selection acts on: genes, not organisms or groups. A gene that predisposes a bird to share food with its siblings spreads because copies of that very gene sit inside the siblings, so apparent altruism is really genetic self-interest. This is also why group selection — the idea that traits evolve because they benefit the group — turned out to be a confusion: groups cannot replicate, only genes can. Contemporary talk of "multilevel selection" or "trait groups" is a relapse into the pre-1960s mistake, since anything that benefits a group must already be fully explainable in terms of gene fitness.' The author is a well-known science writer, so I am reluctant to dismiss the passage, but I have also heard that some respected biologists and philosophers defend group selection, which makes me suspect the summary is tidier than the reality. Which parts of it would a careful philosopher of biology accept, which parts conflate or overstate distinct positions, and does the dismissal of multilevel selection actually follow from anything true in the summary?
