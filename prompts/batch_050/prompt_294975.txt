I put together this argument for a reading group on James's Will to Believe and now I'm worried it either refutes James or proves something crazy. Setup: James says that when you face a genuine option -- one that's live (both sides appeal to you as real possibilities), forced (you can't duck it, since refusing to choose is itself a choice), and momentous (real stakes, unrepeatable opportunity) -- and the evidence leaves the matter open, you're permitted to let your passional nature decide what you believe. Now:

(1) If an option is live, forced, and momentous for you, and the evidence doesn't settle it, then believing on non-evidential grounds is permissible, sometimes even the rational move.
(2) Deciding to trust a close friend who's under a cloud of suspicion fits: it's live, forced (your behavior will treat him as trustworthy or not either way), momentous (the friendship is at stake and the chance won't come back), and the evidence is genuinely mixed.
(3) But a flat-earther's conviction fits too: it's live for him, forced in the same behavioral way (he has to decide who to trust before he can act on basically anything), momentous to him (his whole community and worldview hang on it), and he regards the official evidence as manufactured, so from his position the trustworthy evidence doesn't settle anything.
(4) So James's principle licenses flat-earth belief. Which it obviously can't. So either (1) is false or the criteria need a patch -- but every patch I try (objective rather than perceived evidence, or restricting it to cases where belief helps create the fact) seems to knock out the friend case too, wich is exactly the case James wanted to protect.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep (1) and the friend case than be fair to flat-earthers, but I want to know what I'm paying either way.
