A pattern keeps showing up in arguments about morality, and I want to know whether it — or rather, the defect I think I'm detecting in it — has an established name.

The general move is familiar: someone challenges a moral conviction not by arguing it's false but by giving a causal story about why the conviction exists. Your sense that loyalty to family is obligatory, say, is there because natural selection favored kin favoritism, and selection tracks fitness rather than rightness, so the conviction is unearned. I know this lives somewhere near evolutionary debunking arguments. But what I keep noticing is a specific asymmetry in how the move gets used: the person deploying it never applies it across the board. Their own bedrock convictions — that suffering is bad, that cruelty is wrong — get quietly exempted, usually with a shrug like "those are just obvious." I've now seen this in three flavors: a friend who dissolved my disapproval of nepotism via inclusive fitness while treating her horror at factory farming as simple insight; a writer who traced conservative sexual ethics to disgust adaptations and set them aside, while grounding his egalitarian commitments in nothing more than what any decent person sees; and an interlocutor who, pressed on this, said some convictions survive because tracking them would have been advantageous — which seemed to me to quietly restore the truth-connection he'd just denied everywhere else.

Is there a name for this selective exemption, for debunking that stops at the debunker's own doorstep? It's not quite the genetic fallacy, since the challenge can be legitimate; the trouble is the asymmetry. I don't want a bare label: I want to know how the nearest concepts divide this territory, where the phenomenon sits among them, and where its edges are — for instance, whether it covers the case where carrying the challenge through would also undermine the debunker's trust in their own reasoning, and whether the exemption has to be explicit to count.
