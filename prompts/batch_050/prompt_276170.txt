My girlfriend and I got into an argument last week about when it's okay to stay mad at people, and I realized my gut verdicts don't line up with any rule I can actually state. Background: I read a summary of Strawson's Freedom and Resentment for a reading group and liked the idea that resentment tracks the quality of will someone shows toward you, and that with some people we slip into an "objective stance" where we treat them more like a problem to be managed than a participant in a relationship. But my real reactions are messier than that picture. Here's my list:

1. My sister made a nasty comment about my career at a family dinner. She was jet-lagged and hadn't eaten. I was annoyed in the moment but it evaporated once she apologized. Verdict: fine to be briefly annoyed, wrong to hold a grudge.
2. A resident with advanced dementia at my grandmother's care home shoved me and called me names. Zero resentment, it never even came up. Seems obviously right.
3. A colleague took credit for my idea in a meeting. He grew up in a brutal hypercompetitive household and I honestly think he can't fully switch off the reflex. I still resent him, and I don't think I'm wrong to.
4. A friend lied to me about something minor because his therapist told him to practice boundaries (I know, I know). I resent him MORE than the colleague from case 3, even though his reasons were arguably better.
5. My toddler threw my laptop on the floor. No resentment, though I felt a flash of something like it for half a second and then felt silly.
6. A guy with (I think) schizophrenia yelled at me on the bus. No resentment, same as the dementia case.
7. My grandfather, dead ten years, did things to my mom I only found out about recently. I feel full-blown resentment toward him. My girlfriend says you can't resent the dead because there's no relationship left to repair, but I definitly do.

The pairs that bug me: 3 vs 6 -- in both cases the person arguably couldn't help it, yet only one of them gets the objective-stance treatment from me. 4 vs 3 -- better reasons, more resentment. And 2 vs 7 -- no future interaction possible in either, but my attitudes are totally different. My tentative principle was something like: resentment is fitting iff the person showed ill will or culpable indifference toward you AND was capable of doing otherwise at the time. But I think that fails case 3 (maybe he wasn't capable, and I still resent) and case 7 (no will being shown to anyone anymore). So: give me a principle, or two or three candidate principles, stated precisely, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each clause of the principle, tell me which case motivates it. Then tell me what it predicts about cases I didn't list -- resenting yourself, resenting a company rather than a person, whether forgiving someone who never apologized means giving up resentment or keeping it while moving on, whether it's coherent to resent a fictional villain. And be honest at the end about which of my verdicts your best principle simply can't save, and which one you'd recommend I give up if I had to give up exactly one.
