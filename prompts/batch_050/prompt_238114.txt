I work in statistics and machine learning, and I recently came across Popper's attempt to define verisimilitude — the idea that science progresses by replacing false theories with theories that are closer to the truth — and the results, due independently to David Miller and Pavel Tichý in the mid-1970s, that his definition fails: it cannot rank any two false theories, and any repaired ranking appears to be language-dependent.

Please restate this entire debate in the vocabulary I actually work in: parameter spaces, metrics, loss functions, estimation error, model misspecification, and model selection criteria. I want the translation to be as precise as possible, covering at least the following:

1. Popper's basic proposal — that a theory's verisimilitude is a matter of its true consequences outweighing its false ones — recast in terms of a model's relation to the true data-generating process.
2. The Miller–Tichý objection, recast in terms of why no natural notion of closeness in model space does the work Popper needed.
3. The language-dependence result, recast in terms of reparameterization: whether what the philosophers call language dependence is something statisticians already handle routinely when they choose coordinates and metrics, and if so whether it is a genuine problem or merely a fact about metric choice.
4. The problem of content: Popper wanted bolder, more informative theories to get credit, which seems to have no clean counterpart in a framework where closeness to the truth is all that matters.

Then give me the residue report. Which elements of the verisimilitude problem translate cleanly into statistical terms, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in this vocabulary at all? For the residue, say in each case whether you think it marks a limitation of the statistical framework — something real that estimation theory simply does not capture, such as the truthlikeness of a theory as opposed to the accuracy of its predictions — or whether it marks a claim that was less clear than it appeared and dissolves once you demand a formal counterpart.

Conclude with your verdict: from a statistician's standpoint, is the problem of verisimilitude a genuine open problem, a solved problem that statisticians solved without noticing, or a pseudo-problem generated by demanding one thing — a single ranking of theories by closeness to truth — that no framework can or should deliver?
