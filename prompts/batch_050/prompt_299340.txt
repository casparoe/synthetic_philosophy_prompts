I sit on the transitional justice commission of a (fictional) post-authoritarian country. Ten years ago the old regime's interior ministry ran a border unit that pushed asylum seekers back across the river into a neighboring war zone; hundreds drowned or were shot there. Everything the unit did was explicitly authorized under the regime's Border Security Act at the time, and the officers involved followed the law as it then stood. Our new parliament has just passed a statute criminalizing "organized refoulement with reckless disregard for life," drafted precisely so it covers the river unit, and the prosecutor wants to charge twelve former officers under it. Several commission members object that this is retroactive punishment -- no one disputes the facts, only whether acts lawful when committed can be crimes now. Others point to Nuremberg and say retroactivity was accepted there and the sky didn't fall.

Draft the commission's decision memo recommending whether we should endorse the prosecutions, endorse them only for the commanders, or oppose them outright. It should take the Nuremberg precedent seriously rather than just name-dropping it: say what, if anything, actually justified trying people for crimes against humanity that weren't codified in the defendants' own law, whether that justification transfers to our case (where the conduct was affirmatively legal, not just uncriminalized), and how to weigh the "they were just following the law of the time" argument without making it either decisive or worthless. Give the strongest version of the losing side a fair shake.

Format: Recommendation (bottom line first, ~3 sentences), then The nullum crimen objection (~1 paragraph), What Nuremberg does and doesn't establish (~2 paragraphs), Applying it to the river unit (~2 paragraphs), and Reply to the strongest counterargument (~1 paragraph).
