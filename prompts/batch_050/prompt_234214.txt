So I write a science-and-ideas column, and I somehow got talked into presenting at a workshop on species concepts. The room will be mostly working evolutionary biologists — a couple of phylogeneticists, some conservation genetics people, at least one very patient taxonomist — plus two philosophers of biology who are defnitely going to be more sympathetic to me than the scientists are. My thesis: species is not a natural kind. Biology doesn't carve nature at its joints at that level, and the concept survives because it's administratively useful — endangered species law, collection drawers, grant proposals — not because it tracks anything deep about the tree of life. Prep me for the Q&A. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this audience — from the biologists, not from philosophers — in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way a phylogeneticist or a conservation geneticist would put it, each with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply. Flag where each reply is soft. And include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks — my guess is it's about ring species, or about what "lineage" even means in asexual microbes, but if there's a worse one lurking I want to know now.
