I'm putting together a handout for my grad seminar on minds and machines, and I want a decision tree that sorts positions on when it's legitimate to attribute beliefs to a system. Start the tree from a familiar spot, something like "I catch myself saying the chess engine wants to trap my bishop," and branch on the actual fault lines: whether belief-talk is only ever a prediction strategy, whether there's a fact of the matter about what a system believes beyond what best explains its behavior to us, whether genuine belief requires internal states that play a language-like role, whether consciousness is a prerequisite, and whatever else you think belongs on the tree. Each leaf should get a named position (Dennett-style instrumentalism about the intentional stance, interpretivism, representation realism, eliminativism, or others you think earn a spot), and each branch node should be a question someone could answer without knowing which view it funnels them toward.

A few constraints so it doesnt turn into an encyclopedia entry. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — then locate actual positions in that space, and note any combination nobody seems to occupy with a sentence on whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Somewhere in the tree or alongside it, use these test probes: a thermostat, a chess engine, a large language model, an army ant colony, and a corporation. Flag any pair of leaves your questions can't really separate rather than pretending the tree is finer-grained than it is. And one thing I specifically care about, because my students always muddle it: keep apart the claim that an attribution is warranted because it predicts well from the claim that it's true because of how the system is internally organized.
