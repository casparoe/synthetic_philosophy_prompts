Draft: "The Laws of History Are Back. Did Popper Lose?"

For most of the twentieth century, respectable historians treated the search for laws of history as a cautionary tale. Ibn Khaldun had dynasties rising and collapsing over a few generations as their group solidarity decayed. Toynbee had civilizations growing or dying by how they responded to challenges. Marx had history marching through stages toward communism. By midcentury the whole enterprise looked like pseudo-science in tweed, and the man usually credited with burying it is the philosopher Karl Popper.

In The Poverty of Historicism, Popper went after the tradition of historical prophecy he associated above all with Hegel and Marx. His cleanest argument runs in three steps. First, the course of human history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge. Second, we cannot predict, by rational or scientific methods, the future growth of our knowledge — because if you could describe tomorrow's discovery today, in enough detail for it to count as a prediction, you would already have made the discovery, and it wouldn't be tomorrow's anymore. Third, therefore we cannot predict the future course of human history. No five-year plan and no dialectic can tell you what humanity will know in fifty years, and what we know shapes what we do.

Popper's point, in other words, is that history is simply too complicated to predict: too many variables, no controlled experiments, feedback loops everywhere — roughly the same reason long-range weather forecasts are useless. Still, he granted that history displays trends, like population growth and technological progress, and accepted that these count as laws of a weaker, statistical kind. And since science, on his view, stands or falls with prediction, he concluded that a genuinely scientific study of society is impossible — all that's left is storytelling, plus cautious tinkering with institutions one piece at a time.

Enter cliodynamics. Peter Turchin and his colleagues have spent two decades assembling enormous databases of past societies — harvest records, coin hoards, civil wars — and claim to find real regularities: states seem to pass through cycles of integration and breakdown on the order of a century or two, and the warning signs of crisis include stagnating wages, ballooning public debt, and above all what Turchin calls elite overproduction, a surplus of ambitious elites fighting over a fixed number of top jobs. He is famous for a forecast, made years in advance, that political instability in America would spike around 2020. If the cliodynamicists are right, the prophecy business is back, this time with regression tables. Popper said there could be no laws of history; Turchin claims to be publishing them. Score one for Toynbee.
