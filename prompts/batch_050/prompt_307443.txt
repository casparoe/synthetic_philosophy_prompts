So a bit of an odd request, bear with me. I teach philosophy of religion, mostly Hume, and an old college friend who now works for a diocese roped me into helping overhaul how their medical commission evaluates reported miraculous healings at a pilgrimage site they administer. Right now the process is basically vibes: a couple of doctors and a priest read the file and argue until someone gives up. I want to hand them an actual decision procedure grounded in Hume's argument in Of Miracles (the Enquiry section about testimony and violations of natural law), but operationalized for a world where, unlike in Hume's day, you have hospital records, imaging, and lab work rather than travellers' tales.

The procedure has to respect what I take to be the load-bearing moves in Hume, and push back if you think I've misread them. First, the distinction between an event that would violate a law of nature and one that's merely very improbable or currently unexplained, and whether a medical remission can ever fall in the first category rather than just the tail of a distribution. Second, his weighing maneuver — the evidence for the event set against the probability that the reporters are deceived or deceiving — but with the wrinkle that here the "reporters" are mostly MRI machines and oncologists, which shifts where error and fraud can actually enter the chain. Third, the reference-class problem Hume never had to face: thousands of sick people visit this site every year, most get better or don't, so the commission needs an explicit rule for when one striking recovery out of a huge pool counts as evidence of anything, rather than letting the denominator quietly do all the argumentative work. The procedure should be runnable on a single case file in an afternoon by people with no philosophy background, and it has to mark the points where it simply can't settle things and a person has to make a call — e.g., how much weight to give the religious significance of the cure, which is where I think Hume stops being useful.

Deliverables, in this order. First, the procedure as a table with columns: Stage | Question to answer | What evidence counts | Pass condition | Where human judgement is unavoidable | What happens on failure. Second, run the finished procedure on three cases: (1) a woman with biopsy-confirmed aggressive pancreatic cancer, complete remission six weeks after visiting the site, no conventional treatment that could explain it, five years clean; (2) a teenager whose fractured femur healed implausibly fast after prayers at the site, but the original X-rays are missing and the diagnosis rests on a note from a clinic that no longer exists; (3) a beloved retired nun's recovery from a stroke — the bishop is personally invested in this one — medically well documented but squarely within the normal range of stroke recoveries. At least one of these should come out with a verdict the commision won't enjoy, and say which. Give each case's outcome as a JSON object with fields: case, stage_reached, verdict (affirm / reject / remand_for_more_evidence), decisive_factor, and humean_principle_applied. No bibliography, I know the literature — what I need is the machinery.
