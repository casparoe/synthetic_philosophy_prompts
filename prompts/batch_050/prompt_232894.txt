I run a philosophy podcast, and next month I'm interviewing a philosopher whose new book defends an uncompromising externalism about normative reasons. Her view is more specific than the usual externalist line, so I want to get past the standard rehearsal. As she puts it: a fact can be a reason for an agent to act even if no deliberative route, however idealized, leads from that agent's actual motivations to being moved by it. She rejects even the weakest version of the internalism requirement — the idea, associated with Williams, that a reason must be capable of motivating the agent under some suitably corrected deliberation. Three commitments are distinctive to her version. First, she splits what she calls reason-possession from reason-accessibility and argues that internalists simply conflate the two: whether a fact counts in favor of your action is one question; whether you could ever come to care about it is another. Second, she defends a peer-transmission principle: if a fully informed agent in your exact circumstances would be motivated to act, that fact is a reason for you to act, full stop, even if nothing in your own psychology could ever take you there. Third, she bites the bullet on blame: she says blameworthiness tracks reasons, not motivational capacity, so the objection that external reasons make blame unfair gets the dependence backwards.

Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that presses these commitments rather than inviting her to restate them. I want the sequence to escalate: each question should assume she has survived the previous one, so that her answers progressively foreclose escape routes. Include at least one question on each of the following: the link between normativity and motivation (if a reason needn't be able to move me, in what sense is it a reason *for me* rather than a fact about the world?); whether her peer-transmission principle inherits the conditional fallacy worry that plagues any "you would be motivated if..." formulation, since the hypothetical peer differs from her precisely in the respects that made deliberation from her own motivations impossible; the "too many reasons" objection — on her view, is every true fact that bears on the goodness of an outcome a reason for everyone, including agents who couldn't in principle detect its relevance?; the deliberative role of reasons — what an agent is supposed to *do* with a reason she can never come to care about, and whether calling it a reason still guides anything; and, finally, a closing question about what evidence or argument would actually make her abandon externalism rather than refine it.

For each question, anticipate the reply she is most likely to give — she is careful, and her standard move is to redescribe internalist intuitions as confused deliverances of the accessibility question — and supply a follow-up keyed to that reply. Avoid generic questions and anything answerable by summarizing her book.

After the questions, give me a one-glance summary as a table with these columns: No. (question number); Question (a five-to-eight-word label, not the full text); Commitment targeted (which of the three distinctive commitments, or a background commitment, it attacks); Anticipated reply (one sentence); Follow-up (one sentence); and Concession sought (the admission, distinction, or clarification the exchange is designed to secure, stated so I can tell during the interview whether I got it).
