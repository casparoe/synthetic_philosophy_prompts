I teach a sophomore moral psychology seminar, about 24 students, and I've got one 80-minute session on the honor/dignity/victimhood culture typology. Instead of lecturing at them I want to run a simulation where the room splits into three "societies" that each live under different norms for handling slights, insults, and harms, and then get thrown together.

Design the activity. Roughly what I'm after: in the honor society there are no third-party enforcers, so standing should depend on personally answering disrespect (and losing face if you don't, especially in front of witnesses). In the dignity society slights are supposed to be beneath notice and there's a functioning arbiter who handles genuine harms. In the victimhood society the arbiter exists but mostly responds to public appeals, so airing a grievance in front of an audience and getting it publically affirmed by third parties is how you get ahead. Give me the actual rules, a payoff scheme in points (no real money), roles (arbiter? witnesses? some kind of public forum?), and a timeline with two phases: a within-society phase where each system has to work on its own terms, and a mixed phase where the societies collide and each culture reads the others' moves as weakness, aggression, or manipulation.

The payoff structure is what I care most about: each culture's signature behavior should be locally rational, not just theater, so students feel from the inside why someone raised in that system does what they do -- why an honor-culture player can't just shrug off a public insult even when fighting costs points, why the victimhood-culture player isn't being "overly sensitive" but responding sensibly to the incentives. Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments from the game onto the typology and pushes them on whether any of the three is morally superior, or whether that question even makes sense detached from the circumstances each culture is adapted to.

Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess is everyone figures out which strategy scores best and converges on it, which kills the contrast), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce that I'd have to supply by lecture -- real enculturation from childhood and the actual deterrent logic of violence presumably won't show up in a points game.

One more thing: in the debrief suggestions, seperate what's reasonably established from what's genuinely contested. Whether this typology carves real societies at the joints, whether anything like a pure dignity culture has ever existed, and of course whether any of the three is morally superior are all live disputes -- so don't write the debrief as if "victimhood culture is a pathology" or any other superiority verdict were the scholarly consensus. I want the students arguing about that, not receiving it.
