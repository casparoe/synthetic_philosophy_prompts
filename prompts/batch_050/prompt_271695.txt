I’ve been hanging around LessWrong/ACX/EA-adjacent comment sections long enough to have a dangerous level of confidence about selection effects, and next month I’m giving a brown-bag at a mid-sized foundation defending a policy I’m calling an optimism tax for hard-to-recall projects. The audience is not the usual EA forum crowd: five program officers who think in cost-effectiveness and downside logs, two philosophers of science who keep muttering about model error and experimenter degrees of freedom, one former biosecurity person who hears “garage” and reaches for incident reports, and a skeptical operations lawyer who mostly cares who gets sued. My claim is basically the unilateralist’s curse, but sharpened: when a project is cheap to start, noisy to evaluate, legible enough to attract copycats, and difficult or embarrassing to stop once begun, the actor who moves is often just the upper tail of everybody’s estimate, so funders and hosts should demand a higher bar from lone actors and small ad hoc teams than from coordinated groups, plus precommitments to pauses, recall plans, and publishing negative results. I want to be careful that this isn’t just “trust institutions more” dressed up; the whole point is that even well-intentioned, individually reasonable people can produce a collectively dumb outcome because the decider is selected for optimism.

Please write my Q&A prep in British English, even though I’m writing this in American English and probably with a typo or two. Give me the objections I’ll actually get from this room, not from Twitter: ordered roughly by expected damage times likelihood, each phrased in the objector’s own idiom (grantmaker, philosopher of science, biosecurity, lawyer), with a two-minute answer, what I should concede immediately, what I should hold, and where my reply is soft. Please include the annoying steelman that my rule penalizes weird genius and would have blocked the good outliers, the worry that recall plans are theater because irreversible info hazards can’t be recalled, the objection that coordinated groups have their own curse via groupthink and cover-your-back incentives, and the legal/ops version where extra gates just push risk to jurisdictions with fewer gates. End with the question I’m hoping nobody asks, and a crisp line I can say when I’m cornered that doesn’t sound like I’ve memorized a blog post.
