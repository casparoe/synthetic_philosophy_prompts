You are a professional speechwriter with a specialty in phenomenology, hired to draft remarks that translate a philosophical idea into something a mixed room can feel. Before writing, spend a few sentences brainstorming the central conceit the speech will turn on, then write the full speech.

The occasion: my father is closing the shoe-repair shop he has run for forty-one years, and I'm speaking at the farewell gathering in the shop itself. The room will be split roughly in two. One half is family, longtime customers, and neighboring shopkeepers -- people who knew him through his hands and his counter, not through ideas. The other half is my colleagues from the philosophy department; I wrote my dissertation on the phenomenology of skilled action, and they will notice immediately if I get Heidegger wrong or merely name-drop him.

Build the speech around the distinction between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand: the way well-functioning equipment withdraws from attention while it's being used, becoming conspicuous only when it breaks, fails, or goes missing. The speech should take seriously that my father's entire working life has been a kind of deliberate non-appearance -- forty-one years of competence so reliable that nobody in this room ever had to think about their shoes -- and that the closing of the shop is, structurally, a breakdown: the moment the tool becomes visible is the moment it stops being usable. Use that to shape what the speech can and can't do. It must resist the temptation to make him "present-at-hand" in the bad way -- turning a living practice into a museum piece, praising the craft as a quaint artifact rather than honoring it as a way of being absorbed in a world. But it also can't pretend the breakdown isn't happening.

Constraints: about five minutes spoken (roughly 700 words); no untranslated German jargon; at most one gentle joke that lands for both halves of the room; do not quote Heidegger or anyone else verbatim. End on something both halves can carry out the door.
