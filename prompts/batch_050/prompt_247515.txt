You are a philosopher with particular expertise in Hume's practical essays and in the ethics of death and dying, and you are good at spotting which commitments in a set actually conflict rather than merely sound as if they do. I would like you to think carefully, and before auditing anything, briefly work out what motivates each commitment on its own terms, since some apparent conflicts dissolve once the motivations are clear.

I am a novelist. The central character of my current book is an aging scholar who, after a long illness and much deliberation, calmly ends her own life, and the novel is meant to treat her decision as rational and even honorable rather than as a symptom or a moral failure. In developing her philosophy I have absorbed it into my own thinking, and I now want to know whether the position I have ended up holding -- in life and on the page -- is coherent. Here it is:

1. Hume's case in his essay on suicide succeeds: in the central cases, ending one's life transgresses no duty to God, no duty to society, and no duty to oneself, and whether to continue living is properly settled by weighing the good one's remaining life promises against the misery.
2. Death is nothing to the one who dies. Once a person is gone, nothing that happens afterward can harm or benefit her; the Epicurean point stands.
3. Whether a human life goes well is, at bottom, a matter of the balance of pleasure over suffering within it -- this is the yardstick Hume reaches for, and I think he reaches for the right one.
4. All the same, a parent of young children who kills herself does those children a serious wrong, not merely a sad disservice. This is not a verdict I am willing to soften for the novel or out of it.
5. A life's worth can depend on its narrative shape: whether a project is completed, a promise kept, a story rounded off or left broken. Two lives identical in felt experience can differ in how well they went because of how they were composed, including how they ended.
6. Supporting suicide prevention -- crisis lines, intervention, the cultural presumption against it -- is right and important, and my novel's sympathetic treatment of one suicide is no argument against any of that.

Please audit this set. Which commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each case? Which pairs only appear to conflict and can be reconciled on inspection -- say how? For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what it costs. I am not attached to any particular commitment; treat the whole set as negotiable, and if a single revision would clean up the most, tell me which one. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally troubling.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- whether the position hangs together or not, and where the real problems lie -- then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question or my list back to me.
