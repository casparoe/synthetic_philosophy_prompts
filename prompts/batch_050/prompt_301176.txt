Background, in case it's rusty: Thomson's lamp. A lamp is on at t=0. At t=1/2 it's switched off, at t=3/4 on, at t=7/8 off, and so on, infinitely many switches before t=1. Thomson's argument, in effect: at t=1 the lamp can't be on, since it was never turned on without being immediately turned off again; and it can't be off, since after the initial turning-on it was never turned off without being immediatly turned back on. But it must be one or the other. Contradiction, so the supertask is impossible.

The standard comeback (Benacerraf and followers) is that Thomson only showed the lamp's state at t=1 isn't *determined* by what happens strictly before t=1, and underdetermination isn't contradiction -- the final state is just a further fact you'd have to stipulate. Most people seem to think this defuses Thomson. I'm not so sure, and here's why, via two modifications I want you to take seriously:

1. The deterministic relay. Rerun the lamp, but now at t=1 the lamp's state causally triggers something downstream: if the lamp is on at t=1, a bell rings; if off, it stays silent. Assume ordinary physics plus whatever continuity principles you like. The Benacerraf move says the pre-t=1 history is fully consistent with either stipulation. But now it seems like the *world itself* has to settle the question -- the bell rings or it doesn't -- and yet nothing in the causal history settles it. Is "the laws just don't say, and reality arbitrarily does one" an acceptable answer, or does the causal hookup turn underdetermination back into something contradiction-adjacent?

2. The dimmer lamp. Replace the on/off switch with a dial, and suppose the mechanism moves the dial continuously toward some position (say, brightness exactly 1/2) as the switching accelerates, so that continuity *does* fix a unique state at t=1. If supertasks were impossible merely because of the infinity, this one should be impossible too. If it's fine, then Thomson's argument was never about infinity per se but about discontinuity at the limit.

The essay question: does Thomson's argument, or anything in its vicinity, show that switching-type supertasks are logically impossible -- or does it only show that some supertask descriptions are incomplete, in which case impossibility talk was always overreach? Use the two modifications as your testing ground; don't just survey the debate at arm's length. Weigh the best arguments on both sides (including the "no last switch" way of pressing Thomson's point, which I think is stronger than the way he actually put it), but land somewhere definite and defend the verdict. Hedged both-sidesism gets a failing grade. Also, if you think my two modifications are badly set up or smuggle in bad assumptions, say so -- that counts as engaging, not dodging.
