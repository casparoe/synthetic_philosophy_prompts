I'm in a seminar on philosophical methodology this semester and last week we did a session on thought experiments and intuition pumps (Dennett's term, which he meant sort of neutrally, but half the class treated it as an insult). After class two people from the seminar, Maya and Dan, got into a long argument in the hallway that I watched without really picking a side, and I've been going back and forth on it since, so I want an outside verdict.

The argument started over the experience machine. Maya said the case misleads people: if you flip the setup around and ask people to imagine they've been in the machine since birth and someone offers to unplug them, plenty of people suddenly don't want to leave, which she said shows the original case is covertly pumping a fear of losing the life you already have, not a considered judgment that reality matters more than experience. Dan said that's not a problem with thought experiments, it's just how they work -- when a variant flips your verdict, the method is correcting itself, so "intuition pump" as a criticism doesn't land; the pump and the unpump are the same tool. Maya replied that self-correction only happens if someone actually bothers to run the variant, and famous cases get their canonical verdicts locked in way before anyone does, so the first framing you encounter anchors everything downstream. Dan said that's a claim about academic sociology, not about whether thought experiments are good evidence, and you can't convict a method on the laziness of its users. Then Maya pushed further: some pumped intuitions don't just persuade people, they get baked in as premises -- she brought up personal identity cases where the intuition you get from the story is doing the actual argumentative work -- so a misleading pump isn't a correctable error, it's garbage going in at the ground floor and you can't argue your way out from inside the building. Dan's last move, before he had to run to another class, was that ordinary perception is fallible and frame-sensitive too and nobody proposes giving up on observation, so thought experiments shouldn't be held to a standard nothing else meets.

Can you go through this and adjudicate it move by move -- say who's right at each step and why, rather than just declaring a winner? And if there's something both of them are missing or a distinction that would dissolve part of the fight, I'd like to hear that too, since I might write my final paper on this and don't want to just transcribe their hallway fight.
