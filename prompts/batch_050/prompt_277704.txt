Next month I am presenting a paper at a Holocaust and Genocide Studies colloquium, and I want to be prepared for the question period. The paper defends a reconstruction of the uniqueness debate. My claim is that the Historikerstreit of the 1980s, and much of the comparability literature since, conflated three distinct questions: whether the Holocaust was unique in the perpetrators' intent (total extermination of a people as state policy), unique in its means (bureaucratic-industrial organization of killing), or unique in its outcome. I argue that Zygmunt Bauman's thesis in Modernity and the Holocaust — that the Holocaust was a product of modern bureaucratic rationality rather than a breakdown of civilization — is defensible only on the second stratum, that the first stratum is where a genuine uniqueness claim survives comparison, and that treating the strata separately dissolves most of the old dispute without either sacralizing the Holocaust or flattening it into one case among many.

The audience is roughly fifteen people. Two are social historians of Nazi Germany working in the functionalist tradition — they know the perpetrator research cold and tend to be suspicious of any grand sociological theorizing about "modernity." One is a comparative genocide scholar whose position, stated bluntly, is that uniqueness talk is memory politics dressed up as historiography. One is a philosopher of history who will care about whether my strata are analytic categories or rhetorical ones. The rest are doctoral students.

Please prepare me in the following form:

1. The objections I will actually receive, ordered by how damaging each would be to the paper's reception, each phrased the way that particular audience member would phrase it, in their vocabulary — the functionalists' objections will not sound like the comparativist's.
2. For each objection: what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and a reply deliverable in about two minutes, with a candid note on where the reply is weakest.
3. Which objections have real force against the three-stratum framework itself, as opposed to force only against Bauman's original formulation.
4. The question I am hoping nobody asks, and the best available answer to it anyway.

I especially want to be ready for the empirical pressure on my second stratum — the functionalists will know exactly how much of the killing was low-tech, improvised, and local rather than industrial — and for the charge that separating the strata lets uniqueness claims survive only by shrinking them.
