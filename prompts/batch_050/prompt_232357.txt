I'm a journalist preparing a magazine piece on the statue-and-clay puzzle in material constitution, and I want to close the article with a commissioned poem rather than a summary paragraph. Write a contrapuntal poem — one that can be read as two separate columns (each a coherent lyric on its own) and also read straight across, line by line — in which the left column speaks for the statue and the right column for the lump of clay. The formal conceit must do the philosophical work: the two voices should occupy every shared line exactly as the two objects are said to occupy the same region of space, and somewhere in the poem one column should address the modal difference that drives the puzzle (the lump survives being squashed; the statue does not) in a way that only lands if the reader notices the columns are made of the same words read differently. Aim for 12 to 16 shared lines. Then, in a brief afterword, take a definite stand: tell me which position on material constitution the poem is committed to — genuine coincidence of two objects, constitution without identity, or the eliminativist insistence that there is really only one thing here being described twice — and explain which formal choices in the poem foreclose the rival readings, rather than hedging that the poem leaves it open.
