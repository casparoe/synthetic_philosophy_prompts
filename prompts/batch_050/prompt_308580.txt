At a philosophy society meeting last week, the scepticism session ended with the convenor declaring that the whole problem of the external world was settled decades ago: G. E. Moore simply held up his hand and said here is one hand, and that was the refutation. When I objected that this seemed too quick, he replied that if I could not find a flaw in the proof itself, the burden was on me. I left unsatisfied, because I suspect here is one hand is doing several different jobs at once and that only some of them amount to a refutation. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan might be making — for instance, that it constitutes a formally valid proof with premises more certain than any sceptical premise; that it does not refute scepticism but rather shifts the burden of argument onto the sceptic; that it merely asserts the greater plausibility of common-sense beliefs over the conclusion of any philosophical argument; that it is a performative demonstration that certainty of this sort requires no justification; or that it quietly redefines what would count as a proof so that scepticism loses by stipulation? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false; what a sceptic's best reply would be; and crucially, which of the readings would actually deliver what my convenor needed — namely, a settlement of the external-world problem rather than a refusal to engage with it.
