I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level undergrad seminar on ethics of communication and I'm stuck on the lying vs. misleading question. Act as a moral philosopher who's taught this material for years and graded a few hundred of these exams. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming 2-3 different angles the exam question could take before settling on one — then tell me in a sentence or two why you picked it over the runners-up.

Here's what I need in the end:

1. One essay question, answerable in 75 minutes (call it 900 words). Build it around a pair of vignettes along these lines, which you can tighten up as needed: Sam is a financial advisor who, in a rush to close a deal, flatly tells a client "this fund has never lost money" while believing it has. Alex, also a financial advisor, never says anything he believes false — but spends two weeks scripting a pitch made entirely of true claims ("it's up 40% this year," said of a fund he knows is deep in the red over five years) engineered so the client walks away believing the fund is safe. Both clients invest and lose the same amount. When confronted, Alex's defense is: I never lied to her.

The question has to force more than a recital of the standard definitions (lying = asserting what you believe false with intent to deceive; misleading = asserting truths while implicating falsehoods). The crux I want them to hit: is the fact that Alex "never lied" itself morally significant, and can the standard explanations of why lying is supposed to be worse — the speaker vouches for what's asserted, whereas the misled hearer draws the fatal inference herself and so shares the blame — survive a case where the inference is meticulously engineered and invited? Ideally the question also pushes them to notice that the vignettes confound the lie/mislead variable with impulsiveness vs. premeditation, and to say what a properly controlled comparison would look like.

2. A model answer of about 600 words that would get full marks.

3. A marking scheme out of 25, built around exactly five must-hit points: what each point is, how many marks it's worth, and a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker students fumble it (e.g. defining lying as "intending to deceive," full stop, which collapses all misleading into lying).

Two more things. Make sure the scheme seperates answers that defend an actual position with arguments from answers that just sort the two cases by gut intuition — I want it visible how a B answer differs from an A answer in terms of specific moves made or missing. And at the very end, add a short section called "What would change my mind" where you say which argument or counterexample would lead you to build the exam around a different crux instead, and where you think your own model answer is most likely to be contestable.
