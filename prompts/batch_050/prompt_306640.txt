I'm a PhD student in philosophy with a focus on formal epistemology, and I'm TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on self-locating belief this term. The instructor has asked me to draft the final exam's long essay question, and I want your help. The course covered the Sleeping Beauty problem, the Doomsday argument, and the dispute between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), including Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment as an alleged reductio of SIA. My problem, based on grading the midterm, is that students have memorized the choreography -- halfers versus thirders, "your existence is evidence for more observers" -- but freeze the moment they're asked to derive anything. On the midterm, almost no one could state either assumption precisely, and several students "refuted" SIA by calling the Presumptuous Philosopher absurd without explaining what exactly it licenses you to conclude from the armchair, or why that should trouble a Bayesian who thinks learning that you exist is genuinely new evidence.

I'd like you to act as an expert in formal epistemology with deep knowledge of the anthropic reasoning literature, and to design one essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the Sleeping Beauty debate. My suggestion for the crux, which you should refine rather than merely copy: embed a fresh scenario directly in the question (so students don't have to recall Bostrom's exact setup) in which two empirically distinguishable cosmological theories differ in the number of observers they contain, and ask candidates to (a) show precisely how SIA shifts credence toward the many-observer theory before any telescopes are consulted, (b) evaluate whether this constitutes a reductio of SIA or merely a surprising consequence, and (c) weigh it against SSA's own costs -- its dependence on a reference class that no one has non-arbitrarily specified, and its licensing of Doomsday-style reasoning. The distinguishing move I most want to test is whether a candidate can articulate what a principled restriction on either assumption (say, applying indifference only within a possible world, or only over minimal observer-moments) would have to accomplish to avoid both horns, and whether they notice that the thirder/halfer labels and the SIA/SSA labels don't line up one-to-one.

Please think carefully about assessment design before writing; I'd encourage you to first brainstorm briefly about what separates genuine understanding here from recitation, then produce the deliverables in this order: (1) the exam question itself, worded so that a prepared student could answer it in 75 minutes without needing to recall any specific text's details, since all needed scenarios must be stated in the question; (2) a model answer of roughly 650 words written at the level of a strong undergraduate, naming positions and their proponents only insofar as natural (no citations or quotations required); and (3) a marking scheme built around exactly five must-hit moves, with marks assigned to each out of a total of 25, a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates botch each move, and a final line stating concretely what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer. Where the marking scheme anticipates errors, they should be errors students actually commit -- for instance, treating SIA as identical to the thirder position, claiming the armchair shift violates conservation of evidence, or presenting the Doomsday argument as a literal prediction rather than a consequence of SSA's confirmation structure.
