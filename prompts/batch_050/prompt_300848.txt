I've been reading population ethics stuff on LessWrong and adjacent blogs for years, and the Parfit-style nonidentity cases finally broke my brain this week after a friend and I got into it over whether having kids young and broke is selfish. So I did the obvious thing and tabulated my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. I want to know what principle I'm apparently committed to, because I can't construct one that doesn't overturn at least one verdict I care about. Cases, numbered so you can refer to them:

1. A couple are both on a medication where, if they conceive this month, the kid will have a lifelong but genuinely mild condition -- say a limp and some chronic aches. If they wait four months until they're off it, they conceive a different, healthy kid. They conceive now anyway. Gut: that's wrong. Mildly, but really wrong.
2. A woman is already pregnant and can take a free, risk-free pill that cures the exact same condition in the fetus she has. She declines, for no particular reason. Gut: this is way worse than case 1 -- even though the outcome for whoever ends up existing is identical, one kid with a limp either way.
3. A couple has a kid now at 24, broke, fully aware that if they waited ten years they'd have a different kid who'd grow up with money, tutoring, stability. Gut: completely fine, nobody's business but theirs. But when I stare at it, it's structurally identical to case 1 -- either kid A exists with a disadvantage or kid B exists without it, and A can't complain about a choice that gave him the only life he could have had. I refuse to give this verdict up, though, and I also refuse to give up 1, so.
4. A legislature picks a cheap energy plan over a pricey one; the predictable effect is that the generation born ~60 years from now has somewhat worse health across the board. Because the policy also changes who meets and marries whom, those future people wouldn't have existed under the pricey plan. Gut: still wrong, and roughly as wrong as if the same policy harmed people who'd exist either way.
5. A couple decides not to have a third kid who, by their own estimate, would have had a great life. Gut: not wrong at all, not even a little, and any principle that says otherwise is disqualifying.
6. The one I keep flip-flopping on: conceive naturally now and the kid has a serious condition -- deafness plus significant chronic pain -- but still a life clearly worth living; or wait for screening and have a different healthy kid instead. Some mornings I think it's just case 1 with the dial turned up. Other mornings I think the severity means the kid himself has a complaint, somehow.

What I want: a principle (or two or three candidates, if no single one wins) stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work for which case, and what it predicts for case 6 plus one case I haven't listed. Be honest about which of my numbered verdicts your best principle has to sacrifice -- I'd rather drop a verdict than adopt a principle with one epicycle per case. Also, please keep separate what's basically agreed among people who work on this versus what's genuinely contested (person-affecting vs. impersonal views, the procreation asymmetry, all that), and don't present your preferred population ethics as the consensus when it isn't. Very last line, formatted as \answer{...}: a one-sentence statement of the principle you'd endorse, and the numbers of any of my verdicts it forces me to give up (or "none").
