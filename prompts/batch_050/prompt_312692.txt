Earlier this year I became the managing director of a regional trade association of about forty small residential construction firms, and the job has turned me into an unwilling student of multipolar traps. The dynamics are textbook: every member would be better off if nobody bid below cost, cut corners on safety, or poached trained apprentices from competitors who paid to train them. Each firm, however, gains by doing exactly that, clients have learned to play us against one another, and new entrants who never agreed to anything arrive every season. Moloch, in the Ginsberg-derived sense that has become standard in discussions of coordination failure, is eating my industry, and I now have some ability to do something about it: the association controls a discretionary fund large enough to support roughly three researchers for two years, and the board has asked me to commission conceptual and theoretical work -- game theory, political philosophy, institutional design at the level of ideas rather than case studies of our particular sector.

I need a defended research agenda. Which open questions about multipolar traps and races to the bottom should this fund prioritize, and in what order? I am less interested in the list itself than in the reasoning behind the ordering: for each question, why it is upstream of other work rather than downstream of it, how tractable it is for theorists as opposed to empirical researchers, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what findings over the next couple of years would move it up or down the ranking. Where your criteria conflict -- say, a question that is highly important but only weakly tractable, or one that is neglected because it is probably a dead end -- tell me how you weigh the trade-off rather than concealing it.

To keep the answer from being a survey of the usual talking points, here are three framings I have been turning over, which I would like you to engage directly. First, I suspect traps should be distinguished by where the ratchet bites: in prices, in attention and working hours, or in the externalization of risk onto third parties who never consented -- and that the right escape mechanism differs across these. Second, I suspect the binding constraint in any given trap is one of three distinct things -- reaching agreement on what to do, detecting and punishing defection, or absorbing new entrants -- and that a great deal of coordination talk fails by treating these as interchangeable. Third, I have a nagging worry that partial coalitions (like my association) do not dissolve traps so much as relocate them, pushing the race to the bottom onto non-members, suppliers, or future entrants. For each framing: does it survive scrutiny, and if so, what does it imply for what my fund should support?

Finally, a note on what would actually help me: be frank about which widely discussed implications of multipolar-trap thinking are overrated or simply do not follow, since I will be tempted by them. The deliverable is a defended agenda for a specific funder with a specific constraint, not a literature overview and not a reading list. Please write the answer in American English.
