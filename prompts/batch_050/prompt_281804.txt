I am preparing the final examination for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I would like you to draft one essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The topic is the Judy Benjamin problem — the puzzle about how an agent should update her credences when what she learns is a conditional probability rather than a categorical proposition. Students will have 75 minutes and the question will be worth 25 marks.

The question must not be answerable by recounting van Fraassen's original formulation of the case (the soldier, the radio message, the quadrants) and reporting his preferred answer. I want it to force students to do three harder things: state precisely what the learning event is and why it resists treatment by ordinary conditionalization; take a position, with an argument, on whether the agent's credence in the antecedent should change, comparing at least two update policies that deliver different verdicts; and handle a follow-up twist that probes whether the first update was a genuine belief revision or merely the adoption of a constraint. Specifically, I would like the question to embed this invented variant: Judy's region is divided into four equal quadrants (Red-North, Red-South, Blue-North, Blue-South), her prior is uniform, and she receives a message to the effect that the probability she is in the north, conditional on being in the red region, is 3/4. Parts (a) and (b) proceed as above. Part (c) should then add: a second message arrives stating that the first message was transmitted in error and should be disregarded — should Judy return to her uniform prior, and what does the answer reveal about the status she accorded the first message? Part (d) should ask students to connect the case to the general distinction between learning a conditional and learning that a conditional probability takes a certain value, and to say which of the two the radio message is best understood as, and why the difference matters for the update.

Please then supply:

1. A model answer of roughly 650 words, written at the level of a strong candidate. It should give the posterior distribution the candidate endorses after the first message (with the numbers worked out), and it should at minimum engage: the intuition that the message contains no information bearing on Red versus Blue, so that the credence in Red should remain 1/2; the worry that some update rules do shift the antecedent's probability, and what motivates such rules; and the retraction twist in part (c), where the model answer should acknowledge that simply reversing the update is not obviously correct — whether the prior is restored depends on whether the first update was a minimal revision (in which case the old credences may be recoverable only as a memory, not as a consequence of the second message) and on what the second message is evidence about.

2. A marking scheme allocating the 25 marks across five components, with a short description of what earns full marks in each. For each component, add one sentence naming the characteristic way weaker candidates fail it — for example, treating the message as a proposition that can be straightforwardly conditionalized on, asserting rather than arguing that the credence in Red must stay at 1/2, or claiming in part (c) that Judy obviously returns to her prior without noticing that this requires a substantive assumption about the nature of the first update.

3. A final paragraph explaining, for the benefit of the other graders, what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer: name the specific moves an A answer must contain that a competent B answer will typically lack. The intention is that the A/B boundary should track genuine understanding of the difference between revising on a constraint and conditionalizing on evidence, not fluency with the literature.
