Airline pilots must requalify in a flight simulator every few months for as long as they fly: a captain with thirty years' experience who mishandles a simulated engine failure loses the license, no matter their record and no matter their scores on written tests. Financial advisors, by contrast, are certified on written examinations alone, with no test of what they actually do when sitting with a client. Treat these as deliberate institutional choices rather than accidents of history. What view of the relation between knowing how and knowing that does the aviation practice presuppose -- that skilled flying is a kind of knowledge no written test could in principle detect, or merely a kind we happen to test best in simulators? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the two licensing regimes can rest on one consistent account of knowledge, and describe what pilot licensing would have to look like if knowing how were really just a species of knowing that.
