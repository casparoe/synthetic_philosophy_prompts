I have firm intuitions about which situations are genuine moral dilemmas — cases where, whatever the agent does, she acts wrongly — and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them.

Case 1: A lifeboat officer can seal one of two flooding compartments, saving five people in one or one person in the other, but not both. Sealing the five's compartment is simply right; no dilemma.

Case 2: A parent has one dose of medicine and two children who will each die without it; the dose cannot be split or shared. Whichever child gets it, the parent has done something gravely wrong, and some residual guilt would be appropriate afterward. A genuine dilemma.

Case 3: I have carelessly made two equally weighty promises for the same evening. Inconvenient, but no dilemma: I should keep whichever promise matters more and apologize and make amends to the other party, which fully clears the ledger.

Case 4: A captor credibly threatens to kill ten hostages unless I kill one myself. I am convinced I must refuse and that the ten deaths would be his crime, not mine — yet I notice this verdict feels far shakier than the others, and part of me thinks refusing would be monstrous.

Case 5: A judge can avert a deadly riot only by framing an innocent person. The judge must not frame him; this is not a dilemma, just a terrible situation with a right answer.

Is there a principle that captures these verdicts — in particular, one that explains why Case 2 is a dilemma while the structurally similar Case 1 is not, and that makes sense of my combining residual guilt with blamelessness in Case 2? Please state your candidate principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and give its verdict on one case I have not considered. No more than 300 words.
