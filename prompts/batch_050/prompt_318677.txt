I produce a philosophy of science podcast, and my upcoming guest is a philosopher known for defending the deflationary view of thought experiments: that a thought experiment is nothing more than an ordinary argument dressed up in narrative form, and that it can yield knowledge only insofar as it can be reconstructed as a deductive or inductive argument from premises we already have independent reason to accept. The view is typically pressed against cases like Galileo's falling bodies, Stevin's chain draped over the double inclined plane, and Einstein's elevator.

I want to avoid questions that merely invite him to restate the position. Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each followed by a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give. The set must include at least the following:

- One question on the reconstruction demand. For any given thought experiment there seem to be several equally legitimate reconstructions, and the verdict can flip depending on which one is chosen. How does he determine which reconstruction is the "real" one without circularly selecting whichever delivers the conclusion he antecedently favors?

- One question on Galileo's falling bodies. The reasoning there appears to require, as a premise, that speed of fall is not fixed by weight — which looks uncomfortably close to the conclusion itself. If the thought experiment only works by smuggling in its conclusion, must the deflationary view say Galileo learned nothing from it? Is that a reductio of the view or a bullet he is willing to bite?

- One question on novelty. The view seems to imply that a thought experiment can never tell us anything we did not in some sense already know, since the conclusion must follow from antecedently accepted premises. How does he account for the felt surprise of the classic cases, and for the historical fact that thought experiments have genuinely changed scientists' minds?

- One question on the rival picture of thought experiments as mental simulations or quasi-experiments that draw on tacit, embodied know-how unavailable as explicit premises. What does he say about cases where someone reliably reaches the right verdict from a thought experiment yet cannot state the premises any reconstructed argument would require?

- One question on the elevator case, where the operative background premise — the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass — is itself empirical. If thought experiments may lean on empirical premises, in what sense were they ever a priori, and does the deflationary view collapse into the uninteresting claim that scientists sometimes reason?

- One closing question asking what case or evidence, if produced, would persuade him that the deflationary view is false — and if none could, whether the view is doing explanatory work or merely redescribing.

Assume the guest has heard every standard objection and is practiced at deflecting them; where a deflection is predictable, build the follow-up around it rather than around the objection itself. The tone should be collegial but persistent, and the ordering should make it progressively harder for him to retreat to the formula that every successful thought experiment can in principle be reconstructed — I want him pressed on what "in principle" is doing in that sentence.
