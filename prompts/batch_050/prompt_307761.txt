I help run a weekend mathematics circle for 14 students aged 15 to 17. Last week, after a brief introduction to set theory, one student asked whether 2 is an element of 4. My answer—that one standard construction makes this true, another makes it false, and ordinary arithmetic appears indifferent to the difference—left several students thinking that arithmetic itself must be arbitrary or merely conventional. I would like a 75-minute classroom game that lets them discover the structuralist response themselves before I use that term or explain any particular construction of the natural numbers.

Please design a runnable activity in which teams construct the same finite relational network from materially different tokens, establish a precise translation between their networks, and then encounter questions that the translation cannot answer. At least one representation should make the analogue of 2 being contained in 4 true, while another makes it false, even though both preserve exactly the same arithmetical relations. The activity should distinguish questions about a position within one representation from questions about whether objects occupying corresponding positions in different representations are literally identical. Give the exact setup, materials, team roles, rules, scoring or small nonmonetary stakes, a minute-by-minute timeline, and examples of the questions participants should be asked at each stage. There should be no lecture beforehand, and participants should have to develop the relevant distinctions through play rather than simply being told that only structure matters.

Also design the final debrief. It should connect particular moments in the game to three ideas: some facts are determinate within an implementation, corresponding positions can be translated without the underlying objects being identical, and ordinary arithmetic may concern positions in a structure rather than the intrinsic constitution of those positions. Explain how to keep the discussion from collapsing into the claims that anything goes or that mathematical objects are merely physical tokens. Tell me what is most likely to go wrong the first time I run the game and how to respond. Finally, be candid about which features of the full identity problem a finite classroom network cannot reproduce, especially the infinity of the natural numbers and the possibility that there may still be facts about mathematical objects beyond their structural relations.
