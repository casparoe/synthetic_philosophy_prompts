So I'm a longtime LessWrong/ACX reader, and my little philosophy reading group just spent three weeks on semantic externalism -- Putnam's Twin Earth stuff, Burge's social externalism, the usual hits. I came out of it with a grab-bag of gut verdicts that don't obviously hang together, and I want to know if there's one principle underneath them. Please play the role of a philosophy of language professor who knows this literature cold. Think carefully, brainstorm two or three candidate principles before committing to one, and write your answer in American English.

Here are my verdicts. (1) Classic Twin Earth: Oscar's twin on a planet where the clear drinkable liquid is XYZ, both of them living before anyone knew chemistry -- I think the twin's word "water" just doesn't mean water, it means XYZ. (2) Burge's arthritis case: the guy who thinks he has arthritis in his thigh -- I think he has a false belief about arthritis, not a true belief about some thigh-disease he privately means by "arthritis." Community wins. (3) Jade, which turned out to be two different minerals, jadeite and nephrite: I think nobody was wrong and the word just turned out to cover both. (4) Madagascar: the name apparently used to pick out part of the African mainland and got transferred to the island by mistake, and I think the reference genuinely shifted -- the island IS Madagascar, we shouldn't say everyone is still mistaken. (5) One stubborn guy who consistently calls beeches "elms": I think he's just wrong, his "elm" still means elm, he can't shift reference alone. (6) Slow switching: if I were secretly transported to Twin Earth in my sleep and lived there for thirty years, my "water" would EVENTUALLY come to mean XYZ, though not on day one.

Now here's my problem: my tentative principle was something like "reference goes to whatever natural kind is causally responsible for most of a community's use of the term," but (4) and (5) break it in opposite directions -- Polo's mistake rewrote the reference for everyone, while the beech guy's identical-looking mistake did nothing, and (2) says individual usage defers to experts while (4) says experts' usage got dragged along by one traveler's error. Is there a cleaner principle that saves most of these, and which of my verdicts would I have to give up to get real simplicity? Also, tell me what your best principle predicts for two cases I didn't list: (a) "Vulcan," the planet astronomers posited to explain Mercury's orbit that turned out not to exist -- does the name refer to nothing at all, and does that fit or clash with the jade verdict, and (b) a language model trained entirely on text with no causal contact with actual water -- does its "water" refer to H2O, to nothing, or to something weirder? I care less about which famous philosopher endorses what and more about getting the principle itself stated precisly.
