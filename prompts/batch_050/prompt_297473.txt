I TA for a stats-for-social-science course, and last week I was helping my roommate (she's in the class) with Simpson's paradox. I gave her the standard lesson: a trend can hold in every subgroup and reverse when you pool the data, so "always look at the disaggregated table." Then she asked the obvious question I didn't have a good answer to: ok, but *which* table do you actually trust when they disagree -- the split one or the overall one? I mumbled something about confounding and realized I was mostly just pattern-matching to examples I'd seen. To make this concrete, here's the toy example I made up for her problem set: a new drug is given to 1010 patients and another 1050 patients go without it. Among mild cases, 9 of 10 patients on the drug recover (90%) versus 850 of 1000 without it (85%). Among severe cases, 300 of 1000 on the drug recover (30%) versus 10 of 50 without it (20%). So the drug wins in both subgroups, but overall it's 309 of 1010 (about 31%) on the drug against 860 of 1050 (about 82%) without. My roommate's follow-up was: same numbers, but now tell me three different stories. Story 1: mild/severe is the patient's condition at intake, and doctors tend to give the drug to the severe cases. Story 2: mild/severe is measured two weeks *into* treatment, and the drug itself sometimes moves patients into the "severe" category temporarily. Story 3: the study only includes patients who showed up for a follow-up appointment, and both being on the drug and feeling lousy independently affect whether you bother showing up.

What I want is an explanation, in American English, aimed at someone comfortable with basic probability but new to causal inference, of why the *same table of numbers* rationally demands opposite conclusions depending on which story is true. Walk through each of my three stories using my actual numbers: in story 1 why pooling misleads you, in story 2 why splitting misleads you, and in story 3 what's going wrong with the split table even though the reversal looks identical to story 1. Then state the general rule that tells you which table to trust -- I suspect it has to be a rule about the causal role of the third variable rather than anything you can read off the numbers themselves, but I've never seen it stated crisply, and I want it stated crisply. Finally, the part I definately can't answer myself: is there a setup where you know the full causal story -- every arrow, no uncertainty about the structure -- and *neither* the split table nor the pooled table gives you the number you actually want? If yes, give me the simplest such setup you can construct; if no, explain why the two tables exhaust the possibilities.

After the prose explanation, give me a summary table with exactly these columns: Story | Causal role of the mild/severe variable | Table to trust (split or pooled) | What goes wrong if you pick the wrong one | One-sentence intuition. Keep the table tight; the real work should be in the prose.
