I'm a PhD student in political theory working on the grounds of political legitimacy, and I keep running up against a configuration in the literature on East and Southeast Asian governance — Singapore and China are the standard exemplars — that I cannot find a clean term for. I would like to know whether it has one, and if it doesn't, what the right way to carve it is.

The configuration is this. A government claims the right to rule not on the basis of how it was selected but on the basis of what it delivers: growth, order, public health, housing, longevity. The delivery is real — this matters, since I am not describing states running on fabricated statistics. Citizens, insofar as anyone can tell, genuinely endorse the arrangement, and they cite the outcomes when they do. So far this is a familiar position, and I know the broad strokes of the debate over whether results can substitute for procedure.

The feature I cannot find treated on its own is the loop that closes the configuration. The same government whose legitimacy rests on demonstrated performance also controls the channels through which performance can be assessed: the press, the statistical agencies, the range of permitted comparisons. And the politically relevant question — would an alternative arrangement have delivered as much, or more — is a counterfactual that can only be investigated using information the incumbent either suppresses or supplies. So the citizens' endorsement is rational given the evidence available to them, but the evidence available to them is curated by the very agent whose standing the endorsement is supposed to certify. The competitor also writes the scoreboard and bans the replay officials.

Two instances of the shape, stated abstractly. First: a long-ruling party points to home ownership rates, life expectancy, and per-capita income as its mandate; independent verification of those figures requires access that only the party can grant, and outsiders who publish unflattering comparisons tend to lose that access. Second: a government weathers a severe economic shock that, by its own stated theory of its right to rule, ought to count heavily against it — but the public debate that would register the failure cannot take place, so the theory of legitimacy is never actually subjected to the test it itself proposes. Note that in the second case the loop protects the regime even from its own criterion.

What I want to know: is there an established name for this closed loop — legitimacy grounded in outcomes whose measurement and comparison the claimant controls — as distinct from the general idea of legitimacy-through-results? The nearest concepts I can find each capture only a face of it. Legitimacy by outputs names the general claim but not the loop. Preference falsification concerns private beliefs diverging from public statements, whereas here the beliefs may be entirely sincere. Adaptive preferences concern desires adjusting to deprivation, whereas these citizens are not deprived of the goods in question — they are deprived of the comparison class. If none of these, or anything else, fits, I would be grateful for a coined term with a definition precise enough to settle boundary cases: does the loop obtain if the state's control of information is partial rather than total? If citizens could in principle obtain the information from foreign sources but do not bother? If the outcomes are so strong that the counterfactual is implausible anyway, is the loop still doing normative work, or is it then merely an idle worry? Please do not just hand me a label — what I need is the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
