Next month I'm giving a talk to the epistemology reading group at my department, and I'm genuinely nervous about the question period. My paper defends a deliberately unfashionable answer to Plato's question in the Meno about why knowledge is worth more than mere true belief: I argue that the whole surplus value of knowledge reduces to modal stability, i.e., to the fact that knowledge, unlike lucky true belief, would still have been true in nearby possible circumstances. My hook is an insurance analogy: just as an insurance policy is worth more than a lucky escape even when both leave you equally unharmed in the actual world, knowledge is worth more than accidentally true belief even when both get you to your destination, because knowledge was covered against the error scenarios that true belief merely happened to avoid. I know the standard objections here — Zagzebski's point that a good espresso isn't worth more for having come from a reliable machine, the knowledge-first crowd's insistence that the direction of explanation runs the other way, and the complaint that my "nearby circumstances" talk smuggles in a safety condition that does all the work without explaining anything — but I suspect this particular audience will press angles I haven't anticipated. The group includes one committed virtue epistemologist who thinks agent-reliability has intrinsic credit value, one Williamson-influenced knowledge-firster, a formal epistemologist who will want my insurance analogy cashed out in expected-value terms, and three sharp grad students who've been reading the swamping problem literature.

Please write me a prep sheet for the question period. List the objections in roughly the order I should expect them, phrased the way each of these people would actually phrase them (the knowledge-firster will ask why I'm explaining the value of knowledge in terms of belief at all; the formal epistemologist will ask what probability distribution does the work in my analogy). For each objection: what I should concede outright, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. Include at least one objection that turns on a hard case rather than on general principle — e.g., whether my view entails that a mathematically proven but never-used true belief is worth no more than a lucky guess that happens never to be tested. End with the single question I'm hoping nobody asks, and one sentence on what I should say if they do. Maximum 900 words.
