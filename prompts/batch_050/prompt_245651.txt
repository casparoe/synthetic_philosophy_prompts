I co-host a small AI-and-philosophy podcast, and next month's guest just published a book arguing that inverse reinforcement learning is hopeless as a route to human values: watching someone's behavior, even forever, can never pin down what they want, because the same behavior is compatible with endlessly many reward functions, including absurd ones. She's careful and leans heavily on the identifiability results, so "more data" bounces right off her.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that make her do more than restate the underdetermination thesis. I want, at minimum:

- One question on the shaping point (any observed policy is optimal for infinitely many rewards), with a follow-up for when she gives the example of a reward that's just "a constant" or "whatever this policy maximizes."
- One question pressing the standard comeback -- put a simplicity prior over reward functions and vary the environment -- with a follow-up asking whether the prior is doing all the real work, i.e. whether we've inferred preferences or just assumed them.
- One question on the belief/desire swap problem: the same choice is explainable by odd beliefs plus normal desires or vice versa, so which are we supposed to be learning? Include a follow-up for her likely reply that we should just fix the human's beliefs as known -- with humans we can't, and their self-reports are just more behavior.
- One question on systematic irrationality (hyperbolic discounting, framing effects) and whether "model the bias, then invert" rescues the project or just relocates the underdetermination into the bias model.
- And a closing question about what evidence or result could actually change her mind, since I suspect her position is closer to unfalsifiable than she admits.

For each question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, not the answer I'd hope for.
