Longtime LW reader, occasional lit-forum lurker. So I got into an argument in a comment thread where someone was doing a close reading of a novel, the actual author showed up (or someone linked her interview) saying no, that's not what I meant at all, and three people instantly replied "death of the author" like that settled it. I've seen this move a dozen times and I honestly can't tell what claim it's making, and it smells like an applause light that does different work in different mouths.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could stand for? From the near-trivial (authors can be mistaken or uninsightful about their own work; you don't need to ask permission to interpret) through the moderate (once published, the text plus public context fixes meaning and private intention is just more evidence, not a trump card) up to the radical (intentions are constitutively irrelevant, meaning is whatever readers make of it). For each reading: is it defensible, and does it actually support the standard move of telling someone their reading can't be wrong even when the author says so? And stress-test the survivors against cases where intention clearly does work — promises, legal texts, coded messages — to see whether the slogan only survives by quietly restricting itself to 'literary interpretation' in some sense that needs spelling out.
