I sit on the five-member board of our regional water authority (quasi-public, we manage reservoirs and flood control for a county of about a million people). In three weeks we vote on whether to sign a roughly $4M/year contract with a startup that sells hyperlocal precipitation and river-level forecasts 6 to 18 months out. Their pitch leans hard on physics: the atmosphere is a deterministic system governed by known equations, so the future weather is in principle already fixed — the only bottleneck is compute and data, and they claim thier new solver plus proprietary satellite feeds cracks it. Their CEO literally said the field has been held back by "small thinking about chaos."

I'm the board's token nerd (longtime LW/SSC reader) and this trips every alarm I have. I thought the whole point of chaos was that determinism does NOT buy you predictability: tiny errors in your initial measurements grow exponentially, so a deterministic model still can't tell you about a specific day months out. But I can't fully close the argument, and some counterpoints nag at me. El Niño forecasts genuinely have skill months ahead. Climate models say useful things about distributions decades out while refusing to say whether it rains on a particular Tuesday. Tides are predicted forever out, though that's astronomy not weather. So maybe "deterministic system" licenses some kinds of long-range claims but not others, and the contract is conveniently vague about which kind they're actually selling. Also nagging me: if they're overselling, the market punishes them eventually — but we have to decide before the budget locks.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. Requirements: bottom line up front with a concrete recommendation (sign / don't sign / sign a restructured version — e.g., pay only for forecast products whose skill survives a blind hindcast trial we design, with benchmarks specified). Steelman the company's case first: take seriously the strongest version of "determinism + data + compute = long-range skill," then identify exactly where it breaks, if it does, and be careful to separate what chaos rules out in principle from what it merely makes expensive. The memo should also give the board a decision procedure for future vendors, since this won't be the last pitch like this.

Finally: tell me what would change the memo's verdict. What demonstration or argument from the company would flip a don't-sign into a sign, and where is the memo's own reasoning most likely to fail — i.e., which step are you least confident in?
