I am preparing a nine-month postdoctoral fellowship application in moral and political philosophy. My provisional project concerns whether the simulation hypothesis should reduce confidence in strongly future-oriented ethical views, especially views according to which enormous numbers of possible future lives can dominate present-day moral decisions. My suspicion is that the usual response—simply multiplying the value of the far future by some probability that we are not simulated—misses the deeper problem. What matters may not be the probability of simulation by itself, but whether inhabitants of a simulation can causally affect, predict, or morally count beings outside it, and whether simulated persons have the same aggregation-relevant status as non-simulated persons.

Act as a careful philosopher with expertise in decision theory, population ethics, and the simulation argument. First think through the strongest competing framings, then write a polished research proposal of no more than 1,000 words. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review; I will supply references later.

The proposal should:

1. State one precise research question. It should not ask merely whether longtermism is true, nor whether the simulation argument is sound. A promising focus would be how different simulation possibilities interact with three assumptions often left separate: that future persons count equally wherever they are located, that expected value can aggregate very small probabilities of very large populations, and that our actions now can influence those populations.

2. Explain why existing positions leave the question open. Address at least the obvious skeptical conclusion that simulation risk discounts the far future, the obvious inflationary conclusion that simulated worlds might increase the number of affected persons, and the more subtle possibility that simulation uncertainty changes the structure rather than merely the size of the expected-value calculation.

3. Develop a tractable angle. I am especially interested in distinguishing causal containment from moral containment: a civilization may be unable to affect beings outside its simulation even if those beings can affect it, and this asymmetry might matter for obligations to create, preserve, or improve future simulated lives. Assess whether this distinction genuinely constrains fanatical conclusions or simply relocates them.

4. Describe a nine-month work plan with three phases, including the construction of a small number of explicit toy decision models. The models should be used to clarify concepts and identify decision-relevant variables, not to pretend that we can estimate the actual probability that we are simulated.

5. Specify the intended deliverable and what would count as success. A successful project need not produce a verdict for or against longtermism; it might instead yield conditions under which simulation uncertainty dampens, amplifies, or leaves unchanged fanaticism about the far future.

6. State what would count as failure and what would change your mind. Identify the strongest consideration that would show the proposed causal-containment/moral-containment distinction is irrelevant, incoherent, or already settled, and say where the argument is most likely to break down.

Use concise headings and maintain a suitably skeptical tone: defend the project’s importance without overstating its likely conclusions.
