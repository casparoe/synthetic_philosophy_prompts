Here's roughly where I've landed on preference learning for AI systems, and I'd like an audit before I commit to it in a grant proposal. I hold: (1) Human preferences can, at least in principle, be inferred from observed behavior; this is what makes inverse reinforcement learning a coherent research program rather than an elaborate way of guessing. (2) Modeling humans as noisy reward-maximizers — mostly choosing the best option, with errors that are random and unsystematic — is a decent working approximation of actual choice. (3) Human choices are riddled with framing effects, default effects, and preference reversals, so raw behavior needs to be debiased against a standard of rationality before it counts as evidence about what someone values. (4) When a person's behavior and their stated values conflict, the behavior wins; stated values are cheap talk and carry little evidential weight. (5) The whole point of inferring preferences rather than stipulating them is to avoid building the designer's own values into the system. Some of these seem to pull against each other, but I can't tell which conflicts are real and which merely look that way. Identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which dissolve on inspection, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one tell me which commitment is cheapest to give up or qualify and what that revision costs the overall view. Be specific about what (2) and (3) each assume about the structure of human error, since I suspect that's where the trouble is.
