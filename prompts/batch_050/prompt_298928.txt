Two graduate students in my department, Mina and Jonas, got into a running argument after a seminar and have now deputized me to find them a verdict, which I don't feel qualified to give. The argument started over a hypothetical case their instructor had posed: a bioengineer designs a strain of bacteria carrying a "colony circuit" -- if the frequency of freeloading cheater cells within a colony rises past a threshold, the circuit triggers and the entire colony dies. Colonies with the circuit thus keep cheaters rare (cheaters are eliminated along with their neighbors) and outcompete circuit-free colonies, which get overrun by cheaters and collapse. Mina's opening move was that this case shows why gene-level descriptions always suffice: the circuit spreads because copies of it, on average, end up in more descendant cells, and once you can tell the full story in terms of gene frequencies, invoking the colony as a "unit of selection" is redundant bookkeeping -- the colony is at most an environment the genes build for themselves. Jonas replied that this conflates two questions: of course every outcome can be redescribed in gene-frequency terms, but the fitness differences here accrue to whole colonies -- it is colony-level death, not differential replication of cells within colonies, that does the selective work -- so the level at which you get the right causal and predictive story is the colony, and a theorist who insisted on tracking only gene frequencies would miss why the system is stable. Mina's second move was that genuine higher-level selection requires the higher-level entity to reproduce with lineage-forming fidelity, the way genes do, and colonies that simply die or survive are being culled, not selected as reproducers; at best this is a one-generation filtering that kin selection already explains, since colony-mates are close relatives. Jonas countered that "what persists and is copied" and "where fitness variance is sorted" are independent criteria, that demanding replicator-grade fidelity stacks the deck in favor of genes by definition, and that colonies do propagate -- by fission and dispersal -- often enough for heritable colony-level variance to exist across generations; he added that if Mina's kin-selection point were decisive, then by symmetry one could call all individual-level selection "gene selection with correlated bearers" and dissolve the organism as a unit too. Mina's final move was to concede the symmetry but draw the opposite moral: since both descriptions are mathematically intertranslatable, the units-of-selection debate is largely a dispute about narration, and the only real question is which level is most convenient for a given model.

I'd like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves -- Mina's opening reduction, Jonas's "where fitness differences accrue" reply, Mina's lineage-and-reproduction objection, Jonas's two-criteria counter plus his symmetry argument, and Mina's closing dissolution -- say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right-but-limited, and justify the verdict in terms of what the debate's standard positions (gene's-eye views, multilevel selection, replicator/vehicle framings) actually claim rather than caricatures of them. Then tell me who is more right overall about the colony-circuit case specifically, and whether the final "it's all narration" move genuinely dissolves the disagreement or just relabels the live question of which level does the causal work.
