I am a literary novelist, and my forthcoming novel has put me in a position I did not entirely anticipate. The book is called The Pasewalk Ward, and it is a counterfactual: in October 1918, the British gas attack in Flanders that in fact left a Bavarian dispatch runner temporarily blinded in a military hospital kills him instead. The novel then follows the next twenty-five years of a Germany without Hitler: the humiliation of defeat, the stab-in-the-back legend, hyperinflation, mass unemployment, and the rise, in Hitler's absence, of a völkisch mass movement led by my invented protagonist — a gassed veteran and gifted demagogue who consolidates the radical right, strangles the republic, re-arms, and by the early 1940s presides over a war of conquest in the east and the systematic murder of Europe's Jews. The novel's wager, which I state outright in a narrator's afterword (my editor calls it the part that will get me into trouble), is that Hitler was the executant and accelerator of a catastrophe whose enabling conditions were structural: remove the man and you remove the dates, the style, perhaps the pace — but not the outcome. I spent three years in the archives for this book, so I am not naive about the history, but I am also not a historian, and I do not intend to impersonate one.

I have now been invited to speak about the novel at a university history department's colloquium on the Weimar Republic and the Third Reich — not a literary festival, where the book has been received warmly, but a room of working historians: two or three senior scholars of Weimar political culture, a historian of the Holocaust, at least one biographer of Hitler, and a phalanx of graduate students, with a few literature faculty scattered in. They will be courteous but not indulgent. Several will regard a novelist's counterfactual as methodologically suspect on its face, and I gather that this particular field, whatever its internal disputes, leans toward the view that the Holocaust in its historical form is not something you get by subtracting one man. I have twenty minutes to talk about fiction as an instrument of counterfactual reasoning, followed by a long and, I expect, pointed question period.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I want the eight or so objections I should actually expect from this audience — not the ones a general-interest festival crowd asks — ordered by how much damage each would do to my position if handled badly, weighting for how likely each is to come up. Phrase each objection the way the historian most likely to raise it would phrase it, and tell me who in that room that probably is. Give me, for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes that does not pretend to expertise I lack, and then tell me candidly where that reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Finally, tell me where I can retreat gracefully: which parts of the book's wager — dictatorship, war, genocide — I can concede contingency on without surrendering its core claim, and where the honest move is to defer to the scholarship rather than argue, since I would rather cede a point than be caught bluffing in front of the people who wrote the books I cited.
