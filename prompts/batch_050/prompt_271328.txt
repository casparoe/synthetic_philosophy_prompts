I am a philosophy professor chairing my department's undergraduate grade-appeals committee, and I need a decision memo I can bring to the committee for a vote.

The case: in my colleague's Philosophy of Mathematics course, an exam asked, "Is the number 2 an element of the number 4? Explain your answer." A student answered yes, reasoning that on the standard von Neumann construction of the natural numbers as ordinals, each number is the set of all smaller numbers, so 2 = {0, 1} is a member of 4 = {0, 1, 2, 3}. The instructor marked the answer wrong, writing that numbers are not sets at all, that the von Neumann construction is merely one convenient representation among others (Zermelo's alternative identifies the numbers differently and yields different membership facts), and that the question concerns the numbers themselves rather than artifacts of a set-theoretic coding. The student appeals, arguing that the claim is true on the construction the course itself used throughout the semester.

Draft a decision memo for the committee, maximum 600 words. It should open with a clear bottom-line recommendation (uphold the grade, overturn it, or award partial credit) and then give the reasoning, which must engage the philosophical substance rather than hiding behind deference to the instructor: whether "true relative to a construction" is good enough when the question was asked without qualification, what follows from the fact that rival, equally legitimate constructions give different answers, and whether a working mathematician's ordinary use of "the natural numbers" presupposes a single object with determinate set-membership properties at all. Whatever you recommend, include the strongest argument for the losing side and say why it does not prevail, since the memo will circulate to both parties.
