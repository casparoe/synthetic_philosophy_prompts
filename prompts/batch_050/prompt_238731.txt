Last week my decision theory reading group did the two classic Omega setups back to back and it ended in an actual argument, so I want to see the disagreement played out properly. The two cases: (1) counterfactual mugging -- Omega flips a fair coin, and because it came up heads Omega now asks you for $1000, having committed beforehand that in the tails branch it would have given you $10000 iff you would have paid in the heads branch; (2) XOR blackmail -- Omega perfectly predicts whether you'd pay $100 upon receiving a certain nasty letter about a possible looming disaster, and sends you the letter exactly when the disaster happening and your being-a-payer "disagree" (disaster and you'd pay -> letter; disaster and you'd refuse -> no letter; no disaster and you'd pay -> no letter; no disaster and you'd refuse -> letter).

Write a dialogue between two people from the group, Maya and Jon, both sharp and both familiar with updateless/functional decision theory ideas. Maya is the "lumper": she thinks the two cases are structurally the same shape -- a perfect predictor conditions money-stuff on a counterfactual about your policy -- and that the whole lesson of one-boxing and paying the mugger is that you take the subjunctive dependence between your decision and Omega's behavior seriously, full stop. In particular she wants to press the point that refusing the blackmail is suspiciously convenient: you decide to refuse and thereby "learn" there's no disaster, which looks like getting good news for free, and she thinks anyone who paid the mugger on updateless grounds has no principled way to complain about that trick. Jon is the "splitter": he thinks paying is right in the mugging and wrong in the blackmail, and that the difference is real, not vibes. Make him state his criterion precisely, in terms of (a) what you actually learn when Omega shows up -- being asked in the mugging tells you which branch you're in, while the letter arrives either way so it tells you nothing you didn't know -- and (b) what your policy actually controls -- in the mugging, paying buys you something in the branch you're not in, while in the blackmail the disaster is fixed independently and your choice only correlates with it.

The dialogue needs real back-and-forth, not two monologues. Maya should get at least two genuine countermoves: the free-lunch worry above, and a charge that Jon's talk of "control" smuggles back in causal intuitions he already gave up when he endorsed one-boxing. Jon should respond to the actual charges, and somewhere in the middle have them jointly cook up a tweaked variant case (their invention, not a named one from the literature) to stress-test the criterion -- e.g. a blackmail variant where the letter only arrives in one branch, or where Omega's letter policy also depends on the coin -- and disagree about what it shows. I'd like them to converge toward a shared way of stating the diagnostic question by the end, but still disagree about whether the blackmail result is a bullet to bite or a reductio, so no tidy handshake ending. Roughly 600-900 words, no citations or name-dropping of papers needed, and definately no strawmen -- I want to be able to show this to people on both sides without them rolling their eyes.
