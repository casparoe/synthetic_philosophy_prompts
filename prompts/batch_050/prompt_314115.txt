In 1930, in the essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (later collected in Essays in Persuasion), John Maynard Keynes wrote:

"For I predict that both of the two opposed errors of pessimism which now make so much noise in the world will be proved wrong in our own time – the pessimism of the revolutionaries who think that things are so bad that nothing can save us but violent change, and the pessimism of the reactionaries who consider the balance of our economic and social life so precarious that we must risk no experiments."

A colleague and I disagree about whether history bore this out. My credence that the prediction in this passage has been substantially vindicated, judged from roughly the hundred-year vantage point the essay itself adopts, is about 0.65; hers is about 0.2. I would like your own credence, but the number matters less to me than its derivation.

A few things to note before you give it. Elsewhere in the same essay Keynes projects that the standard of life in progressive countries a century hence will be somewhere between four and eight times what it was in 1930, and he frames the whole piece as a wager that once the economic problem is solved, humanity will face the question of how to live well — a question he thinks we are badly prepared for, since our habits and institutions were built for struggle rather than for abundance. My colleague's low number rests partly on her view that this good-life corollary has plainly failed: rich countries reached something like Keynes's income range, yet working hours fell far short of his expectations, positional consumption and status competition absorbed much of the surplus, and nothing like the moral reorientation he anticipated took place. I reply that the quoted prediction is strictly about the two pessimisms, and that the corollary's fate is a separable question.

So here is what I want from you. State the proposition precisely enough to have a truth value — including how you read "in our own time," given that Keynes died in 1946 and the essay's horizon is 2030 — and give your credence. Then decompose it: the empirical premise (did technical progress and capital accumulation continue at anything like the required pace); the revolutionary horn (did advanced economies escape their troubles without violent overthrow, and would stagnation absent revolution have made the revolutionaries right); the reactionary horn (did the welfare state, demand management, and other experiments proceed without the collapse the reactionaries feared); the interpretive question of what would count as either pessimism being proved wrong; and whether the apparent failure of the good-life corollary should bear on the quoted prediction at all. Assign a rough probability to each component, show how they combine into your final number, identify which component the number is most sensitive to, and say where my colleague and I most likely diverge — and what evidence or argument would move you most.
