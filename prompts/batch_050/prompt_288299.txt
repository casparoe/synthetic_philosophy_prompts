Write a poem in terza rima that functions as a working model of Yablo's paradox, followed by a short prose coda. The Yablo sequence is an infinite list of sentences, each of which says only that every sentence after it is false; it generates contradiction without any sentence referring to itself, which is what makes it philosophically interesting as a challenge to the idea that all semantic paradox requires circularity.

The poem should meet these constraints:

1. The body consists of at least seven tercets in proper terza rima (ABA BCB CDC, and so on). Each tercet must contain a claim about all of the tercets that come after it -- for instance, that they are all false, or all unworthy of belief -- and must never refer to itself or to any earlier tercet. The forward-reaching reference of the content should be visibly mirrored by the form: each tercet's middle rhyme exists only to be taken up by the next tercet, so that rhyme, like assertion, always delegates downward and never looks back.

2. The poem must not close its rhyme scheme in the usual way. Instead, its ending should dramatize why the paradox needs an infinite sequence with no last member: in a finite list, the final sentence's claim about all later sentences is vacuously satisfied, a definite truth assignment propagates back up the chain, and the paradox dissolves. The last tercet should make the reader feel this -- that the poem can only remain paradoxical by refusing to have a final word.

3. After the open-ended body, include one clearly marked envoy stanza that deliberately breaks the rule: a single self-referential conditional sentence, on the model of Curry's paradox (a sentence saying, in effect, that if it is true, then some absurdity follows), which "proves" something preposterous -- that the moon is made of green cheese, or similar -- all by itself. The contrast should be legible: Curry needs only one stanza and must refer to itself; Yablo needs infinitely many and refers to none.

4. Close with a prose coda of four to six sentences, in American English, identifying which formal features of the poem do which philosophical work, and stating precisely where the contradiction is located in the Yablo case (the impossibility of consistently assigning truth values to the sequence) versus the Curry case (the steps by which the single conditional yields an arbitrary conclusion).

The poem should read as a poem, not as notation wearing line breaks, and someone who knows both paradoxes should be able to verify that the structure is right; someone who doesn't should still find it worth reading.
