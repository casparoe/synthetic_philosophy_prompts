Since retiring from a career in experimental chemistry, I have been working my way through the philosophy of mathematics, and I keep returning to an episode that sits uncomfortably between my old world and my new one: the computer-assisted proofs of the last half-century. Take the two canonical cases. In 1976 Appel and Haken announced a proof of the four-colour theorem whose argument reduced the problem to checking a large but finite set of configurations -- roughly two thousand of them -- for reducibility, a computation no human could survey in a lifetime; the announcement was met in some quarters with something close to refusal to call it a proof at all. Then in 1998 Thomas Hales announced a proof of the Kepler conjecture on sphere packing, resting in part on extensive interval-arithmetic computations, and the referees charged with checking it reportedly concluded after years of work that they were highly, but not fully, confident of its correctness; Hales responded by spending well over a decade on a formal verification project so that a proof-checking program could certify the whole thing. Philosophers such as Thomas Tymoczko used the first case to argue that mathematics had acquired an a posteriori, quasi-empirical element -- that accepting such a proof requires trust in machines akin to trust in instruments and in testimony, rather than the surveyable, surveyable-by-one-mind certainty traditionally claimed for proof. Others replied that nothing of the kind occurred: the computer merely does quickly what a human could in principle do slowly, so the proof remains as a priori as long division, and the epistemology is unchanged.

Here is where I want the analysis, because I think my old profession gives me a stick to poke both sides with, and I am not sure it connects. When I trusted a mass spectrometer, my trust had a specific structure: calibration against known samples, independent cross-checks by different methods, a physical theory of how the instrument worked, and a community that would have caught systematic error. Analyze the four-colour and Kepler episodes through the lens of the Tymoczko-style claim, using this comparison seriously rather than rhetorically: which, if any, of those four justificatory routes are actually available for trusting a compiler, a proof checker, and thousands of hours of processor time, and does the answer differ between the two cases? Consider that a proof checker's small verified kernel might be more like a calibration standard than like the instrument itself, and that reducibility computations were later reproduced on independent hardware -- and ask whether that constitutes the kind of cross-check that makes empirical knowledge respectable, or merely multiplies the same kind of fallible token. I also want the analysis to run in the other direction: does the fact that the mathematical community eventually stopped worrying -- not because anyone surveyed the computations but because of accumulated re-derivations and formal verification -- confirm Tymoczko's thesis, refute it by showing that the a posteriori element can be engineered away, or reveal that the dispute was never really about certainty at all but about who counts as a knower? And if the correct moral is that proof was always dependent on testimony and social trust, computers merely having made the dependence visible, say so plainly.
