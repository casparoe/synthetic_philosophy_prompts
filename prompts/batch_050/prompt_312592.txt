I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in formal epistemology (16 students, one 75-minute meeting), and we are about to spend two weeks on accuracy-first arguments for probabilism -- the claim that credences ought to obey the probability axioms because any credence function that violates them is accuracy-dominated: there is a probabilistic alternative that is strictly closer to the truth in every possible world, not merely on average. My students can follow the proof sketch but clearly regard it as a curiosity, and several have voiced the standard resistances without feeling their weight: why should guaranteed accuracy matter more than the chance of being maximally accurate, why take quadratic distance as the right measure of closeness to truth, and why think of belief as answerable to a scoring rule at all. I want them to run into these questions as players before we discuss them as readers.

Design a runnable in-class game, with real (small, non-monetary) stakes, that makes participants feel the force of accuracy dominance and the role of the scoring rule -- something where they assign credences to propositions whose truth values will actually be resolved in the room (card draws, urn contents, facts about their classmates collected beforehand, whatever works), get scored, and discover for themselves that certain ways of distributing credence lose points no matter how things turn out, while others cannot be beaten this way. Ideally the activity would include at least one round under a scoring scheme that rewards something other than honest reporting, so that students viscerally experience why the choice of measure is load-bearing rather than a technicality, and some mechanism by which a player with incoherent credences can be shown -- within the game, not in a lecture -- a rival assignment that would have done strictly better in every outcome that could have occurred. Give me: the materials and preparation I need, the rules stated precisely enough to hand to a student, a payoff or points scheme with an actual small prize structure, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in play onto the components of the dominance argument (the partition of outcomes, the notion of guaranteed versus expected accuracy, the propriety of the scoring rule).

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run this -- students gaming the resolution mechanism, confusion about what their credences were supposed to be, arithmetic bottlenecks, the prize distorting things -- and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which features of the philosophical position the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion: for instance, whether the felt pull of not-being-dominated is itself an epistemic norm or just prudence wearing epistemic clothes, and whether anything in the activity speaks to someone who denies that accuracy is the fundamental epistemic good in the first place.
