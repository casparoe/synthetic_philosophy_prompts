I took a philosophy of religion seminar a few years back and the Pascal's wager unit never sat right with me, and last week over beers my friends and I ended up listing wager-shaped decisions where my gut gives different answers even though the expected value math looks the same. I want to know if there's a principle under my verdicts or if I'm just inconsistent. Here they are.

Pascal's actual wager has some pull on me: if I genuinely assigned a small chance to the Christian God existing and rewarding belief with infinite bliss, betting on belief feels not crazy. But the structurally identical wager for an anti-God who damns believers and rewards only atheists, same small probablity, same infinite stakes, has zero pull on me; it feels like a scam, not a decision problem. Pascal's mugging: a twitchy stranger says give him $20 and he'll pay me back $10,000 tomorrow, and even granting him one chance in a thousand of honesty I'd refuse, and I'm sure that's right. But flight insurance at the airport, one in ten million chance of a crash, feels perfectly reasonable to buy, not mugging-like at all, and I can't say why. A doctor offers me a free lottery ticket with a tiny chance of curing my chronic knee pain and no downside: obviously take it. A cult recruiter offers the same shape of deal, devote my life for a tiny chance of infinite salvation, and I want to say that's totally different from the doctor even though both are small-chance-of-huge-upside. And the weirdest one: if someone convinced me the probability of God was genuinely 50/50, the wager would suddenly feel legitimate in a way it doesn't at 'who knows, small' — which is strange, because 50/50 isn't obviously friendlier to the math than epsilon.

Is there a principle that captures most of these? My tentative guess is that expected-value reasoning is only legitimate when the probability isn't itself hostage to the payoff structure — the anti-God and the mugger get their tiny probabilities precisely because the offer is built to exploit EV math, whereas insurance probabilities come from actuarial tables that don't care about me. But I can't make that precise, and it seems to wrongly acquit the cult recruiter, whose salvation probability also 'doesn't care about me,' so the principle needs repair at best. If no principle gets all of them, tell me which verdict I should drop and what it costs me — though the one I'd most like to keep is the insurance/mugging asymmetry.
