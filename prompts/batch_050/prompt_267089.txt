Exam question: "Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties (qualia) are caused by physical processes in the brain but themselves cause nothing. Explain the paradox of phenomenal judgment as an objection to this view. Then assess how serious the objection is: does it refute epiphenomenalism, or merely reveal a cost the epiphenomenalist might be willing to pay?"

Student's answer: "Epiphenomenalism is the view that qualia are generated by brain processes but have no causal effects of their own; the mental is a byproduct, like steam from a locomotive. The paradox of phenomenal judgment, first formulated by Frank Jackson, points out a strange consequence. When I judge 'I am now experiencing red,' that judgment is a physical event — it issues in speech and behavior, so it must have physical causes. But on epiphenomenalism, my quale of red is causally inert, so it cannot be among the causes of my judgment. A zombie replica of me, lacking qualia entirely, would produce the very same judgment. So my judgments about my own experience would be exactly the same whether or not I had the experiences, which seems to sever the connection between experience and self-knowledge. The zombie-twin case shows that epiphenomenalism is internally inconsistent, since the view both posits qualia and makes knowledge of them impossible. Moreover, epiphenomenalism faces the deeper problem that it posits mental causes intervening in the physical world, which conflicts with the causal closure of physics that the view needs to respect. The epiphenomenalist might reply that judgments can track qualia without being caused by them, perhaps because judgments and qualia share a common physical cause. But this response fails, because reliability without causation is a coincidence. I conclude that the paradox refutes epiphenomenalism outright, and that even the most charitable version of the view cannot account for how we know our own minds."
