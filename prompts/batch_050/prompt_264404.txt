I'm putting together a handout for a third-year cog sci reading group on whether the brain is a computer, and I want a decision tree that sorts students into positions instead of letting them pick whichever label sounds coolest.

Every student starts at the same point: they accept that brains clearly do *something* information-processing-ish, and the question is what work the word "computing" is doing beyond that. From there, build the tree out of the logical space of answers, not out of a list of names — figure out which questions the positions genuinely disagree on first, then locate the positions in that space.

Branch questions I'd expect to matter (reorganize or add as you see fit):

- Is computation something a system does intrinsically, or does something only count as computing relative to an interpretation someone imposes on it? (Searle claims the wall behind him implements every program — if that's right, does "the brain computes" still say anything at all?)
- Can you settle what the brain computes at the level of algorithms without caring how it's physically implemented, or does the wiring matter to the claim itself?
- Does a computation need representations with genuine content, or is bare causal structure enough?
- Does the system's timing and continuous dynamics matter, or only its sequence of discrete states? Use van Gelder's Watt governor as the test case here: it regulates a steam engine beautifully, but nobody wants to say it computes — so what exactly does the brain have that the governor lacks?
- Does cognition leak out into the body and environment, and if it does, is "the brain is a computer" straightforwardly false rather than just incomplete?

Leaves I definately want to see: classical symbolic computationalism (language-of-thought territory), a liberalized mechanistic computationalism that counts connectionist networks as computing too (Piccinini-flavored), pancomputationalist trivialism ("everything computes, so the claim is empty"), anti-computationalist dynamicism, enactivism / radical embodied cognition, and Searle-style biological naturalism (brains produce minds by brute causal powers; computation is observer-relative). If your answer-space contains a combination nobody actually holds, keep it as an unoccupied leaf and add one sentence on whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

Some ground rules:

- Phrase every branch as a question a student could answer *without* knowing which position it leads to. No "do you accept functionalism?" style nodes.
- Use at least three concrete cases as diagnostics: the Watt governor, the Chinese room, and a giant lookup table that reproduces a brain's entire input-output profile. Neuron-by-neuron silicon replacement is allowed if it earns its keep.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate rather than papering over it. My guess is mechanistic computationalism vs. connectionist-friendly functionalism will be one such pair, and enactivism vs. radical dynamicism another — but check whether that's true of *your* tree, don't just take my word for it.
- End each leaf with one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Somewhere between 6 and 9 branch nodes is plenty, and keep the tone of the tree itself casual since it's for students.
