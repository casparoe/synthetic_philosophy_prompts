After a long argument last night, a friend and I remain divided over Bernard Williams’s “one thought too many” objection. My friend claims that Williams exposes love itself as morally self-indulgent: if rescuing one’s spouse rather than a stranger requires any deliberation at all, the rescuer has already treated the relationship as a discretionary preference. I argued that Williams’s real target is narrower—an impartial moral theory that inserts an authorization procedure between a person and the commitments that give that person a life worth living. I also think the objection concerns the structure of practical reasoning, not the number of literal thoughts that happen to cross one’s mind.

Next week I am giving a ten-minute after-dinner speech at the joint retirement celebration of two longtime colleagues: a moral philosopher who built her career defending impartial consequentialism, and her spouse, an emergency physician who has repeatedly made triage decisions in disasters. The audience will be mixed. Roughly half are philosophers familiar with Williams, integrity, demandingness, and agent-relative reasons; the other half are relatives, nurses, paramedics, and former patients who should not need that vocabulary to follow the speech.

Please write the speech. It should honor both retirees without turning the occasion into a seminar, and it must make the Williams problem do genuine structural work. In particular, it should:

- distinguish between what moves a person to act, what would retrospectively justify the action, and what a good public institution should require its agents to say afterward;
- avoid treating “acting without calculation” as automatically virtuous, since unreflective partiality can also be cowardice, favoritism, or prejudice;
- explain why a spouse may be permitted, or even required, to give special weight to a marriage while an emergency physician on duty may be required to ignore personal ties;
- develop a distinction between a reason that displaces intimacy and a reason that disciplines it, making clear whether impartial morality belongs in the first category or the second;
- include one brief, concrete rescue or triage scenario that both halves of the audience can understand, but do not present its correct resolution as obvious;
- end by offering a toast that captures what a long marriage and a long career can teach moral theory about integrity.

Do not resolve the dispute merely by saying that personal relationships are “special,” and do not caricature consequentialists as incapable of love. The speech should make my position plausible while leaving my friend with one serious and clearly stated objection rather than a straw man.
