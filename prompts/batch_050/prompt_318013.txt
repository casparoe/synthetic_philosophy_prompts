I am in my final year of sixth form, and I run my school's philosophy society. We record a monthly interview series in which we put difficult questions to academic philosophers, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends the view, associated with John Locke, that what makes a person at one time the same person as someone at an earlier time is memory: the later person is identical to the earlier one because the later one can remember the earlier one's experiences from the inside. Our guest claims that, properly refined, this account survives the classic objections.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher specialising in the metaphysics of personal identity. Think carefully about where this position is genuinely weakest, then draft eight to ten interview questions that force our guest beyond a restatement of the view. Before writing the final list, please briefly brainstorm the main pressure points so that I can see your reasoning, and for every question include a follow-up keyed to the reply our guest is most likely to give.

I would like the list to cover at least the following ground:

1. The brave officer objection raised by Thomas Reid. As I understand it, the puzzle runs like this: a young boy is flogged at school; he grows up to become a brave officer who performs some heroic deed, and as an officer he can still remember the flogging; later he becomes an elderly general who remembers the heroic deed but has entirely forgotten the flogging. On the memory criterion, the officer is the same person as the boy, and the general is the same person as the officer, yet the general is apparently not the same person as the boy — which seems to break the logic of identity itself, since identity ought to be transitive. Our guest will presumably reply by replacing direct memory with overlapping chains of connected memories, so that the general is linked to the boy through the officer even without remembering the flogging. Design the follow-up to test whether that repair is still recognisably Locke's view, and whether it creates problems of its own.

2. The circularity worry, associated with Joseph Butler: genuine remembering, as opposed to merely seeming to remember, already presupposes that the rememberer is the very person who had the experience, so memory cannot be what constitutes that identity without arguing in a circle. Our guest will likely try to define a notion of apparent memory that does not presuppose identity and build the account from that instead. Press on whether this move quietly reintroduces identity through the back door, since we still need to say which apparent memories count as genuine.

3. The problem of ordinary forgetting: none of us remembers most of what we have lived through, and people with severe amnesia remember almost none of it. The natural reply appeals to what someone could in principle remember, or to indirect psychological connections; I want a question that forces our guest to say exactly how much can be forgotten before the person is gone, and why that cut-off is not arbitrary.

4. One further question of your choosing about a case where memory or psychology divides or duplicates (for instance, a person whose memories are copied into two successors), pressing on whether identity can branch.

Please avoid generic questions about the guest's career or motivations; every question should turn on the actual content of the position. Write the whole answer in British English.
