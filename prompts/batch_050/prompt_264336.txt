So I'm a novelist, halfway through a political book, and I keep having to decide on the page whether my characters did the right thing, and I'd like the moral physics of the novel to at least be consistant. Here are my gut verdicts on the situations as I've written them:

1. The mayor slips a zoning variance to a developer she despises — functionally a bribe — so he'll break ground on the floodwall before hurricane season instead of after the lawsuits finish. The wall holds, a few hundred homes don't flood. Verdict: right call, dirty hands, she'd do it again and I want the reader on her side.

2. A young senator votes for a war she privately thinks is unjust, because voting no ends her career and she's sure she'll do more good over twenty years on the inside than one glorious protest term. Verdict: not ok. The math smells like self-interest wearing a trench coat.

3. The president authorizes torture of a captured bomber who knows where the second device is. It works, a stadium doesn't blow up. Verdict: he should do it — and then resign and take the trial. Doing it and keeping the job and the library is what makes it monstrous. Somehow the punishment afterward matters to me even though it changes nothing about the act.

4. A diplomat quietly lets a friendly foreign intelligence officer get picked up and jailed in a third country, because protecting him would blow up a ceasefire that ends a civil war. Verdict: not ok — even though, and this is what bugs me, the body-count math here is *better* than in case 1.

5. A health minister flatly lies at a press conference, denies the lockdown she knows gets announced next week, because rumor-driven panic buying and flight would kill more people than the lie does. Verdict: fine, honestly. Barely even dirty.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of them, that captures most of this? I know 1 and 4 are in tension — in both, someone gets fed to the gears for the greater good, and the gears are bigger in 4. My only gestured-at difference is that the officer trusted her and the developer didn't, betrayal vs. mere grubbiness, but I can't make that do real work yet. And 2 and 3 both involve doing a bad thing for a big good, yet one is careerism and one is tragic duty, and I'd love to know what load-bearing difference I'm sensing there.

Please state the principle(s) precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, and be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to drop — I'm willing to rewrite a chapter. Also tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't written: a candidate who lies on the trail about her plan to close the town's army base, since the closure is genuinely necessary but admitting it loses her the election to a man who'd also start a land war (this is the one I flip-flop on daily), and a general who wins a battle by letting his own surrounded battalion be written off in the official dispatches as already lost, so the enemy commits reserves to the wrong front.
