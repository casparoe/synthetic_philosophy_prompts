I've absorbed enough GiveWell-style cost-effectiveness reasoning over the years to be suspicious whenever someone treats the choice of welfare metric as a bookkeeping detail. Design me a triage case that makes the choice bite: a fixed health budget must be divided between two programs, and a QALY-maximizing analysis and a value-of-statistical-life (VSL) analysis must rank the programs in opposite orders, while both frameworks agree in rejecting a naive count of lives saved. Constraints: a realistic setting (a hospital network or public health agency, no trolleys or utility monsters); the scenario itself kept to one paragraph; the two affected populations matched for income and wealth, so the divergence cannot be routed through VSL's dependence on willingness to pay; and figures of the sort a real administrator might face, with the arithmetic shown rather than gestured at. After the scenario, do four things: (1) walk through each framework's calculation and verdict; (2) identify the structural feature doing the work — I expect it to be VSL's comparative flatness across age versus QALYs' sensitivity to remaining life expectancy, but tell me honestly if the case actually turns on something else; (3) state whether swapping VSL for a value per statistical life year collapses the divergence, and what that reveals; and (4) give the strongest objection a VSL partisan would raise against reading the case as a point in QALYs' favor. The case is for a seminar on why metric choice is normative rather than technical, so it fails if the verdicts are merely stipulated instead of generated from the frameworks themselves.
