So I need help writing a speech and its kind of a philosophical minefield, so bear with me. I run the volunteer program at a small hospice (about forty volunteers) and next month we're having our annual appreciation dinner, which doubles as a send-off for Margie, who's been volunteering here for seventeen years and is moving to Portugal to be near her grandkids. Margie is honestly a lot: she took the overnight shifts nobody else wanted, she trained basically every volunteer we currently have, she drove patients to appointments in her own car back when we couldn't reimburse mileage, and she once reorganized our entire filing system over a weekend because it annoyed her. Everyone agrees she's irreplaceable, and I'm expected to give the toast.

Here's the complication. About ten years ago, back when I still thought I'd be a philosopher, I wrote my honors thesis arguing that supererogation doesn't exist -- roughly, that "beyond the call of duty" is an illusion, because if an act really is the best thing available to you then doing it is just your duty, full stop, and the whole category of supererogation is a device we invented to let ourselves off the hook for doing less. Margie knows about this thesis and has teased me about it for years ("careful, don't praise me too much, you'll contradict yourself"). And the embarassing thing is I still basically believe it. Meanwhile the audience will be our other volunteers plus some staff and two board members, mostly non-philosophers, people who show up for their scheduled shifts and do them well.

So: write the toast, maybe 5-6 minutes read aloud, warm but not sappy. Constraints, and I think they're in real tension, which is the point: (a) honor Margie as strongly as possible without saying anything I actually consider false -- no "she went above and beyond her duty" if that's an incoherent category on my view; (b) don't imply, even by silence or comparison, that the rest of the volunteers are falling short of what morality asks, because I don't think that and it'd be a lousy thing to say at an appreciation dinner; and (c) don't glorify unlimited self-sacrifice in a way that papers over the fact that we lean on unpaid labor because the funding isn't there -- romanticizing Margie's overwork is part of how institutions like ours exploit people like her. Part of what I want is a framing that threads all three, if one exists, and if it genuinely can't be threaded I'd rather you tell me that than fake it. Since it's a speech, no verdicts needed in the text itself, but please add a short note afterward flagging which moves in the toast take sides on questions that are genuinely contested among moral philosophers (whether supererogation is a real category, whether praising it wrongs the merely dutiful, the over-demandingness business) versus which parts are common ground, so I can see where I'd be quietly committing to something and decide whether to soften it.
