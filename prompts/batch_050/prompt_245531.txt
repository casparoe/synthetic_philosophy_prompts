In debates over whether prosperity and order can legitimate authoritarian rule, defenders of the Singapore and China models seldom argue in the abstract; they lean on two analogies. The first is commercial: a state is like a company, its leaders like a management team, its citizens like shareholders — and just as shareholders replace the board only when returns falter, the governed have no standing to demand a say in who rules so long as the government delivers growth and stability. The second is familial: a state is like a household, rulers like parents, citizens like children — a competent parent does not put dinner to a vote, and deference rather than consent is the fitting response to good care. I would like both analogies audited as arguments for what we might call performance legitimacy: the thesis that delivered results can substitute for the consent of the governed. For each analogy, begin with an explicit mapping of correspondences (profit onto growth, shareholder votes onto elections, selling one's shares onto emigration; parental authority onto political authority, the child's immaturity onto the citizenry's alleged unpreparedness, and so on). Then say where each mapping breaks, taking care to separate disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from those that bear on the intended use — for instance, that a shareholder's stake is alienable and held by right independently of the manager's benevolence, whereas a citizen's stake in a state is neither, or that parental authority is justified by the child's prospective autonomy and dissolves as the child matures, which raises the question of what the political analogue of growing up would be and who gets to declare it has happened. Identify what each analogy quietly imports that an open argument would have to defend: that welfare is the sole end of political association in the way profit is of a firm, that governmental competence can be measured without the very freedoms (press, opposition, independent courts) the model suspends, that the governed stand to rulers as minors stand to adults. Finally, once the disanalogies are on the table, assess what survives of the conclusion each analogy was deployed to license — and if neither can carry it, say whether some better analogy (an unelected pilot, a trustee, a physician) does the work more honestly, and whether it ends up supporting or undermining performance legitimacy.
