I'm a software engineer, so my instinct is that "if X, then Y" is a single operation with a fixed truth condition, like an if-statement. But I've been told natural language quietly splits conditionals into two kinds: indicative ("If Alice didn't sign the contract, someone else did") and counterfactual ("If Alice hadn't signed the contract, the deal would have collapsed"), and that these can diverge in truth value even when built from the same antecedent and consequent.

Construct a single thought experiment, set in an ordinary modern workplace (an office, a hospital, a warehouse — no casinos, no assassins, no time travel), in which the indicative and counterfactual versions of one conditional sentence come apart: one is clearly true (or clearly assertible) and the other clearly false (or clearly unassertible), given the same facts. Constraints: (a) the two sentences must share the same antecedent and consequent content, differing only in grammatical mood/tense; (b) the scenario must be one continuous factual setup, not two separate vignettes stitched together; (c) no character in the scenario may be a philosopher or know anything about the indicative/counterfactual distinction; (d) keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

After the scenario, do three things. First, show the verdict for each conditional and explain why the same facts license them differently. Second, identify exactly which feature of the setup does the work — what would have to be true of the characters' evidence, and of how the world actually is, for the split to occur — and say whether the divergence survives if the characters are fully informed. Third, tell me what this implies for my engineer's instinct: is the indicative/counterfactual split a defect of natural language that a well-designed formal language should eliminate, or does it track two genuinely different operations that both belong in a complete theory of conditionals?
