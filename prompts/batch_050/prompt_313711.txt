Since retiring from a career in experimental chemistry, I have been reading twentieth-century social theory, most recently Adorno and Horkheimer's chapter on the culture industry in Dialectic of Enlightenment. A pattern I keep observing among people my age makes me wonder whether that tradition, or some later literature, already has a name for it.

Two instances. First, a friend of mine, a retired engineer, watches a televised singing competition every week without fail. He can articulate, unprompted, essentially the whole critique: the contestants are interchangeable, the emotional backstories are manufactured, the voting mechanics are engineered to produce investment rather than to measure merit. He delivers this commentary during the broadcast, often laughing, and plainly enjoys the evening enormously. Second, a popular film franchise whose long-time fans seem to take their pleasure largely in cataloguing its formulaic elements -- the obligatory plot beats, the quota of quips -- and whose producers appear to have noticed, since recent installments contain jokes that openly acknowledge the formula. The mockery is not a rebellion against the product; it has been absorbed into the product, and if anything seems to deepen the audience's loyalty.

What strikes me is that these consumers are not deceived in the way I had assumed the culture-industry thesis requires. The picture I took from my reading was of an audience pacified by failing to notice the machinery. My friend notices everything and stays. Nor does this seem to be a "guilty pleasure" in the ordinary sense, since he feels no guilt; nor simple hypocrisy or weakness of will, since he does not watch despite his criticism but partly through it. The ironic distance is itself a mode of enjoyment, and arguably one the industry has learned to manufacture and sell back to him.

Is there an established term for this configuration -- lucid, self-aware consumption of mass culture, in which the consumer's own awareness of the critique has been folded into the product's appeal? If several nearby concepts exist, I would like to know precisely how the phenomenon I have described differs from each and which fits best; a bare label would not satisfy me. I would also welcome boundary cases: does it still count if the irony is wholly private rather than solicited by the show itself, or if the consumer eventually stops watching? Finally, assuming the phenomenon is real and named, does it constitute evidence against Adorno and Horkheimer's account, or did they in some way anticipate it?
