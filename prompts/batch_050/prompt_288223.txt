Begin your response with the grade itself in the first sentence (a letter grade from A to F, together with a percentage), then give the reasoning point by point: state which claims in the answer are accurate, which are mistaken, and precisely what the mistake is in each case, including errors that are easy to overlook because the surrounding claims are sound. The question I set: Eliminative materialists about the mind claim that beliefs and desires do not exist. Set out the strongest version of their argument for this claim, and then evaluate the objection that the position is self-refuting, since the eliminative materialist presumably believes that there are no beliefs. Does the position survive the objection?

The member's answer:

Eliminative materialism, defended by Paul and Patricia Churchland and by Daniel Dennett, holds that beliefs and desires do not exist. The argument runs as follows. Folk psychology — our everyday practice of explaining behaviour by attributing beliefs and desires — is itself a theory, and like any theory it can be compared against its rivals and found wanting. It has remained essentially stagnant for millennia, it does not integrate with the growing sciences of the brain, and it fails to explain central phenomena such as sleep, memory, and mental illness. If the best completed theory of the mind turns out to be neuroscience, and neuroscience contains nothing corresponding to beliefs, then we should conclude that there are no beliefs, just as we concluded that there is no phlogiston when oxidation theory displaced it.

As for self-refutation: the objection misses its target. The eliminativist need not claim to believe anything; they produce sentences, and whether those sentences are true depends on what the world contains, not on how we describe the person uttering them. Describing the eliminativist as believing their own theory already presupposes the folk-psychological framework that is under dispute, so the objection begs the question. The eliminativist can state the position in the brain's own computational medium, the language of thought, which does not traffic in beliefs at all.

A further objection holds that introspection acquaints us directly with our beliefs, so experience itself refutes the view. But introspective reports are folk-psychological interpretations of whatever is occurring in the brain, not theory-neutral observations; the same faculty once reported that the sun moves while we stand still. Nor does the view deny conscious experience: it denies the existence of beliefs and desires, not of experiences, so it is fully compatible with the reality of consciousness.
