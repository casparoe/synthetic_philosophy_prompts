I chair the complaints panel of our state's advertising standards board, a five-member self-regulatory body that rules on challenges to print and billboard ads. Our code's test for a deceptive general claim, written twenty years ago, is purely statistical: a claim of the form "Xs are Y" or "Xs do Y" is permissible only if Y holds of a substantial majority of Xs. Three pending complaints have made that rule untenable, and I need a draft opinion to circulate before Friday's meeting that resolves all three and proposes a replacement standard my non-philosopher colleagues can actually apply to the next intake.

The complaints: (1) A pest-control firm's billboard reads "Mosquitoes carry West Nile virus." A rival objects that county surveillance last summer found the virus in under three percent of trapped mosquito pools, so the claim fails the majority test by an enormous margin; the firm answers that no reasonable person reads it as a census report. (2) A nursery's catalog states "Our roses bloom from June until frost." A customer whose five bushes flowered once in June and stopped complains; the nursery concedes that only about forty percent of the varieties it stocks are repeat-bloomers but insists that claims about a kind always tolerate exceptions. (3) An internet provider advertises "Our customers stream in 4K." Its own filing shows eighty-eight percent of subscriber households have streamed 4K content at least once, comfortably passing the majority test, yet the complainant argues the claim is misleading anyway, since it touts as distinctive something true of essentially every provider's customer base.

A staffer who once took a linguistics course attached a memo pointing out that ordinary generics follow nothing like our majority rule: "ducks lay eggs" is fine though drakes never do, "mosquitoes carry malaria" is fine though almost none do, while "Canadians are right-handed" sounds wrong even though roughly nine in ten are. She also noted that low-prevalence generics seem most acceptable when the property is dangerous or striking, which cuts both ways for us: it vindicates the billboard's wording while explaining exactly why fear-based advertising works, and it suggests generic phrasing lodges kind-level beliefs in hearers the way stereotypes do, so our standard governs more than these three ads. Please draft the opinion: a bottom-line ruling on each complaint, a revised standard stated plainly enough to be applied without a philosophy degree, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument against each ruling you recommend — including, if you clear the billboard, what prevents a sanitizer company from running "Playgrounds harbor deadly bacteria" on the same logic.
