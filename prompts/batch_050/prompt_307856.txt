I would like you to grade the answer out of ten and supply the specific comments that justify the grade, in a form I can adapt for the student's feedback sheet. The question: In the chapter on property, Locke argues that individuals can come to own parts of the natural world without anyone's consent. Explain how the labour theory of acquisition is supposed to generate a property right, what limits Locke places on legitimate acquisition, and whether the introduction of money changes the moral situation. On Locke's own terms, can the theory justify accumulation well beyond personal use?

The student's answer: "Locke begins from self-ownership: each person has a property in his own person and in the labour of his body. When someone mixes his labour with something held in common -- gathering acorns, tilling a field -- he joins something that is his to something that is no one's, and thereby makes the whole his own without needing the consent of others, which would in any case be impossible to obtain for every apple or plot of land. Locke then attaches two restrictions to acquisition. First, the spoilage limitation: one may take only as much as one can use before it perishes, since whatever rots in one's possession is more than one's share and so amounts to taking from others. Second, the proviso that one leave enough and as good for others, which is really the same constraint viewed from the other side: if nobody takes more than they can use, plenty will remain for everyone, so the two limits stand or fall together. Money transforms the situation. Gold and silver do not decay, so once society consents to their use a person can heap up wealth indefinitely without anything spoiling in his hands, and Locke takes this consent to dissolve the limits on accumulation: hoarding money wastes nothing and, because money is durable and compact, it does not prevent others from acquiring their share either. Locke reinforces the point by observing that a day labourer in England lives better than a king over the unenclosed commons of America, which shows that enclosure and accumulation improve everyone's condition and therefore wrong no one, not even those left without land. On Locke's own terms, then, accumulation far beyond personal use is justified: labour creates the title, money removes the natural check on how much title one may hold, and the productivity of enclosed land means even the landless are better off, which is what the proviso was ultimately concerned with."
