I'm advising a city government's innovation office that has funding for about two years of pilot work on futarchy — settling a small set of real policy decisions with conditional prediction markets — but only enough capacity for three serious workstreams. Which open problems with governance-by-prediction-markets deserve those slots, and in what order? The candidates on our list: the thin-market and manipulation worry (whether a few informed or deep-pocketed traders can move prices once real policy is at stake); the metric problem (how to choose and operationalize the outcome measure — some GDP-plus index, survey-based wellbeing, or something else — without the whole arrangement inheriting the measure's distortions); the self-referentiality of conditional markets, which must price welfare under a policy whose adoption depends on the very prices they generate; the feedback problem, where published odds change behavior and thereby spoil the conditional estimates the decision rests on; and the legitimacy problem, the near-certainty that officials will override market verdicts they dislike, making adoption itself endogenous. Rank the problems by how much of the pilot's value depends on each, identify which are upstream of which, and defend the ordering with explicit criteria — severity, tractability at pilot scale, and downstream dependence. Also tell me which problem you would deliberately leave untouched for these two years and why, and what single empirical discovery would most change your ranking.
