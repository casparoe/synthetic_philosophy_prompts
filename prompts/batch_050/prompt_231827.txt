I was at a dinner last weekend where two friends of mine — one a graduate student in epistemology, the other a data scientist — got into a sustained argument about whether you can come to know things on the basis of asking a chatbot, and it mapped surprisingly cleanly onto the reductionism versus anti-reductionism debate about testimony that I remember from college. Neither would concede anything, and I'd like a proper adjudication. Here is the exchange as I remember it. (1) The data scientist opened by claiming that chatbot outputs simply are not testimony at all, because testimony requires a speaker who asserts something and thereby stakes their credibility on it; since there is no asserter in the loop, the outputs should be treated like readings from an instrument, and instrument readings only give you knowledge if you have independent positive reasons to trust the instrument — so the whole reductionism debate is moot here and some version of reductionism wins by default. (2) The philosopher replied that this misclassifies the epistemology: if there is no speaker, then consulting the chatbot is more like perception of a text than like testimony, and nobody thinks perception needs independent positive reasons to justify belief — the anti-reductionist's point was always that some sources are basic, and basicness doesn't disappear just because the source is artificial. (3) The data scientist countered that the anti-reductionist's default entitlement to trust testimony is only ever defended by appealing to features of human communication — that speakers face social costs for lying, that hearers can monitor competence and sincerity, that assertion is governed by norms — and that the chatbot's training process severs every one of those connections, so the entitlement cannot transfer; and since the system's reliability varies sharply by domain and is opaque even to its makers, we in fact lack the positive reasons her own reductionist fallback would require. (4) The philosopher answered that this proves far too much: a great deal of ordinary human testimony reaches us through chains where no sincerity monitoring is possible — anonymous survey aggregates, translations of dead languages, encyclopedia entries with no named author — and nobody concludes we can't know things from them; worse, she said, the reductionist demand for positive reasons is unmeetable even for human testimony, since any track-record argument for testimony's reliability will itself rely on testimony somewhere and therefore be circular, so his position would leave us knowing almost nothing from any source, human or machine.
