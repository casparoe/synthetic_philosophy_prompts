Exam question: In a few paragraphs, explain how Davidson proposes to use a Tarski-style truth theory as a theory of meaning for a speaker's language, and what role the principle of charity plays in radical interpretation. Why does Davidson think we cannot first fix what a speaker believes and only then, as a separate step, figure out what their words mean?

Student's answer:

Davidson's idea is to use a Tarski-style truth theory as an empirical theory of meaning for a natural language. The theory would be finitely axiomatized and would entail, for each sentence of the language, a T-sentence of the form "s is true iff p" -- for instance, "Es regnet is true in German iff it is raining." Each such T-sentence states the meaning of the sentence on the left-hand side, so once we have the full theory we have the meaning of every sentence of the language. And since the theory defines truth for the language, meaning is likewise fixed by the theory.

Radical interpretation faces a circle: what a speaker's utterances mean depends on what they believe, but we can only find out what they believe through what their utterances mean. Davidson's solution is the principle of charity: we interpret the speaker so as to maximize agreement between their beliefs and ours, treating most of their beliefs as true by our lights. This single assumption lets us solve for belief and meaning simultaneously rather than one at a time, which is how we break into the circle.

Charity is essentially a heuristic to get the interpretation going; once we have gathered enough behavioral evidence, we could in principle set it aside and discover that the speaker's beliefs are mostly false. Also, like Quine, Davidson accepts that interpretation is somewhat indeterminate, though he thinks this is no more troubling than the fact that temperature can be measured in Fahrenheit or Celsius.
