A founder I know, Dana, has run her company for a decade and makes every major decision herself. Her track record on hiring and purchasing decisions is as follows. When hiring a senior engineer, she interviewed three of twelve shortlisted candidates and hired the third, because that candidate satisfied every item on the written checklist she had drawn up beforehand; she cancelled the remaining nine interviews, saying there was no reason to keep looking. When six months of searching for new office space turned up nothing that met her original requirements, she rewrote the requirements — dropping, among other things, the dedicated meeting room — and signed a lease on the next property that satisfied the revised list. When choosing between two accounting firms, she compared them first on experience in her industry; since they differed on that dimension, she engaged the more experienced firm without ever comparing their fees. When selecting a payroll provider, she evaluated only the three vendors her peers had mentioned, rather than the full market. And on one occasion, facing a seven-figure equipment purchase, she paid a consultant to generate additional options before deciding, explaining that at these stakes a longer search was worth the cost.

Next month Dana must hire a chief financial officer, the most consequential hire she has ever made. She has interviewed four of ten shortlisted candidates. The fourth satisfies every item on her written checklist. Her assistant points out that the six remaining candidates are all attending the same industry conference next week and could be interviewed at essentially no additional cost or delay. Predict whether Dana will hire the fourth candidate immediately or continue interviewing, and justify the prediction. In doing so, identify at least two distinct decision rules that are each consistent with her entire track record — for instance, satisficing against a fixed but revisable aspiration level, a cue-ordered fast-and-frugal heuristic, or a cost-sensitive search rule that treats her checklists as proxies for an optimal stopping point — state what each rule predicts for the new case, say which precedent in her record is the closest analog, and describe a further decision scenario whose outcome would discriminate among the rules you have identified.
