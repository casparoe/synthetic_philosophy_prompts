So I'm a retired chemist (38 years in polymer research, mostly industrial) and about a year ago I joined a philosophy reading group at the local library, which has been humbling. We're doing philosophy of science at the moment and I got a bit obsessed with Goodman's grue problem from Fact, Fiction, and Forecast. Everyone in the group seemed to treat it as this devastating unsolved thing, and I kept thinking: but this just can't arise in actual science, we have machinery for excluding it. So I wrote a short essay for the group and I'd like comments before I inflict it on them, because I suspect I've walked into some well-known trap and can't see it.

Here it is, slightly trimmed:

---

WHY GRUE CAN'T HAPPEN IN A LAB

Goodman's puzzle, as I understand it: define an object as "grue" if it's examined before some future time t and is green, or not examined before t and is blue. Every emerald we've ever checked is grue, so by standard inductive logic all our evidence confirms "all emeralds are grue" just as well as "all emeralds are green." Yet we'd consider someone insane if they predicted emeralds found after t will be blue. So something must be wrong with naive induction.

My claim is that working scientists already possess the filter that blocks grue, and it's not about entrenchment or which words feel natural. It's this: the predicates that appear in real laws are the ones that figure in time-translation invariant relationships. Any predicate whose definition mentions a specific clock time, like t, is automatically disqualified, because a law of nature has to work the same on Tuesday as on Wednesday. This is baked into physics -- it's basically the symmetry that gives you conservation of energy. A predicate with a built-in switchover date cannot sit inside a differential equation, cannot be integrated, cannot support the counterfactuals scientists actually use ("if we'd heated this sample on Friday instead of Monday, the same thing would happen"). So "grue" fails a criterion that "green" passes, and the criterion is independent of the paradox -- we use it all the time for other reasons, e.g. when we refuse to take seriously a "law" that only holds for data collected before 2010.

Hence the new riddle of induction dissolves: it's not that all predicates are on a par and we irrationally prefer green; grue-type predicates are structurally inadmissible as law-constituents, and confirmation is really confirmation of laws, not of bare generalisations.
