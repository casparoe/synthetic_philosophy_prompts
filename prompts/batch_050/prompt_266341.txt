Two legal regimes I know reasonably well punish speech in patterns I find philosophically puzzling, and I would like their implicit philosophies reconstructed rather than their doctrines summarized.

In the United States, a speaker may publicly vilify racial or religious groups and may advocate violence in the abstract; the state may punish such advocacy only when it is intended, and likely, to produce lawless action immediately (Brandenburg v. Ohio, 1969). Even categories of speech the courts call unprotected cannot be regulated selectively: a city ordinance banning cross-burning only when motivated by racial or religious hatred was struck down as impermissible viewpoint discrimination within an unprotected category (R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 1992). Yet the same system permits content-based punishment of defamation, obscenity, and true threats, and holds employers liable when a workplace is saturated with slurs that would be fully protected if uttered on the sidewalk outside.

In Germany, publicly inciting hatred against segments of the population is a crime with no imminence requirement (§ 130 of the Criminal Code); denying or grossly trivializing the Holocaust is criminal as such; the constitution treats human dignity as inviolable and ranks it above expressive freedom; and the order understands itself as a militant democracy entitled to disable the rights of those who would use them to abolish democracy.

My questions: First, what model of harm does each regime presuppose? Does the American doctrine only make sense on a causal picture, where speech harms by moving listeners to act, while the German regime presupposes that speech can harm by what it expresses or constitutes, independent of its effects on an audience's conduct? Or can each be defended without those pictures? Second, what does each presuppose about equality: that the state must remain formally neutral among viewpoints, or that it owes substantive protection to the social standing of targeted groups? Third, is the American pattern internally coherent: what distinguishes the punishable workplace slur from the protected sidewalk slur -- state action, captive audience, implied consent to workplace rules -- and does any of those distinctions actually hold without a theory the courts never state? Fourth, where is each regime underdetermined, i.e., which results follow strictly from its presuppositions and which could equally be licensed by rival theories (for instance, distrust of governmental competence rather than any theory of harm)? Finally, if the presuppositions were swapped, what would have to change: would Brandenburg and R.A.V. survive adoption of a constitutive-harm view, and would the Holocaust-denial ban survive a purely causal one, or could it be salvaged as a regulation of demonstrable falsehood -- and is that salvage route available under American doctrine at all?
