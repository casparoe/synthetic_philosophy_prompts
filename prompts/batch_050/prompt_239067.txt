So I've been on a big Stephen Jay Gould kick this year -- read Wonderful Life over the summer, then the spandrels paper with Lewontin, and I'm partway through The Structure of Evolutionary Theory now. What keeps striking me is that he died in 2002, right before a bunch of stuff happened in evolutionary biology that seems almost designed to vindicate or embarrass him. Ancient DNA turned out to show that non-African humans carry a small percentage of Neanderthal ancestry (and Denisovan too), which muddies the clean replacement story of human origins. Then there was the whole ENCODE fight over junk DNA, where one side claimed most of the genome is functional and the other side said most of it is still junk -- and junk DNA always seemed like exactly the kind of sloppy, historically contingent mess Gould loved to point at against Panglossian adaptationism. And evo-devo and the extended evolutionary synthesis people keep citing him as a patron saint, though some of them also seem to be going way further than he ever did.

So here's my question: if Gould had lived another twenty years, what do you think his actual positions would have been on these three developments? I don't want generic "he would have been skeptical" handwaving -- I want you to reason from his specific commitments (contingency, exaptation, hierarchical selection, his hatred of adaptationist just-so stories, but also the places where he was actually pretty orthodox Darwinian) and tell me where each development would have delighted him, where it would have annoyed him, and where people who claim him posthumously are probably misreading him. Also, and this is the part I'm most curious about: pick the one place where you think the post-2002 evidence would genuinely have forced him to change his mind about something he argued for in print, and defend that choice against the obvious alternative answer.
