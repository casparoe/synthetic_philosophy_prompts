At a museum exhibit on prehistoric burials, the audio guide explained archaeological inference this way: “Because objects cannot tell us what their owners meant, archaeologists reason by climbing an inference ladder. First they determine whether an object was deliberately deposited rather than displaced by decay, animals, or later digging. Next they use its wear, materials, position, and distribution to infer its practical or social role. Then, by comparing repeated patterns with practices documented among historically or ethnographically known communities, they cautiously infer beliefs. For example, food containers beside a body suggest provision for a continuing postmortem existence, consistent body orientation suggests a shared cosmology, and unusually rich child graves suggest that social rank was inherited rather than earned. Finally, when the same burial package recurs across a large region, archaeologists can infer that an institution was standardizing belief. Since ritual symbols tend to preserve their meanings, close parallels with better-documented societies allow archaeologists to read older symbols as saying roughly the same thing.”

I found the “inference ladder” useful, but I’m unsure how fair the overall summary is. The first stages sound appropriately cautious, while the moves from repeated practices to cosmology, institutions, and stable symbolic meanings seem much shakier. At the same time, maybe archaeologists use words like *institution*, *belief*, and *analogy* in narrower ways than I’m assuming. Please audit the summary sentence by sentence: which claims would a careful archaeologist accept, which are reasonable but underqualified simplifications, and which confuse evidence for social organization with evidence for what people believed? Also explain what alternative explanations would need to be ruled out before accepting the guide’s conclusions.
