Next month I'm giving the last commencement address my college will ever have. After 160-odd years the place is closing for good — enrollment, endowment, the usual story — and as the senior faculty member left standing I got asked to speak. The awkward part is that I've spent my whole career as a historian studying societal collapse, so everyone in the room will be waiting for me to either make the joke or conspicuously avoid it, and I'm not sure which is worse.

Here's what I keep circling around, and where I need the speech to do something honest. When the Bronze Age palaces burned, the people mostly didn't vanish — they stopped keeping records and building palaces and went back to being farmers. The Maya "collapse" left millions of Maya speakers who are still around and reasonably annoyed at being described as vanished. The old Easter Island ecocide story keeps getting walked back by people who actually study it. So there's a genuine question in my field about whether collapse names something that happens to *people*, or something that happens to an organizational chart while the people persist and downgrade to a simpler arrangement. Usually the honest answer is: the complexity goes, the community mostly stays.

But I don't know how to say that to four hundred 22-year-olds without it landing as "your degree is from a fake school now." Because the organizational chart is, in fact, a big part of what they loved — the departments, the traditions, the buildings, the alumni network that's about to become a ghost network. Telling them "the community persists, only the complexity is lost" might be true and also might be exactly the kind of thing a collapse historian says to make himself feel better.

Write the speech. About ten minutes. Some constraints: it can't lie — the college is really closing and that's a real loss, so no unearned "it lives on in our hearts" stuff. It can't be a seminar either — parents, staff, and the grounds crew will be there and none of them care about my field's taxonomy debates. And it has to actually take a position on the thing I genuinely can't decide myself: is what we're losing an institution, a community, or just a set of buildings with a mailing address? Because depending on the answer, this ceremony is either a funeral or it isn't, and a commencement speaker has to know which one they're standing at.
