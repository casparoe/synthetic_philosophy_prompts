Last year I spent five months as my father's caregiver through his final illness, and I have since inherited his seat on the board of the small family foundation he established. My first task is to draft a coherent statement of the principles that will guide our grantmaking, and the experience has left me drawn to suffering-focused ethics. Before I circulate the draft, I want to know whether my own commitments actually hang together, because the board will certainly ask. Here is where I have landed:

(a) Severe suffering has priority over happiness: no quantity of happiness, however great or widely distributed, can compensate for severe suffering. At most, comparatively mild suffering may permissibly be traded against substantial gains in happiness.

(b) Suffering aggregates across individuals: amounts of suffering add up, other things equal it is worse when more beings suffer, and the foundation should aim to minimize total suffering rather than, for instance, concentrating exclusively on the single worst-off individual.

(c) Happiness is genuinely good: holding suffering fixed, a world containing joy and contentment is better than an otherwise identical world without them.

(d) The painless, instantaneous extinction of all sentient life would be an enormous catastrophe, among the worst outcomes imaginable — not a relief.

(e) It can be morally permissible, and sometimes even admirable, to bring a new person into existence, and I intend to have children myself.

(f) Every human life, however fortunate, contains some non-trivial suffering: illness, grief, and bereavement at a minimum.

What I want is an audit, not an overview of suffering-focused ethics. Please go through these and tell me: which pairs or clusters of commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be in tension but dissolve on inspection? I would like the genuine tensions ranked by severity rather than treated as equally bad, and for each one, the least costly revision that would resolve it along with what that revision costs me — in plausibility, in implications for the foundation's giving, or in further commitments I would be taking on. Finally, if I am allowed to revise only one commitment, which single change restores the most overall coherence, and what new difficulties does it create downstream? I have deliberately not indicated which commitment I would be willing to give up, because I do not know myself; I would rather the answer follow from the structure of the problem than from my preferences.
