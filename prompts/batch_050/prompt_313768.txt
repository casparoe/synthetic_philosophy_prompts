I'm a journalist who covers science and mathematics, and I'm trying to write a piece about a disagreement I've run into repeatedly while reporting on randomness -- in cryptography, in statistics, in the foundations of probability -- and I've come to think the disagreement has a structure I don't fully understand. Before I describe it, here's the puzzle that generates it. Everyone agrees that a process can be random: a fair coin, radioactive decay, a properly seeded generator. The fight is over whether a *finished product* -- a particular finite string of outcomes, sitting there with its generating process long gone -- can itself be random or non-random.

As far as I can tell there are two entrenched positions. The first, which I hear from statisticians and some philosophers of probability, holds that randomness attaches only to the process or to our state of knowledge, never to the sequence: any string of zeros and ones you show me was produced by *some* deterministic rule, and the string 00000000 is exactly as probable under a fair coin as any other, so calling one string random and another not is either a confusion or a shorthand for claims about how it was made. The second position, which I hear from people in algorithmic information theory, holds that randomness is a genuine, measurable property of the sequence itself: a string that cannot be compressed -- whose shortest complete description is essentially the string written out in full -- is random in a way that 0101010101... is not, and this is a fact about the object, discoverable without knowing anything about its origin. The odd thing is that both camps seem to concede the other's central premise when pressed: the algorithmic people admit their notion has a dependency on the choice of descriptive language (though they insist the dependency is bounded and doesn't matter asymptotically), and the process people admit that some strings really do *fail* every statistical test in a way others don't.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than confirmed, is that this is a disguised disagreement about what the word "random" is doing in our practices -- that the process camp is implicitly taking randomness-attributions to be explanatory (they explain why we can't predict or compress the output), while the sequence camp takes them to be classificatory (they sort objects by their internal structure), and that the two camps talk past each other because a classification can be perfectly legitimate without explaining anything. But I can also imagine the disagreement is substantive in a way my hypothesis flattens: perhaps there's a real question about whether the bounded language-dependency in the algorithmic notion is philosophically harmless, or about whether the statistical-test intuition that both sides share actually presupposes the very process-talk the sequence camp wants to discard.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute resting on an ambiguity my hypothesis has correctly located? A substantive disagreement about which of two legitimate concepts deserves the name? A case where one side's notion secretly depends on the other's? Or is there a hidden formal question -- something provable or refutable -- doing unacknowledged work underneath? Please ground the diagnosis in what each position actually says rather than in a survey of the field, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the dispute, a result that would force one side to concede, a reframing of the question, or an argument that the impasse is permanent and why. If you think my two-concepts hypothesis is wrong or incomplete, say so directly -- the piece depends on getting the structure of the disagreement right, not on my being right.
