A friend of mine who follows AI-safety debates recently summarized a proposal I have been trying to understand on my own — coherent extrapolated volition, usually credited to Eliezer Yudkowsky — and before I rely on his account I would like to know how much of it to trust. I should say up front that I have no academic training in philosophy or computer science; I have been reading about these questions as an interested outsider, so part of what I am asking is whether I have understood the proposal well enough to even assess his summary.

His version runs as follows:

"Coherent extrapolated volition is a proposal for what a superintelligent AI should want. Rather than hard-coding a goal, you instruct the AI to work out what humanity as a whole would want if we were smarter, better informed, and had more time to think, and to adopt that as its goal. Since people's actual wishes are contradictory, the AI predicts what each individual person would eventually settle on after sufficient reflection, aggregates those predictions into a single coherent preference ordering — averaging where they conflict — and then optimizes for the result. Proponents claim this avoids both the arbitrariness of the programmers' personal values and the chaos of a raw vote. The obvious objections are that the proposal simply assumes our extrapolated wishes converge on one answer, which there is no reason to expect, and that it hands the AI the job of deciding what we ought to want and then locks that answer in permanently."

I have a nagging feeling that this is mostly competent but subtly off in a few places. In particular, "predicts," "aggregates," and "locks in" each seem as though they might be doing more work than the original proposal does — but I cannot rule out that my unease merely reflects the secondhand presentations I have absorbed rather than any real inaccuracy on his part. What I would like is an audit I can act on. Please go through the summary claim by claim (sentence by sentence, if that is cleaner), and for each claim tell me whether a careful proponent of the view would accept it as stated, accept it with qualifications, or reject it as a misrepresentation — and in the last case, what the misrepresentation consists in. Where possible, distinguish three grades: what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification that a specialist would wince at but let pass, and what distorts the proposal in a way that matters for the criticisms. Then, at the end, tell me whether my friend's two concluding objections still stand once the summary is corrected — that is, whether they target the actual proposal or only the version he has described.

One request about how to answer: if his summary is in fact fairer than I suspect, I would rather be told that plainly than be humored; and if my question itself rests on a misunderstanding of what the proposal claims — for instance, if I have wrongly assumed there is a single settled version of it against which his account can be checked — please say so directly instead of politely answering around the problem.
