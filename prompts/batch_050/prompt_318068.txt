I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long-form essay on whether deepfakes really create an epistemic crisis or merely accelerate an older crisis of institutional trust. I am also informally advising a small foundation that can fund about six two-year projects across philosophy, law, HCI, security studies, and newsroom practice. It cannot build frontier models, run a detection arms race, or lobby in more than one or two jurisdictions. Help me turn the essay into a defended agenda.

Use these stipulative notions; they are meant to discipline the answer, not to require sources. By epistemic backstop I mean the last layer of practices that lets a public re-anchor belief when ordinary audio and video stop being default evidence: not catching each fake, but preserving a usable answer to the question what would it take to know this happened. Distinguish three failure modes. Provenance collapse is when origin trails are missing, forged, or ignored. Reconstruction failure is when institutions cannot rebuild an event from independent traces such as witnesses, logs, physics, paperwork, financial flows, and contradictory footage. Legitimacy failure is when even a good reconstruction is rejected because the verifying body is seen as captured. Call the amount of work pushed onto that final layer backstop load. Call the temporary advantage created each time generators adapt faster than detectors an adversarial half-life. Call the slow accrual of doubt around real recordings authenticity debt. Call the most extreme social response reality rationing: a norm in which only a narrow class of events, places, and institutions are treated as worth verifying at all.

Which open questions or lines of work matter most under these constraints, and in what order? I am less interested in a survey than in the ranking and the reasons. Please prioritize among at least these candidates, while adding any that are plainly upstream: provenance standards and signing at capture; forensic detection that does not rely on provenance; institutional reconstruction protocols for courts, elections, insurers, and newsrooms; liability rules that shift incentives for platforms, camera makers, cloud providers, and state broadcasters; the design of an epistemic circuit breaker, meaning a publicly understood procedure for suspending belief during fast-moving crises without creating a vacuum for rumor; interpersonal and small-group norms for testimony, apology, blackmail resistance, and refusal to share; cognitive and design interventions that change what users demand before believing; and comparative work on which communities are likely to adopt reality rationing first.

For each item, say why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is without new model capabilities, how neglected it is relative to its leverage, and what feedback within eighteen to twenty-four months would show that the ranking is wrong. Identify which popular line of work you would deprioritize because it has a short adversarial half-life, offloads backstop load onto already distrusted institutions, or treats media literacy as a substitute for enforceable incentives. Where two items conflict, state the trade-off explicitly: prevention versus recovery, centralized verification versus distributed corroboration, friction for citizens versus friction for propagandists, protection of dissidents and abuse victims versus protection of the record.

End with a defended portfolio: the six projects, the order in which they should start, the two or three implications for journalism, courts, elections, insurance, and private life that would genuinely change practice if taken seriously, and the alleged implications that are overrated or do not follow. No bibliography, reading list, or citations; argue from the concepts given and from widely known features of testimony, evidence, institutions, and adversarial systems.
