I teach metaphysics at a small university, and last week, after my graduate seminar on dispositions, two of my students stayed behind and argued for nearly an hour. The seminar had covered the simple conditional analysis of dispositions -- roughly, that a glass is fragile just in case it would break if struck -- and the two standard families of counterexamples: finks, where the disposition would be removed (or installed) by the very onset of the stimulus, and masks, where the disposition and stimulus are both present but something blocks the manifestation. Here is the exchange as best I reconstruct it.

(1) Nadia defended the conditional analysis against masks. A glass wrapped in bubble wrap that survives a fall does not refute the analysis, she said, because such a glass is not struck in the relevant sense: the stimulus condition for fragility includes the absence of protective interference. Masks show only that stimulus conditions must be specified carefully, not that the conditional analysis fails.

(2) Theo replied that there is no careful specification that is not circular. Any refinement generous enough to include all genuine masks collapses into "struck in conditions under which the disposition manifests," and those conditions can only be picked out by reference to the very disposition being analyzed. So Nadia's refinement is either false -- it wrongly excludes real masked cases -- or it smuggles in the analysandum.

(3) Nadia conceded that masks are awkward for her but pivoted: finks are the decisive cases, and they resist even a circular refinement. Consider a wire that is live now but attached to a device that would instantly render it dead the moment it touched a conductor. The wire is live, yet it is false that current would flow if the wire were connected, because the connection removes the disposition. Here the stimulus genuinely and unambiguously occurs, so no redescription of the stimulus condition can help; the fink targets the disposition itself. Her conclusion: conditional analyses should be abandoned entirely, and dispositions treated as primitive properties not analyzable via counterfactuals.

(4) Theo answered that primitivism is an overreaction. Finks can be handled by temporal indexing: the glass would break if struck while remaining fragile throughout the striking. More importantly, he argued, the fink/mask distinction Nadia's pivot depends on is itself unstable. Whether the bubble wrap "blocks the manifestation" or "temporarily removes the fragility" seems to be a matter of how we carve up the causal chain; a mask that acts quickly enough is indistinguishable from a fink. If the two cases really collapse into one another, then one treatment must cover both, and Nadia cannot concede masks while weaponizing finks.

(5) Nadia's final move: even granting borderline cases, temporal order does the relevant work. Finks operate on the disposition before or at the onset of the stimulus; masks operate on the manifestation afterward. Our intuitions track exactly this ordering, which is why the wrapped glass strikes everyone as still fragile while the finked wire's being live is genuinely contested. The asymmetry is itself data that any adequate account must explain, and her primitivism can explain it -- dispositions are basic causal features, and their persistence through a stimulus is a separate question from their manifestation.

They have now both asked me, in front of the rest of the seminar, to say who won, and I find I cannot do it off the cuff. Please adjudicate the dispute move by move: rate each of the five numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both parties are overlooking.
