So I'm drafting an essay for a grad student workshop and I want feedback before I submit it. The essay is below. It's about transformative experience (in roughly L.A. Paul's sense -- choices where you can't know what it'll be like to live with the outcome, and where the outcome changes what you value) but tries to push the problem somewhere I haven't seen it pushed: collective decisions.

Here's the draft:

---

**The Electorate That Won't Exist Yet**

L.A. Paul argues that certain choices -- having a child, getting a cochlear implant, becoming a vampire in her fanciful case -- resist standard decision theory because you can't imaginatively project yourself into the outcome, and because the outcome changes your preferences, so the self who reaps the consequences values things differently from the self who chose. Fine. But Paul frames this as a problem for individuals. I think the harder version is collective.

Consider a near-future referendum: should the state fund and strongly incentivize empathy-enhancing neural implants? Suppose (idealize a bit) that the implants work, that recipients reliably come to care much more about strangers and future generations, and that most recipients retrospectively endorse having gotten the implant -- they say things like "I can't believe I almost voted no." Now: is the referendum legitimate?

The natural thought is that a vote is legitimate when it tracks the informed preferences of the electorate. But which electorate? The pre-implant electorate can't imaginatively project into post-implant life, so their preferences are uninformed in Paul's sense. The post-implant electorate is well-informed but it's a different body with different values -- and, crucially, its composition depends on the outcome of the very vote in question. If the referendum passes, future policy gets made by people whose values were partly manufactured by the referendum. There's a bootstrap: the decision partly creates the constituency that ratifies it.

You might say: let people try it reversibly first -- a trial period, then decide. I think this fails, and the failure is interesting. The trial is only informative if you take the post-trial self's verdict as authoritative. But the post-trial self is exactly the transformed one. Treating its endorsement as decisive just smuggles in the principle that transformed preferences get to judge their own origins, which is the thing under dispute. And treating the pre-trial self's verdict as decisive reinstates the ignorance problem. There's no neutral vantage point from which to score the trial.

My tentative proposal: legitimacy in transformative collective choices requires what I call counterfactual self-consent -- the choice is legitimate only if the chooser, before transformation, would endorse it under the stipulation that they retain their current values while fully knowing the post-transformation phenomenology. This is meant to thread the needle: informed but not converted.

Then I argue the proposal fails too, for a reason I find genuinely worrying. Full knowledge of the phenomenology, if it were achievable, might itself be transformative. Knowing what it's like to feel others' suffering vividly may already change what you value, so the stipulation (retain current values + know the phenomenology) could be incoherent. If that's right, then in the most important cases there is *no* possible epistemic position from which a transformative collective choice can be rationally evaluated, and the legitimacy question has no answer -- not "it's hard to answer," but literally no standpoint exists. I end by suggesting this generalizes: any society deciding whether to adopt a preference-altering technology faces a hole where democratic theory assumes a fixed demos, and standard fixes (deliberation, hypothetical consent, future-generations trustees) all quietly presuppose the standpoint that doesn't exist.
