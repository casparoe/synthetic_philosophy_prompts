I've come to accept the view that strict criminal liability is basically never justified -- if the state punishes someone who took all the care a reasonable person could take, it's punishing them for sheer bad luck, and that breaks something fundamental about what blame and punishment are for. The usual defenses (deterring really risky industries, the practical impossibility of proving mens rea in thousands of regulatory cases) strike me as administrative convenience dressed up as justice. So: what would count against my position? Don't just run through the standard objections. Tell me which specific arguments or cases a fellow strict-liability skeptic would have to honestly concede as damaging, which ones they could reasonably shrug off, and rank the threats from fatal down to merely awkward. One wrinkle I especially want addressed: negligence liability already punishes people for capacities they may lack -- the clumsy or slow-witted defendant who genuinely couldn't meet the reasonable person standard gets convicted anyway. If I'm ok with that, does my luck-based objection to strict liability prove too much, and if I'm not ok with it, how much of criminal law am I actually committed to tearing down? End with a definite verdict -- should I abandon the view, qualify it in a specific way, or hold firm -- and give the actual reason, not a balanced summary.
