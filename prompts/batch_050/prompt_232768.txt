I run a weekly discussion section for an undergraduate course on rational choice theory — roughly 16 students, one 75-minute meeting. They have just read informal presentations of the money-pump argument for transitivity and the Dutch book argument for probabilism, and they all nod along, but I can tell the arguments feel like clever tricks rather than constraints on them. Design an in-class activity with real (small, non-monetary) stakes in which students' own elicited choices end up generating a guaranteed loss or an exploitable cycle for at least some of them, so that the sure-loss consideration lands as something that happened to them before it is named as a theorem. The activity should work in two phases: first, an elicitation phase in which students individually answer a series of pairwise choice questions (over lotteries, or over prospects you specify) under conditions that make axiom violations likely — e.g., choices designed to induce preference reversals or intransitivities via framing and common-ratio effects; second, an exploitation phase in which the facilitator (or a designated student playing the role of the bookie/pump operator) uses only a student's own recorded answers to offer them a sequence of trades they have already committed to finding acceptable, until the cycle or sure loss is visible on the board. Specify: the exact choice menus to use and why those menus predictably produce violations; the rules of engagement (whether students can see each other's answers, whether they can revise earlier answers mid-stream, whether declining a trade is allowed and what that does to the argument); a payoff scheme using points that convert to something mildly desirable but not money; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief protocol that maps specific moments — the first trade a student accepts, the moment the cycle closes, the revision requests — onto the axioms and the structure of the formal arguments. Two further requests. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students declining trades, students revising rather than paying, students producing perfectly consistent answers by luck or caution) and how to design the menus and rules so these failure modes become material for the debrief rather than ruining it. Second, include an honest coda on what the activity cannot establish: the money pump shows that some answer in the set must go, but is silent on which one, and a student who pays the pump and then says "fine, I'll just keep my preferences and stop trading" is raising a real objection the formal argument must answer — tell me how to use that moment rather than paper over it, and which premise of the coherence arguments (the assumption that the agent will act on each preference in sequence, say) the game has to build in by fiat and therefore cannot make participants feel.
