In my graduate seminar on decision theory, we spent the past two weeks on the so-called "weird" problems — cases where an agent is asked to act on the basis of a counterfactual that never obtained — and my students split into two camps that could not be reconciled by the end of the session. I would like you to help me produce, for next week's meeting, the document those two camps would issue if they ran a proper adversarial collaboration. The first camp defends what I'll call the actualist position: an agent should deliberate only over the causal (or at least actual) consequences of her present act, so in the counterfactual mugging — where Omega, a near-perfect predictor, explains that he flipped a coin, and had it landed the other way he would have given you a large sum, but since it landed as it did he asks you for a smaller payment instead — the rational move is to refuse, since paying benefits no one and the coin's other outcome is now merely hypothetical. The second camp defends the policy-selection position associated with updateless or functional decision theory: what is rational is not the act but the policy, and the policy of paying in exactly this situation is the one an agent would have wanted to commit to ex ante, before the coin was flipped; refusing is locally tempting but globally self-defeating, because predictors reward those who would have paid.

Draft the joint statement both camps could sign. It should contain a numbered section of agreed claims — about the setup of the counterfactual mugging itself, about what Omega's predictive reliability does and does not establish, about the coherence of ex ante commitment, and about what each side's theory actually says in the ordinary cases (e.g., that both agree on the right answer in Newcomb's problem, or wherever they in fact converge) — stated precisely enough that neither side would find its own position misrepresented. Follow this with a disagreements table: each row should give the exact point of divergence, phrased in terms each camp would accept for its own view; identify whether the disagreement is normative (about what rationality requires), conceptual (about what counts as an act versus a policy), or merely verbal; and name the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would force at least one side to update.

Finally, extend the analysis to XOR blackmail, where the predictor's letter (demanding payment on threat of an outcome that, if you are the kind of agent who refuses, will occur anyway with the letter arriving only in the branch where it does) seems to pressure even the policy-selection camp into paying for no benefit in any branch. For each camp, state whether the case is a reductio, a natural extension, or genuinely independent of its position on the mugging; where the two camps agree that some agent-design or commitment mechanism dissolves both problems, say so explicitly; and close with a short section specifying, for each remaining crux, what argument, formal result, or thought experiment both sides would accept in advance as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded.
