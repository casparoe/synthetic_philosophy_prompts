I trained as a nurse and have no formal education in philosophy, but I have been turning one question over for years, ever since the pandemic forced my ward into triage decisions I still think about: are there situations in which a decent person cannot avoid doing wrong, whatever they choose? A friend who studied some philosophy has been pressing me to write down exactly what I believe about this before I bring it to our discussion group, and I would like the set stress-tested first. My commitments, as best I can state them: (a) genuine dilemmas exist — cases where every available course of action is wrong, as when I have one dose of a drug, two patients who will each die without the full dose, and no third option, so that whichever patient I treat, I fail someone I was bound to help; (b) it makes no sense to say that someone ought to do what it is impossible for them to do — my friend tells me this is a principle with a long pedigree, and it strikes me as obvious; (c) obligations combine — if I ought to do one thing and I ought to do another, then I ought to do both, since two genuine duties do not cancel each other out or merely sit side by side; (d) the guilt that conscientious people feel after choices like the triage case is appropriate and tracks something real, rather than being a confusion they should be talked out of; (e) the entire point of morality is to guide action, so a morality that offers no usable verdict in a situation has failed at its job; and (f) sometimes two options are exactly equally good, in which case choosing either is wholly permissible — ties seem to me ordinary and unproblematic.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Please go through these commitments and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to. I am suspicious of exercises of this kind that flag every pair as problematic, so please discriminate: rank any real tensions by severity, and for each one say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me — including, in particular, what happens to (d) under each proposed fix, since that commitment matters to me personally. Where two commitments look at first glance as though they conflict but the appearance dissolves on inspection, say so and explain why. Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the set as a whole can survive with a single change, or whether I am holding something incoherent at its core.
