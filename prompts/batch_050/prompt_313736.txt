PhD student in decision theory here, and I keep bumping into a pattern I can't find a clean name for. Example one: a VC tells all twenty of her founders to take the high-variance moonshot path, because across her portfolio that maximizes expected return -- a few crater, one 100x's, fund is happy. And the advice is genuinely correct from where she sits. But each founder only gets one life, and for them the modal outcome of following it is ruin. Example two: the standard line that any gamble with positive expected value is worth taking, said about a bet each person has to play over and over, where a single loss knocks them out of the game entirely so they never reach the long run. The general pattern: advice that's right when you average across many people, but wrong for the one individual who has to live the outcomes one at a time, no pooling, no reset button. It's not a principal-agent thing, because the advisor often means well and would give the same advice to their own kid. And its not just risk aversion, because even a fully risk-neutral person should refuse once losses compound. Does this specific mismatch have an established name, and if not, can you coin one and define it so it clearly excludes both ordinary conflicts of interest and plain risk aversion? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning -- no preamble, dont restate my question.
