I got into it with some friends at dinner last week about how much morality can actually demand of us, and I've been trying to write down my position since, because I kept insisting mine was consistent and one of them kept insisting it wasn't. Here's roughly where I stand, and I'd like an honest audit of whether it hangs together. (1) Singer is clearly right about the pond case: if you walk past a drowning kid because you don't want to wreck your new shoes, that's monstrous, full stop. (2) But the conclusion people draw from it -- that you're obligated to give away most of your income to effective charities until you're down to near the level of the people you're helping -- strikes me as a reductio of whatever premise generates it, not a conclusion to accept. (3) My instinct is that the real line is emergencies versus background conditions: morality can demand a huge amount from you in a one-off emergency in front of you, but it can't turn your entire life into an ongoing fundraising operation. (4) Relatedly, I don't think physical or cultural distance matters morally at all -- the kid in front of you and the kid overseas count exactly the same, and I think the "she's not *my* neighbor" move is a total cop-out. (5) I do think morality can require real sacrifice -- I'd say you're obligated to skip a vacation to fund something life-saving if the opportunity lands in your lap -- so I'm not just rigging the theory to protect my bank account. (6) And I think giving, say, ten percent of your income every year is genuinely admirable but strictly supererogatory -- praiseworthy if you do it, not wrong if you don't.

Now the part my friend kept hammering: she asked me about the recurring pond. Suppose your commute route past the pond means there's a drowning kid there every single morning, forever, and each time the cost of saving them is roughly a pair of shoes. Either morality makes you ruin your shoes every day (in which case it demands exactly the kind of life-structuring sacrifice I rejected in (2)), or at some point you're allowed to keep walking, in which case the verdict in (1) gets shaky in a way I don't love. I don't have a clean answer and I sort of suspect the recurring case is unfair somehow, but I can't say why.

So: which of my six commitments are actually in tension, and how badly? Which pairs only *look* like they conflict but can be made to fit? For each genuine tension, tell me what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me, rank the tensions by severity, and tell me which single revision would buy me the most consistency. Please answer in American English, and don't just lecture me about what Singer thinks -- I want to know whether *my* position survives, and if it doesn't, which piece is cheapest to drop.
