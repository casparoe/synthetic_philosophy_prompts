I write literary fiction for a living and lead a monthly manuscript workshop of eight serious writers (mixed experience, all adults, sessions of two and a half hours). A recurring failure in the love stories we read is that they collapse in one of two directions. Either the beloved is presented as a bundle of admirable qualities -- wit, kindness, a particular laugh -- in which case the love reads as fungible, since anyone with slightly more of those qualities should, by the story's own logic, win out. Or the love is simply asserted as groundless and inexplicable, in which case it reads as authorial fiat rather than as anything a real person could feel. I have come to think this is the craft-face of a genuine philosophical puzzle about whether love is a response to the beloved's value (an appraisal, roughly in Velleman's sense), something the lover creates rather than discovers (a bestowal, roughly in Frankfurt's sense), or grounded in the shared history of the relationship itself (roughly Kolodny's suggestion). I do not want to lecture my writers on any of this. I want them to run into the problem at the level of the sentence.

Design a structured writing activity for this group that makes each participant feel the pull of all three positions by being forced to write under them. I am imagining something with rounds and constraints: for instance, a round in which a participant must render a character's love persuasive using only the beloved's qualities, followed by a complication -- a rival character who exceeds the beloved on every dimension just named -- that exposes what that account of love cannot say; then further rounds in which quality-talk and history-talk are forbidden or permitted in various combinations, so participants discover firsthand which resources each position actually gives a writer. The activity should produce real, shareable fragments of prose, since that is what my group trusts as evidence.

Please specify: the exact rules and constraints of each round, including word limits and what gets read aloud; a timeline that fits two and a half hours with eight people; what participants should come away seeing about why fictional love is so hard to motivate; a debrief plan that maps specific moments of frustration in the writing onto the three philosophical positions (named only at this stage, briefly and accurately, since no one will have read them); the two or three ways this activity will predictably go wrong in practice -- participants gaming the constraints, conflating a character's stated reasons with the story's shown reasons, and so on -- and how I should handle each; and an honest note on which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce through fiction alone, since feeling that a portrayal is unconvincing is not the same as settling what actually justifies love.
