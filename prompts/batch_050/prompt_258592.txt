For a university workshop on tax policy and political philosophy, I have been constructing hypothetical dockets for a mock commission charged with designing an inheritance system that promotes equal opportunity without treating every act of parental assistance as illegitimate. The commission has ruled that an ordinary family home may pass untaxed up to the regional median value, with any excess value taxed; that a $40 million portfolio of publicly traded securities should be taxed heavily at death; that a controlling stake in a family business should not trigger an immediate tax if payment would force liquidation, but should incur an annual surcharge above a threshold and be fully taxed when sold or converted into dividends; that lifetime gifts and transfers into trust must be treated like bequests rather than used as loopholes; that a trust paying a descendant’s elite private-school tuition is a taxable transfer even though the descendant never receives cash; and that a family-controlled foundation employing the founder’s descendants does not qualify as charity merely because it supports some public projects.

The next case involves a founder who transfers $10 million in cash plus a portfolio of difficult-to-value patents into a 120-year trust. The patents might ultimately be worth almost nothing or more than $100 million. Descendants may never receive cash, own trust assets, or demand distributions. The trust may pay only for accredited education, medical care, childcare, modest housing during full-time study, and startup capital awarded through a competitive process administered by an independent board. However, the founder’s family may replace trustees, amend the eligibility rules within broad limits, and prevent the trust from terminating early. The commission must decide whether to tax the entire estimated value immediately, tax the cash now while deferring tax on the patents until they are sold or licensed, exempt payments for education and health while taxing the other benefits, or distinguish in some other way between human-capital support and the transmission of dynastic control. What ruling would best cohere with the commission’s earlier decisions? Identify the closest precedent, reconstruct at least two general principles that fit the existing record but diverge here, say which principle the commission would implicitly adopt under each possible ruling, and describe one further hypothetical case that would best reveal which principle the commission is actually following.
