I'm putting together a seminar session that pairs David Fincher's The Social Network with the philosophy-of-science debate over simultaneous discovery -- Newton and Leibniz and the calculus, Darwin and Wallace and natural selection, and the sociological claim that "multiples" are the norm rather than the exception. Read the film through that debate. On one reading, the film dramatizes the inevitability thesis: social networking was in the air (Friendster and MySpace already existed, every campus had or wanted a directory), so the Winklevoss dispute is a mere squabble over priority in something that would have happened regardless. On another, the film sides with the view that the execution is the invention -- the twins contributed an idea and a conversation, while what Zuckerberg actually built, feature by feature, was a different and better thing. Which reading does the film as a whole support, and where does each strain against particular scenes (the twins' pitch meeting, the coding montages, the depositions that frame everything, the final scene)? I'd also like to know what the film adds that the philosophical literature lacks: does translating a priority dispute into litigation over ownership, rather than a fight over scientific credit, clarify what's actually at stake in multiples, or distort it? If you think the pairing rests on a false analogy -- say, because a commercial product isn't a discovery in the relevant sense -- say so plainly instead of forcing the reading. Please write in American English.
