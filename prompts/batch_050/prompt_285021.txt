I cover defense and emerging tech for a general-audience science magazine, and I'm reporting a piece on autonomous weapons. Every expert I interview eventually invokes "meaningful human control," which made me realize my own gut verdicts on existing systems are all over the place. I want to know what principle I'm actually running on, if any. Here are my verdicts:

- A WWII-era naval contact mine that detonates when a ship hits it: grim, but historically accepted and not really what the current debate is about. Fine-ish.
- A Phalanx-style close-in weapon system that detects, tracks, and fires on incoming anti-ship missiles completely on its own, faster than any human could approve shots; a human can switch it off, but in the moment it acts with zero human input: I'm fine with this.
- A loitering munition launched into an area that then selects which armored vehicle to dive onto: not fine.
- A homing torpedo that after launch picks and tracks its own ship: honestly I think it's basically acceptable, though I can't articulate how it differs from the loitering munition.
- An aerial drone where the software designates the target and a human operator gets about 10 seconds to confirm or abort: feels worse than the Phalanx even though there's nominally a human "in the loop" -- more like a rubber stamp.
- An anti-personnel landmine: zero autonomy, maximally dumb, and yet it's the one thing on this list that's actually banned, and I think rightly so.

So: is there a single coherent principle that captures these verdicts, or comes close? State it precisely, flag which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and tell me what it implies for a case I haven't listed -- say, a stationary sentry gun on a border that fires on anyone who crosses a clearly marked line. I keep going back and fourth on whether the operative thing is who's targeted, how fast events unfold, or whether the human involvement is genuine.

End your reply with \answer{...} containing your best principle compressed into one line.
