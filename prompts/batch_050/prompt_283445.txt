Two colleagues of mine, Marta and Deniz, got into an argument at a workshop about their firm's proposed four-day week, and it escalated past the policy into first principles. I'd like a referee.

Marta opened: leisure is the default and work is what needs justifying. A life is made out of free time; work is instrumentally valuable at best. So a reduction in hours needs no defense beyond "people would rather be living," and the burden of proof falls entirely on whoever demands the extra hours.

Deniz replied: your leisure rides on other people's labor — the power grid, the hospital, the food supply all have to be staffed while you relax. If everyone treated leisure as the default, there would be nothing left to leisurely consume. A "right to leisure" is really a claim on someone else's work, so the actual default is contribution, and leisure is what has to be earned.

Marta came back: that proves too much — someone somewhere is always working, so by that logic nobody may ever rest. And it misdescribes the real pathology. Karoshi victims in Japan are not people who failed to earn their rest; they were crushed by a norm that equates hours with loyalty. The point of reforms like the French 35-hour week was precisely to break such norms, not to claim a free ride.

Deniz closed: you can't appeal to norms selectively — if workplace norms can coerce people into overwork, consumer norms can coerce people into feeling entitled to leisure they haven't funded. And capping hours never abolishes the work; it redistributes it, since someone still staffs the weekend. So the real question isn't whether work or leisure is the default, but who bears which hours — and neither of your principles answers that.

Give your assessment as a table with the columns: Move (speaker and claim) | Verdict (largely right / partly right / mistaken) | What succeeds | What fails. Then add one final row, Overall, stating who is more right and in one sentence why.
