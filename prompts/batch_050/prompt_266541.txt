Ok so I need some philosophy-of-language brainpower, both for a piece I'm reporting and honestly for my own peace of mind. I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and untill recently I never thought twice about how our newsroom handles the gap between what we literally write and what readers take away. Here's the setup:

1. Fact-checking is sentence-by-sentence. The checker's whole job is to confirm that each individual sentence is literally true. There is no step anywhere in the process where someone asks "what would a reasonable reader conclude from the article as a whole?"
2. We lean hard on hedged verbs in health coverage: a study "links" a pesticide to Parkinson's, a finding "raises questions about" a supplement, results "suggest" a connection. Everyone involved knows the underlying studies are correlational. The sentence is defensible; the vibe is not.
3. The disclaimer ritual: somewhere around paragraph 14 we drop in a line like "association does not prove causation." Editors treat that line as cancelling whatever the headline and lede implied. Once it's in, the piece is considered clean, even though we all know most readers never get to paragraph 14.
4. Corrections policy: we correct factual errors, full stop. If a reader writes in saying a literally true article left them with a completely false impression, the corrections desk replies that we stand by the reporting. Misleading-ness is not a correctable category.
5. Meanwhile our media lawyer tells us we can absolutely be sued over a story where every single sentence checks out, if the overall impression it creates is false and damaging. She calls it defamation by implication. So the law apparently holds us responsible for implications that our own standards explicitly refuse to track.

My editor's position: our duty runs to the literal content only, and what readers infer is their own business. The lawyer's position (and, judging by my inbox, the readers' position): if you knowingly craft an implication, it's yours.

So here's the actual request. Put on your philosopher-of-language hat -- you're an expert on Grice, implicature, the saying/implicating distinction, the whole toolkit -- but you also understand how newsrooms actually function and you don't reach for theory where a mundane institutional explanation will do. Take your time, and start by brainstorming two or three candidate reconstructions before you commit to the one you think is best. Then:

- What view of meaning does this bundle of practices presuppose? The fact-check + disclaimer + corrections setup only seems to make sense given some picture of where "the meaning of a story" lives and what a speaker is on the hook for. Spell that picture out explicitly.
- Which elements strictly presuppose that view, and which merely fit it -- i.e., could be equally well justified on a rival theory of communication? Where is the practice just philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it?
- Is the package coherent? Can you consistently run a literal-truth-only corrections standard, treat the paragraph-14 disclaimer as canceling the headline's implicature, AND accept legal liability for implied falsehoods? My gut says something has to give; tell me what, and which piece is the odd one out.
- Get properly into the weeds on the disclaimer. Does Gricean cancellation even work the way my editors assume? Is an implicature cancelled by a clause sitting where most of the audience never reads it -- in other words, is cancellation a fact about the text, or a fact about the audience's uptake, and which of those does our practice assume?
- Finally: if the newsroom adopted the rival view, that speakers own the implicatures they knowingly craft, what would concretely change? Redesign the fact-checking checklist, the disclaimer ritual, and the corrections policy under that view.
