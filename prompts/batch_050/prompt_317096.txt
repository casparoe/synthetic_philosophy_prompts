Please explain a tension I keep running into between semantic externalism and first-person authority, and help me understand whether it is a genuine problem or an illusion created by an equivocation.

The setup, as I understand it: semantic externalism says that what my words and thoughts are about depends partly on my environment. In Putnam's Twin Earth case, my internally identical twin on a planet where the clear, drinkable, lake-filling liquid is XYZ rather than H2O uses the word 'water' to mean XYZ, not water, even though nothing inside our heads differs. Separately, I seem able to know what I am thinking without investigating the world: I can know that I am now thinking about water simply by reflecting, not by doing chemistry.

The tension is that combining the two claims appears to let me deduce, from the armchair, a substantive empirical fact. If I know a priori that I am thinking a water-thought, and I know a priori (from the Twin Earth argument itself) that water-thoughts are available only to thinkers embedded in an environment containing water (or some relevantly water-like natural kind in their causal history), then I seem able to conclude a priori that my environment contains such a kind. That conclusion feels absurd: introspection plus a thought experiment should not settle questions about the chemistry of my surroundings.

There is also a related worry about slow switching. If I were unknowingly transported to Twin Earth, my word 'water' would, over time, come to mean XYZ without my ever noticing the change. That seems to threaten not my knowledge of the environment but my knowledge of my own mind: the very first-person authority the argument started from.

Please address why the natural escape routes work or fail; whether the slow-switching case is a worse problem than the original argument or merely a restatement of it; and whether the whole tension dissolves once we distinguish knowing *that* one is thinking from knowing *what* one is thinking.

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

- The puzzle stated plainly (one paragraph): reconstruct the tension as an explicit argument with numbered premises and an absurd conclusion, making the strongest version of it you can, in your own words.
- Where the argument leans (two to three paragraphs): identify which premise or inference step carries the weight, and walk through why each of the obvious replies — denying a premise, holding that the warrant fails to transmit from premises to conclusion, or relocating the a priori knowledge to a conditional claim — either fails or costs more than it first appears to.
- Compatibilist strategies (two paragraphs): lay out the main approaches for keeping both externalism and privileged self-knowledge, and state honestly what each one concedes.
- Slow switching (one to two paragraphs): explain why this variant is or is not harder than the original argument, and exactly what it threatens that the original does not.
- Assessment (one paragraph): your candid judgment of whether the tension is real or an artifact of equivocation, and which premise you would abandon if forced to abandon one.

Do not cite specific papers or authors beyond Putnam's Twin Earth case itself; I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
