So I've recently gotten really into the Principal Principle -- the idea that your credences should defer to known objective chances, so if I know a coin has an 80% bias toward heads, my credence in heads should be 0.8 (modulo inadmissible info). I'm mostly convinced, and I'm actually starting to use it as a load-bearing premise in some decision-theoretic stuff I'm working on. But before I lean on it that hard, I want to know what would count against it. Not just the usual grumbling about what "objective chance" even means, but the real pressure points: are there cases where a rational agent seems justified in *not* matching their credence to a known chance? Does it survive if chances turn out to be mind-dependent or if the future is deterministic in a way that makes objective single-case chances dubious? And what about the undermining problem with admissibility -- does that hole get patched or does it show the principle was never as clean as advertised? I'd like the objections ranked from "actually fatal if it holds" to "awkward but survivable," plus a sense of which ones defenders of the principle would themselves concede are the soft spots. Answer in American English please.
