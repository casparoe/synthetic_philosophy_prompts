I host a long-form philosophy-of-science podcast and am preparing an interview with the author of a new book, *The Shortest Explanation*. The book argues that Solomonoff induction finally converts Occam's razor from a vague methodological maxim into a theorem, and that science's preference for simple theories is therefore rationally compelled rather than a convenient habit. The book rests on three central claims:

1. The choice of universal reference machine in defining Kolmogorov complexity is no more troubling than the choice of units in physics, because the invariance theorem bounds the difference between any two machines by an additive constant.
2. Resource-bounded approximations of the ideal predictor (in the spirit of Levin search and minimum description length methods) are computable enough for real scientific practice, so the uncomputability of the ideal is an objection in principle only.
3. The universal prior is the unique prior that smuggles in no inductive assumptions, and therefore provides the first non-circular answer to the problem of induction.

The author is polished and well-rehearsed. Standard objections slide off her: mention uncomputability and she says "approximation is the whole history of applied mathematics"; press the machine-dependence point and she invokes the invariance theorem as if the constant were small; ask whether the universal prior is itself biased and she replies that uniform weighting over programs is the only position that doesn't beg the question.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions, ordered from broad to pointed, that turns on the actual content of these three claims. For each question, also give me the reply the author is most likely to make and the follow-up question that blocks it. The sequence should, among other things, force her to confront: the difference between a bound that holds asymptotically and a guarantee that holds at the finite data scales where scientists actually choose theories; the fact that switching reference machines, unlike switching units, does not transform all descriptions uniformly, so that a perverse machine can make any favored hypothesis the "simplest"; the way minimum description length methods reintroduce the contested choice one level up, in the selection of model classes and encodings; and the worry that a prior concentrated on computable worlds is itself a substantive inductive commitment, so that claim (3) trades an old circularity for a new one. End the sequence with a question about what mathematical or empirical result would count against her project — and whether, if no such result exists, the book has offered an epistemology or merely a definition.

Please write the questions in a register suitable for an intelligent lay audience, and write the entire response in American English.
