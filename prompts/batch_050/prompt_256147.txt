I'm assembling a handout of diagnostics for distinguishing indicative from counterfactual conditionals — phenomena that any adequate semantic theory of the two moods has to explain. So far I have four:

1. Minimal pairs differing only in mood that appear to differ in truth value: compare the indicative 'if the butler didn't poison the count, someone else did' with the counterfactual 'if the butler hadn't poisoned the count, someone else would have' — the first looks assertable on the mere evidence that the count was murdered, the second does not.
2. Antecedents taken to be false: an indicative whose antecedent the speaker regards as certainly false is conversationally odd or misleading, whereas counterfactuals are felicitous precisely in that situation.
3. Information-sensitivity: an indicative can become unassertable purely because the speaker learns something new, without any change in the world; counterfactuals seem answerable instead to causal or nomic structure.
4. Embedding under epistemic modals: 'it might be that' composes differently with the two moods — a might-indicative can be acceptable where the corresponding might-counterfactual is not, and vice versa.

Give me a fifth diagnostic that is not on this list and not a variant of anything on it — so not another minimal pair of the same shape, and not a restatement of information-sensitivity as a point about evidence. Then say whether your diagnostic tells for or against the view that the difference in mood is a difference in truth conditions rather than in pragmatics or conditions of appropriate use.
