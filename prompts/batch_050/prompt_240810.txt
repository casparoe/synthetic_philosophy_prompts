Write a parody of a homeowners' association covenant -- the "Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions" document new residents receive on moving in -- for a subdivision whose board has converted wholesale to communitarian political theory. About 650 words, formatted as numbered clauses with a preamble, written in the plodding, officious legalese of a real HOA document, and deadpan throughout: no winking, no asides.

The governing joke is that the document takes the communitarian critique of liberalism entirely seriously as a basis for neighborhood governance. The preamble should declare that no resident arrives at the subdivision as an unencumbered self, freely choosing ends from behind some imagined veil, but is instead always already a member -- obligated by debts, roles, and histories that precede any signature on the deed. The clauses should then follow this through: a rule that paint-color disputes cannot be settled by neutral procedures acceptable to all rational parties, but only by reference to the subdivision's shared conception of the good streetscape; a clause treating landscaping duties as constitutive obligations, discovered rather than chosen, which bind the homeowner whether or not he would have consented to them; a requirement that each household be able to give a narrative account of its lawn's place in the continuing story of the neighborhood; mandatory attendance at the annual festival, since the community's good takes priority over any individual's schedule of rights; and a provision denying that one may "opt out" of the community by appeal to privacy, since the self that would do the opting out is constituted by the very membership it seeks to escape.

The satire should work on two levels at once. The document must be faithful enough to the actual position -- the critique of the disembedded chooser, the priority of the right over the good coming under pressure, membership and narrative doing real work -- that a reader who knows the Sandel-Rawls debate recognizes that the author has understood it rather than merely name-dropped. At the same time, the standard liberal rejoinders should be visible through the seams of the text without ever being stated: whose community, exactly, gets to define the shared good (it is, of course, whatever the board happens to prefer); whether traditions are discovered or simply inherited power with a nicer name; whether the "unencumbered self" is really such a fiction when the covenant's own enforcement clause threatens fines that presume a chooser who could have done otherwise; and whether a self wholly constituted by its community could ever have standing to object to anything the board does -- a question the document should raise, in its final clause, only by making the objection procedurally impossible.

A nonspecialist should find it funny simply as an absurdly tyrannical HOA letter. A specialist should find it fair.
