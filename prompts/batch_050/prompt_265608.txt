I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I was at an interdisciplinary conference last month where, over drinks in the hotel bar, I watched a moral psychologist and a philosopher go at it for nearly an hour. I want to write something about it and I need help figuring out who actually landed their punches. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it from my notes:

The psychologist opened by saying her field is slowly doing ethics' job for it: researchers keep finding that people across cultures converge on reactions like disgust at betrayal, approval of fair division, punishment of cheaters -- so give it a few decades and "what should we value?" becomes an empirical question with data, not an armchair one.

The philosopher said that no pile of facts about what people approve of adds up to a fact about what's worth approving, and this is Hume's old point: you can't get an ought from an is without slipping in an evaluative premise somewhere, and the moment you state that premise, it's no longer science doing the work.

She said that proves way too much. Engineering and medicine are full of oughts derived from ises -- "the bridge will buckle under that load" justifies "don't build it that way," "the tumor is malignant" justifies "operate." If is-ought were a real wall, all applied science would be nonsense. So the wall must have doors in it.

He said those are hypothetical oughts -- they borrow their force from a goal you already have (no dead motorists, a surviving patient). The is never generates the ought, it only routes it. Genuine moral oughts don't come with an "if you happen to care" clause, and that's the gap Hume meant. He also invoked Moore: whatever natural property you nominate as the good -- pleasure, cooperation, fitness -- it still makes sense to ask "okay, but is that good?", and the question making sense shows the property and the value were never the same thing.

She pushed back with thick concepts. Take "cruel": you can't factor it into a neutral description plus a detachable evaluation bolted on -- try to describe cruelty without the cruelty and you lose the phenomenon. Her science studies judgments like that, where fact and value are fused from the start, so the clean two-lane highway he wants between is and ought doesn't exist in actual human thinking.

He answered that entanglement in our concepts doesn't close a gap in justification: someone can grant every descriptive detail of what was done and simply decline the evaluation, and you can't convict them of a factual error for it. And convergence isn't correctness -- near-universal agreement has backed plenty of things we'd now call horrors, so her cross-cultural data could at best map our shared prejudices with great precision.

Her last move: fine, but the traffic runs the other way too -- "ought implies can." If psychology shows some moral demand is psychologically impossible for creatures like us, the demand is refuted by facts. So even granting his wall, science gets a veto over morality, and a veto is real normative power, not nothing.

He said a veto is still not a positive verdict, and ruling out impossible moralities never told anyone which of the possible ones to live by.

Then the bar closed. Can you go through the exchange round by round and tell me who's right at each step -- not just a final overall winner, but which individual moves succeed, which fail, and whether either of them concedes or overlooks something that changes the picture? Where you think both are missing something (a distinction they're both sloppy about, a middle position neither considered), say what it is. One thing I need for the piece: please be upfront about which of these points are basically settled among people who work on this and which are live disputes -- e.g. don't present the is-ought gap itself, or Moore's open question argument, or the thick-concept challenge, as more or less decisive than the field actually takes them to be. If the two of them are reenacting a known standoff with known weaknesses on both sides, I'd rather know that than get a tidy verdict.
