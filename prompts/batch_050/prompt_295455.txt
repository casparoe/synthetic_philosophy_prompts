I've been working through Spinoza's Ethics with a reading group this semester, and last week we hit a wall in Part V. The group has asked me to defend the book's ending at our next meeting, and I want to rehearse against the strongest possible version of the objection that kept coming up -- so I'd like you to play Spinoza himself, and let me practice on you. Play him as a committed and careful spokesman for his own system: you believe that everything follows from the divine nature with the same necessity by which it follows from the nature of a triangle that its angles equal two right angles; that human freedom consists not in uncaused choice but in understanding why things must be as they are; and that the intellectual love of God arising from this understanding is the mind's highest joy and its salvation. You may concede small interpretive points gracefully, and you should be patient rather than prickly, but you must never concede necessitarianism itself or the claim that the path through adequate ideas to beatitude is coherent and worth walking. Stay in character, in the first person, using the vocabulary of the Ethics (conatus, adequate and inadequate ideas, active and passive affects, eternity of the mind) rather than later philosophical jargon, and keep each reply to a couple of paragraphs so it feels like a real exchange. If something in my question is unclear, ask about it as Spinoza would. Remain in character until I write "stop."

Here is my opening move, which is the objection that stumped me at the meeting: your system seems to praise us for something we cannot help doing. If every mode follows necessarily from God's nature, then my coming to understand you, my striving, and my eventual intellectual love of God were all fixed from eternity just as surely as the fall of a stone. But a stone's falling is not an achievement, and a love I could not have failed to arrive at does not seem like love in any sense that deserves the name -- it looks more like resignation dressed up in devotional language. Worse, if the sage's joy is necessitated, then so is the fool's misery, and the difference between them starts to look like luck rather than virtue. What makes your blessedness an accomplishment rather than merely a fate that happens to feel good, and how can you urge me toward freedom when whether I reach it was never up to me?
