I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on equality and public policy, and I am drafting an essay arguing that inheritance taxation should be justified primarily as a safeguard of political equality rather than as a tool of economic redistribution. Below is my current draft. After it, I describe the kind of feedback I am looking for.

**Draft: The Ballot Behind the Bequest**

Defenders of inheritance taxation usually offer one of two justifications. The first is fiscal: the state needs revenue, and death is a convenient moment to collect it. The second is egalitarian in a familiar way: large bequests transmit unearned advantage, and equal opportunity requires leveling the starting line. I find both arguments weaker than their proponents assume, and I want to defend a third justification, which I will call the political-equality argument: substantial inheritance taxes are justified because concentrated wealth converts into political influence at an increasing rate, and a democracy that permits unlimited bequests is quietly permitting the hereditary accumulation of political power.

Why do I find the standard arguments weak? The fiscal argument is hostage to budget arithmetic: if revenue can be raised more efficiently elsewhere, the tax loses its rationale. The opportunity argument faces the objection that starting lines are never equal — talent, temperament, parental attention, and luck in friendships all differ — so it is unclear why inherited money, specifically, should be singled out unless money does something these other advantages do not.

My claim is that it does. Wealth is unusually *convertible*. A parent who is brilliant cannot deposit brilliance in a child's account, and a parent with good judgment cannot bequeath it on schedule. But money can be converted into campaign contributions, media ownership, foundation-building, lobbying, litigation, and the funding of think tanks and academic chairs — and these conversions appear to exhibit increasing rather than diminishing returns. Ten million dollars buys more than ten times the political reach of one million, because many forms of influence have thresholds: below a certain scale you cannot sustain a litigation strategy, found an institution, or keep an issue on the agenda for a decade. Call this the threshold effect. If the threshold effect is real, then a society that taxes income heavily but bequests lightly has a peculiar blind spot: it regulates the flow of influence while leaving the stock untouched, and it is the compounding stock — the dynasty — that poses the distinctive threat, because only dynastic wealth can pursue political projects across generations, outliving the individuals and even the controversies that generated the fortune.

Here is my central thought experiment, which I call the laundered-influence test. Imagine that a country permitted citizens to bequeath not money but votes: each person could leave their children additional ballots in every future election, in proportion to their estate. Almost everyone, I think, would find this intolerable, and not because voting is sacred but because political standing in a democracy is supposed to be non-heritable. Now notice that large unregulated bequests approximate this arrangement in practice, through the conversion channels above. The bequest of votes is just the bequest of money with the conversion made explicit. If the explicit version is intolerable, we need an account of why the implicit, laundered version is acceptable — and I do not think property rights alone supply one, since no one supposes property rights permit buying votes directly.

I consider two objections. First, the double-taxation objection: the money was already taxed when earned, so taxing it again at death punishes thrift and savings. My reply is that taxation attaches to transfers and events, not to metaphysical identity of money — a salary is taxed though the employer's revenue was taxed, and nobody calls that double taxation with moral force. Second, the existing-regulation objection: if the problem is political influence, we should regulate campaign finance and lobbying directly rather than taxing estates. My reply is that influence regulation is a leaky sieve — it addresses named channels while wealth finds unnamed ones, and the threshold effect means the sieve must be repaired forever. Taxing the stock at the point of transfer is blunter but does not depend on anticipating every channel of conversion.

I conclude that inheritance taxation is mis-sold when defended as revenue policy or as crude leveling. Its best defense is that it is the estate's turn to pass through the same filter democracy applies to everything else that claims to convert private advantage into public power.

Is this premise doing more work than I acknowledge, and does the essay survive if returns are merely constant rather than increasing?
