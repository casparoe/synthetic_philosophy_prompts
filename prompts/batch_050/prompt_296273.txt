Last weekend a friend and I argued until two in the morning about a pair of thought experiments due to Douglas Hofstadter, collected in his book Metamagical Themas, and I have decided to settle the matter by other means: by making a roomful of students live through the dispute rather than hear it summarised.

The first experiment, sometimes called the Platonia dilemma, runs roughly as follows. Twenty people each receive a letter from an eccentric billionaire. Each recipient may, if they wish, post back a single card volunteering themselves. If exactly one person volunteers, that person receives an enormous prize; if more than one volunteer, or if nobody does, nobody receives anything. Communication between the recipients is impossible, and each knows that the others are, roughly, as clever as they are. The second, sometimes called the Luring Lottery, runs thus. A national lottery is announced in which anyone may enter as many times as they like — you simply write on your entry how many chances you are claiming — and the advertised jackpot is then divided by the total number of entries submitted. The larger your own number, the greater your share of the pool; but the more the public enters, the more the prize shrinks towards nothing. Hofstadter's provocation in both cases is the notion he calls superrationality: since every participant faces an identical situation and, if rational, will reason in much the same way, one should choose as though one's own choice were everyone's — enter the lottery once or not at all, and resolve the dilemma with a randomising device that gives you a one-in-twenty chance of volunteering. My friend regards this as dressed-up magical thinking, on the grounds that my entry cannot causally influence anyone else's. I suspect the argument deserves to be felt in the stomach before it is dismissed in the armchair.

That is where you come in. I convene a final-year undergraduate seminar on decision theory and rational choice: fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, no preparation assumed beyond ordinary game-theoretic literacy. Design an activity — a game, or a short sequence of games with real but small stakes, run live in the room — that makes the students feel the pull of the superrational answer before they ever hear the word. Concretely, I want the following:

1. Full rules and materials for the game or games, including a payoff scheme that requires no real money (tokens convertible into something grade-adjacent would do nicely), and the order in which rounds should be run so that the coordination failure happens to the students rather than being described to them.
2. A minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, including when and how to reveal what the class collectively did, since the reveal is presumably where the force of the thing lands.
3. A debrief plan that maps specific moments in the session onto the components of the superrationality argument — the symmetry premise, the slide from predicting others to choosing for oneself, and the difference between this and ordinary cooperation — and that gives my friend's causalist objection its strongest possible airing before I am permitted to rebut it.
4. A prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it (someone will attempt to signal to the others; someone will submit an astronomically large number; the second round will be contaminated by the first) and how to handle each failure, including whether any of them should be allowed to happen on purpose.
5. An honest note on which feature of the original thought experiments a classroom game cannot reproduce. My own suspicion is that it is the one-shot anonymity of strangers who will never meet, since my students will see one another again on Tuesday, but I should like your view.

One firm constraint: neither the word superrationality nor Hofstadter's name may be uttered until the debrief. The students should leave the room unable to decide whether they were cheated by the rules or by one another, and the debrief should make that very indecision the lesson.
