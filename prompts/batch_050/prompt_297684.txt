Two friends of mine, Mara and Jules, fell into a long argument after our reading group discussed the old Epicurean claim that death is nothing to us, and I'd like an outside referee. Mara's position was that the Epicureans are simply right: anything that harms you must at some point be experienced by you as bad, and since the dead experience nothing, your own death cannot be bad for you, however bad it is for the people you leave behind. Jules replied that this confuses something being bad *as experienced* with something being bad *by comparison*: a person who dies at thirty is worse off than that same person would have been had she lived to seventy with a good life, and the comparison itself is what makes her death bad for her, regardless of whether she ever feels deprived. Mara pressed the timing problem: if death is bad for her, when is it bad? Not while she is alive, because death hasn't happened; not after she dies, because there is no longer a her for anything to be bad for. Jules answered that the badness of death doesn't need a temporal location any more than the fact that a cancelled conference talk is bad for the speaker does; a life can rank lower in value than a rival life without the shortfall being felt at any moment.

Mara then brought out what she took to be her strongest card: the symmetry between the time before we were born and the time after we die. Nobody grieves over the aeons of nonexistence that preceded their birth, she said, so consistency requires treating post-death nonexistence the same way, and Jules's comparison argument, if it works at all, should work equally for both. Jules conceded the cases look parallel but offered two replies: first, that death, unlike prenatal nonexistence, cuts off desires, projects, and relationships that are already underway, and it is the frustration of an actually existing life's trajectory that grounds the loss; second, that even if the two periods are metaphysically identical, our asymmetric concern about the future might itself be something a theory of prudential value should vindicate rather than correct. Mara shot back that the first reply overgenerates: on it, a person who happened to have no unfinished projects or future-directed desires would not be harmed by dying, which seems absurd, and the second reply just relabels a brute psychological bias as if it were a fact about value. Jules's last move was to shift ground slightly: even setting aside her own view, the experience requirement Mara is relying on proves too much, since it would also rule out harms most people accept, such as betrayals one never discovers or being slandered after one is dead, so the principle can't be doing the work Mara needs it to do.

Neither would concede, and I stayed out of it because I genuinely can't tell where the exchange should land. Please adjudicate it point by point: for each of the main moves on both sides, say whether it succeeds, fails, or merely relocates the disagreement, and explain why. In particular, I'd like your verdict on whether the comparison-based account really escapes the timing problem, whether the prenatal/posthumous symmetry argument survives Jules's two replies, and whether Mara's experience requirement can be restricted to avoid the counterexamples Jules raises without becoming ad hoc. Finally, if there is some distinction or piece of conceptual machinery that both parties are missing and that would untangle at least part of the dispute, identify it and show where it would have changed the argument.
