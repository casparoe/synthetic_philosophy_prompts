I’m a philosophy professor teaching a 75-minute seminar on Athenian democracy to about 16 undergrads. We’re looking at three familiar failure episodes: the Assembly voting to annihilate Mytilene and then frantically reversing itself the next day; the decision to invade Sicily, where skepticism about the expedition ends up producing a much larger and more disastrous force; and the trial of Socrates, where a popular jury converts political resentment and anxiety into a criminal verdict.

Can you design a classroom activity that lets students *feel* the structural problem here rather than just hearing me lecture about “mob rule”? I’m especially interested in the way a group can move from outrage, to overconfidence, to retrospective blame without any single participant having to be unusually stupid or evil. Ideally each phase of the activity should correspond loosely to one of the three episodes, but I don’t want a straightforward historical reenactment or mock trial. Students should make real decisions under time pressure, with incomplete information, public commitments, and some small incentive or payoff structure that makes it rational to shift responsibility or escalate commitment.

Please include concrete roles, hidden information, voting rules, incentives, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Also explain what moments in the activity should be mapped onto Mytilene, Sicily, and Socrates in the debrief. I’d like the debrief to complicate the easy conclusion that Athens failed because ordinary citizens were ignorant or bloodthirsty. Say what will predictably go wrong when running it — e.g. students treating it as a game, refusing to blame anyone, or using modern legal standards — and how to handle those problems. Finally, be honest about which feature of Athenian democracy the activity can’t reproduce, particuarly anything involving actual fear, status, war, or death.
