A buddy of mine cornered me after our reading group with this argument that expected utility maximization "refutes itself," and I've been stuck on it for days. He laid it out like this:

(1) Rationality is maximizing expected utility relative to your credences.

(2) Your credence in a claim is fixed by your evidence for it, and your evidence doesn't shrink just because the promised stakes get bigger. When the mugger says give me $5 and I'll use my powers outside the matrix to create an astronomical number of happy lives, whatever probability you assign him is what it is — he can't lower it by naming a bigger number. Call this the dial assumption: turning the payoff dial up doesn't turn the probability dial down.

(3) If your utility function is unbounded, then for any credence p, no matter how tiny, the mugger can name a payoff big enough that p times the utility beats the $5. So you pay. But paying the mugger is absurd — he could be anyone, saying anything.

(4) So utility has to be bounded. There's some ceiling M that no outcome, however wonderful, exceeds.

(5) But a bounded function's increments have to shrink to nothing: past some scale, tacking on another trillion happy lives changes the utility by less than any epsilon you pick. Otherwise the sum of increments blows past M.

(6) So past that scale, the difference between saving 10^12 lives and saving 10^20 lives is, utility-wise, a rounding error. And it gets worse: you'd trade a certainty of the astronomically better outcome for an epsilon-sized bump in the probability of the smaller one, because epsilon times a big chunk of M swamps the rounding error.

(7) Being practically indifferent between those outcomes, and letting dust-speck probability differences veto astronomical differences in lives, is every bit as absurd as paying the mugger. So bounded utility is out too.

(8) A utility function is either bounded or it isn't. So (1) is false. Expected utility maximization refutes itself.

Now here's where it gets annoying. I told him I think the slip is somewhere between (2) and (4) — the dial assumption smells wrong to me, like obviously your credence that some guy can deliver 3^^^^3 utils should drop as the number climbs, faster than the number climbs. But he came pre-armed: he says if my credence in the mugger's claim depends on the stakes he's waving around, then I'm letting practical considerations fix my beliefs, and that's just pragmatic encroachment dressed up — credences track evidence, full stop, and "that's a really big promise" isn't evidence about whether he's telling the truth. And he says if I keep the dial assumption, then (3) forces boundedness and (5)–(6) are just math, so I'm trapped in (7) either way.

I'd most like to keep (1) and also not pay the mugger — those are the two things I actually care about — and I'm willing to be a little weird about enormous outcomes if that's the price. Which step do I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost me? If the answer is "reject (2)," I need a way to do it that isn't just pragmatic encroachment with a fake mustache on, because he's going to say that word for word.
