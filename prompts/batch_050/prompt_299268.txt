In debates between Fodor-style modularists and defenders of massive modularity, the massive modularity camp leans heavily on a single image: the mind is like a Swiss Army knife. Rather than one general-purpose blade applied to every problem, the mind is a handle fitted with many specialized tools, each shaped by natural selection for a distinct adaptive problem faced by our ancestors — one for cheater detection, one for face recognition, one for grammar, one for folk physics, and so on. The analogy is used to argue that domain-general learning mechanisms could never have solved these problems in the available time, that we should expect innate, informationally encapsulated, domain-specific systems, and that looking for a unified theory of cognition is a mistake. Audit this analogy: give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the handle, the individual tools, the craftsman, the situations in which one reaches for a particular blade — then identify where the mapping breaks down. Consider, among other things, that knife tools do not develop, do not learn, do not talk to one another, are fixed in number and function, and have an actual designer with foresight, and ask whether these disanalogies matter for the specific conclusions the analogy is used to support or are merely decorative. Does the innateness-and-encapsulation conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the knife carrying it? If a better analogy exists for what the massive modularity thesis actually claims, propose one. Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each of your claims, and single out the one point in your audit where you are least sure you have it right.
