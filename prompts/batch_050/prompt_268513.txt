I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately been reading philosophy of perception, and I want to test whether my intuitions about “cognitive penetration” are tracking anything principled. Please answer in British English.

I have been distinguishing three possible relations, using my own labels: reweighting, where prior knowledge changes which sensory cues are relied upon but not what it is like to see; staining, where a belief or desire changes the phenomenal character itself; and latching, where training installs a fast, encapsulated recognitional capacity that feels perceptual but no longer responds to current reasons. I do not know whether these labels carve nature at its joints.

My verdicts so far are these. Knowing that the Müller-Lyer arrows are equal does not make them look equal: not staining. After learning that a faint garbled recording contains the words “cold harbour”, I hear those words and cannot easily return to the earlier noise: staining, not mere judgement. A seasoned radiologist and I look at the same scan; I see a smudge, she sees a tumour margin at once, but when the clinical history later proves misleading she can re-inspect and withdraw the reading: latching or reweighting, not permanent staining. Wearing reversing goggles for a fortnight changes reaching long before the world looks upright again: neither staining nor cognitive penetration, only sensorimotor recalibration. Told that an inert cream is analgesic, a volunteer reports less pain and flinches less: I am torn between staining of pain and reweighting of report. Being warned that a neutral host is untrustworthy makes her smile look calculated to me, even while I reproach myself for the thought: staining, though morally suspect. Knowing that a white wall is bathed in amber stage-light does not make it look white: not staining. An ambiguous figure can be held as duck rather than rabbit for a few seconds by trying, but the effort feels like moving a spotlight rather than repainting the figure: attention or reweighting, not staining.

The case that unsettles me is this. Suppose a trained atmospheric scientist, using no instrument, learns to discriminate two cloud kinds whose only physical difference is a subtle polarisation statistic in skylight. Before training she insists the sky looks uniform; after months of pairing theory with feedback she says the second kind has a faint silky grain. Sham feedback makes the grain appear for a week, then vanish when the trick is revealed; genuine feedback survives counter-argument but disappears after retinal disease that should leave cognition intact.

What principle best captures my verdicts while separating staining from attention, judgement, memory, placebo report, sensorimotor adaptation, and expertise? Please state it precisely enough to condemn at least one tempting rival; identify which of my verdicts, if any, should be surrendered for the sake of a simpler theory; say what the principle predicts for the cloud case under sham feedback, retinal disease, and a further condition in which the supposed grain remains phenomenally present but ceases to guide action; and name one future experiment whose outcome would decide between staining, reweighting, and latching rather than merely redescribing the data.
