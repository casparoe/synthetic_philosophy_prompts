A few months ago a close friend of mine died, and at the memorial I kept hearing the same consolation: that she lives on in everyone who knew and loved her. Most people offered this as a figure of speech, a way of saying that her influence endures. But I had recently been reading I Am a Strange Loop, where Hofstadter takes this idiom almost literally. His picture, as I understand it, is that a self is a certain kind of self-referential pattern -- a strange loop -- and that because brains form internal models of the people they interact with, everyone who knew my friend carries a low-resolution, coarse-grained copy of her pattern. On this view the "I" was never confined to one skull; it was always distributed, with the person's own brain hosting the highest-resolution version and others hosting diluted ones. Death removes the finest copy but the pattern persists, spread across minds, and Hofstadter presents this as something more than a metaphor -- as a claim about what a self literally is, which is supposed to make the consolation true rather than merely kind.

My sister, who studied philosophy, calls this a category error dressed up as comfort: a memory of someone, she says, is no more a fragment of that person than a photograph is a fragment of the landscape. My brother-in-law takes the other side and thinks Hofstadter's reading is the only version of the consolation that isn't wishful thinking. I would like the analogy audited properly. Set out the mapping explicitly -- what in the idiom corresponds to what in Hofstadter's theory (the person's own brain, the models in others' brains, the dilution with distance and unfamiliarity, death as the loss of the highest-resolution copy) -- and then identify where it breaks. In particular, address what I take to be the crux: the idiom works because we picture the departed somehow continuing in the survivors, but the surviving models don't seem to form one thing -- they don't communicate, can't update each other, can't act as one subject, and presumably have no unified point of view. So is the distributed "I" a persisting self in several vessels, or several static portraits that merely resemble one?

Please take a definite position rather than refereeing. Tell me whether the analogy, as Hofstadter deploys it, can actually carry the weight he puts on it -- whether the consolation is, strictly speaking, true -- and say what the analogy quietly imports about personal identity (that a self is the sort of thing that can be multiply instantiated at varying grain) and whether that import is defensible or question-begging. And if the analogy fails, propose the better one: if "lives on in those who knew her" is really more like a novel surviving in translations of uneven fidelity, or a melody kept going by musicians who never play together, then say which near-miss analogy comes closest, what it gets right that Hofstadter's version gets wrong, and what honest consolation, if any, survives the audit.
