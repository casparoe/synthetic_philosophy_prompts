So I sit on my city's autonomous vehicle licensing board -- we're the body that approves operator permits for the sidewalk delivery robot fleets in the pilot program. Normally the permit holder is a company, and there's a requirement in the regs that every permit holder designate a "responsible operator" who can be served with notices, fined, and have the permit suspended.

Here's the new application thats causing fights. A company running a fleet of delivery bots is structured so that its LLC has zero human members. The membership interests sit in a trust whose trustee is contractually bound to follow the decisions of the company's in-house AI system, which does routing, pricing, maintenance scheduling, hiring of gig contractors, all of it. They've now applied to have the AI system itself listed as the responsible operator on the permit.

Their lawyers lean hard on the corporate personhood analogy. A corporation has no body, no mind, nobody home, and yet the law serves it with notices, fines it, and holds its permits without ever asking whether Acme Corp "really understands" anything. Corporate personhood is a useful fiction, not a metaphysical finding. And on the metrics, they say, their system outperforms the humans: it answers every regulator email in minutes, keeps flawless logs, and its safety record is genuinely better than the human-run fleets in the program.

Our general counsel says the analogy fails exactly where it matters. Corporate personhood works because there are always natural persons somewhere down the chain -- officers, directors, shareholders -- who can be hauled into court, and the corporate form is bookkeeping over their arrangements. The whole point of a "responsible operator" role is to create a hook for accountability: someone whose incentives a fine actually changes. Here there's no one down the chain. Fining this entity is just draining a bank account, and the software deciding things doesn't feel the loss the way an officer facing sanctions does.

The board is split three ways: deny outright, approve (the permit regime is about safety outcomes, and the outcomes are good), or approve with conditions like a hefty bond plus a human "compliance agent" who is contractually on the hook.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's meeting: a clear recommendation (approve, deny, or approve with conditions) with the reasoning, maybe 800 words max. Two ground rules: take the corporate-person analogy seriously -- don't just assert that software "isn't really a person," since the law never cared whether corporations are really persons either -- and don't wave away the accountability problem just because the safety stats look nice. The strongest point on whichever side loses should get a real answer, not a paragraph of throat-clearing.
