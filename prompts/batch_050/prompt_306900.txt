My friend who did a poli sci minor gave me his summary of the Condorcet jury theorem the other night and I want to check it before I repeat it in an essay. His version, roughly: if every voter is even slightly more likely than a coin flip to get the answer right, then as you add more voters the chance the majority gets it right goes toward 100%, and this is basically the mathematical case for democracy -- bigger electorates make better decisions, and it's why twelve jurors beat one judge. He also said the takeaway is that majority rule finds the truth, so when a majority gets something wrong it must mean people weren't really competent after all.

Some of this sounds right to me and some of it sounds too convenient. In particular the jump from "slightly better than chance" to "democracy is vindicated" feels like it's skipping some load-bearing assumptions, and the last bit has an unfalsifiable smell. Which parts of his summary are actually what the theorem says, which parts quietly add assumptions or stretch it past what it shows, and does anything like the pro-democracy conclusion survive once you state it properly? Also curious what the theorem says, if anything, about the cases where voters are on average *worse* than a coin flip.
