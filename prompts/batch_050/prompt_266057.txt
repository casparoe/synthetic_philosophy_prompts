I am preparing a seminar session that pairs Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) — loosely adapted from Akutagawa's short stories — with the contemporary debate over the cognitive penetrability of perception, and I would like a reading of the film through that debate, done properly in both directions.

The standard classroom use of Rashomon treats it as a parable about unreliable testimony and self-serving memory: the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai speaking through the medium, and finally the woodcutter each give incompatible accounts of what happened in the grove, each version flattered in the teller's favour, and the film declines to say which is true. I want to resist that flattening and press a harder question. The penetration debate turns on a distinction between effects on perception proper — what a perceiver actually sees — and effects confined to downstream stages: where attention is directed, what is remembered, what is judged, what is reported. The impenetrability camp holds that beliefs, desires, and self-conception never reach the first of these; penetration theorists argue that they sometimes do; sceptics in the mould of Firestone and Scholl reply that most purported demonstrations of penetration are really attentional, judgmental, or memorial effects wearing perceptual dress.

So: sort the film's distortions along exactly this fault line. Which, if any, of the four accounts dramatise a genuine difference in seeing rather than in remembering, judging, or telling? Consider in particular what the woodcutter's closing version does to the question: he appears to have seen the grove accurately and lied anyway, to conceal the stolen dagger, which looks like a point for the impenetrabilist, since his perception survived his self-interest intact. Does the film taken as a whole in fact side with impenetrability, locating all the corruption in memory and report? Or does the way each account renders the wife — seductress, violated victim, spurned and despairing — suggest that each teller's evaluative stance towards her shaped the encounter as perceived, in which case the film offers the penetrabilist's best case in narrative form?

There is also a problem about the medium itself, which I want taken seriously rather than waved at. Film shows us each testimony as footage from a camera that behaves neutrally within each version; it can depict different stories, but it is not obvious that it can depict different seeings, since it has no way of presenting a perceiver's experience from the inside except by presenting a world. Does this mean that cinema is constitutively unable to represent cognitive penetration — that any filmic rendering of the phenomenon collapses into either a lie (a false report faithfully shown) or an altered world (a world that simply is different)? If so, Rashomon would be a film about the impossibility of the very phenomenon it is usually said to illustrate, which would be a more interesting claim than the relativist commonplace.

Close by telling me what the film contributes to the debate that the experimental literature cannot, and what the seminar should conclude: whether Rashomon is best taught as an argument for penetration, as a dramatisation of the sceptic's sorting problem, or as a demonstration that the distinction between seeing and judging is harder to draw in a lived life than in a laboratory — and say plainly where the film resists whichever reading you favour.
