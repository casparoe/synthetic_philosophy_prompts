I am a PhD student in political philosophy with a formal focus, currently writing a dissertation chapter on axiomatic bargaining solutions, and I want to assemble the strongest possible case against the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution before I attempt to answer it. The objections I have collected so far:

(1) Utopia-point sensitivity: the solution depends on the ideal point, so adding an extreme alternative that neither party would ever seriously accept, but which raises one player's maximal conceivable payoff, shifts the outcome in that player's favor. (2) Informational sparseness: conversely, the solution attends only to the disagreement point, the ideal point, and a single point on the Pareto frontier; two problems whose frontiers differ radically everywhere except along the proportional ray receive identical solutions, which appears to discard morally relevant information about what each party could have had. (3) Weak Pareto optimality only: when the frontier is not strictly decreasing, Kalai–Smorodinsky can select an outcome at which one player could be made better off at no cost to the other. (4) No canonical many-player generalization: the combination of axioms that characterizes the solution for two bargainers cannot be jointly imposed with three or more, so the solution does not extend as cleanly as Nash's does. (5) Unmotivated normalization: the proportionality requirement treats each player's maximal feasible gain as the natural scale, but it is unclear why what a player could obtain in the best feasible case should determine what they deserve in the actual one; a different normalization would deliver a different putatively fair point. (6) Thin strategic foundations: whereas the Nash solution falls out of natural noncooperative models of offer and counteroffer, Kalai–Smorodinsky is implemented only by purpose-built mechanisms, making it hard to see as a prediction of what rational bargainers would actually do.

Is there another objection I am missing? It should be genuinely different from the six above — not a rewording, a special case, or a conjunction of them, and not a complaint that merely boils down to the observation that Kalai–Smorodinsky is not the Nash solution. After stating it, tell me honestly how strong you think it is, and in particular whether it cuts against Kalai–Smorodinsky specifically or against any bargaining solution sharing some feature with it.
