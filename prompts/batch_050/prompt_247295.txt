In a reading group on environmental history, we got into an argument about the eruption of Mount Tambora and the "year without a summer": one participant said the famines, migration, and political unrest that followed show that the climate, not any statesman or movement, was the real protagonist of those years, and when someone objected she shut the discussion down with the old line "geography is destiny." I keep turning that phrase over and can't pin down what it would even have to mean for her move to work. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — for instance: the weak claim that the physical environment constrains what humans can do; the stronger claim that it fixes probabilities or channels development along particular paths; the deterministic claim that it fully settles historical outcomes; and a methodological claim that historians should treat non-human forces as explanatory starting points rather than background. For each reading, say whether it is close to a truism, a substantive and defensible position, or something probably false, and indicate which of them, if any, would actually license using the slogan to end an argument rather than open one. Also address a complication that came up later: when we speak of a volcano or a pathogen as a "historical actor" or "cause," are we using a literal notion of agency, a causal claim in the ordinary sense, or just a vivid way of redistributing explanatory attention, and does the answer matter for evaluating the slogan? Where readings differ in plausibility, please separate what is broadly settled among historians and philosophers of history from what remains genuinely contested, and don't present a contested historiographical stance as if it were the consensus.
