I teach an upper-level seminar on decision theory, and a colleague has asked me to look over a draft chapter she's written for an undergraduate anthology on "puzzles for rational choice." The chapter covers counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, two cases I've taught before but never written up, and I'm trying to decide what margin comments to send her before the deadline. Here's the relevant passage, verbatim:

'Counterfactual mugging. Omega, a predictor with a flawless track record, tells you it flipped a fair coin. Had it landed tails, Omega would have given you $10,000, but only on the condition that you would have paid Omega $100 in the heads case. It landed heads, and Omega now asks for the $100. Causal and evidential decision theory both refuse, since once the coin has landed, paying causes nothing and is evidence of nothing. But since Omega is infallible, your choice was fixed in advance anyway, so refusing accomplishes nothing either. The standard resolution is that rationality requires paying: a policy of paying is worth 50% of $10,000 minus 50% of $100, and the fact that causal decision theory cannot see this after learning the outcome shows the theory is mistaken. The case is essentially Pascal's mugging run in reverse. XOR blackmail. A predictor writes to you claiming it has predicted whether termites will infest your house, and asks for $1,000; the letter is sent exactly when either you would pay or you have termites, but not both. Here evidential decision theory recommends paying, which is clearly irrational -- the letter gives you no information you didn't already have, since whether it arrives depends only on the predictor's trick and not on the state of your house -- and causal decision theory gets the right answer by refusing.'

My gut says the mugging setup is described correctly but the moral drawn from it is stronger than the literature supports, and that the XOR paragraph contains a real mistake about what the letter tells you, though I'd struggle to say exactly what if a student pushed me.
