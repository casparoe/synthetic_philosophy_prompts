Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps of cooperative bargaining theorists: defenders of the Nash bargaining solution and defenders of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. Take as common background the standard setup -- a convex feasible set of utility pairs, a disagreement point, and scale invariance of the solution -- and the standard characterizations: the Nash solution maximizes the product of utility gains over the disagreement point and is characterized by Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance, and independence of irrelevant alternatives; the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution selects the Pareto-optimal point on the line from the disagreement point to the utopia point (the pair of each party's best feasible outcome given the other gets at least their disagreement utility) and is characterized by Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance, and individual monotonicity.

The document should contain the following, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. Include claims about what the axiomatic method can and cannot establish, about which axioms are genuinely common ground, about the status of the disagreement point, and about the distinction between using these solutions as normative recommendations and as predictions of actual bargaining behavior. Do not pad this section with claims so weak that no one in the literature has ever denied them; each agreed claim should rule out some position a reasonable theorist might hold.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: the exact point of dispute in neutral wording; the Nash camp's position phrased as that camp itself would phrase it; the Kalai–Smorodinsky camp's position phrased as that camp itself would phrase it. Cover at minimum: the status of independence of irrelevant alternatives versus individual monotonicity as desiderata; whether the utopia point is a meaningful object or an artifact of the feasible set's outer boundary; and whether the monotonicity examples that motivate Kalai–Smorodinsky show a defect in Nash's solution or merely that the two solutions answer different questions.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp -- I expect candidates to include whether bargaining solutions should be evaluated primarily by axiomatic appeal, by equivalence to strategic bargaining procedures, or by fit with observed bargaining outcomes, and whether either side's axiom has counterexamples the other side accepts as damaging. For each crux, specify a concrete case, result, or body of evidence that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts or claims about that case (for instance, what each side says about strategic implementations of the two solutions, or about how each solution treats expansions of the feasible set that favor one party).

4. A final short section identifying any part of the dispute that, on your analysis, is verbal -- where the two camps use the same vocabulary (e.g., fairness, reasonableness) for different notions -- and explain why the disagreement there is about words rather than about bargaining.

Write the document as something both camps could actually sign: no strawmanning, no verdicts on who is right, and each side's position stated strongly enough that its own adherents would not demand a rewrite.
