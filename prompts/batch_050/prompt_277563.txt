In an argument about criminal sentencing, an interlocutor recently deployed the old slogan "a government of laws, not of men" to defend mandatory, mechanically applied sentencing guidelines. His reasoning was roughly this: the rule of law is realized to the extent that outcomes are determined by fixed legal rules rather than by the judgment of particular officials, so every reduction in judicial discretion -- ideally down to a formula into which facts are plugged and a sentence falls out -- is by that much a victory for the rule of law. Discretionary mercy, on his picture, is rule-of-law failure that happens to produce results we sometimes like.

I find the conclusion suspect, but I suspect the trouble lies in how the slogan is being read, and I'd like the slogan itself audited. Please proceed as follows.

First, lay out a menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing. I can gesture at some candidate shapes -- that law should operate through general, stable, publicly knowable rules rather than ad hoc directives; that official decisions should be legally constrained rather than resting on personal will; that like cases should be treated alike regardless of who the parties are; that no official stands above the law; perhaps even the near-literal and plainly silly reading on which laws apply themselves without human intermediaries -- but I want the menu developed properly, distinguishing theses that are trivially true from ones that are substantive and defensible from ones that are false, and noting which thesis people who reach for the slogan most plausibly have in mind.

Second, for each thesis on the menu, say whether it actually licenses my interlocutor's inference from "rule of law" to "minimize human judgment." In particular, consider whether the rule-of-law tradition gives him support: does the ideal as articulated in its classic formalist statements -- think of Fuller's list of desiderata for law, or Raz's insistence that the rule of law is one virtue of a legal system among others and essentially formal in character -- treat discretion as inherently rule-of-law-corrosive, or only certain kinds of discretion? And does a more proceduralist picture, of the sort associated with Waldron -- on which the rule of law's dignity lies partly in treating persons as agents who can receive reasons and contest their application -- perhaps invert my interlocutor's conclusion, so that stripping judgment and answerability out of sentencing would be the rule-of-law loss, not its triumph?

Third, take a definite stand. I don't want a balanced survey ending in "it depends." Tell me: which single reading of the slogan is the one worth defending as an account of what the rule of law actually is; whether that reading condemns, tolerates, or positively requires a place for individualized judgment in sentencing; and -- one concrete verdict -- whether a hypothetical sentencing regime that was fully mechanical, perfectly predictable, and entirely uniform would be a rule-of-law ideal realized or the rule of law hollowed out while its shell is preserved. Give reasons for each choice, and if part of my framing above already begs the question, say so and correct it rather than working within it.
