I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar in epistemology, and for the week on the preface paradox I should like to open the session with a poem rather than an argument. Write a villanelle in the voice of a conscientious historian completing a long monograph. The two refrains are her twin convictions: that every claim in the book is true, and that at least one of them is false. The form must do philosophical work. Through the tercets, the refrains should alternate without either withdrawing in the other's presence, so that the reader feels why each attitude is rational in isolation — she has checked every claim individually, yet she knows her own fallibility and the sheer number of claims — and their forced adjacency in the closing quatrain should stage the inconsistency itself rather than dissolve it. She need not end paralysed: let the final lines take a stand on whether she ought to publish. Somewhere in the poem she should register that her predicament is not the lottery player's, since she holds positive reasons for each of her claims rather than merely probabilistic ones. A reader acquainted with the literature should be able to tell that the poem understands the difference between a consistent set of beliefs and one closed under conjunction, rather than merely name-checking a paradox. Please write the poem, followed by a one-paragraph programme note explaining how the villanelle's machinery carries the epistemology, in British English.
