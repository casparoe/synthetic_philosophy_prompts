Last weekend I got into a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend of mine who works in animal cognition. I had described how my neighbor's dog seemed to wait until both owners had left the house before raiding the trash, and I said the dog was plainly reasoning about what we could and couldn't see. My friend invoked Morgan's canon at me -- roughly, the principle that animal behavior should never be explained by a higher psychological faculty if it can be explained by a lower one -- and said my dog story was textbook anthropomorphism, explicable by a simple associative rule learned from past scoldings. I pushed back that "lower" and "higher" are doing unearned work in the principle, and that her alternative needed a suspiciously long list of separate stimulus-response rules where my explanation needed only one compact piece of reasoning. Neither of us conceded, and it made me realize I don't actually know what would settle disputes like this in principle rather than by taste.

So here is what I'd like you to do: construct a thought experiment that puts Morgan's canon under controlled pressure. Design a realistic animal-behavior scenario -- a plausible species, plausible behavior, observable with ordinary field or lab methods, nothing exotic like mirror self-recognition or language training -- in which the behavioral evidence equally supports a "lower" explanation (associative learning, innate disposition, simple cue-tracking) and a "higher" one (means-end reasoning, planning, or representation of another's perspective). The catch is that the two explanations must differ in their simplicity profiles in opposite directions: the lower explanation should be ontologically cheap (no fancy cognition) but parameter-heavy (many ad hoc rules or associations, each fitting only part of the data), while the higher explanation should be psychologically committal but compact (one mechanism that predicts the whole pattern). The scenario should make Morgan's canon and a likelihoodist or Bayesian model-comparison approach deliver conflicting verdicts, and the conflict should arise from the evidence itself, not from stipulating which explanation is true. Constraints: one scenario, described in a single paragraph; the behavior must be something an ordinary observer could in fact record; no appeal to neurological data; and do not use scrub-jay caching, since that case has been discussed to death.
