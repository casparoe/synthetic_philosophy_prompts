Exam question: "The butterfly dream is among the most famous passages in the Zhuangzi. (a) Reconstruct the puzzle the passage presents, stating precisely what it is that Zhuangzi finds himself unable to know. (b) Contrast the role that dream-doubt plays in this passage with the role it plays in Descartes' Meditations. (c) The passage ends by invoking the transformation of things. In light of that ending, should Zhuangzi be read as a skeptic? Defend your answer."

Student's answer: "Zhuangzi relates that he once dreamt he was a butterfly, fluttering from flower to flower, content and wholly unaware that he was Zhuang Zhou. Upon waking he was unmistakably Zhuang Zhou again, yet he could no longer tell whether he was a man who had dreamt of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming that it is a man. Because the dream was, while it lasted, indistinguishable from waking life, he concludes that there is no real difference between the two states and that the question of which one he truly occupies admits of no answer. Descartes makes a closely parallel move in the Meditations: since dreams can mimic waking experience perfectly, the senses cannot be trusted, and everything acquired through them is thrown into doubt. The difference is that Descartes treats this doubt as temporary scaffolding, dismantled by the cogito and by the proofs of a non-deceiving God, whereas Zhuangzi provides no such exit. Zhuangzi's skepticism is therefore the more radical of the two: he is pressing Descartes' point without the escape hatch, and his conclusion is that we can never know whether we are awake, and hence that perceptual knowledge is impossible. This reading is confirmed by the closing remark about the transformation of things: just as a man may change into a butterfly, waking may change into dreaming, and any firm distinction we draw between self and world, or between one standpoint and another, is liable to dissolve. The wisdom of the passage consists in accepting that nothing can be known with certainty. The story, which closes the first of the Inner Chapters, Free and Easy Wandering, is thus best read as a skeptical argument, one stronger than Descartes' precisely because it admits of no refutation."
