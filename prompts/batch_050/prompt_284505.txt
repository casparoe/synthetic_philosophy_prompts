I am working on a paper about the Leibnizian cosmological argument and want help stress-testing what I take to be its pivotal step. In *On the Ultimate Origin of Things* (1697), Leibniz offers an illustration that does the real work of the essay: imagine, he says, a geometry book that has existed forever, each copy transcribed from an earlier one. Even if every copy is fully explained by the copy it came from, he claims, we would still lack an explanation of why there are such books at all — and the same holds for the series of contingent states of the world, however far back it runs. The series might be eternal, with every member explained by its predecessor, and yet something would remain unexplained unless there is a sufficient reason outside the series.

The objections to this move that I already know and do not need repeated:

(1) The Humean objection that the series is nothing over and above its members: once each member is explained, there is no remainder left to explain, and demanding a reason for the series itself is demanding an explanation of something that is not an additional fact.

(2) The composition objection: inferring that the series as a whole needs a reason because each member has one is a fallacy of composition, of the same kind as inferring that a heap of small stones must itself be small.

(3) The brute-fact response, associated with Russell's side of his debate with Copleston: explanation must terminate somewhere, and nothing in logic or experience rules out its terminating with the contingent series itself; the demand for a reason for the whole is one we are entitled simply to decline.

(4) The overgeneration objection: if an eternal series of copies would still stand in need of explanation, then an eternal necessary being would stand in need of explanation too, so the reasoning cannot terminate anywhere unless a stopping point is simply stipulated — in which case the contingent series was an equally eligible stopping point from the start.

As far as I can tell these four exhaust the standard repertoire, so please do not give me a variant of any of them. I want a fifth objection that is genuinely new: not a rewording, special case, or combination of the ones listed. It should engage the book example itself — that is, it should challenge what Leibniz takes the example to show about the explanatory structure of an eternal, internally self-explaining series, rather than merely reasserting that explanation must stop somewhere. Then: (a) say which of the four listed objections yours is closest to and how it differs, (b) state how Leibniz's best contemporary defender would respond to it, and (c) give me your honest verdict on whether the objection survives that response.
