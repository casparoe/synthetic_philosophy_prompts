So imagine this actually happens next year: someone claims a proof of a big open conjecture (say the Riemann hypothesis, doesn't really matter which) where the key step was done by a giant neural net. The network searched through some enormous space of algebraic structures and spat out a bunch of "certificates" — little objects that each satisfy certain properties, and the math shows that if 40 million such certificates exist, the conjecture follows. You can verify each individual certificate by hand in an afternoon, and people have spot-checked thousands of them and they all check out. But the search process itself is a total black box: nobody can rerun it, nobody knows why the network found what it found, and the training runs can't be reproduced.

Compare this to Appel and Haken's four colour theorem proof, where a computer reduced the problem to a big but finite set of configurations and checked each one with boring, deterministic, re-runnable code — which at the time already made a lot of mathematicians uneasy.

Please argue that the neural net proof is epistemically *worse* than Appel-Haken — that even if every certificate we check passes, we have genuinely less reason to believe the theorem than the four colour case gave us. Pin down exactly where the extra doubt comes from: is it the lack of reproducibility, the unsurveyability, the fact that the search could have silently missed a whole class of cases, or something else? Or if you think the two cases are actually on par (trust is trust, the checking is what matters), argue that instead — but you have to pick a side.
