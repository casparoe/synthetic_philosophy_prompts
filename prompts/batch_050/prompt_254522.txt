After a long and unresolved argument with a friend, I would like an impartial grade for the following take-home exam answer. Please grade it out of 20 and give specific, point-by-point comments justifying the mark: identify what is accurate, what is incomplete, and especially any subtle conflations or misattributions that a quick grader might miss. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; assess the philosophical reasoning as stated.

Exam question: A chess club teaches novices the knight-move rule by examples and drills. No novice has ever faced position P, in which the board has been rotated, one square is covered by a cup, and a bystander has just shouted a distracting number. Two competent members disagree about the knight’s legal move in P, yet each sincerely claims to be following exactly the rule everyone learned. Using Kripke’s reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following, explain why an appeal to the learner’s inner instructions, past intentions, dispositions, or an algorithm seems insufficient to settle the uniquely correct continuation. Then assess whether the club’s shared practice—what members would accept, correct, and teach—can supply the needed normativity without collapsing into the view that whatever the majority happens to say is thereby right.

Student answer: Kripke’s plus/quus paradox shows that there is no such thing as meaning one operation rather than another, because any finite course of behavior is compatible with endlessly many rules. If all my past uses of knight move fit both the standard rule and some bent rule that diverges only at P, then nothing in my head—no image, formula, memory, or feeling of understanding—can pick out the standard rule. Dispositions cannot help either, since they are finite, can misfire under noise such as cups and shouting, and anyway only describe what I would do rather than what I ought to do. Kripke therefore proves that private rule-following is impossible and that correctness consists in community agreement: in position P, the right move is whatever the chess club would license after discussion.

This skeptical result is avoided by Wittgenstein’s behaviorism, which replaces mysterious inner meanings with public criteria. On the skeptical solution, we do not need a Platonic fact of meaning; we need only assertibility conditions within a practice. If the club trains, corrects, and excludes people in a stable way, then that practice gives content to saying that one member made a mistake. Even if every member were momentarily confused by the rotated board, their eventual convergence would define the rule, so systematic communal error is conceptually impossible. The appeal to an algorithm also fails only because algorithms must be physically implemented; a written procedure in the clubhouse would settle P by causing the right behavior. Thus the normativity of rules is restored by majority practice, and the paradox becomes a harmless reminder that meaning is social rather than mental.
