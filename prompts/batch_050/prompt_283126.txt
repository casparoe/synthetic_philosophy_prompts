Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I'm a novelist — literary fiction, mostly family dramas, nothing remotely scientific — and I've just finished a draft of a book about a physicist named Miriam who spends thirty years building a theory of quantum gravity, only to watch it get empirically killed off by a rival approach. The twist, which I'm told isn't much of a twist because it happens all the time in real physics, is that her core equations survive *inside* the rival theory, just with every symbol reinterpreted: what she thought described tiny vibrating loops of stuff now describes, I don't know, amplitudes over some lattice. Her way of making peace with professional death is to declare herself a structural realist — science never really latched onto the nature of things anyway, only the mathematical relations between them, and those relations are precisely what survives when the ontology gets binned. Her rival in the book, an older emeritus type, thinks this is a bedtime story she tells herself, and honestly while writing their big argument scene I realized I couldn't decide who was right.

Then, because the universe has a sense of humor, a podcast about science and philosophy somehow got the idea I know what I'm talking about and asked me to guest-host an episode interviewing an actual philosopher of science who defends epistemic structural realism — the view in roughly Worrall's tradition, where the argument is that we should be realists about structure because it's the part of theories that carries over through revolutions, the way Fresnel's equations got absorbed wholesale into Maxwell's electromagnetism even though the ether died. I can hold my own on the novelistic side but I'm genuinely out of my depth in the literature, and this guy has clearly had every standard objection thrown at him a hundred times and has a smooth answer for each.

So: write me a sequence of about eight to ten interview questions that would let me do justice to both of my characters, ordered so that each one makes the previous escape hatch harder to reuse, and for each question give me a one-line note on the reply he'll most likely give so I know when to deploy the follow-up. Concretely I want: at least one question pressing the Newman-style objection — that if structure means purely abstract relations, then saying the world "has" that structure comes to almost nothing, since any sufficiently large set of things can instantiate any pattern you like — with a follow-up keyed to the standard response that science *describes* the structure rather than merely exhibiting it. At least one on whether conceding total ignorance about the nature of the relata means the view has quietly stopped being realism at all, with a follow-up ready for when he says continuity of structure across theory change is enough (I want to ask about cases like caloric theory, where people argue even the structure didn't really survive). At least one asking why he stops at the epistemic version instead of going ontic — if the relations are knowable and the relata principially aren't, why not just say the relations are all there is? And a final question that hands him Miriam's situation as a test case: does her consolation actually follow from his view, or is she conflating "my equations survived" with "something I believed was true"? If he flinches on that last one I'll consider the book finished.
