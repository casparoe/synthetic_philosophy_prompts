Students of set theory usually meet the axiom of choice wrapped in a picture due to Bertrand Russell: given infinitely many pairs of shoes, one can select a shoe from every pair by a rule — always take the left one — but given infinitely many pairs of indistinguishable socks, no such rule is available, and selecting one sock from each pair seems to require a special axiom. Teachers and textbooks lean on this picture to do two jobs at once. First, it is meant to show that a choice is sometimes secured by a definition rather than by an axiom. Second, it is meant to make the axiom itself look innocent: you are, after all, just picking one element from each set, which anyone can do for five sets, so why not for infinitely many? I want an audit of this analogy and of the two uses just described.

Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the chooser, of the act of choosing, of the rule, of a single pair, and of the completed selection. Then locate where the picture breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the two intended uses from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Consider at least the following pressure points. The image of a chooser picking socks in sequence smuggles in an ordering — a way of laying the pairs out in a row — that an arbitrary family of sets need not have; how much of the analogy's plausibility comes from that smuggled structure? The claim that no rule distinguishes the socks is plausibly a fact about the expressive resources we allow ourselves rather than a fact about the socks; does that undermine or instead clarify the shoes/socks contrast? And finite choosing is not a small instance of the axiom but a theorem of the axiom-free fragment, so is the slide from five pairs to infinitely many a generalization of the same act or a change of subject? Finally, take the shoes half of the example seriously: it suggests a classification of mathematical situations into shoe-like ones, where a definable principle does the work, and sock-like ones, where it cannot. Sketch what that classification might look like across a few standard results, and whether it tracks anything real.

Then assess the conclusions the analogy is made to carry. Does the picture actually support the verdict that choice is obvious, or does it merely relocate the dispute to the question of which families of sets exist in the way the picture imagines them to exist — already lined up, already paired? Say which of the two intended uses survives the audit, and if the analogy fails as an argument for obviousness, say what it is genuinely good for in a first lecture, and offer a better one-sentence picture of the axiom if you think one exists.
