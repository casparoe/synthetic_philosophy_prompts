I sit on the disciplinary panel of a state licensing board for structural engineers, and I need to circulate a written opinion before our meeting thursday. The case: a member with a spotless 25-year record found out her firm had been certifying fire-safety inspections on mid-rise apartment buildings that nobody actually performed. She reported it to the state regulator (good), but to do it she copied an entire shared project drive and gave it to the regulator and one journalist -- and that drive also had files on dozens of unrelated projects and clients, some of it commercially sensitive, none of it connected to the fraud. The firm (whose partners are licensed members too) filed a complaint arguing the confidentiality breach is itself a sanctionable offense. Her position is basically that the duty to the public cancels the duty of loyalty, full stop. Theirs is that she could have made a narrow, targeted report and simply chose not to. Our code says members must hold client information confidential "except where disclosure is necessary to prevent a substantial threat to public safety," and nobody on the panel agrees about whether her disclosure counts as "necessary" given how much extra material went out the door.

Draft the opinion I'd circulate: a concrete recommended outcome (no sanction / reprimand / censure / worse, with any conditions attached), the reasoning behind it, and a genuinely serious treatment of the strongest argument on whichever side loses -- not a strawman. Then end by flagging where the opinion is most likely to be wrong: what fact I've stated, or what premise in the reasoning, would flip the recommendation if it came out differently.
