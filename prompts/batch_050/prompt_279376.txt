I have no academic background, but I've been thinking for a while about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I keep going back and forth between two positions. The first, which I gather goes back to Epicurus, holds that death cannot harm me: once I'm dead there is no one left for anything to be bad for, nothing about death is experienced, and I don't regret the eons before my birth, so why should I mind the eons after my death? The second, the deprivation view, holds that death is bad for me precisely because it cuts off good experiences I would otherwise have had, and that a harm need not be felt by anyone at any particular moment to be real. Both sides seem to talk past each other -- one keeps repeating that there is no subject after death, the other keeps repeating that a lost life is obviously a loss.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps should be able to produce after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both can accept as written -- for example about what it takes for something to be bad for someone, about the fact that dying can be bad even if being dead is not, and about what the dispute is not about; (2) the exact remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording the relevant side would itself endorse, including the question of when, if ever, death's badness is located in time; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one side to give ground; (4) for each crux, a concrete case or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it (such as the asymmetry in our attitudes toward prenatal versus post-mortem nonexistence, or a person who dies content versus one whose plans are cut off mid-course), with each side's predicted verdict on that case recorded in advance; and (5) an honest note on whether any part of the disagreement dissolves into a verbal one once terms are fixed, and if so, which part.

Keep the statement neutral: nothing in it should presuppose that either camp has already won.
