Since I retired from my chemistry job I've gotten hooked on philosophy, and I help run a small ethics reading group at the public library — about 15 of us, mostly retirees plus two grad students who keep us honest. We've just spent a month on virtue ethics, and now it's my turn to run one 75-minute session on the situationist critique (Doris, Harman, the Milgram and Good Samaritan experiments, the old studies where kids cheat in one setup but not the next).

Design me an in-class activity, not a lecture. I want people to feel the critique in their own behavior before we ever name it: something where small situational nudges — time pressure, a casual authority-ish instruction from me, a staged interruption at the door — measurably change what participants do, right after everyone has confidently predicted that character, not circumstances, would decide who acts decently. I'm a sucker for the predict-then-reveal structure of a good physics demo, so please build in a round where people write down forecasts of their own and each other's behavior that we check at the end. Practicalities: no real money, nothing that would genuinely distress anyone, rules and materials and timing all spelled out, and it has to survive being debriefed in the same session.

Then give me the debrief plan. Map specific moments of the activity onto the actual argument, and walk me through handling the two replies our grad students will definately raise: that Aristotle already said the fully virtuous are rare, so watching ordinary people crumble proves nothing, and that a one-off parlor game can't tell us anything about stable dispositions anyway. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce (real stakes, repeated observation across many situations) and whether the whole thing being an engineered situation undercuts it as evidence or actually makes the point. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is people catch on that they're being manipulated and start overcorrecting, but you tell me.
