I teach moral philosophy and am currently preparing the final session of my graduate seminar on the structure of moral normativity. A colleague, who delights in uncomfortable conclusions, has pressed on me an argument she calls "the collapse." Its conclusion — that supererogation is an incoherent category and that our practices of moral praise rest on a systematic mistake — is one I cannot accept, yet each premise strikes me as defensible, and I have twice failed to talk my own students out of it. I would like your help.

You are a moral philosopher with particular expertise in normative ethics and the logic of "ought," "wrong," and "reasons." Please think carefully and methodically. Begin by reconstructing the argument in its strongest form, making explicit any charitable repairs it requires, and only then turn to diagnosis.

Here is the argument as my colleague states it:

(1) If, among the acts available to an agent, act A is morally better than act B, then the agent has more moral reason to do A than to do B.
(2) If an agent has more moral reason to do A than to do any available alternative, then the agent morally ought to do A.
(3) If an agent morally ought to do A, then omitting A is wrong.
(4) In any choice situation, at least one available act is at least as morally good as every available alternative.
(5) From (1)–(4): whenever there is a uniquely best available act, that act is obligatory and every alternative is wrong. And even where several acts tie for best, any act worse than the best is wrong, since by (1) the agent has more reason to do a best act, and by (2)–(3) failing to do what one has most reason to do is wrong.
(6) Now suppose, for reductio, that some act is supererogatory: morally optional, yet strictly morally better than at least one other permissible option. By (5), nothing strictly worse than the best can be permissible. So the allegedly permissible inferior option is not permissible after all, or the allegedly optional superior act is required. Either way, contradiction. Hence no act is supererogatory, and the merely permissible survives, if at all, only in the narrow seam of exact ties.
(7) Furthermore, special moral praise — the kind we lavish on saints and heroes, as opposed to the bare acknowledgment owed to anyone who does their duty — is appropriate only for going beyond what is required; doing exactly what is required is no more than is owed. Since by (6) nothing exceeds duty, such praise is never apt. Our practices of moral admiration are in systematic error.

My colleague embraces all of this. She adds, cheerfully, that morality is simply maximally demanding and that we should learn to live with it.

A few stipulations about what I would like to preserve, so you know where my resistance lies: I want to keep it that supererogatory acts exist and that praising saints and heroes is often fitting. I am also reluctant to surrender (2) and (3), since the ought–most-reason–wrongness linkage underwrites a good deal of the seminar's earlier material.

What I want from you is a verdict, not a survey of the literature's options. Take a definite position: identify the single premise or step you judge we should reject, and give your reasons for choosing it over the alternatives. In doing so, please address three things directly. First, explain why the premise you reject looked so plausible — my colleague is not stupid, and the diagnosis should predict her confidence. Second, state plainly what rejecting it costs: what theoretical commitments we must then take on, and what we can no longer say. Third, show that you have priced the apparently cheaper exits — in particular, resisting (4) by appeal to widespread ties or incomparability, and reading "ought" in (2) as merely recommendatory rather than requiring — and explain why each of those costs more than it appears to, or fails to save what I want saved. If your diagnosis implies that my stipulations above cannot all be jointly preserved, say so and tell me which one to give up.
