So here's the situation: about two years ago I promised my uncle on his deathbed that I'd scatter his ashes out at sea, and I still havent done it, and it eats at me way more than it logically should. He's dead. Nobody else knew about the promise. Nobody is relying on me for anything. And yet it clearly feels like a real obligation and not just a nice thing to do. So I've been going in circles on the actual question underneath: why does a promise bind at all?

I keep flip-flopping between explanations. Sometimes it seems like it's all about the expectations I created in the other person. Sometimes it seems like it's more about me freely committing myself, like I handed over a little piece of my future freedom. Sometimes it seems like promising is basically a social game with rules and breaking the rule is wrong the way cheating at cards is wrong, expectations or not. And sometimes I suspect the whole thing is overrated and all that's ever at stake is whether someone gets hurt.

Here's what I want: a "which theory of promising do you actually hold?" quiz, like the personality quizzes online except it sorts me by what I say about cases instead of asking me which label sounds good. Six to eight questions. Every question should be a concrete scenario with two or three answer options -- never "do you think promises create expectations?" because I could answer that without believing it in my gut.

Please include at least these cases somewhere: the promise to a dead person (obviously), a promise the promisee has completely forgotten about, a promise you only made because saying no in the moment would've been super awkward, a promise to yourself (like swearing you'll quit smoking), a promise between two people planning something immoral (do I owe my partner in crime?), and a promise broken to do something clearly much better (skip the lunch I promised to pull a kid out of a frozen pond).

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the actual positions: the expectation/reliance view, the social-practice-or-convention view, the rights-transfer view (the "you give the other person a claim on you" one), the view that it's about honest communication and not abusing someone's trust, and a deflationary option where promises don't add any obligation beyond the reasons already in play. Call them by whatever philosophers actually call them, I don't know the names.

Two more things. First, if there are pairs of positions the quiz can't really tell apart, say so instead of pretending it can -- I have a suspicion the communication view and the expectation view land together on almost everything. Second, at the end, walk through where the dead-uncle case and the promise-to-yourself case come out on each theory, because those two seem like the ones where the theories should diverge the hardest and I want to see if my gut and my head are even on the same team.
