I teach an introductory political philosophy seminar at a university, with 20 students and a 75-minute session. We are about to begin the unit on Rawls, and I want the students to encounter the central problem of the original position before they read a word of it. Design a classroom activity in which students must collectively agree on the rules of a resource-distribution game they will then play for real (small) stakes, without knowing which position they will occupy in it — and which is engineered so that they feel, in their own deliberation, the pull of the three ideas I most want them to carry into the reading: why the worst-off position becomes the natural anchor of the discussion, why someone will nonetheless object that a rational chooser should just maximize expected utility across positions, and why an agreement reached in ignorance can feel impossible to defect from later yet also strangely unstable once positions are assigned.

The specific wrinkle I want built in: the students should not know, while deliberating, how their positions will be assigned — whether by pure lottery, by performance on some task, or by the facilitator's discretion. I want the choice of assignment mechanism to be part of what they must regulate, so that they discover for themselves the difference between being ignorant of your outcome and being ignorant of the process that produces your outcome, and so that someone inevitably proposes meritocratic assignment and has to defend it to people who might lose by it. The stakes should be real but trivial (something like a small amount of course credit, candy, or priority in picking paper topics), and the payoff schedule across positions should be skewed enough that the worst position is genuinely unappealing.

Please give me: (1) complete setup instructions — materials, the payoff structure with actual numbers, what is written on the board before deliberation begins, and what the students are and are not told; (2) a minute-by-minute timeline, including how deliberation is structured, when and how the assignment mechanism is revealed and executed, and how the game is then played out; (3) at least one mechanism that lets students revisit or renegotiate their agreement after positions are assigned, so the question of the stability of promises made in ignorance arises from events rather than from my saying so; (4) a debrief plan that maps specific predictable moments of the session onto the difference principle, the maximin-versus-expected-utility dispute, and the strains of commitment, with the names of these ideas withheld until the end; (5) the ways this predictably goes wrong — students who game the deliberation, a group that reaches agreement too fast, coalitions that form once a likely assignment mechanism leaks — with concrete facilitator responses; and (6) a candid paragraph on which feature of Rawls's construction the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, that my students know their own conceptions of the good and their own risk attitudes throughout, and that no classroom stakes can mimic a lifetime's prospects), and what I must supply in lecture to compensate.
