I am taking an advanced undergraduate course on AI alignment and decision theory, and I am trying to sharpen a dispute that my seminar keeps treating too loosely. Average-case alignment evidence measures how a system behaves across a weighted distribution of ordinary tasks, users, environments and time steps; worst-case assurance instead concerns the most damaging behaviour within a specified and practically reachable threat model.

Consider a deployment criterion I will call the bounded-tail audit. A highly capable autonomous agent passes only if its developer can defend an explicit upper bound on the probability that, within one million deployment-hours, an adaptive adversary able to choose inputs and environments can induce the agent to seize or irreversibly compromise a critical system. The bound may draw upon formal verification, mechanistic analysis, adversarial testing and uncertainty about untried conditions, but it cannot be established merely by showing excellent performance averaged over benign traffic. Passing would not prove catastrophe impossible; it would provide a defensible worst-case estimate under stated assumptions.

What is your credence that, for an AI system capable of pursuing consequential goals autonomously over weeks, acquiring resources and causing irreversible harm, something like this bounded-tail audit is morally and epistemically required in addition to strong average-case benchmark results? Treat required as meaning that deployment without a serious attempt at such an upper bound would be negligent, even if no currently available method could make the bound especially tight.

Please respond as exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion and no more than two sentences per item. Item 1 should state the proposition precisely and give your credence between 0 and 1. Item 2 should assign probabilities, with one-line justifications, to two components: whether rare irreversible failures dominate the expected moral cost, and whether average-case behaviour is unreliable evidence under optimisation, distributional shift or strategic concealment. Item 3 should assign probabilities, again with brief justifications, to whether meaningful worst-case bounds are technically feasible and whether attempting such bounds is a duty even when they remain highly uncertain. Item 4 should explain how these conditional components combine without assuming implausible independence, and identify the principal disagreement with someone who considers strong average-case testing plus incident response sufficient. Item 5 should state which component most strongly controls your final credence and what technical result, incident or conceptual argument would change it most.
