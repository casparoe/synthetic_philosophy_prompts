I recently taught a seminar unit on perpetrator behavior built around the Browning–Goldhagen dispute, and I came away realizing that my own judgments about blame don't obviously line up with either side, so I'd like help working out what principle I'm actually committed to. Here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases, loosely modeled on the kinds of men in the reserve police battalion Browning describes. First, the man who, when the commanding officer offered the older reservists the chance to step out of the first mass shooting without punishment, stepped out: I judge him to have done the right thing, but not to deserve much moral credit, since it cost him almost nothing. Second, the man who stayed in the ranks, shot people face-to-face all day, and afterward requested a transfer and drank himself to sleep: I judge him seriously blameworthy, but significantly less so than he would have been had no exit been offered at all. Third, the man who not only stayed but volunteered for the hunt-down operations and came to relish them: I judge him the worst of all, worse than the second man by a wide margin. Fourth, the man who refused outright, was transferred, and suffered no real consequences: I take his case to show that refusal was genuinely available, and on that basis I lower my estimate of everyone else's excuse.

Here's where I notice the tension, and I'd like it taken seriously rather than smoothed over. When I think about the second man, I'm drawn to a situational diagnosis: conformism, deference to authority, fear of ostracism, the gradual escalation of the killing routine -- and the more I explain his conduct that way, the more his blame seems to shrink, almost to the vanishing point. But when I think about the third man, I'm drawn to a character-and-culture diagnosis of the Goldhagen sort -- he absorbed a murderous ideology and acted on it eagerly -- and yet I notice that this explanation, too, seems exculpating the more I press it: if his beliefs were installed in him by his culture, he arguably couldn't see his victims as victims at all, and blaming him starts to feel like blaming someone for the century he was born into. So I end up wanting to say that the situational explanation mitigates when it explains the reluctant killer, but that the cultural explanation does not mitigate when it explains the eager one, and I can't tell whether that's a principled asymmetry or just my sympathy tracking relatability. I also can't reconcile my claim that the first man deserves little credit because refusal was easy with my inclination to give the second man substantial mitigation because complying was easy.

What I'd like from you: state, as precisely as you can, one principle (or at most two competing candidates) about how causal explanation of an action interacts with moral blame that delivers as many of my verdicts as possible -- including the credit judgment, which is easy to neglect. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one you think should be overturned if forced. Then say what the principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a clerk who processed deportation lists from a desk and never saw a victim, and a man from the same battalion who secretly sabotaged one transport at real risk to himself but participated in three earlier actions without protest. If it turns out that no single principle gets both the reluctant and the eager killer right, I'd rather hear that plainly than receive a principle with ad hoc clauses bolted on.
