I am a retired biochemist who, since hanging up my lab coat, has been making up for lost time in philosophy — mostly philosophy of mind, which I find both delightful and exasperating. By a series of accidents I now sit on my former university's research ethics committee, and a case has landed before us for which our remit was plainly never drafted. A machine learning group has applied for approval of a training run in which populations of reinforcement learning agents are shaped almost entirely by strongly negative reward signals: the agents receive large penalties for entering certain regions of their environment, the policy is updated over tens of millions of episodes, and the researchers note in passing that early in training the penalty signal fires almost continuously. One member of our committee, a zoologist, argues that if an aversive signal is doing the same functional work in a learning system that nociception does in a rat — driving avoidance, shaping a persistent policy, being discounted against other goals in roughly the way pain is traded off against hunger — then our obligation to review should follow the function, not the substrate, and that we would never wave through an equivalent protocol on animals. Another member replies that a temporal-difference error is a scalar in a register, that there is nothing it is like to be a gradient update, and that reviewing this would make us a laughing stock and open the door to regulating every thermostat and chess engine on campus.

I have been asked to draft the committee's written opinion before our next meeting, recommending that we either assert jurisdiction, decline it, or decline it while attaching conditions the lab would genuinely have to meet. I should be grateful if you would write this document for me, in a form I could circulate more or less as it stands. It must contain an actionable bottom line, but I want the reasoning to do real work: specifically, I would like it to grapple with whether reward signals could constitute valenced experience — something with positive or negative phenomenal character — rather than merely correlating with it, and with what evidence, in principle, could settle that question one way or the other for a system like this one. It should also give the strongest version of the losing side's position, whichever way the recommendation goes; I have sat on enough grant panels to know that a decision which ignores the best argument against it simply invites an appeal.

One further complication, which I think should be faced squarely in the document rather than footnoted: if we assert jurisdiction here, we will need criteria that can actually be applied prospectively to future proposals — otherwise we are legislating ad hoc. But any criterion we can presently state (number of episodes, magnitude of penalties, architectural similarity to biological learners) seems embarrassingly arbitrary, and any non-arbitrary criterion seems to presuppose an answer to the hard problem of consciousness, which I gather philosophers have not quite got round to solving. Please address whether a committee can rationally act under that kind of principled uncertainty, and if so, what form the precaution should take — a flat refusal, a monitoring requirement, a burden of proof on the researchers, or something else entirely.
