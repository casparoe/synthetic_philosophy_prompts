I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and at a recent reading-group session on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, one of my peers offered an analogy that the rest of the group found very persuasive and that I found suspicious. His claim: Wittgenstein's hinge commitments -- the things that must stand fast for doubt and inquiry to get going at all, such as that there is an external world or that I have two hands -- are like the axioms of a formal system, and specifically like Euclid's parallel postulate. Just as the parallel postulate is unproven within the system and can be dropped or negated to yield non-Euclidean geometries that are every bit as internally legitimate, so too, he argued, hinges are ungrounded starting points that a community or individual could in principle swap out, and no set of hinges is more rational than any other. He used the analogy to defend a fairly strong epistemic relativism: disagreements at the hinge level are like disagreements between geometers working in different systems, beyond rational adjudication, and the best we can do is note which system someone is operating in.

I'd like you to audit this analogy carefully. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the axioms, what plays the role of the theorems, what corresponds to the act of adopting or dropping a postulate, and what corresponds to the multiplicity of equally legitimate geometries. Then identify where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I suspect matter: axioms are few, statable, and adopted by decision, whereas hinge commitments seem to be uncountably many, mostly never formulated, and acquired through upbringing and immersion in practices rather than chosen; axioms can be negated one at a time while the rest of the system is held fixed, whereas it's unclear what "negating" a single hinge while keeping everything else in place would even amount to; and the geometer stands outside the systems and can survey them, whereas we arguably cannot occupy a standpoint outside our own hinges from which to compare them with alternatives. But please assess these on their merits and add whatever I've missed, separating the disanalogies that actually bear on the relativist conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic.

Finally, tell me whether the relativist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the axiom analogy was doing the argumentative work -- importing the ideas of free choice, equal legitimacy, and external surveyability that the hinge picture itself doesn't supply -- then say so plainly. And if there is a better analogy for hinges than axioms (my advisor suggested the rules of a game, or the riverbed image Wittgenstein himself uses elsewhere in the same work, but neither of us is confident about either), please offer one, explain what it gets right that the axiom analogy gets wrong, and note what misleading implications of its own it might carry.
