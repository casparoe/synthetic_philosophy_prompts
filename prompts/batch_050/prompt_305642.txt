I'm a software engineer with no formal training in economics or philosophy, and over the past year I've fallen into the rabbit hole of the big debate about why some nations are rich and others poor. It started when a friend lent me the popular institutionalist account (the Acemoglu–Robinson argument that inclusive vs. extractive institutions do the work), after which I read the geography-first rejoinder and, later, pieces arguing that deep cultural traits are the real engine. What frustrates me is that each camp tells a story I find internally compelling while I'm reading it, and each dismisses the others with what look, to my untrained eye, like the same five historical anecdotes rearranged. In my day job I'd never accept a causal claim about a production system from this kind of evidence — no controlled experiments, one realization of history, a couple hundred maximally non-independent observations, and every plausible cause correlated with every other plausible cause plus with the outcome's own past. So before I read another word from any camp, I want the standard of evaluation itself.

Please write the desiderata that any causal explanation of cross-national wealth differences must meet to count as adequate, with the consideration or case motivating each requirement. I want to know which phenomena it has to get right (e.g., the so-called reversal of fortune among colonized regions, paired cases like the two Koreas or Nogales straddling the border, outliers like Botswana and Singapore, the timing of the Industrial Revolution in north-western Europe specifically); which distinctions it may not collapse (proximate causes like capital accumulation vs. deep causes, explaining income levels vs. explaining particular growth episodes, necessary vs. sufficient vs. contributory conditions); and what evidential standards are even available given that randomized assignment of institutions or geography is impossible — i.e., what weight to give natural experiments, instrumental-variable strategies, and within-country variation, and what to do when those are absent. For each requirement, say whether it is a genuine non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp. What I most want is your judgment on whether some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether demanding a single deep factor that explains the broad pattern is in tension with respecting contingency, timing, and path dependence, or whether explaining everything and being falsifiable are in tension here.

Then, briefly, score the three main contenders (institutions-first, geography-first, culture-first) against the list — but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not the scorecard, and flag explicitly which of the requirements does the most work in knocking out candidates and which points in the scoring are judgment calls dressed up as causal inference. If the honest conclusion is that the desiderata, properly stated, imply we cannot know why some nations are rich in the strong sense the popular books promise, say that plainly and tell me what weaker question is actually answerable.
