For an English elective on science fiction and philosophy, our class read Daniel Dennett's short story "Where Am I?", in which the narrator's brain is removed, kept alive in a vat, and connected to his body by radio links while the body goes off on a dangerous mission; later his body is destroyed, he is given a new one, and a computer programmed to replicate his brain is built as a backup, with a switch that toggles which of the two controls his body. I found the story fascinating but genuinely disorienting, and afterward I asked my older cousin, who took a philosophy of mind course in college, what the story is supposed to show. She summarized it like this: "Dennett's point is that the person is wherever the brain is. When the body dies, the narrator survives because his brain is still in the vat, which proves that personal identity just is brain identity. Then, when they build the computer copy, it is an exact duplicate of the brain, so now there are two of him, and Dennett's conclusion is that both are equally him — that's why the story is a refutation of the idea that the self is anything more than a physical object, and an argument that uploading your mind to a computer would literally be you surviving."

I have a nagging feeling that this is too tidy for a story that seemed designed to frustrate every tidy answer, but I can't put my finger on where the summary goes wrong, and she did study this material while I haven't. Could you audit it part by part? Which claims would Dennett actually endorse as readings of the story, which are overstatements or misreadings of what happens in it (for instance, about whether the computer is a duplicate, or about what the switch reveals), and which present a conclusion as Dennett's when the story might be making the opposite point? I'd also like to know whether her final claim about uploading follows from anything the story establishes, since that is the part I most want to be able to argue about with her.
