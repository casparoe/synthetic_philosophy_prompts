In my philosophy of language seminar, the professor distributed a handout of self-referential sentences along with her own verdicts on each:

1. L: "L is not true." — Her verdict: neither true nor false.
2. T: "T is true." — Her verdict: neither true nor false.
3. C: "C is not true, or 2+2=5." — Her verdict: neither true nor false.
4. D: "D is true, and 2+2=5." — Her verdict: false.
5. A pair, A and B, where A says "B is true" and B says "A is true." — Her verdict: both lack a truth value.

For my term paper I want to predict how she would rule on a new case, and defend that prediction. E: "E is not true, or the number of chairs in this seminar room is even" — where it happens, as a matter of empirical fact, that the chair count is even.

Two general rules both seem to fit her track record: (i) a sentence gets a truth value only if its truth or falsity is ultimately settled by non-semantic facts, with merely self-involving sentences left valueless; (ii) any sentence that quantifies over or predicates truth of itself is defective and hence valueless. Rule (i) and rule (ii) agree on cases 1–5 but diverge on E.

Which rule better explains her actual verdicts, what should she say about E, and which of the earlier cases is the closest analog to E? If her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so, and describe a further sentence whose verdict would force a choice between the two rules.
