I'm a PhD student in bioethics and for a workshop I have to submit a one-page statement of my views on advance directives. Before I circulate it, can you stress-test the package? Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Advance directives have genuine authority over later treatment, and the ground of that authority is the autonomy of the earlier self -- the person as author of her own life, not welfare maximization by proxy.
(2) I accept a broadly psychological-continuity view of personal identity, so in cases of very advanced dementia there's a real question whether the patient is even the same person who signed the directive.
(3) In the psychiatric case (Ulysses contracts for bipolar disorder, say) I think directives should bind even when the patient revokes mid-episode and begs us to ignore the document.
(4) In the dementia case I have the Dresser-ish intuition that a contented patient's current wishes can override her earlier directive, at least sometimes.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I'm especially unsure whether (3) and (4) can be held together without a principled difference between the two cases, and whether (2) quietly saws off the branch (1) is sitting on. Please rank the tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give, and tell me the single cheapest revision -- dont just flag everything as equally problematic.
