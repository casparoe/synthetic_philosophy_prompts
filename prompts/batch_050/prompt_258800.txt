I'm a second-year PhD student working on the metaphysics of facts (with a side interest in early analytic philosophy), and I keep getting cornered by a regress argument that one of my commitee members absolutely loves. I've written it up as cleanly as I can. The conclusion strikes me as crazy, but everytime I try to kill one of the premises it fights back. The argument: take the fact that Socrates is wise. (1) That fact is a genuine unity -- it's something that can obtain, something truth-apt, which a mere plurality (Socrates over here, wisdom over there, full stop) cannot be. Lists aren't true or false. (2) If several items constitute a unity, there must be something in virtue of which they're unified; unity doesn't come for free. (3) This unifier is either a further constituent of the fact or it isn't. (4) Suppose it is a constituent -- call it the exemplification tie E holding between Socrates and wisdom. Then what we actually have is the plurality Socrates, E, wisdom, and by (2) we need a further unifier E' for those three, which is again just one more constituent of a bigger plurality, and so on. The regress is vicious because at every stage the unity of the whole remains unaccounted for. (5) Suppose instead the unifier is not a constituent. Then it stands outside the fact, and the fact itself -- the thing that was supposed to obtain -- remains an unstructured heap; the unity now belongs to something larger (fact plus external tie), which just relocates the problem rather than solving it. (6) So nothing with internal complexity can be a unity: at best there are simples, and "facts" like Socrates being wise are fictions of grammar. Now, (6) can't be right -- I intend to keep asserting that Socrates is wise. So which step do I reject? I have a soft spot for (2) since it sounds like a truism, and I'd rather hang onto (1) as well.

My own attempted patch, for what it's worth: I tried stipulating that E is not an item on a par with Socrates and wisdom but a "mode of combination" of them, so that asking what unifies E with its relata is a category mistake. My committee member replied that this is just the first step of (4) with a do-not-ask-again sticker on it: a mode of combination is either something or nothing, and if it's something we can quantify over it and we're back in the regress, while if it's nothing it does no explanatory work. Is that reply fair, or is there a principled way to hold that some things relate without themselves needing to be related?

So: where does the original argument go wrong, if it does? Please name the step, explain why it looked compelling, and then price the main ways out for me -- the Fregean move about concepts being "unsaturated" and self-completing, the early-Wittgensteinian picture on which objects slot directly into states of affairs with no connecting links required, and the Bradleyan line that the regress is real but harmless rather than vicious. I want to know what each one costs before I commit to one in the dissertation. Also, please answer in American English.
