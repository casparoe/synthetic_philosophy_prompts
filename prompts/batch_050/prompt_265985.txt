I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep running into the same conversation-ender. Most recently: I pitched a piece on a research program I won't name here, and a physicist I was interviewing waved it off with "if it isn't falsifiable, it isn't science." My editor uses the same line to kill pitches, and I've heard it deployed against everything from string theory to evolutionary psychology to psychoanalysis. It clearly does a lot of work for people, but when I pressed the physicist on what would actually falsify his own preferred theory, the conversation got vague fast.

I'd like a careful disambiguation I can keep by my desk. What are the distinct, precise theses that slogan could be expressing? I suspect they range from something near-trivial (scientists should be able to say what would change their minds) through Popper's actual proposal (a demarcation criterion resting on the logical asymmetry between verifying and refuting universal claims) to something much stronger (unfalsifiable statements are meaningless, or worthless, or not worth reporting on). For each reading: state it precisely, say whether it's a platitude, a defensible substantive position, or simply false, and flag the standard complications a good answer can't ignore -- probabilistic hypotheses that no single result strictly refutes, auxiliary assumptions that let any theory dodge a failed prediction, existential and historical claims, and the difference between a theory being unfalsifiable in principle and merely untestable with current instruments. Most importantly: for each reading, tell me whether it would actually justify what the physicist and my editor used it to do -- dismissing a whole research program as not worth covering -- or whether the slogan only earns that conclusion on a reading nobody should accept.
