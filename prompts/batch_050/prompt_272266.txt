I'm TAing an intro philosophy of language course this semester, and I've been handed one 50-minute discussion section on Quine's indeterminacy of translation — the gavagai argument from Word and Object. The students have done a little Frege and Russell on reference, but nothing on behaviorism or meaning holism, and from talking to previous TAs I know this is the week where half the section walks out thinking Quine just said "translation is really hard, you can never be totally sure," and the other half walks out thinking he proved meaning doesn't exist and nothing means anything. Both of those seem wrong to me, in different directions, and I'd like the hour to steer between them.

So don't explain the argument to me — plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place before the gavagai scene can do any work? My sense is the radical translation setup has to feel motivated rather than rigged: the students need to feel *why* the field linguist is confined to observable stimulus conditions before they'll see the indeterminacy as something other than ordinary ignorance that more fieldwork would cure. Should gavagai itself be the carrying example, or should I warm them up with something simpler first? If gavagai carries it, tell me what the example must have and must lack — e.g., I assume I need to keep the rival translations (rabbit, rabbit-stage, undetached rabbit part, temporal slice of rabbit) genuinely tied to the same stimuli, and I probably need to resist the urge to gesture at "ostension is ambiguous" too early, since students already know pointing is ambiguous and will think that's the whole point.

Then give me the misconception map. The ones I'm expecting: (a) this is just a practical epistemic problem, more data would settle it; (b) the linguist could simply ask the native whether they meant the whole rabbit or a part of it; (c) it's just the familiar underdetermination of scientific theories by evidence, applied to language; (d) it's a claim about exotic cultures seeing the world incommensurably differently; and (e) it shows there's no fact of the matter about anything, full stop. For each one, tell me where in the sequence it predictably forms and exactly where I should head it off — some of these presumably need to be killed before gavagai is even introduced, others only after the analytical-hypotheses move. Also tell me what to openly bracket at this level: I'm guessing the generalization to one's own language, proxy functions, and Davidson-style charity responses are all too much for fifty minutes, but say which of those deserves a one-sentence pointer anyway. Finally, one closing question I can put to the room that would show whether they got the argument rather than just the vibe. And throughout, please be honest about what's settled and what's genuinely contested — my understanding is that the structure of the argument is not in dispute but whether it succeeds is very much alive, and I don't want to teach "the argument fails because of the principle of charity" or "behaviorism is dead so the argument is dead" as if either were the consensus.
