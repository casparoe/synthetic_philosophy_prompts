Last weekend I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend who works in health policy. She was defending the occasional use of lotteries to allocate scarce treatments, and her defense went like this: when several medically identical patients cannot all be treated, entering them into a random draw converts what would otherwise be sheer brute luck into something more like a fair gamble, so the losers can no more complain of injustice than someone who loses a wager. I replied that being entered into a gamble is not the same thing as choosing to place one, and that a patient who comes out on the losing side of a draw she never agreed to has suffered brute luck under a new name. Neither of us budged, and I have been turning the exchange over since.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on egalitarian justice, and I want this term's final exam built around precisely this crux. Please draft one essay question, answerable in sixty minutes, in which a public health authority defends a treatment lottery explicitly on the grounds that it launders brute luck into option luck, and candidates must first reconstruct the strongest version of that defense and then assess it from within the luck egalitarian framework. The question needs to be written so that a candidate cannot pass by reciting Dworkin's brute luck and option luck distinction, rehearsing the hypothetical insurance mechanism, or wheeling out the standard harshness objection: the real work should be done by the question of whether option luck can exist at all where there was no genuine opportunity to decline the gamble, and by the difference between an imposed lottery and the voluntary arrangements through which luck egalitarians normally allow misfortune to become a matter of personal responsibility.

For the deliverable, give me the exam question itself followed by a marking scheme consisting of exactly five numbered must-hit points, with no introduction and no concluding remarks. Each numbered point should be one or two sentences long, should state how many marks it carries (the five points should total twenty), and should end by naming the characteristic mistake that should cost a candidate that point. The scheme as a whole should make clear what separates an answer that merely defines its terms correctly from one that sees why the declinability condition is where the argument is won or lost.
