I'm not an academic, but I recently got into a long argument with a coworker about the Challenger disaster after we both watched a documentary about it, and the argument turned out to be about something more general than the facts. The basic story seems well established: on a cold January morning in 1986, NASA launched the shuttle despite engineers at Morton Thiokol having raised concerns the night before that the O-rings in the solid rocket boosters might not seal properly at low temperatures. During the teleconference, the engineers were initially against launching, management pushed back, Thiokol's own executives took the final vote off the record and reversed the recommendation, the shuttle launched, and it broke apart 73 seconds later. The Rogers Commission and Feynman's famous dissenting appendix then made it all look like an open-and-shut case of warnings ignored. My coworker says the people who launched were obviously negligent because the evidence was right there. My suspicion, which I can't articulate well, is that "obvious" is doing a lot of work here -- that the warning looks crystal clear only because we know how the morning ended.

I'd like you to analyze the Challenger launch decision as a case study in hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, using the theory to read the episode and then the episode to test the theory. On the first direction: map carefully what the people in that teleconference actually had in front of them (incomplete temperature data, no prior launch this cold but also no clean statistical relationship, schedule and political pressure in the background, and an engineering judgment rather than a demonstrated fact), and explain what hindsight bias -- the tendency, once an outcome is known, to see it as having been inevitable and the evidence as having clearly pointed to it -- predicts about how we now judge that meeting versus how it could reasonably have looked from inside it. Same for the narrative side: the standard telling has heroes (dissenting engineers), villains (management), a reversal under pressure, and a consequence; does that story structure itself smooth over parts of the evidence that were genuinely ambiguous at the time, and does the commission's verdict participate in the same smoothing it was supposed to correct?

Then run it in reverse, because this is the part my coworker and I couldn't settle. If the evidence really was ambiguous enough ex ante that a reasonable manager could have decided to launch, does the episode vindicate the hindsight-bias analysis, or does it just relocate the blame from "ignored a clear warning" to "failed to manage ambiguous warnings properly" -- which might be the same accusation in more sophisticated clothing? And there's a further worry I want addressed head-on: the hindsight-bias framework seems to make it nearly impossible to ever say a warning was genuinely ignored, since any condemnation of the decision can be redescribed as post hoc clarity. Is there a principled way, using this very framework, to distinguish cases where the outcome only seems predictable in retrospect from cases where it was actually knowable beforehand -- and on which side of that line does Challenger fall? Finally, tell me where the theory falls silent or misdescribes the case rather than stretching it to fit: what does the institutional setting (a contractor dependent on NASA, a go/no-go vote taken without the engineers, the burden of proof quietly shifting from "prove it's safe" to "prove it will fail") show that a purely cognitive account of bias cannot capture, and does the episode suggest the theory explains individual judgment well but institutional failure poorly, or the other way around?
