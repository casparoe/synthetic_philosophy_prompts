I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on rational choice theory. As a course assignment, each of us has to design and lead one 75-minute session; my assigned topic is the money pump argument and the diachronic dimension of rationality. The group is about fourteen students who have done the standard readings on transitivity and cyclic preferences, so they know the textbook argument — which is exactly the problem. They all nod along that intransitive preferences are irrational because a pump is possible, but I don't think any of them feel why a sequence of individually sensible choices could be ruinous, or see where the argument's weak points are. I want an activity that makes them live inside it before anyone names it.

Please design a runnable in-class game with the following features, and explain the reasoning behind the design choices:

First, a mechanism that actually induces a preference cycle in participants rather than asking them to imagine one — for instance, three options that differ along several dimensions so that pairwise trade-ups each look attractive while the whole set cycles. Give me the concrete options and payoff numbers, using a token currency rather than real money.

Second, a broker role (played at first by me, later by participants) whose scripted sequence of small trade-up offers walks a "victim" around the cycle, charging one token per swap, so that someone can pay repeatedly to end up where they started. I want the rules to keep the diachronic structure visible: each offer is made and accepted separately, with no foresight about what comes next, since the interesting question is whether rationality governs single choices or whole patterns of choices across time.

Third, at least two later phases that dramatize the standard escape routes — one in which victims may announce and lock in a plan in advance (the resolute strategy), and one in which they may reason backward about their own future choices before acting (the sophisticated strategy) — so the class can see what each strategy costs and what it saves.

Fourth, a debrief plan mapping specific moments of the game onto the argument's moving parts: the distinction between being actually charged and merely forgoing a better outcome, the assumption that preferences stay stable across the rounds, and the question of whether pumpability shows the victim's irrationality or merely a vulnerability that requires a cooperative, well-informed exploiter.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be candid about which feature of the money pump argument the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion.
