I moderate an online reading group in population ethics, and I want to build a sorting quiz for our members: "Which suffering-focused ethic do you actually hold?" The group attracts people who call themselves negative utilitarians but turn out, on questioning, to mean wildly different things by it — some think suffering can never be outweighed by pleasure, period; some think only extreme suffering has that status; some just think preventing suffering should dominate our budget while still accepting trade-offs in principle. The quiz should sort by verdicts on cases, not by which label people find attractive.

To fix terminology without requiring anyone to have read the literature: by *lexical* negative utilitarianism I mean the view that no quantity of pleasure or happy lives, however large, can compensate for even a small amount of suffering. By *lexical threshold* negative utilitarianism I mean the view that this lexical priority holds only up to a threshold of suffering intensity: mild discomfort is an ordinary bad that pleasure can outweigh, but there is some level of agony such that no amount of happiness compensates for it. By *weak* or *non-lexical* negative utilitarianism I mean the view that suffering and happiness are commensurable but suffering counts for more — often with the extra wrinkle that badness rises faster than linearly with intensity, so the worst experiences get disproportionate weight without being infinitely weighty. I'd also like the leaves to include classical total utilitarianism (full commensurability, symmetric weights), tranquilism (experiences are valuable only insofar as they are free of craving or disturbance, so no pleasure is needed and its absence is no loss), a preference-based negative view (nothing is good except the satisfaction or non-frustration of preferences, so creating satisfied preferences adds no value), and pure anti-natalism as a distinct practical leaf rather than an axiology, since it can be reached from several of these.

Here is what I need:

1. Eight to ten questions. Each must be a concrete case, not a direct question about principle: trade-off scaling problems (a world of immense flourishing that contains one life of unrelieved torture; one containing one mild headache; vast numbers of pinpricks versus a single instance of agony), creation asymmetries (a painless procedure that creates a blissful being; one that prevents a miserable being from existing), extinction and nonexistence cases (a barren universe versus one containing only mild discomfort; whether pressing a button that painlessly ends all life would be good, obligatory, or monstrous), and at least one case probing whether the respondent thinks pleasure can even in principle reach the intensity of the worst pains. Answer options should be verdicts on the case, not theory names.

2. A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves above. The key should be honest about its limits: explicitly flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot separate from these cases (I suspect tranquilism and the preference-based view will be hard to distinguish on trade-off cases alone, and anti-natalism will be underdetermined by design — say what extra question would be needed, and if no clean question exists, say that too).

3. A short mapping of the logical space: which combinations of answers to the underlying issues (commensurable or not; lexical or not; threshold or not; creation asymmetry yes/no; intensity-symmetric or not) correspond to which named position, and which combinations are unoccupied — with a one-line diagnosis of each empty cell (incoherent, coherent but unstable, or just unexplored).

A note on what I want from you epistemically: I'm asking you to reconstruct these positions as they are actually held, including their embarrassing corners, not to advocate for any of them. Where the literature or the logic genuinely underdetermines a distinction, mark it as underdetermined rather than forcing a verdict. And if something in my setup is confused — if one of my "positions" collapses into another on inspection, if my characterization of lexical threshold views misdescribes what anyone holds, or if the quiz format can't do what I'm asking it to do — I'd rather you say so plainly and explain why than politely produce a flawed instrument.
