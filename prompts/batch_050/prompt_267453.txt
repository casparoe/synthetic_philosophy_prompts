I've been turning over a question in my head and want help finding the principle underneath my own judgments. The question is when I'm on the hook for something bad that I didn't actually cause. Here are my gut verdicts on six cases:

The accountant who knowingly keeps the books for an arms dealer is responsible for some of the harm, even though he never touches a weapon. The cashier who sells a phone to a man she happens to know is planning a scam is not responsible -- he would have gotten a phone anywhere, and selling phones is an ordinary thing to do. I pay taxes that fund a war I voted against, and I don't think that makes me complicit; I can't meaningfully refuse. I buy cheap shirts that I strongly suspect were sewn in a sweatshop, and I do feel somewhat responsible for the sweatshop, even though the worker's conditions wouldn't change by one iota if I shopped elsewhere. The clerk who processes the paperwork for a shady eviction scheme, and who would be replaced within a week if she quit, is -- I want to say -- off the hook, precisely because someone else would do it. And the guest who stays silent when the host makes a bigoted joke at dinner is, I think, a little complicit, even though his silence caused nothing and his objection probably would have changed nothing either.

I can already see the tension: in the shirt case I hold myself responsible despite my replaceability, and in the clerk case I let replaceability excuse. And in the dinner case I assign blame for a contribution of literally nothing. Is there a single principle that captures most of these verdicts -- maybe something about what you're willing to be part of rather than what difference you make -- and if so, which of my six judgments has to go? Also tell me what the principle you land on says about a case I can't settle: a friend of mine organizes the venue for a conference whose keynote speaker has harassed junior colleagues, but my friend had no say in the invitation.
