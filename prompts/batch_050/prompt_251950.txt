I am a PhD candidate in philosophy serving as a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in normative ethics. I am grading midterm exams and would like an independent assessment of one student's answer before I finalize my own marks. The exam question and the student's complete answer are reproduced below.

Exam Question (25 points): Present the "torture versus dust specks" thought experiment. Reconstruct the sequence (or "spectrum") argument that appears to force the conclusion that one person's undergoing severe torture is worse than any number of people each suffering a trivial harm such as a dust speck in the eye, identifying each premise on which the argument relies. Then evaluate the threshold or lexicality response, according to which severe harms and trivial harms differ in kind such that no quantity of trivial harms can outweigh a severe one. Does this response succeed? Give one objection to it and state whether you take the objection to be decisive.

Student Answer:

The thought experiment asks us to compare two outcomes. In the first, one person is tortured severely for fifty years. In the second, an astronomically large number of people each receive a single dust speck in the eye -- an irritation that passes in moments and leaves no trace. Nearly everyone judges the second outcome vastly preferable. Yet there is considerable pressure toward the opposite verdict, and the sequence argument makes that pressure precise.

The argument proceeds as follows. Begin with outcome O1, in which one person suffers the full fifty years of torture. Now consider O2, in which a much larger number of people each suffer slightly less -- say, a few minutes less of an otherwise identical ordeal. If suffering aggregates, O2 is worse than O1: the tiny reduction in each person's suffering is outweighed by the enormous increase in the number of sufferers. Iterate: at each step, slightly reduce the intensity of the harm while vastly multiplying the number of people subjected to it. After enough steps we arrive at On, in which an astronomical number of people each suffer nothing worse than a dust speck. Each outcome in the sequence is worse than its predecessor, so by the transitivity of "worse than," On is worse than O1. The premises are therefore: (1) the comparative judgment at each step, driven by aggregation; and (2) transitivity. Since the intuitive verdict says On is far better than O1, something has to give.

The threshold response rejects premise (1) at some point in the sequence. On this view, once the individual harm drops below a certain level of severity, the harms to different people no longer aggregate against severe harms at all: trivial harms and severe harms are lexically distinct, so no number of dust specks can be worse than the torture. Because this view denies that the sequence is uniformly worsening, it effectively abandons the transitivity of "worse than," since it refuses to carry the comparative verdict through every link of the chain. This is the price the threshold theorist pays for preserving the intuitive verdict.

This dialectic is essentially a variant of the mere addition paradox and the repugnant conclusion: in both cases, a principle of total aggregation is shown to collapse when applied to sufficiently large populations, forcing us either to accept an absurd conclusion or to abandon aggregation wholesale. The lesson of the dust specks case is thus the same lesson Parfit taught about population ethics.

The strongest objection to the threshold view is that any cutoff is arbitrary: wherever the threshold is placed, a difference in harm too small for anyone to notice flips a potentially enormous moral difference, and that is implausible. I consider this objection decisive, and conclude that the threshold response fails; we should accept that sufficiently many dust specks are worse than the torture.
