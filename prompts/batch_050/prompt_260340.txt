The cluelessness objection to consequentialism has been argued about for decades without visible movement. One side holds that because any trivial act reverberates into the remote future — my hurrying to catch a train might alter which people exist a century hence, and thus whether some future tyrant is ever born — consequentialism can never actually rank options, and so fails even by its own standard. The other side holds that such unknowable effects wash out symmetrically, so expected-value reasoning is untouched and the objection confuses an epistemic limitation of agents with a defect in the theory. Everyone in this literature seems to know both arguments cold, and nobody budges. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about the future at all, but about what a moral theory is for: one treats an inability to rank options as fatal because a theory exists to guide choice, while the other treats it as beside the point because a theory exists to state the standard of rightness, and cluelessness only bites if guidance is what you demanded. Is that the right diagnosis, or is a genuine disagreement — about probability over the deep future, or about the structure of value — doing the work underneath? Diagnose the impasse using what the two sides actually argue, and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result about expected value, a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question — or an argument that no progress should be expected, and why. Finally, state what would change your verdict: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, or where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
