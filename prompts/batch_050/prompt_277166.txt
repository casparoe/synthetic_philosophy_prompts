Write a deadpan internal memorandum, at most 500 words, from the staff of an imagined Isotype Institute circa 1938, replying to a tiresome philosopher-critic who keeps asking how the institute's international picture language would express things like negation, doubt, injustice, or the thought that a statement has not yet been verified. The memo should be written in the spirit of Otto Neurath's International Picture Language: The First Rules of Isotype (1936), which held out pictures as a bridge across the divisions of national languages and laid down strict rules for visual statistics -- greater quantities shown by repeating symbols rather than enlarging them, no decorative perspective, every sign reduced to its most legible silhouette. The humor must come from the memo's scrupulous fidelity to that vision: each of the critic's questions is answered with total procedural seriousness (proposed new symbols, a committee on the correct silhouette for perhaps, a note that certain notions are best left unpictured), so that the reader slowly realizes the language can only stay universal by quietly deciding in advance what is worth saying. Land, without ever stating them, the standard worries: that a neutral picture language smuggles its metaphysics back in through the symbol designers, that countable things crowd out normative ones, and that anti-metaphysical purity is itself a metaphysics. The memo should close with a brisk official directive. No anachronisms, and no winking -- the writers must never suspect they are funny.
