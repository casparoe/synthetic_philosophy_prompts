I teach a sophomore political theory survey, and next Thursday I have one seventy-five-minute session on Rousseau's claim, in Book I, Chapter 7 of The Social Contract, that a citizen who refuses to obey the general will can be compelled to obey it and that this compulsion amounts to being forced to be free. My students have just spent two weeks on Locke, so they arrive with a working model of legitimate authority as resting on individual consent to limited government, and roughly half of them, having skimmed the passage, have already concluded that Rousseau is simply an authoritarian wearing democratic clothes. Plan the explanation rather than delivering it. Specifically: the order in which the pieces have to go in — in particular, what must be established about the difference between the general will and the mere will of all, and about obeying a law one has given oneself, before the compulsion claim can sound like anything other than a euphemism for tyranny, and why the claim cannot be taught before the social pact itself is in place; the one carrying example to build the whole session around, which must be a case in which a person is genuinely coerced by a law and yet it is at least arguable that the coercion constitutes rather than restricts their freedom — tell me what features the example must have and which familiar candidates (a seatbelt mandate, paying taxes, jury duty) fail which of those features; the misconceptions the students will predictably form, in the order they will form them — I expect at least the conflation of the general will with majority opinion, the reading of the passage as benign paternalism (the state forcing you to do what is good for you), and the assumption that freedom here just means positive liberty in Berlin's sense — and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected afterward; the way the session should use their fresh Locke as a foil without letting the contrast harden into a simple Locke-good-Rousseau-bad verdict; how I should handle the two standing rival readings of the passage, as a charter for totalitarianism and as a doctrine of collective self-legislation, given that I want the students to feel the pull of both rather than be told which is correct; the question I can pose in the last ten minutes whose answer would show the idea has actually landed rather than merely been absorbed as a slogan; and what I should openly decline to explain at this level — the Legislator, civil religion, the founding paradox — with a one-sentence rationale students would accept for deferring each.
