I'm a retired behavioral ecologist — spent 35 years watching animals make mistakes for a living — and since hanging up my binoculars I've fallen hard into philosophy of mind, mostly via a reading group that meets at the public library on Thursdays. We just spent a month on teleosemantics, the idea that what a mental or internal state is *about* gets fixed by what the mechanism producing it was selected to do, and I'm stuck on something my reading group couldn't resolve. So here's my situation: there's a small independent-scholars grant from a local foundation (six months, modest money, basically covers library access and train fare to bother my old grad students) and I want to pitch a philosophical project. I need you to write the actual proposal for me, one or two pages.

The half-formed idea is this. Teleosemantics seems forced into an awkward spot by evolutionary mismatch, which is something I know a lot about from fieldwork. When a moth spirals into a porch light, or a sea turtle swallows a plastic bag because it moves like a jellyfish, the theory apparently has to say the animal is misrepresenting: the moth's navigation system represents the porch light as a distant celestial beacon, or however the story goes. But then there's always a move available — say the content was never "star" in the first place, but something more abstract, like "bright point to hold at a fixed angle," and now the moth isn't wrong at all, it's navigating correctly by a cue that happens to have gone unreliable. So which is it? If the theorist can always redescribe the target property so the organism comes out right, content ascriptions start to look hopelessly indeterminate. If the theorist *can't* do that, the theory predicts that huge swaths of the animal kingdom, in any human-altered environment, are laboring under constant illusion — and I'm honestly not sure if that's a reductio or just a surprising discovery. The debates I've read seem preoccupied with swamp creatures and other science fiction cases, not with systematic mismatch in real populations, which strikes me as the more telling test.

So the proposal should do the following: state the question precisely (something like: what principle determines when teleosemantics must diagnose error versus when it may legitimately redescribe content at a more abstract level); explain why the standard defenses and objections to the theory leave this fork untouched; lay out the approach — I imagine working through a set of documented mismatch cases and stress-testing candidate tie-breakers, like counterfactuals about what selection would have favored, or whichever abstraction level best explains the animal's whole behavioral repertoire, but argue for whatever tie-breakers you think are strongest — and say why a case-driven analysis beats the obvious alternatives (another round of thought experiments, or a formal model). Then: what a positive result looks like (a principled rule for ascribing error versus charity), what would count as failure, and note that showing no such rule can be drawn would itself be a result worth publishing, since it would pin the indeterminacy charge on teleosemantics in a concrete way. Finish with a realistic scope for six months of one retiree's time. No bibliography or citations please — I'll add those myself, I have strong opinions about formatting and my grandkids already make fun of me for it.
