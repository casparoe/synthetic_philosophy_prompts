**Homework question:** Explain Hume's account of how we arrive at the idea of necessary connection, and why he offers two definitions of "cause." Then briefly explain how the so-called "New Hume" debate bears on whether Hume took causation in the objects to be anything over and above regular succession.

**Student's answer:**

For Hume, all ideas are copies of impressions, so the idea of necessary connection must be traced to some impression. When we observe a single instance of one event following another — say, one billiard ball striking a second — we perceive only contiguity, priority, and conjunction, never any power or force tying the events together. After observing many such instances, we acquire the idea of necessary connection; the impression from which this idea is copied is an impression of sensation that arises once the constant conjunction has been observed often enough. Because this impression is all the idea contains, causation itself, as far as we can meaningfully conceive it, is nothing more than regular succession plus the feeling of expectation it produces in us.

Hume accordingly gives two definitions of cause. The first defines a cause in terms of constant conjunction: an object precedent and contiguous to another, where all objects resembling the former stand in like relations to objects resembling the latter. The second defines a cause in terms of the mind: an object whose appearance invariably leads the imagination to form the idea of its usual attendant. These two definitions are two ways of saying the same thing, one from the side of the objects and one from the side of the observer, and Hume treats them as fully interchangeable.

The New Hume debate concerns whether this regularity reading is correct. Interpreters such as Galen Strawson argue that Hume was in fact a causal realist who believed there are necessary connections in nature and, crucially, that careful observation allows us to perceive these powers in particular cases, contrary to the traditional reading. Defenders of the traditional regularity reading reply that this cannot be squared with Hume's copy principle and his repeated insistence that we have no impression of power.
