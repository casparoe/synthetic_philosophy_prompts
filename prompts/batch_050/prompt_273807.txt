For a workshop on atrocity crimes I've been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my considered views on genocide as a legal and moral category, and I'd like it stress-tested before I send it around. Here is what I currently hold:

1. What makes genocide a distinctive wrong is the destruction of a group as such; it is not reducible to the sum of the harms inflicted on individual victims, and any account that collapses it into mass murder of many individuals has lost the concept.
2. Moral standing belongs to individual persons alone; collectivities — nations, ethnic groups, cultures — have no interests beyond the interests of their members.
3. The special intent requirement is essential to the concept and justified: the intent to destroy a group is precisely what makes genocide the crime of crimes, set above other mass atrocities.
4. The label makes no difference to moral gravity: mass killing of equal scale that lacks the special intent is just as bad as genocide, and it would be a mistake to let the perpetrators' motives aggravate the wrongness of the killing itself.
5. Cultural destruction can itself be genocidal: a group can be destroyed as a group through the elimination of its language, institutions, and practices, even if every member survives.
6. The designation matters practically: classifying an atrocity as genocide, rather than as some other mass crime, should trigger distinctive obligations of intervention on third parties.

Tell me whether this set hangs together. For each pair in genuine tension, state the tension precisely, rank the tensions by severity, and for each one identify the cheapest revision that resolves it and what that revision costs. Also identify any pair that looks inconsistent but survives scrutiny, and explain why it survives. I haven't decided which commitment I would sacrifice if forced, so present the audit without steering me toward one; but do conclude by saying which single revision would restore consistency at the lowest overall cost.
