I manage quality assurance for a small calibration laboratory, and last week I had a long and inconclusive argument with a friend from my undergraduate philosophy days about how we certify instruments. The dispute began when I described a routine job: validating a replacement pressure gauge by running it alongside its predecessor for a fortnight and confirming the readings agree. My friend objected that this proves nothing, since a consistently broken gauge and its equally broken successor would also agree, and he dug out a passage from David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section IV, Part II, which he says settles the matter against me: "I have found that such an object has always been attended with such an effect, and I foresee, that other objects, which are, in appearance, similar, will be attended with similar effects." His claim was that every validation we perform is just this transition dressed up in documentation, and that if Hume is right that such transitions lack a rational foundation, then our certificates are worthless. I replied that Hume's point, whatever exactly it is, cannot possibly condemn the whole of metrology, since cross-checking instruments against independent standards plainly does add something; but when he pressed me on what exactly it adds and how I would tell a legitimate validation from a merely circular one, I found I could not give him a rule. I have since read enough to know that contemporary epistemology distinguishes several phenomena here -- premise-circularity versus rule-circularity, the so-called easy knowledge problem, and bootstrapping arguments of the sort where one establishes a gauge's reliability using only its own outputs -- but I cannot translate any of it into something my technicians could apply at the bench.

What I would like is a decision procedure my staff can actually run when reviewing a proposed validation, one that operationalizes whatever survives of these philosophical distinctions for someone with no philosophy at all. It should give the questions to ask in a fixed order (for instance, whether the procedure's conclusion appears among its own inputs; whether the reliability of the instrument class is assumed anywhere upstream; whether agreement between two instruments counts as independent evidence given their shared design and shared reference standard; and what, at the bottom of the chain, is simply trusted and on what grounds), state what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, say where the procedure terminates in a refusal, and mark explicitly the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and a senior person must sign off. Please then apply it to three cases drawn from our actual workload: first, the side-by-side comparison of the new and old pressure gauges described above; second, two thermometers of identical manufacture agreeing with each other in a triple-point-of-water cell; and third, a laboratory's track-record argument that its balances are reliable because they have passed every previous annual audit conducted with the same reference weights. I would like at least one of these to come out with a verdict I will not enjoy, if that is where the reasoning honestly leads, and I would like the procedure's treatment of the Hume passage to say plainly whether my friend has identified a genuine defect in our practice or conflated our problem with a different and deeper one.

Two further requests. First, I want the reasoning to be self-critical: tell me which premise or step of the procedure you are least confident in, and state explicitly what argument, counterexample, or consideration would lead you to reverse a verdict -- particularly the verdict on the case I will dislike -- so that I know what to listen for if my friend returns with reinforcements. Second, please write the entire answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables; I intend to paste it into a letter, and I would like it to read like one.
