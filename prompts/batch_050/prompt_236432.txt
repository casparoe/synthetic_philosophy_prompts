A colleague in my philosophy-of-biology reading group has built an argument around a passage from Darwin’s Preface to *The Descent of Man*:

“I may take this opportunity of remarking that my critics frequently assume that I attribute all changes of corporeal structure and mental power exclusively to the natural selection of such variations as are often called spontaneous; whereas, even in the first edition of the ‘Origin of Species,’ I distinctly stated that great weight must be attributed to the inherited effects of use and disuse, with respect both to the body and mind.”

His argument is meant to show that group selection explanations of human cooperation are confused. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I also cannot cleanly locate the mistake.

(1) In the passage, Darwin rejects the assumption that he attributes all changes of structure or mind “exclusively” to natural selection of spontaneous variations; he explicitly leaves room for use and disuse, direct effects of conditions, and correlated growth.

(2) A group-selection explanation of a human social trait — say, courage in defense of one’s tribe, punishment of defectors, or willingness to share food beyond one’s offspring — purports to explain a change in “mental power” by selection among groups.

(3) Therefore, such an explanation attributes a change of mental power to natural selection of spontaneous variation.

(4) But Darwin has just warned against attributing changes of mental power exclusively to natural selection of spontaneous variation.

(5) Moreover, if selection really acted at the group level, the group-level advantage would have to be more than a correlated by-product of lower-level advantages; otherwise the “group selection” is only a redescription of selection on individuals or genes.

(6) So a non-vacuous group-selection explanation must claim that natural selection, operating at the group level, is the distinctive and privileged cause of the trait.

(7) Hence group selectionists are committed to exactly the kind of exclusive selectionist attribution Darwin disavows in (1).

(8) The same style of argument would equally condemn kin-selection and sexual-selection explanations whenever they describe a trait as favored because it benefits relatives or mates rather than the isolated individual, since those too privilege one causal route for a “mental” change.

(9) Since (8) is absurd, the only stable position is that cooperation must be explained at the level of the individual organism, with talk of higher-level benefit at most metaphorical.

My colleague insists that (9) follows even if one is not a gene-selection zealot: he says the unit of selection has to be somewhere, and Darwin’s pluralism blocks any attempt to put it “in the group” for psychological traits. I would like to preserve three things if possible: Darwin’s anti-exclusivism, the intelligibility of multilevel selection, and the distinction between a trait’s spreading because of group advantage and its merely correlating with group advantage.

Which premise or transition should I reject? Please identify the exact point where the argument gains its apparent force — especially any shift between “not exclusively,” “not primarily,” “not distinctively,” and “not at all” — and explain why that move looked legitimate given Darwin’s wording. Then price the main exits: strict individual-level selectionism, inclusive-fitness reasoning, gene’s-eye bookkeeping, and multilevel selection. For each, say what it must concede about the passage and about whether “the group benefited” can ever be an explanatory fact rather than a summary of lower-level events. Do not supply a reading list; reason from the concepts themselves.
