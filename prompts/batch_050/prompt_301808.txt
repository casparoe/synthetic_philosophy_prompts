Last weekend I had two friends over -- both do ML stuff, I'm the odd one out studying poli sci -- and they got into a real argument over dinner about this "low-impact AI" idea, basically whether you can train an agent to avoid side effects without telling it exactly what you value. The setup they kept using: you give a robot some task in your house and add a penalty for changing the world too much, so it doesn't break your lamp on the way to fetching the remote.

Marta's position was that this whole approach is hopeless without a full value specification. Her example: penalize the robot for moving the world away from how it started, and the "safe" robot now has a reason to keep things from changing at all -- it'll stop your kids from redecorating, maybe stop *you* from knocking the lamp over, or it'll break the lamp and then carefully restore it, which she said is obviously not what anyone meant by safe. She said every fix is the same story: you patch one loophole, the agent finds another, because "change" is not the thing you actually care about. What you care about is which changes are good, bad, or neutral, and that's a value judgment, full stop.

Diego pushed back for like an hour. His move: the problem isn't impact penalties, it's the dumb baseline. Don't compare to the initial state -- compare to what *would have happened if the agent had done nothing*. Then breaking-and-repairing looks bad, because in the do-nothing world the lamp never broke, and the robot has no incentive to freeze the kids because the kids redecorating happens in the do-nothing world too, so it registers as neutral. He conceded you need to be cleverer -- like re-checking the counterfactual each step rather than once at the start, so the agent doesn't get credit for delaying a mess -- but said this shows the concept is fixable in principle. Marta's comeback, which is where it got heated: the counterfactual "what would have happened" isn't some objective fact sitting in the world, you need a whole predictive model of the future to compute it, and any tractable version picks out *which features of the world to track* -- and that choice is exactly where the values sneak back in. Track lamp position and not the kid's drawing, and you've already decided what matters. Diego said humans manage "don't make a mess" fine with vague commonsense defaults, so it's not values all the way down, it's just defaults. Marta said humans manage it *because* they have values, and the defaults are doing the work he claims isn't needed. Then they both looked at me and I had genuinely nothing.
