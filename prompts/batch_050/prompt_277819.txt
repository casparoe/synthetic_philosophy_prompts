Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about when a bunch of things add up to a further thing — the so-called special composition question — and we ended the evening less certain than when we started. I would like help reconstructing my actual position from my case judgments. Please respond as a metaphysician with expertise in mereology: think carefully, and before settling on an answer, briefly brainstorm at least three candidate principles, including at least one permissive and one restrictive, then evaluate them against my cases.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. Sand poured out of a truck onto a beach: no new object, just grains arranged heapwise.
2. An ordinary wooden table: yes, one object, and the planks and screws are its parts.
3. The table together with the napkin currently lying on it: no. My friend called the alleged fusion "an object only a philosopher could love," and I think she is right.
4. Two strangers whose hands briefly touch on the same subway pole: no composite object, not even for the duration of the contact.
5. Two neodymium magnets snapped together: still just two magnets, held fast.
6. Two steel pieces permanently welded: here I waver, but I lean toward one object now, where before there were two. I cannot defend the asymmetry with the magnets, which bothers me.
7. A living cat: clearly one object — the clearest yes on the list.
8. An ant colony: I say no object, just ants cooperating, even though the colony forages, wars, and repairs itself in ways no single ant does.
9. A murmuration of starlings: no object, even though it moves as if choreographed.
10. A binary star system: no object, just two stars bound by gravity.

My tentative principle, offered at 1 a.m. and now suspect: composition occurs exactly when the plurality exhibits unified causal activity, where the members constrain one another's behavior and the collective does things no member does alone. My friend pointed out that this principle endorses the ant colony (which does everything on its list) and probably the sand heap (a dune resists wind and migrates as a unit), both of which I denied, and that it might exclude the welded joint if the pieces just sit there inertly, which I wanted to include. I am not willing to simply stipulate that living things are special, since that feels like the conclusion wearing a disguise.

What I would like from you:

- The most precise principle, or small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, with each clause motivated by the specific case that forces it.
- An honest accounting of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and which single verdict you would advise me to abandon if I want the simplest viable principle.
- What your preferred principle predicts for cases I did not list: a coral reef, a symphony performance, a distributed computing cluster working one problem, and a human body the moment after death.
- Finally, please state explicitly what would change your recommendation: which consideration, argument, or further case would lead you to a different verdict, and where you judge your own reasoning most likely to fail.
