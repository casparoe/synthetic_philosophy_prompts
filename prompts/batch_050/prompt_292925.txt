Is there a name for this phenomenon? In discussions of criminal responsibility and moral psychology I keep encountering a pattern that doesn't seem to have a settled label, and I'd like to know whether the literature has one before I coin my own.

Two cases, one extreme and one mundane. A repeat violent offender is interviewed after his latest offense. He is fluent: he can explain why the act was wrong, predict in detail how the victim suffered, deploy the relevant moral principles more articulately than most of us could, and uses all of this accurately when reasoning about other people's conduct. Yet he reports — and the clinicians interviewing him find it credible — that at the moment he acted, none of it was before his mind. He did not judge the act permissible and proceed on that judgment; he did not register the prohibition and override it; the wrongness of what he was doing simply figured nowhere among the thoughts he was having, the way a fact you know perfectly well can fail to come to you during an exam. The knowledge appears to be there whenever he is asked, and nowhere whenever he acts.

Second case: a manager signs off on a decision she could not, if challenged, defend. A week later, when a journalist calls, she reconstructs immediately and unprompted exactly why it was wrong. But she insists — plausibly, as far as anyone can tell — that none of these considerations crossed her mind while she was signing; she was thinking about the deadline.

What strikes me is that commentators slide between saying such people "don't really know right from wrong" and saying they "know but don't care," and both descriptions seem to miss the structure: the knowing looks intact but is somehow never switched on at the moment of decision. I gather that the debate over whether psychopaths possess moral knowledge bears on this, but what I've seen of it focuses on what such offenders feel or fail to feel, not on this peculiar temporal pattern in what they know.

So: is there an established concept for this specific thing — in moral psychology, action theory, or philosophy of mind? If there are several nearby concepts, which fits best, and why exactly do the others fail? I am especially unsure how it should be distinguished from akrasia, where the better judgment is presumably present and gets defeated; from self-deception; from what lawyers call willful blindness, which seems to involve actively keeping oneself ignorant rather than passively failing to activate knowledge one already has; and from what social psychologists call moral disengagement, which seems to involve talking oneself out of one's standards rather than never consulting them. If no established concept fits, I would rather you coined a term than rounded my cases off to the nearest neighbor: give it a precise definition, mark the boundary cases (does the man in a rage who "only afterward realized what he'd done" fall under it, or is that a different thing?), and say what difference the distinction makes, if any, to whether the psychopathic offender knows that what he does is wrong.

I'm not after a bare label; what I want is the carving — where this phenomenon ends and its neighbors begin.
