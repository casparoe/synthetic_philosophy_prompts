I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on causation for my disertation, and I'm stuck on a construction problem I want to road-test before I put it in a chapter.

Quick background so we're on the same page: the standard trouble for counterfactual accounts of causation by omission is that dependence overgenerates. If I'd flown across the country and watered my neighbour's plant last Tuesday it would have lived, but nobody thinks my staying home caused its death. The usual fixes — norm- or default-based accounts (the gardener's omission counts because watering was her job, mine wasn't) or Dowe-style views that absences are never genuine causes at all — are built to handle exactly those everyday cases. What I haven't seen constructed cleanly is pressure from the opposite direction: an omission that intuitively IS a cause of an outcome even though the outcome does not counterfactually depend on the omission, without using the boring tricks.

So build me a thought experiment to these specs:

1. Realistic and short. A garden, a hospital ward, a server room — one paragraph. No demons, no omniscient observers, and crucially no second agent or back-up system waiting in the wings, because overdetermination, preemption, and trumping are the well-worn ways to get causation without dependence and I want those ruled out.
2. One agent omits to do something, and a bad outcome occurs.
3. The counterfactual "had the agent acted, the outcome would not have occurred" comes out false, or at least not determinately true — and the reason has to be built into the physics or biology of the case, not into some other agent's contingent intentions. (Hint, ignore if you have a better idea: indeterminism is the obvious lever, since a fix that only usually works breaks the counterfactual. But then you have to make sure the outcome in the actual run of events traces to the omission itself, not to the chancy process just happening to fire badly.)
4. Ordinary intuition should still say the omission caused the outcome, and you have to earn that intuition from the scenario rather than just asserting it.

Then run the checks. State the relevant counterfactual and show exactly why it fails. Say what an ordinary speaker would presumably judge and why. Then walk through three views: a straight counterfactual account, a norm/default-based account of which omissions count as causes, and a Dowe-style denial that absences cause anything. For each, say whether it vindicates or rejects the intuitive verdict and at what cost. One thing I genuinely don't know: the counterfactual theorist's usual repair moves — making the event more fragile, retreating to quasi-dependence, or switching to chance-raising — do any of them even apply to absences, given that an absence has no intrinsic character to duplicate across worlds and isn't obviously the kind of thing that raises chances? Work that out rather than gesturing at it.

Finally, tell me which of the three views handles your case best and defend that pick. But be honest about the soft spot: what consideration, objection, or counter-case would change your mind, and where is the reasoning you just gave most likely to fall apart?
