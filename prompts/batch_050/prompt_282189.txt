I am designing a seminar exercise for a course on rational choice theory, and I need a decision tree that sorts a respondent's reaction to the Allais paradox into a named position — a flowchart someone works through without ever being asked which theory they endorse. Below I specify exactly what I need, since a generic summary of the paradox is not what I'm after.

The tree should begin from the canonical setup, restated in plain terms: a choice between a sure million and a lottery over zero, one million, and five million; then a second choice between two riskier lotteries built by removing a common consequence from the first pair. Assume the respondent has just revealed the classical pattern — the safe option in the first pair, the long shot in the second — and each subsequent node diagnoses what they make of that pattern.

Requirements:

1. Seven or eight branch nodes, each phrased as a question about a concrete case or a commitment the respondent can assess without knowing any terminology. At least three nodes must use *new* cases rather than the original pair: for example, a sequential version where the common consequence is resolved first and the respondent must choose whether to pre-commit; a version where the probabilities are described as compound lotteries that reduce to the originals; and a version in which the prizes are experiences (e.g., certain mild pleasure vs. a gamble including ecstasy and nothing) rather than money, to test whether the response survives de-monetization.

2. Six or seven leaves, which must include: the bullet-biting expected-utility position that treats one of the two choices as a simple mistake; a smooth non-expected-utility view that keeps transitive preferences over lotteries but lets indifference curves fan out; a reference-dependent view on which outcomes are coded as gains and losses relative to a shifting status quo; a view built on anticipated regret between what was chosen and what would have been; a probability-weighting view on which small probabilities and near-certainties are distorted; and a dynamic-choice view on which the static pattern is fine because rationality governs sequences of choices, not single ones. You may add a seventh leaf (e.g., a deflationary position denying that preferences over lotteries are well-defined enough to be constrained) if your tree generates it naturally.

3. Build the tree from the logical space first: identify which questions genuinely separate the positions, then locate the positions in the answer space. If some combination of answers is uninhabited, note it and say briefly whether that is because the position would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

4. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — I suspect regret-based and probability-weighting views will need a specially constructed case to come apart, and I want either that case or an explicit admission that they are extensionally indistinguishable at the level of binary choices.

The intended reader is a graduate student who has seen the paradox once, in passing. Every question should be answerable from the description of the case alone.
