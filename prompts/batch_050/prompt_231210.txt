I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but over the past year I've been reading, on and off, the literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I've been running a small reading group on it with colleagues. My interest isn't idle: I build forecasting systems for a living, and I keep hitting a concrete version of the problem. One of my models outputs a probability — say, an 80% chance that a server fleet saturates within the hour — while a separate diagnostic tells me the model has been systematically overconfident on days resembling today. Should the system report 80%, the output of its best object-level computation, or a lower figure, deferring to the evidence about its own unreliability? Either choice seems defensible, and the two choices seem to map onto the two camps in what I've been reading: the "level-splitters," who think a belief can rationally diverge from what your evidence about your own reasoning recommends, and their opponents, who think rational belief must answer to your total evidence, higher-order evidence included, so that believing p while believing you shouldn't believe p is never fully rational.

What puzzles me is that this debate appears completely stalled. As far as I can tell, both sides have known all the standard cases for years — the impaired-reasoning cases, the cases where misleading evidence impugns your own track record — and every argument now has a standard reply. Level-splitters lean on cases where ignoring the object-level evidence feels absurd; their opponents lean on the apparent incoherence of avowing the akratic state. Nobody converts anybody.

My tentative hypothesis is that the dispute isn't really about which beliefs are rational. I suspect the camps are using "rational" to do different jobs — one as a standard for the output of a reasoning process considered on its own, the other as a standard for the agent as a whole — and possibly also disagreeing, underneath that, about whether evidence about one's own processing can be weighed in the same units as object-level evidence at all. But I may be wrong; perhaps there's a substantive disagreement I've missed.

So: diagnose the impasse. Is this a verbal dispute about "rational," a substantive dispute about what counts as evidence, two different questions being answered under one heading, or bedrock clashing intuitions about particular cases? Give reasons grounded in what each side actually argues, not a survey of positions. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal or probabilistic result, a new kind of case, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. And if my engineering framing imports assumptions that distort the philosophical debate, I want to know that too.
