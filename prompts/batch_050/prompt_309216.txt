You are a philosopher with particular expertise in moral responsibility and in P. F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes in his essay Freedom and Resentment. Please think carefully and work in stages: first brainstorm candidate rules, then test them against the record, then commit to a prediction. Do not rush to the verdict; the reasoning about rules is the main deliverable.

I sit on the professional conduct committee of a national licensing body for chartered accountants. Some years ago the committee adopted an explicitly Strawson-inspired framework under which every substantiated violation must be assigned to one of three tracks. In the answerable track, the member is treated as a full participant in the moral community: findings are framed as deserved, sanctions express the profession's resentment, and the member is called to account. In the excused track, the member remains within the participant relationship -- we still say the act was wrong, and we still regard the member as one of us -- but the particular violation is excused, so no sanction expresses blame, though restitution and correction may be required. In the objective track, we suspend the reactive attitudes altogether: no blame, no demand to answer, only management -- conditions on practice, supervision, or suspension, framed purely as risk control, the way one handles a problem to be contained rather than a person to be confronted.

Our rulings to date:

1. A member who fabricated working papers during an untreated manic episode, diagnosed afterward and since stabilized: objective track.
2. A member who overbilled two clients during months of round-the-clock care for a dying parent, sleeping a few hours a night, and who confessed before anyone noticed: excused track, with restitution required.
3. A member who padded expense claims over three years, in stable personal circumstances, and defended the practice as standard in his office: answerable track, sanctioned.
4. A member with early-onset Alzheimer's disease who signed off on negligent work in the period before diagnosis: objective track, license restricted.
5. A member who backdated a document under explicit orders from a manager who threatened to fire her and blacklist her in the industry: excused track.
6. A member with well-managed, longstanding depression who knowingly misrepresented his qualifications to win an engagement: answerable track, sanctioned.

The case now before us: a member in his thirties, intelligent and fully lucid, who over five years systematically steered clients toward products paying him concealed commissions. A neuropsychological evaluation commissioned by his lawyer reports no mental illness and no impairment of understanding or impulse control, but documents a childhood of severe deprivation and abuse and describes what the evaluator calls a profound failure of moral formation: the member understands our rules as rules and can recite the code, yet appears to feel no guilt, shows no resentment when wronged himself, and exhibits no sense of being one participant among others bound to them; the evaluator adds that he has treated these proceedings throughout as an adversarial game to be optimized. The evaluator concedes that the member perfectly well understood what he was doing and that we prohibit it.

Please answer the following, in order:

(a) Set out at least three distinct rules the committee could plausibly have been applying, each consistent with all six prior rulings. Choose rules that diverge on the new case, and state each one precisely enough that its verdicts can be derived rather than guessed.

(b) State what each rule predicts for the new case, with the derivation.

(c) Commit to a prediction: which track will the committee most likely choose, and why? Identify which prior ruling is the closest analog to the new case, and say which of your candidate rules you take to be the one the committee has actually been running.

(d) Describe the further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between your two leading candidate rules -- that is, the case on which they deliver different tracks -- and say what each would rule.

One final request. Because our framework is explicitly Strawsonian, please conclude by addressing the framework itself: if the track record I have described has drifted from what Strawson's own treatment of excuses and exemptions would counsel for the new case, in either direction, say so plainly, and say whether the drift is a defensible development of the view or a misunderstanding of it.
