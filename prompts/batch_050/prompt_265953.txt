I am a professor of philosophy preparing a paper for a workshop on African ethics, and I would value detailed critical comments on the central argument before I submit the draft. The paper attempts to extend relational conceptions of personhood — the family of views, associated with the ubuntu tradition, according to which one becomes and remains a person through one's relations with others rather than through the possession of some intrinsic property such as rationality or a soul — to the case of the dead. My working thesis is that if personhood is genuinely constituted by relations, then the dead do not cease to be persons all at once; rather, their personhood diminishes gradually as the web of living relations in which they figured — relations of memory, of obligation, of projects carried on in their name — thins and finally dissolves. I call the relevant relations mnemonic relations, and I argue that while a community sustains them, the deceased retains a diminished but morally significant personhood, sufficient to ground duties: it can wrong the dead to defame them, to break promises made to them, or to destroy their unfinished work.

The argument, as it currently stands, runs roughly as follows. (1) Personhood is constituted by reciprocal relations of recognition and care, not by intrinsic features of the individual. (2) Reciprocity admits of degrees and of asymmetries: a newborn infant cannot yet reciprocate, yet we do not deny it personhood, because the relation is sustained by the expectation of future reciprocity; similarly, I claim, the dead can figure in relations sustained by the memory of past reciprocity. (3) Therefore the dead retain personhood in proportion to the vitality of the mnemonic relations that communities maintain towards them. I then test the thesis against a case: a deceased poet's daughter discovers her mother's unpublished manuscripts and, judging them embarrassing, wishes to burn them, which the mother had expressly forbidden. I argue that the destruction wrongs the mother herself, not merely her admirers or posterity. I also flag, but do not resolve, what I take to be the sharpest objection to the view: when the last person who remembers the deceased dies, does the deceased's personhood lapse at that moment — and if so, is it incoherent to hold that the daughter wronged her mother at a time when the mother's personhood was later destined to lapse anyway?

Please comment on the following, in order of importance: first, whether the inference from (1) and (2) to (3) is valid, and if not, what additional premise would be needed and whether it is plausible; secondly, which premise you take to be the weakest, and how a defender of a more orthodox relational view might resist my extension while retaining the claim about infants; thirdly, whether the manuscript case is decided correctly, or whether a relational theorist should instead say the wrong falls entirely on the living; and fourthly, any obscurities in the notion of a mnemonic relation as I have sketched it. I am not asking for bibliographical suggestions or references to the secondary literature — I would like engagement with the argument as stated. Please write your comments in British English.
