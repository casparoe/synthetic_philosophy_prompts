In my philosophy of mind course we have just finished a unit on Dennett's notion of a free-floating rationale: a genuine reason for the existence of some trait, behaviour or practice that no reasoner has ever represented or needed to represent. The stock examples are biological — the reason a gene or a reflex is there, articulated by no one — but our lecturer insists the idea applies to human practices too, and that is where the seminar got stuck. Several of us could not see what work the word rationale does once you have told the full selection story: if a practice persists because it was copied from the successful, isn't the rationale just shorthand for that history? Others thought the rationale is something over and above the history, because not everything that gets selected has a reason behind it. Our tutor has set us the job of building a case that would settle this, and I would like your help constructing it properly.

Here is what I need. Build a pair of scenarios, each describing a human social practice that persists through imitation and differential success, where the two scenarios are as near-identical as you can make them: same practice, same selection dynamics, same actual benefit accruing, and in both cases no participant has any inkling of the benefit. The pair should differ in exactly one structural respect, chosen so that in one scenario we should attribute a genuine free-floating rationale to the practice and in the other we should not — even though the practice is equally beneficial and equally uncomprehended in both. In other words, isolate the variable that marks the difference between a benefit that is the reason the practice exists and a benefit that merely happens to accompany a practice that exists for other reasons.

Constraints. No biology: no genes, organisms, immune systems or animal behaviour, since those are the cases everyone already has views about. No markets, prices or invisible-hand mechanisms, for the same reason. The practice should be mundane — something on the level of a craft tradition, a professional custom, a rule of etiquette or a institutional habit — and each scenario should be a single paragraph. You must make the participants' ignorance of the benefit psychologically plausible rather than stipulated (if anyone in the story could easily notice the benefit, the case fails, because then there is a candidate reasoner). And the selection story must be sketched concretely enough that one can see why the practice persists, not just asserted.

After presenting the pair, do the following. First, state the candidate rationale in each scenario and confirm that both scenarios genuinely satisfy the no-reasoner condition. Secondly, name the single variable you varied and argue that it is the right one: that is, show that it tracks the intuitive verdict about where a rationale is present, and defend it against the obvious rival suggestion that the only real difference lies in what we happen to know or care about. Thirdly, apply the counterfactual test — had the benefit been absent, would the practice have persisted anyway? — to both scenarios, and say whether that test delivers the same verdict as your variable or comes apart from it. Fourthly, give the strongest response available to the sceptic who holds that free-floating rationale is nothing but a picturesque redescription of selection history: does your pair force that sceptic to concede a distinction, or can she absorb both scenarios without loss? Finally, say what happens to your argument if the variable you isolated is removed and the two scenarios collapse into one — I want to know whether the case generates its verdicts or merely stipulates them, since that is exactly what my tutor will press on.
