Before retiring I spent thirty-five years as an experimental hydrologist, and only in the last few years, reading philosophy of science in my spare time, have I begun to wonder about a family of practices I once performed quite unreflectively. They are these. First, when a measurement is subject to several distinct sources of error, we combine the uncertainties by adding them in quadrature — which is to say, we treat the error sources as uncorrelated without, in most cases, having done anything to verify that they are. Second, we regard agreement between measurements made by different methods, instruments, or laboratories as far stronger confirmation than agreement between repetitions of a single procedure; a replication in a rival lab counts for something that a tenth repetition at my own bench does not. Third, and here is what now strikes me as odd, we are also suspicious when agreement is too good — when data fit a hypothesis more closely than the stated uncertainties should allow, we begin to suspect shared bias, over-fitting, or worse. So the practice seems to demand agreement, but only roughly the amount of agreement that independence would predict, no more and no less.

My question is what this web of practices presupposes, philosophically. To begin with: what notion of independence is actually doing the work? There seem to be at least three candidates — causal independence (no common cause acting on the error sources), statistical independence (uncorrelated deviations), and something more epistemic (one observation's bearing on the hypothesis not depending on another's) — and I can no longer tell whether my former practice assumed these coincide, or whether it can survive their coming apart. Further, the practice appears to embody a standing presumption of independence: treat error sources, instruments, and laboratories as independent unless there is positive evidence of correlation, while never demanding positive evidence of independence. Is that presumption defensible as anything more than convenience? Can it be stated precisely — as a principle about priors, or about what an absence of evidence licenses — without collapsing into either a tautology or a falsehood? Is the asymmetry coherent, and is it consistent with the third practice, which seems to presuppose that we know in advance how much disagreement independence would produce? Finally, where is the practice underdetermined — which rival epistemologies of evidence would license it just as well — and what would concretely have to change in the laboratory if we abandoned the presumption and instead required investigators to rule out dependencies affirmatively before combining or comparing results?
