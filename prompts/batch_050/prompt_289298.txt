So a book on consciousness I picked up has a chapter on Douglas Hofstadter's ideas, and it summarizes his notion of tangled hierarchies and strange loops like this: 'In an ordinary hierarchy, each level governs the one below it, and nothing below ever reaches back up. In a tangled hierarchy, though, climbing up far enough loops you back to the bottom, so causation runs in a circle and no level is really in charge. Escher's Drawing Hands is the classic case: each hand draws the other into existence, which is a genuine paradox showing that self-reference violates normal logic. But every such tangle also contains what Hofstadter calls an "inviolate level" -- the topmost level of the hierarchy, which stands safely outside the loop. For Drawing Hands that's Escher himself. Gödel showed arithmetic works the same way: because a formal system can describe itself, it loops back on itself, and that's why it must contain true statements it can't prove. Hofstadter's conclusion is that since consciousness is also a strange loop, the brain must contain an inviolate level too -- a part of the self the rest of the self can't touch -- which is why a mind can never fully understand itself.'

I read a bit of Gödel Escher Bach years ago and my sense is this is mostly right but that something about the hands and the "inviolate level" stuff is off, though I can't put my finger on it. Can you audit the summary properly? Which parts would Hofstadter actually accept, which are subtly wrong or conflating two things he keeps apart, and is anything just flat-out mistaken? And specifically: does the book's conclusion -- that a mind can never fully understand itself -- actually follow from anything correct in the summary, or does it only follow from the misstated version?
