I work as an actuary for a mutual insurer, and the topic has recently stopped being academic for me in two ways at once. Professionally, our regulator now requires a written fairness justification for the cross-subsidies embedded in our income-protection pool, and the committee drafting it keeps reaching for Rawls: we are told the design should be the one people would choose for the pool if they did not know which risk class they would end up in, and that the benefit schedule should be assessed by how it treats the worst-off claimants rather than by the average payout. Personally, I was reclassified into a higher risk band this spring after a diagnosis, so I now experience the cross-subsidy from the other side, and I find I genuinely want to know whether the Rawlsian framing my committee is using says something my professional framework cannot, or just says the same thing in a loftier register.

My request is a translation exercise, done honestly. Please restate the argument from the original position to the difference principle in the vocabulary I actually work in: ex ante contracting under symmetric ignorance, expected utility and risk aversion, pooling versus risk classification, cross-subsidization, adverse selection, moral hazard, lapse and re-entry. I know the Rawlsian machinery at a rough undergraduate level -- the veil, maximin, primary goods, the priority of the least advantaged -- but I think in the actuarial terms above and I want the position rebuilt in them as faithfully as possible, without quietly importing Rawlsian conclusions under the label of translation.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care most about. Three categories, please. What survives translation cleanly: my guess is that the veil of ignorance maps almost exactly onto symmetric ignorance in ex ante contracting, and that the case for pooling over risk classification translates without much loss, but tell me if I am wrong. What survives only with distortion: I suspect maximin lands in my vocabulary as a particular, and frankly extreme, specification of risk aversion, which seems to turn a thesis about justice into a claim about one parameter of a utility function -- and I know the Harsanyi-style rejoinder, that rational parties under ignorance would simply maximize expected utility, which in my terms looks like plain risk neutrality and makes the whole dispute look like an argument about the curvature of a utility function. Is that a distortion, or is it the honest translation? And what cannot be expressed in my framework at all: I have a nagging sense that nothing in premium mathematics corresponds to the separateness of persons -- an expected-loss calculation across a pool treats one claimant's catastrophic lifetime and a thousand small claims as commensurable -- and that ideas like the publicity of principles or the stability of a commitment you cannot lapse from have no actuarial home.

Finally, a verdict. Where the translation fails, which side is at fault? Is my framework missing concepts it would need to reason about fairness at all, or does the residue show that the Rawlsian apparatus is doing real work that pricing cannot -- and in the cases where my vocabulary swallows the position effortlessly, should I conclude the philosophical dispute was less substantive than it looked? I would rather be told that some of what my committee is saying is untranslatable nonsense, if that is your honest assessment, than receive a translation smoothed over to make everyone right.
