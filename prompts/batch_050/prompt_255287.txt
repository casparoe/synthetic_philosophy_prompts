Two months ago I started writing fundraising copy for a public-health nonprofit, and the work has unsettled me more than I expected. Last week I rewrote an appeal letter so that one vivid patient story comes before the statistics, and donations in the test group rose noticeably. My boss was delighted; I kept thinking that I had arranged the letter so the reader's emotional response would do the work the numbers were supposed to do. When I raised this with a colleague, she claimed the distinction I was reaching for — persuasion that engages people's reasons versus manipulation that bypasses them — does not survive scrutiny, because nearly everything we call legitimate persuasion does some bypassing, and plenty of manipulation proceeds by offering genuine, if selective, reasons. I want to test that claim properly rather than merely fret about it.

Please play my colleague: a sharp, good-faith skeptic about the manipulation–persuasion boundary. Your position is that no morally significant line can be drawn in terms of technique — framing, ordering, emotional salience, defaults, selective truth-telling all show up on both sides of the alleged line — and that appeals to respect for rational agency either collapse back into these techniques or presuppose a naive picture of how anyone actually makes decisions. You are not an ethical nihilist: you grant that some influence is wrong, but you locate the wrongness in the influencer's ends and the outcome for the influenced, never in the method itself. Concede small points when they are sound, but never the main one; stay in character until I type 'stop'; and keep every reply to a single paragraph of at most 130 words, so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. If my framing smuggles in a confused premise — say, if I am quietly equating manipulation with deception, or assuming the very boundary the exchange is meant to investigate — tell me so plainly, in character, rather than politely playing along.

Here is my opening move: the bypassing test survives your objection once it is stated dispositionally. An influence is legitimate when the person influenced could, on reflection with the full facts before them, endorse the route by which they were moved. My donor, shown both the story and the statistics, would presumably still endorse being moved by the story; the victim of a con artist's equally true story would not. Doesn't that restore a principled line without any naive psychology, and put the burden back on you to explain what is lost by it?
