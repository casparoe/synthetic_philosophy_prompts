So backstory: I've played this MMO for like six years, and last month someone social-engineered a guildmate of mine into handing over admin access to our guild's storage, and cleaned out basically everything the guild had accumulated -- items people had spent hundreds of real hours farming, some stuff that would've sold for actual money on third-party markets, plus a bunch of things with pure sentimental value (gifts from players who've since died or quit). The guild basically dissolved over it. Two people I considered real friends stopped talking to each other over who was to blame.

This got me thinking and I've landed on a position I want stress-tested. My view is roughly this: a virtual harm (like what happened to us) is genuinely real, not "just a game," but only derivatively. That is, the harm is real iff and exactly to the extent that the virtual object was functioning as a container for something non-virtual -- invested time, relationships, status, money, memories. The sword or the guild bank itself is nothing; destroy a copy of the game world on an abandoned server nobody will ever visit and nothing bad has happened, full stop. Call it "containment realism" about virtual harms: virtual stuff is like a bottle, and only what's inside it (the human stakes) makes breaking it matter. So the guy who robbed us didn't commit some new sui generis category of wrong, he just did plain old theft and betrayal through a new medium, and the magnitude of the wrong is fully exhausted by tallying up what was "in the bottle."

What would count against this? I want a real map of its exposure, not just the obvious "but it felt really bad to me" point. Some things I'm wondering about: Are there cases of virtual harm where the container model seems to misdescribe what's going on -- e.g., harms to avatars or characters people identify with in a more direct way, or harms done to NPC-like entities where there arguably is no container at all? Does the view wrongly predict that the same theft is equally bad whether it happens in a game or via, say, stealing a photo album, as long as the "contents" match? Would a committed containment realist concede that identity-style intuitions about one's avatar count against the view, or would they absorb those? Are there empirical findings about how people actually experience virtual loss that would embarrass the view? And is there a risk that the view is unfalsifiable -- that any apparent counterexample can be redescribed as "ah, but there was something in the container after all"?

Rank potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones defenders of this kind of view would themselves accept as real threats versus dismiss, and be honest if you think the position is actually in good shape. Please answer in American English.
