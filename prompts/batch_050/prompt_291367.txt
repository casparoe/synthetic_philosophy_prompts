I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a philosophy of biology seminar, and we're two weeks into the unit on functional explanation. I've been testing my intuitions against cases, and I want to know whether there's a single principle behind my verdicts -- or, failing that, which verdict I should give up.

Here are my gut responses. The heart's function is to pump blood, not to produce the thumping sound, even though it does both with equal reliability. A child born with a severe cardiac defect, whose heart never once manages to pump, still has a heart whose function is to pump -- it's malfunctioning, not functionless. My nose reliably holds up my glasses, but that's not its function. The rock I use as a paperweight does, I think, have holding papers down as its function, at least in my office. A bacterium with a brand-new point mutation that lets it metabolize a novel industrial solvent, in the very first generation before any selection has acted on it: I'd say breaking down the solvent is what that enzyme is for, its function, even now. But a lightning strike in a swamp that assembles a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a human heart, which then happens to circulate fluid through the duplicate body it landed in -- there I'm inclined to say pumping is not its function, merely something it does. And the human appendix: my sense is it has no function today, whatever it once did and whatever small contributions it currently makes to gut flora.

The last two cases before the appendix are the ones I can't reconcile: neither the mutant enzyme nor the swamp-heart has any selection or design history behind it, yet I want to grant the enzyme a function and deny the heart one. My professor suggested intentions do the work in the rock case, but nothing intended the enzyme either.

Is there one principled account of function that captures all of these verdicts, or a small set of candidates? If a clause needs motivating, tell me which case motivates it. Please also state, honestly, which of my verdicts your preferred account cannot accommodate and must overturn; give its predictions for two cases I haven't raised (a trait that was adaptive in ancestors but is now neutral or harmful, and a structure co-opted for a purpose it never evolved for, like feathers used for flight); and say whether the enzyme/swamp-heart asymmetry I feel can be defended or is just prejudice.

After the prose, please include a table with columns: Case | My verdict | Verdict under your proposed account | Agree? (yes/no) | Basis for the account's verdict (one sentence). Include rows for all my cases plus the two new ones.
