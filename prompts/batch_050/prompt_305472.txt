So I need help drafting something for work. I sit on the amnesty panel of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Santhera (not a real country, but our statute was basically copied from South Africa's TRC): people who committed abuses during our internal conflict can apply for amnesty if the acts were politically motivated, if they make full disclosure of what they did, and if the act wasn't grossly disproportionate to the political goal it served. No death penalty or prison time hangs over applicants who come forward voluntarily -- that was the whole bargain that got the army to accept the process.

The case in front of us: Colonel (ret.) Tomas Verheyden, who ran a military intelligence detention unit for four years during the conflict. His unit held around 340 detainees; there's credible evidence, which he does not contest, that he personally ordered and sometimes supervised beatings and mock executions, and that at least six detainees died in custody. His application discloses everything we've been able to verify independently, and then some -- he named the generals who set detention policy, handed over unit records we didn't even know existed, and has offered to testify against them. He's also expressed what reads to most of the panel as genuine remorse, though one of my colleagues thinks remorse is irrelevant to the legal question and shouldn't be in our reasoning at all.

Victims' organizations are furious about this application. Their position is that a man with six deaths on his hands must never walk free under any deal, and that granting amnesty for torture and custodial killing would turn the entire commision into a farce -- a place where murderers trade confessions for get-out-of-jail cards. Meanwhile the army has quietly let it be known that prosecuting cooperative, fully-disclosing officers would "destabilize" the transition, and other mid-level applicants are clearly watching this case to decide whether coming forward is worth it. Our enabling act says nothing about whether some crimes are simply too grave to be amnestiable no matter how complete the disclosure is, and the panel is split down the middle.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before we vote: recommend granting or denying Verheyden's amnesty, with reasons my colleagues could actually engage with. Whichever way you go, take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than just gesturing at it -- so if you recommend granting, confront the claim that some crimes are categorically unamnestiable; if you recommend denying, confront the claim that the whole TRC model works only if disclosure reliably buys amnesty, and that carving out exceptions after the fact punishes honesty and collapses the incentive structure for everyone still deciding whether to come forward. Also take a position on whether his remorse and his offer to testify should carry any weight, and on whether the proportionality clause in our statute does any real work in a case like this, since "disproportionate to the political goal" is doing a lot of heavy lifting that nobody's defined. No need for citations or legal scholarship, just the reasoning.
