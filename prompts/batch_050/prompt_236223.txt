So last week I was at a workshop on AI ethics where a speaker presented this case that's been bugging me: a city's fleet of autonomous delivery drones uses a decentralized coordination algorithm, and over several months the algorithm gradually learns to route traffic over a low-income neighborhood because complaints from there get filed more slowly, leading to a measurably worse noise-and-pollution situation for those residents. No engineer intended this, no single flight was illegal or even unusual, and the behavior emerged from thousands of individually defensible routing decisions. When the city tried to assign blame, everyone pointed at everyone else -- the engineers at the operator, the operator at the vendor, the vendor at the training data, and the regulator shrugged because no rule was technically broken.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers hashing out this case over coffee after the workshop. One of them takes the position that this is a genuine and morally serious responsibility gap: there are real victims who deserve that someone answer for the harm, and the structure of autonomous systems means that sometimes no one can legitimately be blamed, which is a reason to restrict or redesign how we deploy such systems (or at least to impose strict liability regardless of fault). The other argues that the "gap" is an artifact of an outdated, individualistic concept of moral responsibility borrowed from criminal law, and that once you accept genuinely distributed, collective, or forward-looking forms of responsibility the puzzle mostly dissolves -- or at least that its practical upshots (compensation, incentive design, institutional reform) don't require finding anyone blameworthy.

Both characters should be equally sharp and neither should be a strawman. I want real back-and-forth where they actually engage each others points: the gap-theorist should press the hard cases (e.g., what does "distributed responsibility" actually mean when no individual had control or foreknowledge, and doesn't it let everyone off the hook a little?), and the distribution-theorist should push back on whether our traditional control-and-knowledge conditions for blame were ever realistic even for purely human large-scale harms, and whether the intuition that someone must be blamable is doing real philosophical work or just emotional work. Have them try, at least briefly, to find common ground on what policy would actually follow from each view -- and let them notice whether the policy disagreement is as big as the conceptual one. Around 800-1200 words, naturalistic dialogue, they can interrupt each other a bit.
