I'm the secretary of a small housing co-op (14 people, extremely into process) and I just finished drafting our first real constitution. I was proud of one clause in particular: "Any provision of this constitution, including this clause, may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the membership." No sacred cows -- the rules can even change how they change. I got the idea from hearing about Nomic, that game where changing the rules is the whole point of playing.

Then I made the mistake of mentioning it to a friend who's in law school. She told me about the paradox of self-amendment, which she said goes like this: if a rule has authority over every rule including itself, then either it can use that authority to make itself unchangeable (contradiction, since it was supposed to be changeable) or it can't (contradiction, since its authority was supposed to reach every rule). So a self-amending clause is logically incoherent, and if our amendment clause is incoherent, every ammendment we pass under it is built on sand. Her proposed fix was to entrench the amendment clause, i.e. make the rule about how rules change the one rule that can never change. She said the U.S. Constitution does something like that with the bit about no state losing equal Senate suffrage without its consent.

Now I'm stuck, because the entrenched version honestly feels worse to me: it seems to just relocate the paradox, since couldn't we first vote to amend the entrenchment clause and then amend whatever we wanted? But she's the one who's actually read this stuff. So concretely: does the paradox of self-amendment really show my clause is incoherent and would taint our amendments, and is entrenching the amendment rule the only safe way out? Lead with the actual answer in one sentence, and keep the whole reply to a single paragraph -- I have to explain this at Thursday's meeting and people's eyes glaze over fast.
