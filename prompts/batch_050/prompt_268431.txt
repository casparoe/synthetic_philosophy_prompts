I am designing a unit for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of credences, devoted to the motivation for probabilism — the thesis that rational degrees of belief ought to conform to the axioms of the probability calculus. My notes contain what I take to be the three arguments that exhaust the standard repertoire:

(1) The Dutch book argument. If your credences violate the probability axioms, then a set of bets can be constructed, each of which you judge fair or favorable given your credences, which jointly guarantee you a net loss. Since it is irrational to accept a package of bets that loses money in every possible world, rational credences must be probabilistic.

(2) The accuracy-dominance argument. Credences aim at the truth, and given any legitimate measure of a credence function's closeness to the truth, every non-probabilistic credence function is strictly dominated: there is some probabilistic credence function that is closer to the truth in every possible world. Since it is irrational to hold a belief state when an available alternative is guaranteed to be epistemically better, rational credences must be probabilistic.

(3) The representation-theorem argument. If an agent's preferences over acts satisfy suitable axioms of rational choice, then those preferences can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to a unique probability function. Rational agents therefore behave exactly as if their degrees of belief were probabilistic, and there is arguably nothing more to having credences than being disposed to choose in ways that admit such a representation.

My real question: can you supply a fourth argument for probabilism that is genuinely distinct from these three? Every other motivation I have encountered is either a refinement of one of the above, a defensive move on its behalf, or an argument for some further Bayesian commitment (conditionalization, regularity, countable additivity) that already presupposes probabilism — so a restatement, a special case, or a mere conjunction of items (1)–(3) does not count, and neither does an argument whose conclusion outruns or falls short of probabilism itself. Ideally, your argument should also escape the signature weaknesses of the three: the Dutch book's reliance on pragmatic rather than purely epistemic considerations, the dominance argument's dependence on a contested choice of accuracy measure, and the representation theorem's merely as-if conclusion about what credences are.

After presenting the argument, please assess it: state candidly whether you think it succeeds or where it breaks down, identify which of the three it is closest to in spirit and why it nonetheless counts as distinct, and indicate what further premises or formal results would be needed to turn it from a sketch into a theorem.
