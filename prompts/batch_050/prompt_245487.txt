For an undergraduate course on ethics and public policy, I am preparing a workshop on population ethics and the pronatalism debate, and I want a decision tree that walks students from a shared starting point to a named population axiology. Begin at a node roughly equivalent to "we should care about how well the lives of future people go," and have each subsequent branch turn on a claim the views genuinely disagree about: whether value aggregates across persons at all, whether the mere number of people matters or only how well off people are on average, whether there is a critical level below which added lives subtract from value, whether we can affect value only by affecting people who will exist anyway, and how a view handles the repugnant conclusion and the mere addition paradox. End the tree in leaves such as total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, a critical-level view, a person-affecting view, a lexical or sufficientarian variant, and antinatalism, and note any combination of answers that no one actually defends, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored). Phrase every branch question as a concrete case or comparison (e.g., choosing between two futures with different populations) so a student can answer without knowing the labels, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. Close with a short note stating how confident you are that the tree carves the logical space correctly, and single out the branch or leaf you are least sure you have drawn in the right place.
