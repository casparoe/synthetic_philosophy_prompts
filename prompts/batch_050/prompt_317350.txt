I have provisionally adopted Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge: S knows that p just in case (1) p is true, (2) S believes p via some method M, (3) were p false, S would not believe p via M, and (4) were p true, S would believe p via M. I am already familiar with the headline criticisms -- that the theory denies closure of knowledge under known entailment, that it yields counterintuitive verdicts on skeptical hypotheses, and that the "same method" clause appears to do load-bearing work without a principled account of how methods are individuated. Please don't simply re-run those. What I want is a systematic map of the theory's exposure: what would have to be true for tracking to be wrong, and how exposed is it really?

In answering, please address the following:

First, assess two cases I have constructed as candidate counterexamples. (a) The Vacuous Reactor: an engineer believes "the reactor is stable" on the basis of its gauges. The gauges are in fact defective in a way that would misreport instability, but the reactor's design is such that in every nearby world in which it is unstable, the explosion destroys the gauges and the engineer along with them. So condition (3) is satisfied vacuously: in the closest not-p worlds, she forms no belief at all. Does the theory absurdly credit her with knowledge regardless of method, or is "you can't be wrong when you're dead" a feature rather than a bug? (b) The Lucky Astrologer: an agent forms the belief that p by reading a horoscope, but unbeknownst to her the astrologer has genuine inside information on this one topic, such that the horoscope would not have asserted p had p been false. Tracking appears satisfied by a hopeless method. Is this a refutation, or does it merely show that everything hinges on method individuation?

Second, identify what tracking predicts that its main rivals -- safety-based accounts, process reliabilism, and virtue epistemology -- do not, and ask whether any actual or possible pattern of intuitions about cases would discriminate between them decisively.

Third, distinguish what committed defenders of the theory would themselves concede as counting against it (for instance, is the denial of closure a conceded cost or an advertised benefit?) from what only its critics treat as damaging, and rank the full set of defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.

Finally, consider whether the theory's available retreats -- flexible method individuation, absorbing the closure denial -- render it unfalsifiable in practice, and whether unfalsifiability in a theory of knowledge is the vice it is usually taken to be elsewhere in philosophy.
