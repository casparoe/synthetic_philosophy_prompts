I am a professor of philosophy who has spent the better part of two decades on population ethics, and I have recently been drawn—somewhat against my will—into the debate over whether habitat destruction might, on balance, be good for wild animals. The rough argument circulating among my colleagues is that most wild animals are small, short-lived, r-selected creatures (insects, small fish, rodents, ground-nesting birds) whose lives plausibly contain more suffering than satisfaction, so that converting a biodiverse old-growth forest into a parking lot or a pasture eliminates an enormous quantity of net-negative existence. A graduate student of mine has now drafted a dissertation chapter defending a refined version of this claim, and before I respond to it I want to step back from the particular thesis and pin down what any adequate theory of the net welfare of an ecosystem would have to accomplish.

Write the desiderata for such a theory: a specification of what a framework for assessing the net welfare of a habitat must deliver before it can ground conclusions about whether destroying that habitat helps or harms. I want the requirements list itself to be the substance of your answer—for each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Among the things I expect you to address: how the theory must handle the comparison between many short, possibly net-negative lives and few long, possibly net-positive ones; what baseline it must compare a habitat against (barren land, agriculture, a different ecosystem, nonexistence) and why the choice of baseline is itself a philosophical commitment rather than a technicality; how it must deal with our deep uncertainty about whether insects and other invertebrates are sentient at all, without making the whole enterprise hostage to that question; how it must treat population dynamics—that destroying habitat often does not simply remove animals but changes which species and how many offspring exist; and which distinctions it cannot collapse, such as that between a life worth living and a life that adds value to the world, or between welfare density and total welfare. Flag which requirements you take to be genuine desiderata and which are merely the prejudices of a particular camp (total utilitarians, person-affecting theorists, environmentalists with non-welfarist commitments), and—this is the part I most care about—identify any subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the desiderata themselves are inconsistent that is a result worth having. If you think one requirement does more than any other to knock out the popular candidates, say so.

Conclude with a brief scorecard of the obvious contenders—total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, a critical-level view, and a person-affecting view—against your list, but keep this short; I can do the grading myself. The whole response must not exceed 900 words.
