Take Switzerland's response to Russia's 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine and analyze it as a test case for the theory of armed neutrality. The salient facts, briefly: Switzerland, permanently neutral since 1815 and defended by a conscript militia rather than a professional army, adopted the EU's economic sanctions against Russia -- an unprecedented step for it -- while refusing, on neutrality grounds, to let Germany or other states re-export Swiss-made ammunition to Ukraine, and while resisting pressure to join NATO or to release frozen assets beyond what sanctions required. Domestically, this triggered a fight over a constitutional initiative to lock in a stricter neutrality. What I want is a disciplined mapping in both directions. On the one side: what does the theory of armed neutrality -- neutrality as a security strategy that substitutes credible deterrence and non-provocation for alliance guarantees -- say Switzerland's options and payoffs actually were, and which of its decisions does the theory explain well? On the other side: where does the theory break? It was built for wars between competing great powers; does it have anything coherent to say about a war of aggression condemned as illegal by most of the international community, where strict impartiality arguably rewards the aggressor? And does the episode reveal that the theory was never really about legal impartiality at all, but about something else -- credibility, domestic cohesion, or bargaining position -- that the sanctions decision served and the ammunition refusal damaged?
