Over the years I've absorbed the standard rationalist-community framing of coherence theorems: if your preferences violate the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, you can be money-pumped, so any coherent agent behaves as if it were maximizing expected utility. That framing treats utility as defined by choice behavior — not as some inner glow of satisfaction, but as a compact redescription of what an agent would pick across lotteries.

Given that, I've never been able to make sense of the behavioral-economics claim that people routinely "choose against their own interests." If utility is nothing over and above revealed preference, then when I choose the chocolate cake over the salad, the cake had higher utility by definition — so in what sense could that choice have been bad for me? Isn't "people systematically choose the lower-utility option" a straightforward contradiction, on par with saying that people systematically prefer what they don't prefer?

To make it concrete: suppose a friend sincerely wants to quit smoking, endorses quitting when reflecting at leisure, yet keeps buying cigarettes when the urge hits. The revealed-preference picture seems forced to say that, on each occasion, the cigarette outranked abstinence in his utility ordering. So where does the extra normative standard come from, according to which his "true" utility favors quitting? Is the behavioral economist smuggling in a second, non-behavioral notion of utility — and if so, what exactly is that notion, and how would a strict defender of revealed preference respond without collapsing the distinction between what people want and what is good for them?
