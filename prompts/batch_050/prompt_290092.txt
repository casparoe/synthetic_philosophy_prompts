Yesterday I spent the better part of four hours arguing with a friend about acausal trade, and we ended the evening further apart than we began. Her position was roughly that cooperating with agents one cannot causally affect — agents in other possible worlds, other branches, or simply other rooms with no communication channel — is either rational decision theory extended consistently or an elaborate excuse to feel cooperative without paying any costs, and she could not decide which. My position was that the whole notion collapses the moment you ask what actually grounds the correlation between my decision and that of an agent I will never meet, and that talk of 'trading' with such agents is causal vocabulary doing non-causal work. Neither of us could state, at the end, what a successful theory in this area would even look like. That is what I now want.

Write the desiderata for a theory of acausal trade or acausal cooperation — what any adequate account must deliver before arguments like ours can even be adjudicated. Specifically, I want the requirements list: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the decision-theoretic results it must remain consistent with, together with the consideration or case motivating each requirement.

Some of the territory I expect the list to cover, though you should not treat my framing as exhaustive or necessarily correct: it must specify what grounds the correlation between two agents' choices when there is no causal channel between them (shared decision procedures, logical similarity, one being a simulation or model of the other), and it must say what counts as sufficient evidence of such a correlation without making the bar so low that one 'cooperates' with everything or so high that the theory never applies; it must distinguish genuine acausal considerations from ordinary evidential reasoning about causally connected agents, and from mere hopeful thinking dressed in formal vocabulary; it must explain why a vastly more powerful agent would have any reason to 'trade' with a vastly weaker one, or else bite the bullet that it would not; it must be consistent with, or explain its treatment of, the standard problem cases in the neighbourhood — Newcomb-style problems, the prisoner's dilemma between agents known to be similar, cases like Parfit's hitchhiker — without simply reducing to whichever decision theory one already favours; and it must say something about when acausal considerations are decision-relevant at all, given that there are presumably astronomically many possible agents one might 'cooperate' with and the expected values involved threaten to be unmanageable or dominated by measure-theoretic noise.

For each requirement, state the case or consideration behind it, and mark which requirements you take to be genuinely non-negotiable and which are commitments of particular camps (functional decision theorists, evidentialists, causalists, and so on). The part I most want to see: whether any pair of desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — I have a suspicion that demanding robustness against exploitation by arbitrary simulated or hypothetical agents and demanding that acausal trade be genuinely decision-relevant pull against each other, but I cannot make it precise — and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. If the list itself, properly stated, already rules out most positions in this space, that is a result I want flagged explicitly rather than softened.

A table is fine for the requirements themselves. Finish with a brief note on how the main existing contenders fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria: my friend and I can do the scoring ourselves, and it is the standard of adequacy we lacked, not another verdict.
