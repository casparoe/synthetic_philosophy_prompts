I want a thought experiment built to order. Its purpose is to test whether a system's history can make a difference to what, if anything, its internal states are about, while its present dispositions are held fixed. The two positions to be pitted against each other are Searle's — roughly, that artifacts possess only the intentionality that minded designers and users lend them, never any of their own — and Dennett's — roughly, that aboutness is a matter of what a system is good for, and that it makes no principled difference whether that usefulness was conferred by deliberate design, by natural selection, or by blind training. Build the case to the following specifications.

First, center it on two systems that are physically and behaviorally indistinguishable across all circumstances and that differ only in provenance: one acquired its information-exploiting capacities through deliberate design by agents who intended those capacities to track what they track, while the other arrived at the very same capacities through a process involving no intentions at all. Searle's view should deliver the verdict that only the first system's states are genuinely about anything; Dennett's should treat the two as on a par.

Second, keep it realistic: a present-day or near-future engineering, industrial, or biological setting. No roomfuls of rulebooks, no coin-sorting machines, no thermostats, and no molecule-for-molecule duplicates assembled by freak accidents. Do, however, note explicitly where your construction inherits the structure of such familiar cases, and whether that inheritance is a liability.

Third, the scenario itself must be a single paragraph, and it must generate the verdicts from the two views rather than stipulating them: it may not assert that either system means or represents anything.

Fourth, after the scenario, give each view's verdict on both systems along with its reasons; identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed; then sketch the strongest objection available to whichever view fares worse, and the other side's best reply.

Finally, close by saying which view you take the case to support and how firmly you hold that conclusion: state what argument or consideration would change your verdict, and where your construction is most likely to be cheating.
