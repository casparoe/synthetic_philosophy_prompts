As a novelist, I work daily with a distinction I suspect philosophers underuse: the difference between drafting a world and inhabiting one. I can draft, in a sentence, a character who survives being scanned and reassembled from different matter, or a society where torture is intrinsically admirable; but when I inhabit such premises chapter by chapter, tracing their consequences for the people inside them, some scenarios quietly disintegrate while others hold firm under pressure. I have come to treat this as evidence for a real position: that conceivability, properly understood not as casual imagining but as the survival of a scenario under sustained, consequence-tracing elaboration, is a genuine guide to what is metaphysically possible. Tell me what would count against this view. Which cases would be decisive, and which merely awkward: scenarios that survive every honest elaboration and yet turn out impossible, or possibilities that no amount of imaginative labor can render conceivable to creatures built like us? Which defeaters would a serious defender of conceivability herself concede as damaging, and which would she dismiss as confusing failure of imagination with failure of possibility? And is there anything about my novelist's version of the test, its dependence on a trained human capacity rather than a formal one, that makes it more vulnerable than the standard philosophical versions, or less?
