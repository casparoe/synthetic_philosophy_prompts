I am a PhD student working on the history and philosophy of monetary economics, and I keep running into a rhetorical move that I suspect is doing more work than it should. In discussions of Hume's essay "Of Money" (1752), people quote his remark that money "is none of the wheels of trade: It is the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy," and then use the lubricant picture to settle substantive questions: that money is merely a veil, that its quantity cannot matter for real output, and that Hume should be read as a strict quantity theorist for whom monetary injections wash out entirely into prices. Most recently this came up at a workshop where a colleague deployed the oil metaphor to dismiss my reading of the later parts of the very same essay, where Hume describes an interval in which newly issued coin circulates through the economy, quickens industry and employment, and only gradually raises prices -- a passage that seems to me to grant money real short-run effects, and one my colleague waved away as an inconsistent afterthought.

I would like a proper audit of the lubricant analogy rather than another round of intuition-trading. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly: what in Hume's economy plays the role of the oil, the wheels, the engine, the friction -- a table is fine. Then identify where the mapping strains or breaks. Some disanalogies seem obviously important to me (oil is not itself traded, held as a store of value, lent at interest, or denominated as the unit in which debts and wages are fixed; oil does not enter through particular hands at particular points and get spent before dispersing), but I want your judgment about which of these actually matter for the neutrality conclusion the analogy is made to support, and which are merely decorative failures that any metaphor would exhibit. I am especially interested in what the analogy quietly imports from the source domain: lubrication is exogenous to the machine, is neither produced nor consumed by it, and its quantity is irrelevant to the machine's output -- and it is not clear to me that money shares any of these features.

Finally, give me a verdict. Does the strict-neutrality reading survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the metaphor carrying the whole argument and the workshop dismissal of the "in the interval" passage question-begging? And if the lubricant picture is a bad one, is there a better analogy for the role Hume actually assigns to money across the essay as a whole -- one that accommodates both the long-run proportionality of prices to the money stock and the short-run stimulus to industry -- or is the honest conclusion that Hume's own view sits in tension with his own metaphor?
