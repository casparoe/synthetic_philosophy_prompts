Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Quinn's self-torturer case: a device with a dial of many settings is implanted in someone, each notch upward brings an imperceptibly small increase in pain along with a cash payment, and so, preferring each imperceptible-increment-plus-payment to its predecessor, the person keeps advancing the dial until they are in agony and would gladly surrender all the money to return to the lowest setting. My friend takes the case to refute the transitivity requirement on rational preference: every step is rationally preferred to the one before, yet the endpoint is dispreferred to the start, so rational preferences can cycle and transitivity is optional. I think that conclusion is too quick — the case shows not that intransitive preference can be rational but that something is wrong with assessing choices one at a time, and that rational evaluation must operate at the level of policies or whole sequences rather than individual steps. Please argue for my position. In doing so, take seriously the strongest point on my friend's side: that each local choice, considered on its own, genuinely does leave the agent better off by the agent's own lights, so that condemning any particular step seems to presuppose the very policy-level standpoint I am trying to vindicate — and explain how that standpoint can be justified without circularity.
