I teach a graduate seminar on practical reason, and for three semesters now the same fight has kept breaking out between my reasons-internalist contingent and my reasons-externalist contingent. Quick neutral stipulations so we're on the same page: by internalism I mean the Williams-style view that an agent A has a reason to φ only if A could come to be motivated to φ by sound deliberation starting from her actual subjective motivational set — her desires, commitments, dispositions, patterns of emotional reaction — where deliberation includes correcting false beliefs, working out means to ends, and exercising imagination, but does not include just being told she has a reason. By externalism I mean the denial of that: there are reasons that apply to an agent even if no amount of sound deliberation from her actual motivations would ever get her to care.

I've gotten tired of refereeing, so I've made them do an adversarial collaboration instead, and I want you to draft the joint statement they should produce. Here's what it needs:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. These have to be real agreements, not peace-and-love filler. For instance, both sides should be able to sign that a man who wants a gin and tonic and falsely believes the glass in front of him contains gin (it's actually petrol) has no reason to drink what's in the glass — which shows the internalist was never committed to "whatever you happen to want, you have a reason to do." Include that one, plus at least two more agreements that are NOT the standard seminar talking points — places where each side concedes something it costs them a little to concede.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated precisely, with each side's position worded so that side would accept the wording of its own view. No "the externalist thinks reasons float free of human psychology" caricatures — if a formulation would make either camp wince, rewrite it.

3. The cruxes, and for each crux a concrete case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. One crux I definately want in there: the case of the ideally deliberating promise-breaker — someone fully informed, vividly imagining all the consequences, deliberating as well as a person can, who still feels no pull whatsoever toward keeping her promises. The internalist has to say she has no reason to keep them; the externalist says that's a reductio. Get both predictions down in writing, and have each side state the strongest version of the OTHER side's reading of the case. Then find at least one further crux of your own that isn't just "can you escape moral reasons by being nasty enough" or "external reasons can't explain action" — those two have been worn smooth.

4. A closing section on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal. My hunch is that a chunk of it trades on two senses of "a reason" — what would explain someone's acting versus what counts in favor of their acting — but I want the document to actually argue for or against that diagnosis, not just gesture at it.

One more thing: force the internalist side to spell out what "sound deliberation" includes. That phrase is where they always wiggle, and the externalists are right to be suspicous of it. Write the whole thing in American English, please — half my students write "behaviour" and the other half hold it against them.
