So I'm in a reading group on confirmation theory and last week we did the ravens stuff, and I put together an argument that I think shows the whole setup is broken, but I can't find the exact crack and it's bugging me. The argument: (1) A universal generalization of the form "all Fs are G" is confirmed by observing something that is both F and G, and more generally an object confirms a hypothesis when it satisfies the hypothesis's conditions. (2) "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens" — same truth conditions, just contraposed. (3) If two statements are logically equivalent, whatever confirms one confirms the other, since they're the same claim wearing different clothes. (4) My green sofa is a non-black thing and not a raven, so it confirms "all non-black things are non-ravens," and therefore by (3) it confirms that all ravens are black. Fine, that's the classic bit, Hempel swallowed it, whatever. But now: (5) "All ravens are white" is logically equivalent to "all non-white things are non-ravens," and my sofa is also non-white and a non-raven, so by exactly the same steps it confirms that all ravens are white. (6) Evidence that confirms two hypotheses confirms their conjunction (if my sofa speaks for each of them, it speaks for both together). (7) The conjunction "all ravens are black and all ravens are white" is true only if there are no ravens at all. (8) So I can confirm, from my living room, that ravens don't exist — and indeed that nothing exists, since I can rerun this with swans, electrons, whatever. That conclusion is insane, so the argument has to break somewhere.

Where? My reading group split three ways: some people want to deny (3) and say equivalence doesn't preserve confirmability, but that seems to give up something really basic about evidence being about content rather than wording. Others say (4) and (5) are both actually fine and the sofa does confirm both, just by some vanishingly small amount, so (6) or the move to (7) is where it dies — but then I want to know exactly which inference is invalid and why it looked valid. A third faction says the problem is that confirmation has to be relativized to background knowledge and once you do that the whole derivation doesn't go through as stated, but when pressed they couldn't say which numbered step smuggles in the background-dependence. I don't need a full bibliography or a history of the debate, I just want a diagnosis: name the step (or steps) that fail, explain why it looked innocent, and tell me what it costs to reject it — e.g. if I give up (3), do I lose the ability to confirm theories stated in different vocabulary, and if I keep everything through (5) and kill (6) or (7), does that break ordinary scientific reasoning elsewhere? If it matters: I'd rather keep (1)–(3) than keep my intuition that sofas are irrelevant to ornithology, but I want to know what I'm actually paying either way.
