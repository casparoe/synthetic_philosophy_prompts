I'm a machine learning engineer and I basicly think in graphical models all day — DAGs, priors, conditional independencies, message passing, loopy belief propagation, convergence diagnostics, the works. I keep running into the epistemic regress problem and the three-way fight between foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism, and I have a strong suspicion that the whole thing is secretly an argument about graph topology: foundationalists want a well-founded DAG where some root nodes carry unconditional priors, coherentists are happy with cyclic graphs as long as the joint distribution is globally consistent, and infinitists want an infinite chain where the marginal on any node is still well-defined because influence decays fast enough.

So do the translation properly. Take each of the three positions and restate it as precisely as you can in the vocabulary of probabilistic graphical models. Say what a justified belief is, what a chain of justification is, what it means for justification to bottom out, to close into a loop, or to never terminate. Don't just gesture at "root nodes = foundations." Actually say which formal properties are doing the work: does a coherentist web of belief correspond to an undirected Markov random field where only the joint is normalized and no node is privileged? Does infinitism correspond to an infinite Bayes net where posterior marginals converge as you condition on deeper and deeper ancestors? Does foundationalism require that the root priors be *uninformative by some principled standard*, or just that the chain stops somewhere?

Then give me the part I actually care about: the residue report. What in the philosophers' debate does NOT survive translation into graphical-model vocabulary? For each thing that fails to translate, tell me whether that's because my vocabulary is genuinely missing something (and what), or because the notion it can't express was never doing real work in the first place. End with a verdict on whether the regress debate, seen through this lens, is one real disagreement or several different ones wearing a trenchcoat — no diplomatic both-sidesing.
