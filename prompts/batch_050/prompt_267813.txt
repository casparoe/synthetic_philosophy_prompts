I'm a high school student working on a history paper about whether non-human things — germs, climate, volcanoes — can count as genuine historical actors, and I've noticed my gut verdicts on cases don't obviously add up to one principle. I'd like help figuring out what I actually think.

Here are my verdicts:

1. The Black Death killing a huge share of Europe's population, creating labor scarcity that helped weaken serfdom: the plague bacterium is a legitimate historical actor here. It makes sense to say it "did" something.
2. The potato blight causing the Irish famine and pushing mass emigration to America: also a legitimate actor, same as case 1.
3. Malaria making parts of West Africa deadly for European settlers, thereby shaping where colonization became settlement versus extraction: legitimate actor.
4. The 1815 Tambora eruption causing the "year without a summer": clearly the volcano caused the weather. But when someone adds that the gloomy summer caused Frankenstein to be written, I get uneasy — it feels like the volcano is being given credit for a human creative act.
5. Claims that cold climates make people naturally disciplined and hot climates make people lazy: I reject these entirely. This isn't history, it's dressed-up prejudice.
6. The argument (from a famous popular book) that Eurasia's east-west axis let crops and livestock spread, giving its societies durable advantages: I mostly accept this, but it feels different from cases 1–3 — nobody would call geography an "actor."
7. Saying a volcanic eruption "caused" the French Revolution, via crop failures and bread prices: I want to reject this even though the causal chain looks structurally identical to case 2 (blight → hunger → mass social change). This is the pair I can't reconcile.

My questions: Is there one principle that captures verdicts 1–7 — for instance, something about how many human decisions sit between the non-human cause and the outcome, or whether the cause removed options versus merely shifting incentives? Please state the principle precisely and tell me which verdict motivates each clause. Then tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed: (a) the hypothesis that drought on the steppe pushed the Mongol conquests, and (b) COVID-19 causing school closures that permanently changed college admissions policies. Finally, if the principle can't save all seven verdicts, tell me honestly which one(s) I'd have to give up — I suspect it's 2 or 7, but I can't see how to keep one without the other.
