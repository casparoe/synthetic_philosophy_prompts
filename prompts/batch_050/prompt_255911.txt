So I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, took a moral philosophy course at the local uni for fun, and now I'm deep into Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments (yes, the other book, I only found out last year he wrote one that isn't about markets). Before I embarrass myself at the reading group, can someone audit my take? Here's what I currently believe, all at once:

1. All moral approval and disapproval runs through sympathy in Smith's sense: I imaginatively change places with the agent, and I approve of their sentiment insofar as I find I can share it, disapprove when I can't.

2. The impartial spectator is not some extra faculty or mysterious inner judge. It's just what happens when the actual feedback loops of living among other people get internalized. Conscience is social opinion that's moved indoors, nothing over and above that.

3. Entire societies can be morally wrong. Smith himself seems to grant this when he talks about the Greeks and infanticide, and the whole discussion of how custom can pervert our sentiments. So the theory has to have room for condemning the prevailing opinion of an age.

4. The theory works fine without any theology. It's a piece of naturalistic moral psychology, and as a scientist that's exactly what I like about it. No need for an Author of nature doing any load-bearing work.

5. When our sympathetic judgments are properly corrected and informed, they track genuine moral truths — they're not just a record of what the neighbors happen to feel.

6. Our tendency to sympathize with the rich and admire rank distorts our moral sentiments — and yet Smith thinks that very distortion helps hold the social order together, and both of those things can be true at once.

My question: does this set actually hang together? I have a nagging feeling at least one pair of these is in real trouble but I keep flip-flopping on which. Please rank the tensions by how serious they are rather than treating everything as equally problematic — including telling me which apparent conflicts dissolve once you look closer, because I suspect I'm worrying about at least one non-problem. And where something genuinely has to give, tell me which commitment is cheapest to drop and what I'd lose by dropping it.

One more thing, and this is the scientist in me talking: whatever verdict you give me, say what would change your mind about it. Which interpretive argument or bit of Smith's text, if it turned out different than you think, would flip your ranking? Where is your reasoning most likely to be going wrong?
