You are a philosopher with expertise in legal philosophy, coercion, and the ethics of markets. Please reason carefully, step by step, before reaching any verdict.

An incident at my company has stayed with me. A contractor, while migrating our email servers, discovered messages proving that one of our executives was having an affair with a vendor's sales director. He wrote to the executive offering to keep quiet in exchange for €20,000. He was fired and reported to the police, and at a dinner afterward two colleagues fell into a long argument about whether what he did should be a crime at all. I took no side, but I would like a referee.

Marta's position: it is straightforwardly criminal. He forced the executive to choose between his money and his reputation, and extracting payment by putting someone under a threat is extortion whatever the threat's content; the executive's "consent" would have been given under duress and therefore worthless.

Daniel's position: every element was legal. The contractor was legally free to reveal the emails (no NDA covered them), legally free to stay silent, and legally free to ask anyone for money. It cannot be that combining two or three lawful acts yields a crime — that is an insistence, not an argument. Moreover, the executive would have preferred to pay: he was offered an option he would gladly have taken over exposure, so the contractor arguably left him better off.

Marta replied that the comparison is wrong: we routinely criminalize selling what may legally be given away — votes, kidneys — and silence about someone else's private life is not a commodity the finder owns, so he had no right to set a price on it.

Daniel answered that this proves too much: settlement agreements with confidentiality clauses do exactly that every day, and on her principle every such settlement is blackmail. He added the changed-demand test: had the contractor threatened to reveal the emails unless the executive reported himself to the board, no one would call it blackmail; since the threat is identical, whatever makes the original criminal must be the demand for money — yet demanding money is legal.

Marta's last move: the difference lies in entitlement. A creditor may lawfully threaten to sue unless repaid, because repayment is owed; the contractor was owed nothing, and monetizing stumbled-upon information unconnected to any legitimate claim is precisely the mark of extortion. Daniel replied that this merely relocates the puzzle: why should the absence of a prior claim turn a legal threat into a crime, when the threatened act remains just as legal and the victim's preference for paying remains the same?

Please adjudicate. (1) Go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about each specific point: the duress-and-consent claim, the "all elements were legal" argument, the vote-selling analogy, the settlement-agreement objection, the changed-demand test, and Marta's final entitlement principle. (2) Say who is more right overall, or whether the dispute dissolves once a certain distinction is drawn. (3) Tell me what, if anything, both are missing — in particular, whether there is an account of what blackmail actually wrongs that neither of them articulates.
