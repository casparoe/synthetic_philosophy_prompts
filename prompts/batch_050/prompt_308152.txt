My roommate and I keep arguing about what a species is, and I've started writing down her gut verdicts on cases because they seem weirdly consistent but I can't figure out what rule she's running. Here's the track record so far:

- Dogs and gray wolves: same species. I pointed out that a chihuahua and a wolf look less alike than plenty of pairs she calls different species, and she shrugged and said they interbreed all over the place, looks don't matter.
- Lions and tigers: different species. When I told her zoos have bred them and the female hybrids can even have cubs, she said zoo freak shows don't count, they'd never do it on their own in the wild.
- Those salamanders around California's Central Valley that form a ring, where each population interbreeds with its neighbors all the way around but the two populations that meet at the southern end can't interbreed: one species, she says, because the whole ring is one connected chain. When I asked how the two ends can be the same species as each other when they can't mate, she said each is the same species as its neighbors and that's transitive enough.
- The white oaks in the woods behind her parents' house, where the different oak species hybridize constantly and apparently always have: still different species, because they somehow stay recognizably different anyway. So here the hybridization doesn't count.
- A fossil lineage that changes gradually over two million years with no real gaps, where the early ones and late ones look pretty different: one species the whole way, because any line you'd draw across it would be abitrary.

So: gene flow settles dogs/wolves, but hybridization doesn't settle the oaks, and looking different doesn't settle the fossils.
