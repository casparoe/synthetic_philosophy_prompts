So I've been on a Bayesian kick for a few months now — youtube essays, a beat-up secondhand textbook on probability, that kind of thing, no formal training. My brother-in-law is doing a stats PhD and over the holidays he walked me into this argument and I still can't find the exit, even though the conclusion feels obviously too strong. It goes roughly like this. (1) There's a well-known result that two Bayesians who start with different priors but keep updating on the same growing pile of evidence will eventually agree — the priors "wash out." (2) But honest, informed, genuinely smart people keep disagreeing about stuff like God, free will, gun policy, whatever, even after decades of shared evidence being publicly available. (3) So either those people aren't actually updating on the evidence, or the theorem doesn't apply to them. (4) Plenty of them clearly do update — they change their minds when new studies drop, they shift confidence, all that. (5) The math in (1) is sound, so the problem must be the assumptions: the theorem needs everyone to agree on how the evidence bears on the hypotheses, i.e. to share the likelihoods. (6) But agreeing on what the evidence means *is* most of what the disagreement was about in the first place. (7) So the theorem only kicks in once the hard part is already settled — it presupposes the agreement it was supposed to explain. (8) Therefore the washing-out result tells us nothing about real disagreement, and all this "just update your priors" talk is empty. He delivered (8) like a mic drop, and I couldn't answer him.

Here's why I can't accept it: if he's right, then a whole tradition of people treating convergence as a reason to trust patient, open inquiry is built on nothing, and it also seems like it would prove that no two people with different starting points could ever rationally converge on *anything*, which is demonstrably false — I watched my own views on nutrition converge with my doctor's over a couple years of reading the same studies, and we definately didn't sit down and pre-agree on how every study should be interpreted. So I think something slipped somewhere between (5) and (7), but I can't name it. Maybe the step from "we don't share likelihoods" to "the theorem is silent" is doing something sneaky, or maybe "agreeing on what the evidence means" is ambiguous between two things and the argument needs the strong one. If you had to reject one step, which is it, why did it look fine, and what do I lose by rejecting it? If it matters, I'd rather give up (6) or (7) than anything earlier, but I want to know what I'm paying either way.
