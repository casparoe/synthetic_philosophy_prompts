I help moderate a mid-sized online community, and over the past year we have been arguing about whether to adopt rules against hate speech. Our user base is split between Americans and Europeans, so the debate keeps drifting into the well-known contrast between the American First Amendment tradition, under which even openly racist speech is mostly legal, and the approach in much of Europe, where things like Holocaust denial and incitement to hatred are criminal offenses. I have no training in law or philosophy, but I have noticed that one sentence does almost all the work on the anti-restriction side, in our forum disputes and in public debate generally: "the best answer to bad speech is more speech."

The problem is that the sentence seems to get used to mean several different things, and I suspect some of those meanings are truisms while others are empirically or morally contentious and one or two might be plainly false. Sometimes it sounds like a prediction about how arguments play out in practice. Sometimes it sounds like a claim about what private individuals or platform operators are morally permitted to do. Sometimes it sounds like a thesis about what a government may legitimately legislate. Sometimes it seems to amount to a near-logical claim that suppression always backfires. And sometimes it seems to be little more than a conversation-stopper.

What I would like is a disambiguation, not a defense or a debunking. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making. For each one, say: (a) the claim stated carefully, without the slogan's vagueness; (b) whether it is a platitude, a defensible but contested thesis, or actually false, with a sentence or two of justification; (c) what kind of evidence or argument would settle it (empirical data about radicalization and counterspeech, a moral principle about permissible responses to wrongdoing, a constitutional doctrine, etc.); and (d) whether that reading, if true, would actually support the conclusion it is usually deployed for, namely that hate speech should not be restricted. On (d), please be careful about scope: a reading might support that conclusion for a legislature but not for a private moderator like me, or vice versa, and I want to know which is which rather than have the slogan's force silently transfer from the state case to my case.

After the analysis, please summarize the menu as a table with exactly these columns: "Reading" (a short label), "Precise claim," "Verdict" (one of: platitude / defensible-but-contested / false), "What would settle it," and "Supports non-restriction?" (answer with: government only / private moderation only / both / neither). If two readings turn out to be the same claim dressed differently, merge them and note that in the table rather than padding the list.
