Someone in my reading group shut down an argument last week by saying, in effect, Kierkegaard settled this: the crowd is untruth. The argument was about whether our housing cooperative should scrap its elected board and just put everything to a full-membership vote, no delegates. He meant the line as a conversation-ender: aggregating everyone's opinion isn't just a different procedure, it's somehow false at the root.

I've read The Present Age and I honestly can't tell what precise claim that line is supposed to be making. So give me the menu. As far as I can see it could mean at least: (a) an epistemic claim, crowds are worse at getting facts right than individuals -- which seems just wrong as a general claim (Galton's ox and all that) and probably not what a 1840s Dane could have meant; (b) a metaphysical or semantic claim, there is no such thing as the public, it's an abstraction the daily press conjures up so that no one in particular is speaking when everyone is, and statements about what the public thinks literally lack a bearer; (c) an ethical claim, in a crowd each person gets to dodge responsibility, and since truth for K demands someone willing to stand alone, the crowd is untruth the way a forged signature is false rather than the way a wrong sum is; (d) a psychological claim about leveling and anonymity releasing cheap passion and envy; (e) a religious claim, where truth just is the single individual's inward relation to God, so anything mass-scale is untruth by definition.

For each reading: platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or false? Wich one did Kierkegaard most plausibly mean, and does the slippage between factually false and inauthentic/unreal do real work for him or is it a translation headache? Most importantly: which readings, if any, actually support my friend's use -- does the line give any reason at all against majority voting, or is he borrowing religious authority for a mundane institutional-design opinion? Bonus wrinkle: K published under pseudonyms and once deliberately baited a satirical paper into attacking him, then spent the rest of his life railing against the press. Does that biographical mess undercut the line or is it exactly the point?
