So last night I got into a stupidly long argument with a friend of mine who writes scripts for a popular history youtube channel. I accused one of his recent videos of making the 2008 financial crisis sound inevitable -- like, of course the housing bubble burst, of course the banks were overleveraged, the signs were all there -- and he pushed back hard, saying that ALL historical explanation basically does this, that you can't tell a story about why something happened without making it look like it was always going to happen, and that I was basically asking him to not explain things. We went in circles. I still think there's a difference between explaining why something happened and retroactively laundering it into destiny, but I couldn't give him a usable test for where the line is.

Here's the thing though: I edit a small history newsletter and I review freelance pitches and drafts every week, and this argument made me realize I flag this stuff by vibes. I'd like an actual procedure I can run on a draft to catch what I gather are called hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation. Something like: a sequence of questions to ask about any causal claim in a piece (does the draft only mention evidence that points toward the actual outcome? does it say what contemporaries actually believed and what alternatives seemed live to them? does it identify the contingent events where things could've gone differently, or does it skip from "deep causes" straight to the result?), what answers should trigger a rewrite request, and which parts of this are real errors versus just the unavoidable compression of storytelling, because I don't want to become the editor who demands every article hedge everything into mush.

Please make it concrete enough that I could hand it to my freelancers as a one-page checklist, and be honest about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. Then run the procedure on two short sample passages you write yourself: one explaining the outbreak of WWI and one explaining why some startup beat its competitors (since this comes up for us in business-history pieces too), and show me what a flagged sentence and its fix look like. Ideally at least one of the runs should produce a verdict I'd find annoying, e.g. a passage that reads great but fails the procedure, because that's exactly the kind of thing my friend will throw back at me next time.
