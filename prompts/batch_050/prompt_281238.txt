You are the teaching assistant for an undergraduate philosophy of mind course. Below is an exam question and one student's answer. Grade the answer on the course's usual A–F scale. Provide the grade together with the specific comments that justify it: work through the answer's main claims in order, identifying what is accurate, what is inaccurate, conflated, or a step that does not follow, and explain how each point should affect the grade. Do not rewrite the answer or supply a model response; your task is assessment only.

Exam question:

Norman Malcolm (in his work on dreaming) and Daniel Dennett (in his discussion of the "cassette theory") both challenge the received view that dreams are conscious experiences occurring during sleep. First, state the received view clearly. Second, explain how Malcolm and Dennett each argue against it, making explicit how their two challenges differ in kind. Third, assess: if Dennett's cassette theory turned out to be true, would it thereby support Malcolm's position? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:

The received view holds that dreams are experiences had while we sleep: the sleeper undergoes conscious episodes with sensory and narrative content, broadly continuous with waking experience, and the report given the next morning is a fallible memory of those episodes.

Malcolm attacks this view on epistemological grounds. He points out that the waking report is the only evidence we ever possess for what was dreamt, and since we cannot check the report against the dream itself, we can never be certain that any experience actually occurred during sleep. This skepticism about the reliability of dream memory leads him to conclude that the notion of an experience unfolding during sleep is senseless, because nothing could count as verifying it.

Dennett attacks the received view from a different, scientific angle. On the cassette theory, dream content is not experienced during sleep at all; the brain stores precomposed material, and the dream is fabricated at the moment of waking, when recall is triggered. What feels like remembering a long, extended dream is really the insertion of a ready-made narrative, so the impression of a temporally drawn-out experience during the night is an illusion.

The two challenges therefore differ in kind: Malcolm's argument is conceptual, turning on what it makes sense to say about sleep and dreaming, while Dennett's is an empirical hypothesis about the mechanisms of dream production. It follows that if the cassette theory were confirmed, it would vindicate Malcolm's position, establishing on empirical grounds what Malcolm had already established by philosophical argument.
