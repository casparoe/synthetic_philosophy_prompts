Two friends of mine got into a prolonged argument at a dinner party over a puzzle one of them had read about, and they have deputized me to find a verdict. The puzzle: first, choose between (i) $1 million for certain and (ii) a lottery paying $5 million with 10% probability, $1 million with 89%, and nothing with 1%. Second, choose between (iii) $1 million with 11% probability (nothing otherwise) and (iv) $5 million with 10% probability (nothing otherwise).

My friend Daniel says he would take (i) and then (iv), and sees nothing wrong with that. My friend Priya says the pair is provably inconsistent: both choices contain an identical 89% component, and once you cancel it, each choice reduces to the same comparison -- 11% chance of $1 million versus 10% chance of $5 million -- so taking (i) commits him to (iii). She adds that someone holding both preferences could be led to pay to switch between options and then pay to switch back.

Daniel replies that the 1% chance of walking away empty-handed after turning down a guaranteed million is not a number on a page; it is the prospect of lifelong regret, and that risk attitudes can rationally depend on the whole shape of a distribution rather than decomposing into separable pieces. Priya counters that this is smuggling: if regret matters, it must be priced into the outcomes, and then either the outcomes really are as described -- in which case the cancellation argument applies -- or they are not -- in which case the puzzle was never about him. Daniel's final move: then no fully described lottery ever occurs in real life, so the consistency requirement Priya invokes is a formal demand with no grip on actual choosers.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move -- the cancellation argument, the exploitation point, the regret reply, the smuggling counter, and Daniel's last move -- saying for each who has the better of it and why, and what, if anything, both are missing. Keep the whole verdict under 400 words.
