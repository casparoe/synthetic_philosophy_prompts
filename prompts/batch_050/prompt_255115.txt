Essay sketch: "Unity as a Diachronic Achievement"

1. Setup. Commissurotomy patients are standardly taken to pose a problem for the thesis that consciousness is necessarily unified, because under experimental conditions (e.g., stimuli restricted to one visual hemifield) the two hemispheres seem to support dissociated streams of experience. The debate then largely divides between views on which the patients host two subjects or two streams (at least under test conditions), a "switch" picture on which a single stream alternates between hemispheres, and a "partial unity" picture on which some aspects of consciousness are unified while others are not.

2. My thesis. I want to argue that all of these positions share a questionable assumption: that unity is a synchronic property, something a subject either has or lacks at an instant. I propose instead that what matters for the unity of consciousness is diachronic integrability: roughly, whether a subject's experiences are disposed to be jointly available for the rational control of deliberation, memory consolidation, and action over time, even if those experiences are not co-conscious at any single moment.

3. The key move. In everyday life, split-brain patients engage in "cross-cueing": they transfer information between hemispheres indirectly, via head movements, facial expressions, subvocalization, and other bodily channels. This is normally treated as a confound that masks the patients' underlying disunity. I want to invert this reading: cross-cueing is partly constitutive of the patients' unity, not a cover-up of its absence. The brain uses whatever channels remain to preserve integrated agency across time, and the resulting unity is real even though it is mediated and effortful.

4. Consequences. (a) Split-brain subjects may be disunified in the lab, under conditions engineered to block integration, while remaining unified in daily life; the "two minds" verdict rests on privileging snapshot measures over diachronic ones. (b) Unity comes in degrees and is task-relative rather than being an all-or-nothing structural fact. (c) The apparent conflict between experimental and clinical-observational findings about these patients dissolves once we stop treating an instant as the right unit of analysis.

5. Acknowledged problems. (i) Criterion worries: I need a precise, non-trivial formulation of "joint availability over time" that does not make every minimally coherent agent unified by definition. (ii) The scaffolding objection: if external or bodily channels can constitute unity, does a notebook or a conversation partner partially unify consciousness too? I need to say what distinguishes constitutive integration from mere causal support. (iii) It may be objected that what I describe is unity of agency or of a narrative self, not unity of consciousness, and that the synchronic notion is the only one that answers to the phenomena the debate was originally about.
