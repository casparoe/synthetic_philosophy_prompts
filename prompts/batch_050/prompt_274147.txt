I serve on the heritage committee of a mid-sized town — five volunteers, none of us historians — and we decide, perhaps eight or ten times a year, what our town should do with its past: whether to approve a statue, whether to fund the restoration of an old building, whether to put up a plaque, whether to rename a street, what the town museum's next exhibition should be. The money involved is small but the feeling is large; every decision draws a crowd to the council chamber, and the council ratifies our recommendations unless we deadlock, which we often do.

A few years ago, on my own and without any academic guidance, I read Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and it gave me the only framework I have. As I understand it, he distinguishes three relationships a living person or community can have toward the past: one that looks backward for great models to imitate and draws energy from them; one that preserves and reveres what is old simply because it is ours and we came from it; and one that drags the past before a tribunal, condemns what deserves condemning, and breaks with it so that life can go on. Each, he says, serves life in the right dose and harms it in the wrong one — the first degenerates into myth-making that falsifies the past to make it inspiring, the second into a suffocating piety that preserves everything and so smothers the present, the third into a corrosive habit of condemnation that leaves people rootless and unable to build.

I would like you to turn this into a procedure my committee can actually run on a proposal. Specifically:

- The questions to ask, in order, and what counts as an adequate answer to each. My guess is that a good procedure first asks which relationship to the past a proposal embodies, and only then asks whether that relationship is one the town currently needs more of or already has too much of — but I am guessing, and I would rather you construct it properly.
- A workable version of the diagnosis Nietzsche insists on: that each mode is medicine in one condition and poison in another. We cannot just ask whether a proposal is monumental, antiquarian, or critical and stop there; we need a way of assessing the condition of the patient, the town itself. Give me concrete questions for that assessment — the kind five laypeople could argue about productively in a two-hour meeting without a professional historian in the room.
- Clear markers for where the procedure must stop and hand the decision back to us as a judgment of value, rather than letting us pretend the procedure decided for us.
- An honest statement of what the procedure cannot do — where following Nietzsche's scheme faithfully will still leave the hard case hard.

Then run the finished procedure on three proposals we have faced in spirit:

1. Restoring, at real expense, a prominent statue of the town's industrial founder, much loved in local memory, whose mills we now know depended on child labor and who broke strikes violently. Half the committee wants the statue gone; half wants it untouched; nobody wants the compromise plaque that I suspect your procedure will either recommend or rule out.
2. A proposal to preserve a derelict workhouse on the edge of town, at considerable cost, on the grounds that it is the last building of its kind here — a proposal I experience as pure antiquarian piety directed at a building associated almost entirely with misery.
3. A proposal for a new monument celebrating the town's role in a popular reform movement, intended quite frankly by its sponsors to inspire the young — which I read as an open invitation to monumental myth-making, since the town's actual role was smaller and more ambiguous than the sponsors claim.

Run the procedure on each case, and where its verdict is one I will not like — I have tipped my hand on all three — say so plainly rather than softening it. Finally, tell me which parts of Nietzsche's own treatment had to be simplified or dropped to make any procedure possible at all, because I would rather know the scheme was distorted than spend years administering a caricature of it.
