I'm preparing a seminar session on the "republican critique of spectacle," and the centerpiece is Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert arguing against establishing a theater in Geneva. My students all assume they know what a present-day Rousseau would say about entertainment media -- something like "he'd ban TikTok and be smug about it" -- and I want to push them past that caricature. So here is the exercise I'd like you to perform, in a way I can use as a model answer: reconstruct, as rigorously as you can, what Rousseau's actual position on algorithmic short-form video platforms would be, arguing from his stated premises rather than from vibes.

Please take seriously the structure of the original argument rather than just its conclusion. Rousseau's case against the Geneva theater was not that depiction or fiction is inherently corrupting; it turned on fairly specific claims -- that theater flatters and entrenches the passions an audience already has rather than educating them, that it substitutes passive spectatorship for active civic and social life, that the actor's profession involves a kind of self-alienation, and crucially, that the verdict is context-dependent: what would ruin a small, virtuous republic might be a harmless outlet in a large, already-corrupt city. He also explicitly praised certain communal spectacles -- open-air festivals, public games, participatory celebrations -- as morally healthy. Given all this, work out: which of these premises actually transfer to an attention-optimized, algorithmically personalized feed (and note that this medium differs from theater in ways that cut both directions -- there is no shared audience, no live actors, but far deeper immersion into daily life); whether his context-dependence point means his verdict should differ by the society or community in question today; and what he would make of the modern phenomena that look most like his beloved festivals -- participatory fan communities, amateur performance culture, communal livestreamed events -- since a faithful answer has to say where he'd approve, not just where he'd condemn.

Finally, address the two strongest internal tensions a sharp student would raise. First, the reply that Rousseau himself wrote fiction and an opera, so either his premises don't condemn theater per se or he was a hypocrite -- which reading better fits his position, and does the answer change how he'd treat a medium whose content is largely created by ordinary users rather than a professional class of performers? Second, many of his premises are empirical claims about effects on morals and civic engagement: if the best available evidence about these platforms turned out mixed or contrary to his expectations, which parts of his view would survive intact (because they rest on something deeper than causal conjecture) and which would collapse? I'm less interested in a verdict than in a disciplined exercise in applying a dead philosopher's framework to something he never saw, with explicit flagging of where you're extrapolating beyond anything he actually wrote.
