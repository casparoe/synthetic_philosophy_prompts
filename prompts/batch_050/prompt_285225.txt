So I'm a novelist, and my current book has a character who, after a wierd episode, genuinely can't rule out that everything since then has been a dream. Researching him I fell down the dream-skepticism rabbit hole -- Zhuangzi's butterfly, Descartes, all of it -- and here's what's bugging me. One side says you can't know you're not dreaming right now, so you don't know most of what you think you know. The other side says this is confused, that "I know I'm awake" is exactly the sort of thing we do know, and the skeptic is just misusing the word "know." Both arguments have been on the table for centuries and as far as I can tell nobodys ever switched sides. My hypothesis is that the fight isn't really about dreams at all -- it's about whether knowledge has to survive every conceivable doubt or only reasonable doubts, and neither side can defend their standard without quietly assuming it. Is that right, or is something else doing the work -- a hidden disagreement about what "know" means, or about which cases count as data, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? Diagnose it, don't referee it, and tell me what, if anything, could actually move it: a distinction, an argument form, a change of question. For the novel I mostly need to know whether my character's predicament is a real problem or a language trap, but honestly I want to know for myself too.
