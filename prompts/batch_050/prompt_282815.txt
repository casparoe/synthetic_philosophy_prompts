For an upper-level seminar on philosophy of language and literature, I am pairing Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby with Russell's "On Denoting" (1905), and I want a reading that does justice to both. Early in that essay, Russell observes that "It often happens that we know that a certain phrase denotes unambiguously, although we have no acquaintance with what it denotes," and, more strikingly, that "there seems no reason to believe that we are ever acquainted with other people's minds, seeing that these are not directly perceived; hence what we know about them is obtained through denoting."

Read the novel as a sustained test of this claim. West Egg knows Gatsby almost wholly through definite descriptions -- the Oxford man, the German spy, the bootlegger, the man who once killed a man -- several of which arguably denote the same person unambiguously while no one is acquainted with what they denote; even Nick's narration reconstructs Gatsby out of rumor, documents, and reported speech. Where does the novel vindicate Russell? Does the tragedy turn on Daisy's being asked to love, de re, a man she has been given only de dicto, under descriptions that James Gatz engineered for himself? And where does it resist? Consider the reunion, Gatsby's insistence that the past can be repeated, and his demand that Daisy declare she never loved Tom: do these amount to a plea for acquaintance as against denoting, and does Nick's closing endorsement of Gatsby pretend to an acquaintance the narrative itself never actually supplies? Close by asking what the novel contributes that Russell's own examples (the center of mass of the solar system, the present King of France) cannot: does fiction grant us something like acquaintance with Gatsby -- a person who, on Russell's own principles, denotes nothing -- or does it instead show that all our knowledge of persons, fictional or real, is knowledge by description all the way down?
