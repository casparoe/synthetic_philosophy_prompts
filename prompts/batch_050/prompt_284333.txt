I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on the formal foundations of distributive ethics, and my reading group has spent the semester circling Harsanyi's social aggregation theorem. The recurring cast of characters: a committed Harsanyian, who reads the theorem as nearly settling the debate — if individual preferences satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, social evaluation does too, and social evaluation respects unanimous individual preference, then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities, full stop — and a sympathetic critic working in the tradition of Diamond's fairness objection and prioritarianism, who grants the mathematics but denies the premises carry the normative weight claimed for them. They keep re-fighting the same battles: whether a "social preference relation" is a real agent's attitudes or merely a policy criterion; whether ex ante Pareto (unanimity over prospects) and ex post Pareto (unanimity over outcomes) can be run together without collapsing Diamond's coin-flip case; whether the theorem does anything to fix the weights or to license interpersonal comparisons of utility; and whether separability across unconcerned individuals is an innocuous bookkeeping condition or the smuggled-in core of utilitarianism.

Please draft the joint statement these two could actually sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, restricted to things both genuinely accept — e.g., precisely what the theorem establishes given each axiom, what each axiom's failure would cost the conclusion, and what the theorem demonstrably does not deliver on its own (the weights, the impartial-observer connection absent symmetry assumptions, a basis for interpersonal utility comparisons); (2) a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in wording that each side would endorse as a fair description of its own view, flagging any disagreement that turns out, on inspection, to be verbal (I suspect their uses of "social preference" and of "fairness" are candidates, but leave the verdict to you); and (3) a list of cruxes, where for each crux you name a specific case, thought experiment, or formal result that both sides accept as bearing on it — Diamond's two policies (certain benefit to one person versus a fair coin between two), cases engineered to pull apart ex ante and ex post unanimity, and hypothetical evidence that real social evaluations violate the vNM axioms are natural candidates, but choose whatever both parties would concede is probative — and record in advance what verdict or prediction each side commits to about it.

Two constraints. First, neither party should be a strawman: the Harsanyian should know the difference between the aggregation theorem and the impartial observer theorem and should concede freely where the formalism underdetermines the ethics; the critic should concede freely where the axioms are doing less work than the rhetoric suggests. Second, where you find that a crux has no case both sides would accept as bearing on it — that is, where the disagreement is insulated from any shared test — say so explicitly rather than manufacturing a test, since identifying unfalsifiable-by-agreement cruxes is itself a useful output of the collaboration. Format the deliverable as the agreed-claims section, then the table, then the cruxes, under a short preamble stating the collaboration's scope.
