Two colleagues of mine — one a committed moral presentist, the other a committed historical contextualist — have spent a year arguing about whether we may condemn figures such as Jefferson for practising slaveholding, or Churchill for his views on empire, given the standards of their eras, and their seminar has produced more heat than light. Draft the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them could produce, and present it as exactly six numbered sections of one to three sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the claims both can sign about what moral blame requires in the way of epistemic access, including an agreed distinction between the question whether a figure could have known better and the question whether, knowing better, they could have been expected to act on it; (2) the claims both can sign about the difference between judging a person retrospectively and deciding whether to honour them now through statues, buildings, and curricula, including whether the second decision is answerable to the first; (3) the precise statement of their core disagreement, worded so that each side would accept the formulation of its own position; (4) their cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — which I expect will include whether the existence of articulate contemporary dissenters (abolitionists in Jefferson's world, critics of empire in Churchill's) defeats the 'man of his time' defence, and whether excusing the dead commits us to excusing our contemporaries in other cultures; (5) two stipulated test cases both accept as bearing on those cruxes — a slaveholder in a society with a visible abolitionist minority who reads the pamphlets and is unmoved, and a slaveholder in a society with no recorded dissent whatsoever — with each side's verdict on each case recorded in advance; (6) their joint verdict on which part of their year-long dispute, if any, was merely verbal, and why.
