I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent much of the past year reporting on religious-liberty disputes and the philosophers who write about them. A friend who teaches political philosophy at a nearby university has just been called away on family leave, and she has asked me to serve as a second reader on a batch of final-exam scripts from her undergraduate course on religion and political theory. I know the Rawls material tolerably well from my reporting, but second-marking is a different skill from journalism: it requires catching the small conflations that a fluent, confident answer can smuggle past a tired reader. I would rather not tell you my impressions of the script below, so as not to anchor your judgment; I would like an independent, examiner-style assessment.

Here is the exam question, which is worth 40 points: (a) Explain the difference, for Rawls, between a modus vivendi and an overlapping consensus, and why he thinks only the latter can make a constitutional regime stable for the right reasons. (b) State what the ideal of public reason requires, whose conduct it constrains, and on which questions it binds. What is the proviso, and what does it permit? (c) Apply your answer to this case: the mayor of a drought-stricken city issues an official proclamation declaring a day of prayer and fasting. The proclamation quotes the mayor's own scripture and closes by noting the city's voluntary water-conservation targets. Has the mayor violated the ideal of public reason? Defend your answer.

Here is the student's answer: (a) For Rawls, a modus vivendi is an agreement each party accepts out of self- or group-interest given the existing balance of power; because adherence is contingent on circumstances, the arrangement collapses whenever power shifts, so it is unstable in principle. An overlapping consensus is deeper: citizens holding different reasonable comprehensive doctrines, religious and secular alike, come to affirm the same political conception of justice on shared grounds of reasonableness. Since everyone endorses the conception for the same moral reasons rather than from temporary advantage, the consensus survives changes in fortune, which is why Rawls thinks only it can stabilize a regime for the right reasons. (b) Public reason requires that political decisions be justified by appeal to shared political values, such as freedom and equality and fair terms of cooperation, rather than to sectarian doctrine. Its constraints bind judges above all, but also legislators, executives, candidates for office, and indeed anyone engaged in public political advocacy, including clergy addressing their congregations, newspaper columnists, and citizens campaigning, at least where constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice are concerned. The proviso softens the restriction: reasonable religious doctrines may be introduced into public discussion provided that, in due course, sufficient political reasons are offered to support whatever the religious reasons were invoked to support. (c) The mayor's proclamation violates the ideal of public reason. It is an official act, it rests on scripture, and the closing reference to water conservation is too incidental to satisfy the proviso. Had the mayor confined the proclamation to the conservation targets, or issued the religious appeal in a pastoral rather than an official capacity, no violation would have occurred.

Please grade the answer as a careful examiner would, assuming it is marked out of 40. Structure your response in three sections, in this order. First, Grade and Overall Judgment: a letter grade and a numerical mark, with a summary of your reasoning in roughly 60 to 80 words. Second, Point-by-Point Assessment: roughly 350 to 450 words organized by parts (a), (b), and (c), stating what earns credit, what should lose marks and why, and the specific correction you would write in the margin of each error you find. Third, What Full Marks Would Have Required: roughly 100 to 120 words on what a flawless answer would have added or done differently.
