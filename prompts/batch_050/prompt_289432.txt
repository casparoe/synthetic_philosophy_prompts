I'm putting together a grad seminar on metaphilosophy next spring and I want to structure the whole first unit around a single question: what would a theory of philosophical progress have to look like? Every paper on this seems to start by declaring victory for its own preferred notion of progress and then retro-fitting the criteria, so I want the criteria first, theories second.

Act as a professional philosopher who has thought carefully about metaphilosophy and methodology, and before you write anything, brainstorm at least six or seven candidate desiderata and the cases that motivate them -- then cut the weak ones and tell me which you cut and why. I want to see the thinking, not just the polished list.

Here's the shape of what I need:

1. The desiderata list proper: what any adequate account of whether philosophy makes progress has to deliver. For each one, give the case or phenomenon that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice. Some things I want you to at least consider as candidates: the fact that solved problems keep getting spun off and rebranded as new disciplines (physics, logic, linguistics, psych), which might make philosophy look stagnant by a kind of survivor bias; the persistence of expert disagreement on the big questions even after centuries; the difference between converging on answers and sharpening/fragmenting the option space; whether progress has to be recognizable by the people making it or only visible from outside; and whether an account can handle progress-by-dissolution (showing a question was confused) without counting every deflationary move as a win.

2. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for ANY account and which are just the hobby-horses of particular camps. Take a stand here -- don't sort them into "reasonable people disagree."

3. The part I care most about: identify any pair or triple of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, and explain why. If the list itself turns out to be a result (i.e., no theory can meet all of it), say so plainly.

4. Only then, briefly, run the main contenders against the list: convergence-on-truth accounts, progress-as-option-proliferation accounts, and dissolution/therapeutic accounts. Keep this short; the criteria are the deliverable, the scorecard is homework.

Final thing: I want a committed response. At the end, tell me which single desideratum does the most eliminative work, and give me YOUR verdict on whether philosophy makes progress given your own list -- not a menu of positions for me to choose between. If your verdict is "yes but only in a deflated sense" or "no and here's the uncomfortable reason," fine, but commit to it and give reasons. A list that every camp can nod along to is a failed answer as far as I'm concerned.
