Amartya Sen's liberal paradox shows that no collective decision procedure can jointly satisfy unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and a minimal liberalism condition — at least two individuals, each socially decisive over at least one pair of states differing only in some matter personal to them. Sen illustrated this with two people and a controversial book, and that illustration has been recycled ever since. I want a fresh case built to order.

Construct a thought experiment in which the paradox bites inside a small, realistic institution — a household, a two-person partnership, a three-person housing co-op, a small firm — subject to these specifications:

1. No reading material. The personal matters over which parties hold decisiveness must be something other than whether someone reads a book; the case must not recapitulate Sen's example in new clothes.
2. The liberties in question must be decision rights the parties would plausibly be granted in real life — over their own dress, working hours, diet, or use of their own room — not exotic or contested entitlements.
3. The other-regarding preferences that drive the conflict must be at least partly benevolent: each party's preference about the other's personal sphere should stem from concern for the other's good, not from nosiness, disgust, or spite. The point of the exercise is to test whether the paradox survives the move from meddlesome to paternalistic preferences, so do not smuggle in ill will.
4. Use two parties if possible, three at most, and describe the social states concretely enough that a reader could reconstruct each party's complete preference ordering over the relevant states from your description alone.

After presenting the scenario, do the verification work rather than just asserting that the case works: (a) write out each party's ranking over the relevant states; (b) show step by step how the two decisiveness assignments and the weak Pareto condition jointly generate the cycle or impossibility — which right gets exercised, which state that forces, and why Pareto then rules out what the other right demands; (c) identify the single feature of the case doing the real work, and say what happens to the result if that feature is removed — for instance, if the benevolent preferences are replaced by mutual indifference, or if the rights are treated as alienable and the parties may bargain over their exercise; (d) state how a theorist who regards the paradox as a mere artifact of meddlesome preferences would attack your construction, and whether the paternalistic version withstands that attack.

Finally, say what would change your mind: which consideration — a restriction on admissible preferences, a re-description of the states that collapses the alleged conflict, an argument that benevolent other-regarding preferences should be laundered or excluded — would lead you to conclude that your case does not in fact instantiate the paradox, and identify where the construction is most likely to fail.
