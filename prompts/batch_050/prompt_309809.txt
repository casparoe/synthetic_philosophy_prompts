I serve on the twelve-member steering committee of a community land trust, and we must choose one of four competing designs for our new building. In past decisions we have used simple plurality voting, and the meetings have grown increasingly tense: several members admit they voted not for the design they genuinely preferred but for whichever one they thought could defeat the design they most disliked. At our last meeting, one member proposed adopting a ranked-ballot system so that, as she put it, everyone could finally vote honestly. A second member countered that any conceivable voting method can be gamed somehow, so we would merely trade one kind of tactical voting for another. A third member suggested we simply let our most knowledgeable colleague make the call, which the rest of us rejected as objectionable in a different way.

I have been tasked with recommending a decision procedure, and before I do, I want to understand the landscape. Is the second member right that sincere voting can never be fully secured? If so, is that a fact about the particular methods we have tried, or about every possible method — and under what assumptions would such a sweeping claim hold (for instance, about the number of alternatives, how ties are handled, whether every proposal must be capable of winning, and how each voter's preferences feed into the outcome)? Where exactly does the rejected third suggestion fit into the analysis — is letting one person decide the only way to escape the problem, and if so, why? Finally, if a perfectly strategy-proof procedure is unattainable, what considerations should actually guide the choice among imperfect voting rules? Please explain the underlying reasoning, not just the bottom line, since I will have to defend whatever I recommend to the full committee.
