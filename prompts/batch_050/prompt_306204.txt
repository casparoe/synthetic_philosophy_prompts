For a graduate seminar in the philosophy of physics I am assembling the live positions on what is physically real in a gauge theory such as classical electromagnetism. The ones I already have: (1) strict gauge-invariantism -- gauge transformations are mere redescriptions of the same physical state, so only gauge-invariant quantities (field strengths, charges, Wilson loops) are real and the vector potential is surplus mathematical structure; (2) the holonomy interpretation -- the fundamental physical magnitudes are gauge-invariant but non-separable properties of loops, motivated by the thought that the Aharonov-Bohm effect cannot be accounted for by any assignment of local gauge-invariant properties to the regions the electron traverses; (3) the reduced-phase-space construal -- physical possibilities are individuated by gauge orbits rather than by points of the original state space, so the apparent indeterminism of gauge theories is an artifact of counting representations rather than possibilities; (4) the deflationary comparison -- gauge freedom is exactly on a par with coordinate freedom in geometry, and asking which potential is real is as misguided as asking which coordinate chart is real. I want a fifth position that is not a restatement, special case, or mere combination of these four. State it precisely enough that one can say what it counts as real that the others do not (or vice versa), and then tell me honestly whether it survives the two standard tests: the Aharonov-Bohm effect and the threat of indeterminism that gauge symmetry is usually invoked to explain away.
