I am a novelist, and I am trying to decide how the narration of my current book should treat its central figure: an aging Kapellmeister of the 1840s who has read Schopenhauer and staked his entire understanding of his art on a single doctrine. The doctrine is stated in the supplements to the third book of Schopenhauer's The World as Will and Idea (trans. Haldane and Kemp, Vol. III, ch. XXXIX, "On the Metaphysics of Music"):

"That music acts directly upon the will, i.e., the feelings, passions, and emotions of the hearer, so that it quickly raises them or changes them, may be explained from the fact that, unlike all the other arts, it does not express the Ideas, or grades of the objectification of the will, but directly the will itself. ... Music as such knows the tones or notes alone, but not the causes which produce these."

I do not want the novel to condescend to him, but neither do I want it to rest on a claim that would collapse the moment it met a competent psychologist. So I would like your honest credence, with the working shown, in the following proposition, which I take to be the most defensible reading of the passage: that the emotional power of purely instrumental music — its capacity to move us without text, program, or depicted scene — does not arise from its representing or imitating anything in experience (particular emotions, events, or natural sounds), but from its reproducing, in abstract temporal form, the structure of willing itself: arousal, tension, resistance, deferral, and resolution.

Before the numbers, give me two or three sentences on what the passage actually commits Schopenhauer to, distinguishing the full metaphysical reading from the weakest psychological claim his words will bear. The proposition above belongs to the latter, and I want to be sure I am not grading my character's creed on a claim he never made.

Then decompose your credence into at least the following terms, with a number and one line of justification for each, and show how they combine:

- the probability that the felt emotion of absolute music is genuinely non-representational, rather than carried by association, depiction, or an imagined narrative supplied by the listener;
- the probability, conditional on that, that the formal grammar of music — dissonance, suspension, the postponed and eventual return to the tonic — systematically tracks the dynamics of desire and its satisfaction, rather than being merely an affectively charged pattern;
- the probability that this correspondence, if real, is what accounts for music's peculiar depth and apparent seriousness, rather than that depth being projected by listeners onto intrinsically neutral form;
- any further term the conjunction needs (for instance, whether the whole effect is a local convention of the tonal tradition Schopenhauer happened to know, rather than something about music as such).

Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence — from the psychology or neuroscience of music, or from musical practices outside the tonal tradition — would shift your estimate most. Please keep the entire answer under 450 words.
