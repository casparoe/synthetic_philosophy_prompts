Almost every constitution contains a rule describing how it may be amended, and many go further: they make some provisions harder to amend than others, or declare certain provisions unamendable altogether. The United States Constitution subjects the equal suffrage of the states in the Senate to a consent requirement that ordinary provisions escape; Germany's post-war constitution declares amendments touching human dignity or the democratic and federal character of the state inadmissible, offering no procedure by which they could lawfully be made; India's Supreme Court has held that Parliament's express power of amendment does not reach the constitution's basic structure, although the text itself says nothing of the kind. Alongside all this, many of the same legal systems insist that an ordinary legislature cannot bind its successors: no act of one parliament can prevent a later parliament from repealing it.

What view of legal authority does this patchwork presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does entrenchment presuppose that a founding generation may legitimately bind its successors — and if so, how is that consistent with the refusal to let one legislature bind the next? Does it instead presuppose that a constitution has an identity persisting through time, such that certain changes would count not as amendments but as the replacement of one constitution by another — and what theory of identity over time would render that distinction non-arbitrary? Consider also the self-reference problem: suppose an entrenched clause is attacked indirectly, by first amending the amendment clause that protects it and then proceeding as before. Has the system been lawfully changed or defected from, and what must a rule of change presuppose about its own status within the system in order to resist that manoeuvre? Distinguish, in your answer, what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and say where it is underdetermined — where several rival theories of authority would license identical doctrine. Finally, state what would have to change in constitutional practice if we instead adopted the view that each present majority is sovereign and entrenchment is therefore void.
