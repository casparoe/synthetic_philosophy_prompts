Ahead of a workshop on historical injustice, I was asked to circulate a written statement of my commitments on state apologies and reparations so the other participants can probe them. Here is what I put down:

(1) States are continuing corporate agents: the state that exists today can be the very same agent that committed a wrong two centuries ago, and this is what allows duties of apology and repair to attach to it now, despite a complete turnover of citizens and officials.

(2) No living person is blameworthy for the slave trade or for colonial expropriation. Blame requires authorship or endorsement, and any case for reparations that rests on inherited individual guilt is a non-starter.

(3) The core case for reparations is compensatory: descendant communities are owed compensation because they are materially worse off today than they would have been had the injustice not occurred.

(4) I accept the non-identity problem in full. Had the slave trade not occurred, the world would have been reshuffled so thoroughly that none of the actual descendants would ever have existed; so no living descendant was made worse off, in the counterfactual sense, by the wrong.

(5) A genuine apology, as opposed to an expression of regret, requires the apologizer to acknowledge itself as the author of the wrong. Statements of the "mistakes were made" or "we regret the suffering" variety do not count.

(6) There is no moral statute of limitations: the passage of time, and the death of every original victim and perpetrator, do not by themselves weaken the obligation.

Please audit the set. Tell me which commitments pull against each other, ordered by how serious the tension is, and for each one say precisely what would have to give and what the cheapest available fix costs. I would also like you to identify any pair that looks inconsistent at first glance but dissolves on inspection, since I don't want to abandon anything unnecessarily. Finally, if one revision would do more than any other to stabilize the whole package, name it. I'd like an audit, not a survey of the literature on historical injustice.
