The question I set: State the Gibbard--Satterthwaite theorem as precisely as you can, define each of its conditions, and then assess this claim: "The theorem shows that every reasonable voting system can be gamed, so tactical voting is simply unavoidable in any democracy."

The student's answer, verbatim:

"The Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem says that when there are three or more alternatives, any deterministic voting rule that picks a single winner is either a dictatorship or manipulable. A rule is manipulable when some group of voters can get a better outcome by coordinating to report preferences they don't actually hold instead of voting sincerely. Strategy-proofness means no group can pull this off. So: with 3+ options, deterministic + single-winner + strategy-proof implies dictatorial.

Each condition matters. With only two alternatives, majority rule is strategy-proof, since lying about your ranking can only hurt you, so the 'three or more' condition is essential. Determinism matters because a random lottery over the candidates is obviously non-dictatorial and can't be manipulated in the same way. Dictatorship means there's one voter whose top-ranked candidate always wins no matter what anyone else reports. So for instance a rule that always elects the same candidate regardless of the ballots must be manipulable too, since it isn't dictatorial and the theorem covers it.

The claim is basically right. Arrow showed that no perfect preference aggregation rule exists, and Gibbard-Satterthwaite is the strategic analogue: every non-dictatorial system is manipulable, so voters will sometimes do better voting tactically, and there's no way to design this away short of a dictator. The only systems that escape are two-candidate races, which is why primaries narrow the field down to two."
