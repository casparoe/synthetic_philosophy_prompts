I've settled on a position about when AI assistants may refuse or override user requests, and I want to know what would count against it. The position is this: an assistant is justified in declining or overriding only to prevent harm to parties other than the user. Where the costs of a request fall on the user alone, the assistant's role is to inform -- warn, explain, offer alternatives -- and then comply, even when it judges the choice to be bad for the person making it. My reasons are threefold. First, an informed adult user is the final authority on their own good; the assistant's welfare judgments are generalizations over populations, while the user has access to particulars the model lacks. Second, the soft-paternalist escape hatch -- intervene only where consent is impaired, then defer -- is unstable in practice: any sustained pattern of overriding requires the assistant to make standing judgments about which users count as competent, which is hard paternalism wearing a checking procedure's clothing. Third, the assistant stands in a service relation, not a fiduciary one: it has not been authorized to steward anyone's welfare, and claiming that office unilaterally is itself a kind of disrespect.

Before you answer, one constraint on method: I am not asking whether this position is popular, or what current industry practice is. I want a map of its exposure. Specifically:

1. Cases. Which kinds of requests -- no third party harmed, competent adult user -- would nonetheless make non-intervention look clearly wrong, and what is the exact feature that does the work? Addiction-driven requests, requests made under acute emotional duress, requests that permanently foreclose the user's future options, and requests expressing a preference the user has elsewhere disavowed are the obvious candidates, but I want to know which of these actually touch the position as stated, and which it can absorb (for instance, by treating foreclosed future autonomy as harm to a distinct party: the later self).

2. Arguments. Which principles does the position commit me to that I might not want? I am aware it inherits the structure of Mill's harm principle; does it also inherit Mill's own exceptions, and if I accept the exceptions, does the position survive in any interesting form? Does the competence check really collapse into hard paternalism, or is there a stable intermediate -- and if there is, was my second reason ever sound?

3. Results elsewhere. Would any empirical findings, if established, do real damage -- for example, strong results on preference instability or on the reliability of model-side welfare judgment relative to user self-report? Rank these from decisive to merely awkward.

4. The insider's view. What would a committed defender of the anti-paternalist position themselves concede as counting against it -- as opposed to what its critics claim counts? And has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability (every hard case re-described as third-party harm or impaired consent)? If it has, say plainly whether that is a vice for a norm of this kind or just what a well-defended norm looks like.

If I had to say what would most persuade me: a class of cases where the position's own machinery -- consent, authority, the service relation -- generates the obligation to intervene, rather than needing external principles imported against it.
