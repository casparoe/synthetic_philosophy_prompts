So a guy from my reading group cornered me after our session on Searle and Dennett and laid out this argument, and I keep going in circles on it. The conclusion is obviously nuts but every step looks defensible on its own. Here it is as best I remember it:

(1) A state has original intentionality only if its being about something doesn't depend on some other thing already being about something. Aboutness has to bottom out somewhere.

(2) Take Dennett's two-bitser, the vending machine with the state that "detects" quarters. What that state is about depends entirely on the designers. If it had been built to sort tokens, or shipped somewhere a different coin fits the slot, the very same physical state would be about something else. So its aboutness is inherited, not original.

(3) Human brain states are in the same boat. What makes my fly-swatting circuitry about flies rather than about small dark moving blobs is the function evolution settled on, just like what makes the machine's state about quarters is the function the engineers settled on.

(4) But natural selection isn't an agent. It doesn't intend anything, it doesn't mean anything by anything, it's just a filter. It's less like a designer than the engineers are, if anything.

(5) And you can't derive aboutness from a source with no aboutness. That's just what (1) says.

(6) So our intentionality is derived, exactly like the two-bitser's.

(7) But derived intentionality is only "as if" intentionality. The two-bitser doesn't literally detect quarters, it just behaves as though it does, and we're the ones doing the detecting when we describe it.

(8) Therefore nothing is ever really about anything. My thought about coffee is only as-if about coffee.

He grinned and said this proves meaning is an illusion. I refuse to accept (8), but when I push on individual premises he just repeats them slower. Between us, I suspect something fishy happens around (6) to (7) -- it feels like "derived" is doing two different jobs -- but I can't pin it down cleanly enough to win the argument. If forced to choose, I'd rather keep the claim that my thoughts are genuinely about stuff than keep whatever definition of "derived" Searle was working with, but I want to know what that choice actually costs.

Tell me which step you'd reject, why it looked fine at first glance, and what each way out (roughly Dennett's way vs roughly Searle's way) makes you give up. American English please, and keep it under 400 words -- this is for an argument over beers, not a dissertation.
