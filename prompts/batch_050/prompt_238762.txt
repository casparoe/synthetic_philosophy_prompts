Hey, could use some help with something from my political theory seminar. We're reading Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre (we used the Allan Bloom translation) and I keep getting stuck on what looks like a straight-up contradiction in the argument.

Early in the letter Rousseau argues that a theater can't actually change a people's morals. His reasoning (paraphrasing here) is that the stage only survives by pleasing its audience, so it has to flatter the tastes and values that are already there -- a playwright who put on plays that clashed with the audience's morals would just empty the house. So on his view the theater follows morals, it doesn't lead them; it can reinforce what's already present but it can't reform anything.

But then the whole point of the letter is that Geneva must NOT allow a theater, because he thinks establishing one would wreck Geneva's specifically republican morals -- people would waste their time and money, pick up Parisian fashions, get lazy and factionalized, and the actors themselves would bring in a corrupting way of life.

So: if the stage is impotent to change morals for the better because it must always please, how is it potent to change them for the worse? Either the flattery constraint binds or it doesn't. My prof mentioned in passing that the tension dissapears once you see that the two arguments run through different mechanisms, but I honestly don't follow.

Can you explain:

1. What the mechanism of corruption is actually supposed to be, given that it apparently can't work through the *content* of the plays (since content has to conform to the audience's existing morals)? Is the worry about the plays at all, or about the theater as an institution -- what it does to how people spend their evenings, who they mix with, what they admire?
2. Whether the "theater can only follow morals" argument is even as strong as he thinks. Couldn't a clever playwright shift public taste *gradually*, one small step at a time, always staying just inside what the audience will accept? Is there anything in Rousseau's framework that rules that out, or is he assuming taste is more static than it is?
3. Whether the whole argument is specifically republican -- i.e. is he committed to saying a theater is harmless (or at least differently harmful) in a monarchy like France, where the morals are already what they are, so the flattery dynamic just entrenches them? Does the letter need that asymmetry to work?

Also if there's a standard term in the secondary lit for this kind of "impotent for good, potent for evil" structure of argument about cultural institutions, I'd love to know it, but mostly I want to understand whether the letter is coherent here or whether d'Alembert's side has a real opening.
