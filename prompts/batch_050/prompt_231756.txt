I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and am preparing an essay on how popular culture represents automation. In Chapter 15 of Capital, Volume I, Karl Marx writes:

“That is, however, by no means the aim of the capitalistic application of machinery. Like every other increase in the productiveness of labour, machinery is intended to cheapen commodities, and, by shortening that portion of the working-day, in which the labourer works for himself, to lengthen the other portion that he gives, without an equivalent, to the capitalist. In short, it is a means for producing surplus-value.”

Read Charlie Chaplin’s Modern Times through this passage rather than through the familiar opposition between technology as inherently liberating and technology as inherently dehumanizing. How does the film distinguish machinery’s productive capacities from its specifically capitalist use? Analyze the opening assembly-line sequence, the feeding-machine demonstration, the factory breakdown, and the Tramp’s later passage through work, unemployment, policing, and domestic life.

Does the film ultimately support Marx’s claim that capitalist machinery aims less to lighten labor than to extract surplus-value, or does it replace that political-economic explanation with a psychological one centered on nervous overload and loss of bodily control? Pay particular attention to whether machines themselves dominate workers or instead mediate relations among workers, owners, managers, police, and the unemployed. Also indicate where the film resists a strictly Marxian reading, especially in its treatment of consumption, the Gamin, and its final movement away from industrial labor. Conclude by explaining what Modern Times adds to Marx’s argument that political-economic analysis alone might miss.
