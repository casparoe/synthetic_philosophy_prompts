I'm in a philosophy reading group, and after a month on free will its my turn to bring something next week, so instead of a talk I want to read them a poem. The topic is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility -- roughly: what you do flows from the way you are, so to be truly responsible for what you do you'd have to be truly responsible for the way you are, but that would require having chosen the way you are, and any choice can only be made on the basis of some earlier way you already were, so the demand for self-creation regresses forever and no one can be ultimately responsible. Strawson frames this in terms of ultimate desert, the kind of responsibility that could make an eternity of reward or punishment just, which is what made me think of Dante.

So: write the poem in terza rima, like the Inferno, because the form seems to do the argument's work. The interlocking rhymes (ABA BCB CDC...) mean every tercet owes its shape to the one before and hands the debt on to the next -- which is basically the regress: each way-you-are is explained by an earlier one you never chose. And I want the poem to exploit the fact that terza rima has no natural stopping point. Dante ends his cantos because he has to; I'd like this poem to break off mid-chain, so that the failure to close is the argument's conclusion -- the speaker can never trace their character back to a first choice they fully own. Six to nine tercets, in the voice of someone trying to do exactly that and failing. One thing to get right: the poem should land on Strawson's actual point, that ultimate desert-entailing responsibilty is impossible, not that we can't hold each other responsible in everyday ways or that choice is an illusion in some spooky sense. Someone who's read the paper should recognize the argument in the poem's bones, not just in its vocabulary.
