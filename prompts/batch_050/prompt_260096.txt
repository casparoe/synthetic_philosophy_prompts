I'm a novelist at work on a thriller, and the plot turns on a device I borrowed, loosely, from a philosophy article I read years ago: an eccentric billionaire wires a fortune to a stranger, contingent on the stranger sincerely intending, at midnight tonight, to drink a vial of slow-acting poison tomorrow afternoon -- with the understanding that the money is his to keep whether or not he actually drinks, and that the poison will cause a wretched but non-lethal illness. My editor asked whether my protagonist could plausibly win, and in drafting a reply I realized I have settled views on this family of puzzles that I'd like audited before they harden into the book's logic. Here is where I stand. First, I take the standard verdict: a perfectly rational person cannot pocket the fortune, because he cannot form the required intention while knowing that tomorrow he'll have no reason whatsoever to drink. Second, I believe the rationality of an intention is fixed by the reasons for having it, not by the rationality of the act intended -- so it can be eminently rational to intend something it would be irrational to do, as with a deterrent one hopes never to carry out. Third, I think a person can genuinely and sincerely intend to do something she fully expects she won't do -- my dieting friends intend to skip dessert in perfect sincerity while knowing their own track records, and I refuse to describe them as merely pretending. Fourth, I'm drawn to the thought that rationality is resolute: if a plan was rationally formed, then carrying it out is rational even at the moment when execution costs you and no fresh consideration speaks for it -- otherwise rational agents would be unable to coordinate with their own future selves. Fifth, I think it's clear that drinking the vial tomorrow afternoon, money already banked, would be flatly irrational -- self-punishment to no end.

What I need is a consistency check, not a primer on the literature. Which of these five commitments genuinely pull against one another, and how severely? Which pairs only appear to conflict but can be shown to sit together comfortably once the relevant distinctions are drawn? Please order the real tensions by seriousness, say for each what would have to give and at what cost, and tell me which single revision would do the most to clean up the set. I'd also appreciate a sentence or two on what your preferred resolution implies for my protagonist: under the repaired view, is there any coherent version of him -- not a paradoxical super-rational saint, just a plausible, clever person -- who could walk away with the money, or should I rewrite the scene so that he loses?
