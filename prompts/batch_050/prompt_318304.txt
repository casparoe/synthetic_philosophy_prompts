So I work on a geometry-heavy simulation codebase (collision testing for a robotics tool, if it matters) and I keep tripping over something that I guess is philosophy. Our team style guide has a rule that basically says: when you don't know anything about a parameter, use a uniform distribution. So when we need a "random line through a circle" for testing, people just sample uniform on whatever variable is handy. But I found out the answer depends on which variable you pick. For the probability that a random chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, you get 1/3 if you pick two uniform random endpoints on the circumference, 1/2 if you pick a uniform radius and a uniform point on it as the chord's midpoint, and 1/4 if you pick the midpoint uniform over the disk. All three are "uniform." My teammates mostly shrug and pick whichever is cheapest to compute.

Apparently this is a known thing, Bertrand raised basically this exact example in 1889 in Calcul des Probabilités, and from what I understand Keynes later argued in A Treatise on Probability that the principle of indifference only works when the alternatives can't be further split up into sub-alternatives of the same form, i.e. you have to apply it to the indivisible cases, and the paradoxes come from applying it to the wrong level. (Paraphrasing, don't hold me to the exact wording.)

My question is about the style guide rule itself, as a practice. What does a "default to uniform" convention actually presuppose? That the principle of indifference is valid? That one parameterization is the natural one and we just have to find it? Or is the rule really presupposing something more defensible, like "the distribution doesn't matter for testing purposes, so arbitrariness is fine"? Which rival views does the rule quietly rule out -- e.g. that probabilities only make sense tied to an actual physical generating mechanism, or Keynes' fix that indifference must be applied at the indivisible level, or Jeffreys-style invariance priors? And where is the rule underdetermined, i.e. compatible with several of these at once? Finally, for each of those views, tell me concretely what would change in how we write the simulation code if we adopted it.

I was unable to fetch a verbatim source passage, so work from my paraphrases of Bertrand's example and Keynes' condition -- if I've garbled either, correct me.

- "strictly_presupposed": array of claims the rule can't make sense without
- "merely_compatible": array of claims it fits but doesn't need
