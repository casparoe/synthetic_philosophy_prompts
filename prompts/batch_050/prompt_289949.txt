I'm putting together a problem set for a graduate seminar on the foundations of decision theory, and I want one worked example that drives home a point students reliably miss: that aversion to risk is not the same thing as diminishing marginal utility, since an agent can value money linearly and still reject actuarially favorable gambles. To that end, construct a decision problem for me using risk-weighted expected utility theory (due to Lara Buchak), defined as follows for the purposes of this exercise. Given an act whose outcomes, ranked from worst to best, have utilities u₁ ≤ u₂ ≤ … ≤ uₙ with probabilities p₁, …, pₙ, the act's risk-weighted expected utility equals u₁ plus, for each subsequent outcome i, the utility increment (uᵢ − uᵢ₋₁) multiplied by r applied to the probability of doing at least that well, i.e. r(pᵢ + pᵢ₊₁ + … + pₙ), where r is a non-decreasing function with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. A convex risk function such as r(p) = p² makes the agent risk-avoidant: she discounts the probability of every gain above the worst outcome, and so evaluates an act largely by its floor rather than by its average.

Constraints on the construction: two options only, call them A and B, with at most three outcomes per option; dollar payoffs between $0 and $120; probabilities that are simple fractions such as halves, thirds, or quarters. An expected utility maximizer with u(x) = √x must strictly prefer A, while a risk-weighted expected utility maximizer with linear utility u(x) = x and risk function r(p) = p² must strictly prefer B. The expected dollar value of A must exceed that of B by at least $5, so that the risk-weighted agent is visibly leaving money on the table. Finally, neither option may first-order stochastically dominate the other. Note that this last condition is not an arbitrary restriction: the theory as defined above, with a non-decreasing risk function, never prefers a dominated act, so if your construction violates the condition then at least one of your two preference computations must be wrong. Treat it as a checksum on your own arithmetic.

Deliverables: state the two options precisely; show both agents' calculations in full, with the risk-weighted calculation written in the ranked-outcome form given above so that each r-weighted increment is visible; then identify which structural feature of the formalism is doing the work in producing the divergence, and what happens to the verdict if it is removed (say, by setting r(p) = p). After that, answer two follow-ups briefly and with numbers. First, does the divergence survive replacing r(p) = p² with the more strongly convex r(p) = p³, and does it survive replacing the expected utility agent's u(x) = √x with a logarithmic utility? Second, an orthodox expected utility theorist will object that the risk-weighted agent's preference for B is merely concave utility in disguise. Explain why that reply is always available for any single pairwise choice that respects stochastic dominance, and then describe the structural shape of a multi-choice pattern — you need not supply complete numbers — on which a risk-weighted agent with linear utility and every expected utility agent, whatever her utility function, genuinely must come apart.
