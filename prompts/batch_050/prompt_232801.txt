I am a high school student preparing an independent-study presentation on the Principal Principle and rational deference to probabilities. I would like to know whether my position is internally consistent.

Consider this hypothetical case: At 8:00 a.m., a highly reliable weather service announces that the objective chance of rain at my school that afternoon is 70 percent. The service’s model is well calibrated overall, but I also know that under this particular weather pattern it has historically overestimated rain by about 20 percentage points. In superficially similar past situations, it rained only 40 percent of the time. Finally, suppose deterministic physical laws, together with the complete current microstate of the atmosphere, already settle whether it will rain, although nobody possesses that complete microstate.

My commitments are:

1. If I know that the objective chance of an event is 70 percent, and I possess no inadmissible information, then my rational credence in that event should also be exactly 70 percent.

2. Evidence about whether the announced 70 percent figure really is the objective chance is admissible. Information is inadmissible only when it bears directly on the event in a way not already reflected in the objective chance.

3. The model’s known tendency to overestimate rain under these particular conditions is admissible evidence, so my rational credence should be lower than 70 percent.

4. Even after I take that known bias into account, the service’s 70 percent figure could still be the actual objective chance, rather than merely the model’s stated probability.

5. Determinism does not by itself mean that every genuine objective chance must be either 0 or 1. A nontrivial objective chance of rain can still exist even if the outcome is fixed by unknown microscopic facts.

6. The 40 percent historical frequency does not by itself refute the announced 70 percent chance, since single-case chances need not match the frequency in a rough reference class.

7. When evidence is sparse or conflicting, rational credence can be interval-valued. In such cases, it can be irrational to manufacture a precise credence rather than adopt a range.

Please audit these commitments rather than merely explaining the Principal Principle. Identify every genuine tension, including tensions among three or more commitments, and rank them by severity. For each genuine tension, explain exactly which claims generate it and what the least costly revision would be. Also identify any apparent tensions that disappear once an appropriate distinction is made. Finally, tell me which single revision would restore the most overall consistency, without assuming in advance which commitment I should be most willing to abandon.
