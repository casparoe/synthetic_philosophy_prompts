I am a graduate student preparing a seminar presentation on Hume's metaethics, and I keep getting stuck on one stretch of A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 1, Section 1, titled Moral Distinctions not deriv'd from Reason. Here is the passage, in Hume's original spelling:

"Since morals, therefore, have an influence on the actions and affections, it follows, that they cannot be deriv'd from reason; and that because reason alone, as we have already prov'd, can never have any such influence. Morals excite passions, and produce or prevent actions. Reason of itself is utterly impotent in this particular. The rules of morality, therefore, are not conclusions of our reason.

No one, I believe, will deny the justness of this inference; nor is there any other means of evading it, than by denying that principle, on which it is founded. As long as it is allow'd, that reason has no influence on our passions and actions, 'tis in vain to pretend, that morality is discover'd only by a deduction of reason. An active principle can never be founded on an inactive; and if reason be inactive in itself, it must remain so in all its shapes and appearances, whether it exerts itself in natural or moral subjects, whether it considers the powers of external bodies, or the actions of rational beings."

I would like your help understanding this passage, in four parts.

First, reconstruct the argument as a small set of premises leading to the conclusion that the rules of morality are not conclusions of our reason. I especially want to know exactly what work the sentence about an active principle never being founded on an inactive one is doing, and what Hume means by calling reason impotent or inert. He cannot plausibly mean that beliefs never influence conduct, since a belief about where the bridge is out plainly keeps me from driving across it; so what is the narrower claim, and does the argument still go through once it is stated carefully?

Second, the passage seems to assume that whatever moves us to act must involve something beyond reason. But Hume's rationalist opponent could reply that any judgment influences conduct only in the presence of a standing desire, and that this is just as true of prudential calculation as of morality, so Hume's argument either proves too much or leaves the rationalist untouched. Does this reply hit home, and what resources, if any, does the passage itself contain for answering it?

Third, I am puzzled about how this influence argument relates to the remark Hume makes later in the same section about authors who move from claims about what is the case to claims about what ought to be the case without explaining the transition. Are these two distinct arguments against moral rationalism, or is the later remark merely a restatement of the earlier one? The influence argument seems to concern what moral judgments do, namely motivate us, while the later remark seems to concern how moral conclusions are established; if they are genuinely distinct, is there a case that the influence argument quietly presupposes the later point rather than standing on its own?

Fourth, I would like help connecting all of this to the naturalistic fallacy. It is often said that Hume anticipated Moore's open-question argument. Does anything in this passage support that reading, or does Hume's own procedure, which derives conclusions about the nature of morality from observations about human motivation, itself risk sliding from facts to norms in the way the later remark warns against? In short, is Hume here diagnosing a fallacy about deriving the normative from the non-normative, or is he making a different, more psychological point about the sources of moral judgment?
