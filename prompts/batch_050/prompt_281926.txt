As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I have watched the same move end the same discussion many times. Someone proposes that a community, organization, or country replace first-past-the-post with a Condorcet method, approval voting, or a rated system; someone else replies with some version of the slogan "every voting system is manipulable," usually with the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem name-checked as the authority, and the conversation dies. I have a rough sense of what that theorem establishes, but I strongly suspect the slogan is being made to do more work than the theorem licenses. Please write your answer in American English.

First, disambiguate the slogan. Lay out, as a list, the distinct precise theses that "every voting system is manipulable" could be expressing, varying at least the following dimensions: what the quantifier ranges over (resolute single-winner rules, multi-winner rules, social choice correspondences, randomized procedures, or real-world institutions including primaries, candidate entry, and agenda setting); what "manipulable" means (the bare existence of some preference profile at which some voter profits by misreporting, versus manipulation being frequently profitable, versus its being computationally tractable to find, versus its being detectable); and what conditions are smuggled in (the number of alternatives, unrestricted versus structured preference domains such as single-peakedness, and whether the target is honest aggregation of reported preferences as opposed to clone candidacies, strategic entry, or vote trading).

Second, give a verdict on each reading: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but probably false, or something the theorem simply does not say.

Third, state at the level of a careful gloss rather than a proof what the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem itself establishes, note which of the disambiguated readings it corresponds to, and indicate what, if anything, changes for randomized or probabilistic aggregation rules.

Fourth, adjudicate the rhetorical use. Which of the disambiguated readings, if any, would actually support the conclusion that the choice among first-past-the-post, Condorcet methods, and approval voting is immaterial? If none does, articulate the strongest defensible takeaway about strategic voting for electoral-reform debates: whether the theorem ranks methods at all, and what it leaves open regarding the frequency, severity, coordination requirements, and computational difficulty of profitable manipulation under different rules.
