Quick context: I got engaged about six weeks ago, and at the little celebration dinner my friends Dana and Marcus got into this argument that I have genuinely not been able to stop thinking about, because it's made me kind of paranoid about my own relationship in a way I can't shake. It started when Dana said she loves her boyfriend because he's funny and patient and actually listens, and Marcus said that answer, taken seriously, proves something uncomfortable. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on love and emotions, think carefully, and I'd actually like you to start by brainstorming the strongest version of each side before you judge anything. Don't just explain the debate at me, referee it.

Roughly how it went:

Dana's opening point: love that isn't grounded in anything about the person isn't really love of that person. If you can't point to anything -- their humor, their decency, whatever -- then what you have is infatuation or dependency or habit, not love. Being loved for your qualities is also what makes being loved flattering. If it could have been literally anyone, it's not a compliment.

Marcus: okay, then you have a problem. If you love him *because* he's funny and patient, and tomorrow you meet someone funnier and more patient, consistency says you should switch. But nobody thinks that's how love works, and nobody thinks it should. So either you admit love isn't really grounded in the qualities, or you admit your love is one better option away from being irrational.

Dana: that's too fast. The qualities can explain how the love started without being a permanent ranking I have to keep updating. Once we're together, the shared history is doing the work -- this specific person, this specific life we've built. It's not a leaderboard.

Marcus: that sounds like changing the subject to me. Either the reasons for the love are the qualities, in which case they're fungible -- anyone with the same qualities earns the same love, and your boyfriend is replaceable in principle -- or the real ground is the history and the attachment, in which case the qualities were never the reason and my point stands: love isn't a response to value, it creates the value. You love him, and *that's* why he matters to you, not the other way around. Asking for reasons is a category error, like demanding a justification for grief.

Dana: but if love bestows value rather than responding to it, then love is basically arbitrary, and arbitrary love is insulting, not romantic. Also, you can't just decide to love someone. If bestowing value were the whole story, why can't you bestow it on command? The fact that love is involuntary suggests it's a *response* to something about the person.

Marcus: you can't decide to find something funny either, but nobody thinks humor is a measurement device. Unchosen doesn't mean justified-by-facts. And look at parents and kids -- nobody thinks a mother loves her newborn because of the baby's qualities, the baby has no qualities yet. That's clearly value flowing from the love, and romantic love is the same structure, just less visible.

Dana: everyone agrees parental love is the weird case, that's exactly why people distinguish it from romantic love, so you can't use it as your model for everything. And honestly even the mother loves *her* baby -- the particularity is doing something there that your picture can't explain either.

Then it kind of dissolved into jokes and we never resolved it, and I couldn't figure out who won, which is annoying because I keep mentally rehearsing what I'd say if my fiancee asked me why I love her and both answers now sound like traps. Is the "you can't choose to love" argument any good? Does the parent case show what Marcus thinks it shows? And what is each of them missing that the other one sees? End with an actual verdict on who's more right overall, not a both-sides shrug -- and if the honest verdict is that they're both half-right, at least tell me which half.
