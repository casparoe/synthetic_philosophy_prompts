I teach an intermediate logic course, and every year the same thing happens: I can get students through the proof of the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, and they accept that ZFC, if consistent, has a countable model. But then someone raises a hand and asks how that model can satisfy the theorem stating that the set of real numbers is uncountable, and the room falls apart. Half the students decide the theorem must be inconsistent with Cantor; the other half shrug and file it under "formalism is weird." Neither group has grasped the standard resolution -- that "uncountable" is model-relative, and the bijection witnessing the model's countability exists outside the model, unavailable to anyone reasoning within it. For the last session of term, I'd like a poem that dramatizes this rather than states it.

Write a sestina about Skolem's paradox. Use these six end-words, in the usual rotating order: *count*, *outside*, *many*, *name*, *missing*, *within*. I'd like the stanzas to alternate perspective: some spoken from inside the countable model, by a voice that can prove there is no bijection between the naturals and the reals and is right to say so -- the bijection genuinely does not exist in its world; others spoken from outside, by a voice that can see the whole model is countable and that the "reals" inside are only countably many, and is right to say *that*. The poem's difficulty -- and it should feel like a difficulty, not a lecture -- is that both voices are correct, and the sestina's structure, with the same six words returning in shifted positions each stanza, should enact the shift in perspective: the same words, re-situated, meaning something different relative to where one stands.

The envoy (the final three-line stanza, using all six end-words) is where the resolution has to land: the paradox dissolves not because either voice is wrong but because cardinality is not absolute -- what is missing is missing only *within*. A reader who knows the paradox should recognize the resolution precisely, including why the inside voice's proof is not a mistake; a reader who doesn't should still get a good poem about two honest speakers talking past each other across a boundary neither can cross. Please give it a title, and after the poem add two or three sentences of prose -- addressed to me, not to my students -- noting which stanza does which work, so I can check the poem against the mathematics before I decide whether to use it.
