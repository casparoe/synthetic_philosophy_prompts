I have recently joined a citizens’ commission responsible for revising the governance charter of a neighborhood whose residents are sharply divided by language, religion, income, and length of residence. The immediate questions include how much authority the neighborhood should have over housing, schools, public space, and local cultural norms, and when individuals should be protected from decisions made in the name of the community. My background is in mechanism design and repeated games rather than political philosophy, so that is the vocabulary in which I can think most rigorously.

Please restate the central communitarian critique of liberalism, together with the strongest liberal response, using concepts such as agents and coalitions, preference formation, information asymmetry, commitment problems, repeated interaction, exit and voice, coalition-proofness, baseline entitlements, externalities, institutional path dependence, and the endogenous effects of rules on the people governed by them. Treat liberalism not merely as laissez-faire and communitarianism not merely as majoritarianism: include the liberal ideas of individual rights, fair procedures, and legitimate pluralism, and the communitarian ideas that agents are socially formed, inherit obligations, and can be constituted by shared practices rather than merely having them as objects of choice.

Translate at least these claims:

1. Neutral procedures are not morally neutral when they operate on unequal baselines.
2. Some attachments and obligations are discovered or inherited rather than voluntarily chosen.
3. Protecting exit may weaken voice and turn political disagreement into residential sorting.
4. A community can cultivate virtues, but may also reproduce domination through socialization.
5. Individual rights function partly as constraints that prevent current majorities from exploiting their power over minorities and future dissenters.
6. A liberal order may undermine the very social practices needed to form citizens capable of sustaining it.

For each claim, state whether it translates cleanly, requires a significant reinterpretation, or has no adequate counterpart in mechanism-design language. Then provide a residue report distinguishing three categories: what the translation captures well, what it distorts by treating preferences and identities as exogenous, and what it cannot express without quietly adding normative premises that are not part of the formal framework. Where a translation is tempting but misleading, identify the hidden assumption responsible.

Conclude by applying your analysis to our charter problem: which provisions would the translated liberal framework emphasize, which would the translated communitarian framework emphasize, and which unresolved issue would remain invisible if we relied exclusively on the mechanism-design vocabulary? I am less interested in choosing a winner than in understanding whether the untranslatable residue reveals a limitation of mechanism design, a confusion in the original political theories, or both.
