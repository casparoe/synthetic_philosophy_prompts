I am a high school senior giving a twenty-minute presentation to my school's philosophy club, and I want to be ready for the discussion period. My topic is moral trade: roughly, the idea that two people who disagree about what morality requires can sometimes each get more of what they take to be moral value by cooperating than by acting alone — for instance, I contribute to a project you consider important, and in exchange you contribute to one I consider important, when neither of us would have supported the other's project independently.

The thesis I will defend is that moral trade is not just permissible but genuinely rational, even when both parties keep their original moral convictions, and that it does not collapse into moral relativism or into merely "agreeing to disagree." However, I want to defend it with three restrictions: (1) the trade must arise from sincere moral disagreement, not strategic pretense; (2) neither party may be asked to perform an act they regard as seriously wrong in itself, as opposed to merely forgoing a good; and (3) the trade must not give either side an incentive to manufacture or exaggerate moral disagreements in the future.

The audience will be about fifteen students and our faculty advisor. I know the room: several committed utilitarians who will press me on whether restricting trades is just leaving moral value on the table; two or three students with Kantian or deontological sympathies who will press me on complicity and on whether aiding a cause you believe is misguided is a kind of lying to yourself; one student who thinks all moral compromise corrupts character; and our advisor, who will push hardest on whether my three restrictions are principled or just patched on to avoid counterexamples.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually receive from this particular audience, ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they would do to my thesis, each phrased the way that type of questioner would phrase it. For each objection, tell me what I should concede immediately, what I should hold firm on, a reply I can deliver in about a minute, and where that reply is weakest. Also address these complications somewhere in the prep: whether a trade differs in kind from an ordinary compromise; whether the second restriction rules out the very cases that make moral trade interesting; whether repeated trading between the same people changes the analysis; and whether the practice is stable once both parties fully understand each other's reasoning. End with the objection I should most hope nobody raises, and the best I could say if it comes.
