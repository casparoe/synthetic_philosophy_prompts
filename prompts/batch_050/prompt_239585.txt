I run a small software consultancy with about fifteen employees, and over the past three years we have sunk a large amount of money and developer time into building a proprietary scheduling product that keeps failing to find customers. Every quarter I face the same choice: write the project off, or invest another round because we are 'so close' and because walking away would make all the previous spending a pure loss. A colleague recently told me my situation resembles the dollar auction, the game in which participants bid on a dollar bill under the rule that both the highest and the second-highest bidder must pay their bids, so that once two people are in, each can always justify one more small bid to avoid losing everything, and the auctioneer reliably ends up collecting far more than a dollar.

Please describe the most important implications of the dollar auction for how I should think about escalation of commitment in decisions like mine. I am interested in more than the obvious moral of 'don't throw good money after bad': I want to understand what the structure of the game teaches that the plain sunk-cost fallacy does not already cover. For example, does the lesson change depending on whether I am in genuine competition with a rival who will also have to pay to stay in (as in bidding wars, price wars, or an arms race with a competitor), versus struggling alone against a merely passive situation? Should I reason differently at the moment of entering a commitment than when deciding whether to continue one, and are there practical devices (pre-set budgets, handing the continue-or-kill decision to someone with no history on the project, auctioning the project internally) that the logic of the game suggests? Finally, please address the hardest case honestly: sometimes the competitor really does blink first and the stubborn party wins the prize, so explain under what conditions escalation is the correct strategy rather than a trap, and how I could tell in advance which kind of situation I am in.
