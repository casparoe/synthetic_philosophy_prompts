I'm a junior taking a seminar on decision theory, and for my term paper I want to pin down what principle, if any, underlies my own considered judgments about a set of standard and not-quite-standard cases. I'll report my verdicts first, then say what I'm asking for.

1. Ordinary Newcomb's problem, with a highly reliable predictor: I one-box. Taking both boxes strikes me as leaving money on the table for the sake of a causal story about boxes whose contents are already fixed.

2. Parfit's hitchhiker: I'm stranded in the desert, a driver will save me only if he predicts I'll pay him $100 once we're safely in town, and he's an excellent reader of people. We arrive in town. I pay the $100, even though nothing now depends on it.

3. Counterfactual mugging: a trustworthy being tells me it flipped a fair coin yesterday. Had it landed tails, it would have given me $10,000 conditional on predicting that, in the heads branch, I'd hand over $100 upon being told the outcome. It landed heads, and now it's asking for the $100. I pay, and I don't feel cheated.

4. XOR blackmail: I receive a letter saying, in effect, that an excellent predictor has arranged things so that my house suffers a costly termite infestation if and only if I don't pay $1,000 -- the letter itself being sent only in the branch where paying would avert nothing causal, since the predictor either seeded the termites or sent the letter depending on what I was predicted to do. I refuse to pay. Paying blackmail to avert a disaster the blackmailer orchestrated around my predicted response feels like rewarding extortion, not like one-boxing.

5. One-shot prisoner's dilemma against a verified atom-for-atom copy of myself, with both of us knowing the setup: I cooperate. Against a randomly selected stranger of unknown psychology, I defect.

6. Counterfactual mugging again, but this time I learn the deal only after the coin has landed heads, and before being asked for the money I'm offered the chance to buy, for $5, a certificate entitling me to the $10,000 in the tails branch had it occurred. I wouldn't buy it -- what's done is done -- and yet in case 3 I pay the $100. I notice these two attitudes sit awkwardly together, since paying in case 3 seems to value the tails branch exactly as the certificate would.

Here is what I'd like. First, a precisely stated principle of rational choice -- or a small family of candidates -- that reproduces as many of these verdicts as possible, with each clause of the principle tied to the case that motivates it. I'm aware the literature offers updateless, timeless, and functional-decision-theoretic ideas, but I want the principle built bottom-up from the verdicts rather than assumed in advance. Second, an honest reckoning with the tension I flagged: cases 3 and 4 look structurally alike (payment justified by what a predictor did in a branch that didn't happen), so tell me whether a principled line can be drawn between being counterfactually mugged and being XOR-blackmailed, or whether intellectual honesty requires giving up one of my verdicts there -- and likewise for the case 3 versus case 6 conflict. Third, say what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a counterfactual mugging in which the coin is biased 90-10 in favor of my being asked for the money, and an XOR blackmail letter from a predictor known to be right only 70 percent of the time. If the best available principle forces me to abandon one or more of my verdicts, say which and why, rather than papering over the conflict. Please write your answer in American English.
