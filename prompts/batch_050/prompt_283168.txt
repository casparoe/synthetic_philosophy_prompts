I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I'm working up a long piece on panpsychism's recent comeback in consciousness studies. The bit I keep circling is the combination problem: if little bits of matter have little experiences, how do those ever add up to one big unified mind like yours or mine. I want a thought experiment for a sidebar that makes this feel like a live engineering question rather than a dorm-room puzzle, and I want it built to order.

So: build me a single scenario, physically realistic (near-future neurosurgery or brain-interface tech, no telepathy, no soul stuff, no demons), in which two leading responses to the combination problem are forced to give different answers to one question: how many conscious subjects are there, and at what point does that number change?

The two views to pit against each other:

1. The causal-integration answer: proto-experiences combine into a full-blown subject when and because the parts come to form one causally unified system with its own integrated dynamics.
2. The phenomenal-bonding answer (Goff's term): little subjects fuse into a bigger one only when their experiences stand in a primitive bonding relation, which mere causal rewiring doesn't obviously manufacture.

Specifications, and please take them all seriously:

- The scenario has to move in the combination direction — two persons gradually becoming one — not the usual split-brain direction. I want to block recycled answers.
- The transition must be graded, atleast three distinct stages, so each view has to say at which stage the subject count changes (or squirm trying).
- Don't stipulate who's conscious at any stage. The verdicts have to be derived from the two views, not asserted.
- The scenario itself: 200 words max, since it has to actually run in the magazine.

Then show your work: (a) for each view, the subject count at each stage and why the view delivers exactly that verdict, keeping the two views' verdicts clearly seperate in the write-up; (b) which single feature of the case does the work of forcing the divergence, and what happens to the case if you remove it; (c) the counterattack — how a defender of whichever view comes out looking worse would try to show the case is rigged.

Open with your bottom line in one sentence — the scenario in a nutshell plus the exact stage where the two views part ways — then the case itself. No warm-up paragraph, no restating the assignment.
