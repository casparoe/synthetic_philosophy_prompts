I’m on my hospital’s Innovative Treatments Committee, and we have to decide a compassionate-use request by Friday. The patient, a 42-year-old former carpenter, has had severe neuropathic pain for six years after a workplace injury. She describes it as a constant burning pain around 7/10, with electrical shocks when she moves. Standard medications, physical therapy, nerve blocks, and spinal-cord stimulation have either failed or caused intolerable side effects. She is not depressed, has capacity, and says her main reason for wanting the procedure is that she can’t stand the pain anymore.

The proposed experimental procedure is a form of deep-brain stimulation intended to leave the sensory side of pain intact while removing its affective and motivational force. If it works as intended, she would still be able to say that the sensation is burning, intense, located in her leg, and worse when walking, but she would no longer find it unpleasant or feel driven to escape it. In that respect, the intended outcome resembles pain asymbolia: pain is recognized, but no longer hurts in the ordinary practically urgent sense.

The catch is that the separation may not remain limited to this one chronic pain. Early data suggest something like a 15% chance of persistently reduced aversion to other bodily sensations, including acute injury, and a smaller chance of broader emotional flattening. Her partner worries that the procedure would not really make her life better but would only make her less able to register or protest that it is bad. The treating team responds that this confuses a useful alarm with the misery produced when the alarm cannot be turned off.

Please draft the committee’s written opinion recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions. It should be usable as an actual decision document: state the bottom line early, specify any consent language, monitoring, stopping rules, and post-procedure safeguards you would require, and address whether the badness of pain lies in its felt unpleasantness, its motivational urgency, its interference with the patient’s life, or some combination of these. In particular, explain what difference it makes if the patient still has intense pain but no longer minds it. Take the partner’s objection seriously instead of treating it as squeamishness, and give the strongest version of the case for whichever option you reject. Keep it around 900-1,200 words, and end with exactly one machine-readable line in this form: \answer{APPROVE}, \answer{REJECT}, or \answer{APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS}.
