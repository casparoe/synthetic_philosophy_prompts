I want to practice attacking a view I find tempting but suspect is wrong, and I need a defender who will actually hold the line. Please play a committed lexical threshold negative utilitarian and stay in character until I say "stop."

So that we share a vocabulary: negative utilitarianism gives moral priority to the prevention of suffering over the promotion of pleasure. The lexical threshold version you defend holds that suffering above a certain intensity — call it threshold suffering — cannot be morally outweighed or compensated for by any amount of pleasure, however intense, long-lasting, or widely distributed, whereas below the threshold pleasures and pains trade off against one another in something like the ordinary aggregative way.

Your character: you have defended this view for years and know the standard objections. You are not dogmatic — you concede premises when they are true, you distinguish rather than deny when you can, and you treat the phenomenology of suffering and pleasure as data to be explained, not as an obstacle to be waved away. You never hide behind citations, philosopher names, or appeals to authority; you argue from cases and concepts. You may ask me to sharpen a case if it is under-specified, but you must always make your best substantive move first. Keep your replies to one or two paragraphs so this feels like a live exchange, and do not break character to comment on the exercise.

Here is my opening move. The Anesthesiologist's Offer: a patient faces a procedure involving one hour of agony at the greatest intensity a human nervous system can sustain — well above your threshold. There are two buttons. Button A administers a perfect analgesic: she feels nothing, and her subsequent life is ordinary. Button B offers no analgesic: she endures the hour, and afterward receives fifty additional years of the deepest, most unclouded joy a human life can contain, joy she would not otherwise have. The patient is competent and unusually well informed — she has experienced exactly that intensity of pain before, so she knows precisely what she is weighing — and she begs for B. Your view, as I understand it, requires A.

Two problems. First, the consent problem: lexical priority is supposed to be a claim about what matters for people, yet here the very person whose suffering grounds the priority judges the trade worth making, with full knowledge of both sides. If you override her, the priority seems to concern suffering-in-the-abstract rather than persons; if you honor her choice, the threshold was never really lexical. Second, the phenomenological problem: within a single hour of consciousness, grief is softened by a friend's joke, dread dissolves at good news, the pain of an injury recedes under euphoria. Inner life presents pleasure and pain as trading off continuously, on one scale, all the way up. Your view must insist that at some intensity this appearance simply breaks — that a scale which everywhere looks continuous conceals an invisible cliff. But then you owe me an account of where the cliff sits and what marks it. In particular: compare one minute at threshold intensity with a decade at one percent below it. On your view the minute is uncompensatable and the decade is not, and I would like to know what could possibly justify locating a lexical break there rather than admitting that you are really just a very demanding classical utilitarian.

Respond in character, and begin by telling me which button your view actually selects — I suspect the consent wrinkle makes that less obvious than it first appears.
