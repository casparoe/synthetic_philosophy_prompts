I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I am about to commit to a particular account of truthlikeness (verisimilitude) in a thesis chapter on scientific progress. Before I do, I want to step back and fix the standard of adequacy itself, because every account I have encountered seems to win by quietly tailoring the requirements to its own strengths. My working purpose is this: the notion must make sense of the commonplace claim that a sequence of strictly false scientific theories can nonetheless constitute progress towards the truth. But I would like you to treat that as my purpose, not as an unquestioned desideratum — if you think the purpose itself is confused or smuggles in assumptions, say so.

Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of verisimilitude must meet for this purpose. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should cover, at minimum, the following kinds of question, though you should not feel bound by my grouping:

- Must the theory permit false theories to differ in truthlikeness at all, and what would count as cheating here (for instance, building the ordering into the examples chosen)?
- Which intuitive ordering principles should it vindicate — for example, principles about how adding truths to, or removing falsehoods from, a theory affects its closeness to the truth — and are any of these seemingly innocent principles in fact incriminating, in that accepting them collapses the whole notion?
- Must the ordering be invariant under reformulation of the same theory in a logically equivalent vocabulary, and how much weight should that invariance requirement carry given the consequences it is known to have?
- How must the theory relate truthlikeness to confirmation and evidential support — are these two notions that an adequate account must keep apart, and what goes wrong if they are conflated?
- Must the account extend beyond simple propositional cases (say, claims about how many objects of some kind exist) to theories involving magnitudes and approximation, and to cases where rival theories do not share a common vocabulary?

Two further requests. First, mark which desiderata you take to be non-negotiable and which are partisan demands of particular camps in the literature, and identify any subset of the desiderata that is jointly unsatisfiable — if the requirements cannot all be met at once, that is the result I most want to see stated precisely, with the reasoning behind it. Second, identify which single desideratum excludes the most serious contenders, and then give only a brief scorecard of the main families of accounts (Popper's original proposal, likeness-based approaches, content-based approaches, and quantitative approaches) against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than the scoring, since the thinking is in the former.

Please write your answer in British English.
