After a long argument with a friend about duties of rescue -- the conversation began, as these conversations do, from the familiar case of the child drowning in a shallow pond -- I realized I could not actually predict what she thinks. Her verdicts, as nearly as I can reconstruct them: (1) if you are alone at the pond, you must wade in, even in a new suit; (2) a donation that saves a distant life at roughly the cost of the suit is equally obligatory, since distance and visibility change nothing; (3) if ten other people are standing around the pond doing nothing, you must still wade in, and their failure does not reduce what you owe; (4) if the only way to reach the child in time is to take a stranger's unattended boat, you may take it, provided you compensate the owner afterward; (5) having already pulled one child out of the pond that morning, you must pull out a second, tired as you are.

Our argument ended on a case neither of us would concede: on your way to the airport to fly to your sister's wedding, where you are her only family, you pass the pond alone, and the child will drown unless you stay and miss the flight. Is staying obligatory?

Predict her verdict. More than the verdict, I want the reconstruction: which rules are consistent with all five earlier verdicts (I can see at least two -- an unqualified principle that one must prevent grave harm whenever the cost is not comparably grave, and a duty of aid bounded by some agent-centered prerogative to favor one's own projects and commitments), which plausible rules her verdicts rule out, and which of the surviving rules best explains the overall pattern. If the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so plainly, and tell me the single additional verdict I should elicit from her that would most efficiently settle the matter. Finally, identify which of the five verdicts is doing the most work in your prediction -- that is, what I might discover about her actual reasoning that would most likely overturn it.
