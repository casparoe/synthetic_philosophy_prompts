I am a novelist working on a book with a deliberately unstable structure, and I find I need philosophical guidance rather than workshop advice of the usual sort.

The novel alternates between two strands. In the odd-numbered chapters, a first-person narrator named Elias recounts his present-day life. In the even-numbered ones, a third-person narrator follows a drifter known only as Marek. Late in the book it emerges that Marek is Elias — the same man twenty years earlier, before he took his stepfather's name. No sentence in the early chapters states this, and I have tried to write them so that nothing becomes false once the reader knows the truth, though a good deal becomes, I hope, differently informative. The reader who finishes the book and begins it again reads, in some sense, a different novel.

The apparatus I keep circling is Frege's puzzle about identity statements: a statement of the form a = a is trivially true, while a true statement of the form a = b can be a genuine discovery, even though both, if true, say of one and the same object that it is identical with itself. Frege's response, as I understand it, was to distinguish what a name refers to from the mode of presentation under which it picks out that referent — its sense — so that the informativeness of a true identity statement is located in the senses of the two names rather than in any fact about the world beyond them.

My question is this: what are the most important implications of the puzzle, and of the sense–reference response to it, for the construction I have described? Please do not explain the puzzle back to me, and do not offer generic remarks about dramatic irony or unreliable narration, both of which I already possess in quantity. What I want are the less obvious consequences for the writing itself. Three pressure points interest me especially. First, if a character's or a reader's knowledge is individuated by modes of presentation rather than by facts about referents, what exactly would count as cheating in the early chapters — which sentences would be illegitimate even though strictly true? Second, fictional names arguably have no real referents at all; if so, does the identity between Marek and Elias pre-exist the reveal the way the identity of the morning star and the evening star pre-existed its discovery, or do I legislate it into being — and does the answer change what the reveal can accomplish? Third, what does the puzzle imply for the second reading the book is designed to invite?

Please also tell me where you think the analogy breaks down — where an implication would seem to follow from the philosophy but does not survive translation into narrative practice.
