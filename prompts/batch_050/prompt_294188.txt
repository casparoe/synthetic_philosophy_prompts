Explain what the problem is, being careful to distinguish its two components (sometimes called the incoherence problem and the quantitative problem). "Everettian quantum mechanics says the universal wavefunction evolves unitarily according to the Schrödinger equation, and there is no collapse. When a measurement happens, decoherence causes the wavefunction to split into branches, one for each outcome, and every outcome occurs in some branch. This raises the probability problem, which has two parts. First, the incoherence problem: if all outcomes actually happen, it's unclear what it could even mean to assign probabilities to them. Probability normally requires uncertainty about which single outcome will occur, but in Everett there is no such uncertainty -- everything occurs. Second, the quantitative problem: even if we could make sense of probability, why should it be given by the Born rule, i.e. the squared amplitude? Branches come with amplitudes, but nothing in the formalism obviously forces us to weight branches by amplitude-squared rather than, say, just counting them.

The decoherence that produces the branches solves the incoherence problem, because once decoherence has occurred the branches are effectively independent classical worlds, and an observer in a branch can be uncertain about which world they're in -- this is 'self-locating uncertainty.' So the incoherence problem is really just a confusion that goes away once you take decoherence seriously.

For the quantitative problem, Deutsch gave a decision-theoretic proof that derives the Born rule from the principles of rationality alone, without any extra assumptions beyond unitary quantum mechanics. He shows that a rational agent who knows she will branch must bet as if the probabilities were the squared amplitudes. I find this convincing because it shows the Born rule isn't an extra postulate -- it falls out of pure rationality applied to the branching structure. So the probability problem is solved: decoherence handles the incoherence half, and Deutsch's proof handles the quantitative half."
