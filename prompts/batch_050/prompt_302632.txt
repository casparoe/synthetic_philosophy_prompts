In the philosophy of science reading group I attend, the debate over scientific realism has been stuck for years. Everyone grants the historical record the pessimistic meta-induction rests on — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether were all empirically successful and all discarded — and everyone grants that our current best theories are predictively successful in a way that would be miraculous if they were not at least approximately right. Structural and entity realists claim to dissolve the tension by restricting realism to whatever survives theory change; their opponents reply that what survives was retained precisely because it was empirically successful, so the move is circular, or else so thin that it no longer deserves the name realism. My own hypothesis is that the dispute is not really about the historical evidence at all but about whether inference to the best explanation is a legitimate ampliative rule: realists treat the success of science as a datum that demands explanation, anti-realists deny that explanatory goodness confirms anything, and neither side can argue for its rule of inference without already using it. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden factual or formal question doing the real work? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, empirical, methodological, or bedrock intuition — and what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper distinction, a formal result about what is preserved across theory change, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Please answer in continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
