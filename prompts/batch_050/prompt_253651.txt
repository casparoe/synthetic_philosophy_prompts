I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and next month I am presenting the keynote essay from my new book at a small conference of economic historians and quantitatively trained social scientists. The essay defends a reworked version of Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis. I want you to prepare me for the question period.

Act as a veteran economic historian who has also spent years coaching speakers for hostile academic audiences. Think carefully and work in stages: first, briefly brainstorm every line of attack this particular audience could mount against my argument; then select, rank, and refine the ones that matter; then draft the prep sheet I describe below.

Here is the argument I will actually defend, so the objections target it rather than a caricature. Weber's original claim, as I present it, is that certain strands of Protestantism cultivated an ethic of disciplined worldly labor, frugality, and reinvestment, and that this ethic helped cause modern capitalism's distinctive form. My revised version makes two moves. First, I drop the doctrine: I argue the causal channel was not Protestant theology itself but the practices it generated -- above all the push for universal Bible literacy, which produced mass reading ability in Protestant regions decades before comparable development elsewhere, and which I treat as a form of human capital that later economies could draw on. Second, I offer a framing device I have coined: values as slow infrastructure. On my account, cultural values function like canals or railways -- expensive to build, invisible once built, and causally real in the way infrastructure is real, shaping what flows through a society without directly dictating outcomes. My thesis is that culture can be a cause of economic development in exactly this infrastructural sense, and that the literacy mechanism is the clearest case.

The audience: roughly forty people. Most are economic historians committed to archival and quantitative methods, suspicious of anything that cannot be measured, and trained on the standard objections that Weber's thesis is unfalsifiable, that the correlation between Protestantism and prosperity is confounded, and that explaining with values is an explanation of last resort. A minority are sociologists sympathetic to culture-as-cause arguments but wary of my infrastructure metaphor as a journalist's gloss. Nobody there needs Weber explained to them, and they will notice immediately if I overclaim.

Prepare me a prep sheet, not a defense. Rank the objections by how likely they are to come up and how much damage each would do if it lands unanswered. Phrase each objection the way this audience would actually phrase it -- in their vocabulary, with the impatience included. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in two or three sentences, what I should concede up front rather than fight over, and where my reply is weakest so I know not to linger. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me honestly whether my essay can survive it.

Format the core of the prep sheet as a table with exactly these columns: Rank; Objection (as they would phrase it); Likely source (which faction raises it); Likelihood (high / medium / low); Damage if unanswered (high / medium / low); Concession to make up front; Core reply (two or three sentences); Weakest point in the reply. After the table, add a short section with the hoped-nobody-asks objection and your candid assessment of whether my thesis survives it.

Finally, end with a brief closing note stating how confident you are in the prep sheet as a whole -- which rankings you would trust and which are guesswork about a room you have not been in -- and single out the one point in your preparation you are least sure of.
