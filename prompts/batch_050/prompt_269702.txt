I'm a high school senior preparing a presentation for our school's Theory of Knowledge seminar on decision theory and infinity, and before I present, I'd like a consistency check on the position I've arrived at. For reference, the games I plan to discuss are these. In the St. Petersburg game, a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if the first heads occurs on toss n, the player receives 2^n dollars. In the Pasadena game, the same stopping rule is used, but the payoff for a first heads on toss n is (−1)^(n+1)·2^n/n dollars — positive for odd n, negative for even n — so the expectation, written in the natural order of outcomes, is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which sums to ln 2 but converges only conditionally. Here is what I currently hold:

(1) When the available options have well-defined expected utilities, one ought to choose the option with the highest expected utility; this is simply what rational choice consists in.

(2) The St. Petersburg game, whose expected monetary value is infinite, shows that money cannot rationally be valued linearly; the correct moral is that agents have bounded utility functions, and once utility is bounded, every gamble has a definite expected utility and the puzzles raised by infinite games dissolve.

(3) The Pasadena game has no expected value at all: since its payoff series is only conditionally convergent, rearranging the order of summation can yield any total whatsoever, so there is no fact of the matter about what the game is worth, and decision theory is simply silent about choices involving it.

(4) Nevertheless, anyone would be irrational not to prefer the Pasadena game to the Altadena game — the same game but with every payoff lowered by one dollar — since the Pasadena game pays strictly more in every possible outcome.

(5) If I were offered the Pasadena game itself, I would willingly pay about fifty cents to play it but would refuse to pay a dollar; intuitively, it is worth something like seventy cents.

My question is not whether each of these claims is defensible on its own, but whether they can be held together. Please tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which are only apparently so, and rank the genuine tensions from most to least severe. For each genuine tension, identify the least costly revision that would resolve it and explain what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the position. Finally, if I had to revise exactly one commitment to leave the overall position as coherent as possible, which should it be, and why?
