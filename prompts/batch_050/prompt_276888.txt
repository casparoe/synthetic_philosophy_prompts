I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on information theory and inductive inference, where we've covered Kolmogorov complexity, Solomonoff's universal prior, and the minimum description length principle as its practical descendant. For my term project I've been asked to design a model-selection procedure for a hypothetical research lab that fits predictive models to noisy experimental data: the analysts there are competent statisticians but have no background in algorithmic information theory, and they choose between candidate models several times a week, with decisions ranging from low-stakes curve fits to choices that determine which experiment gets funded next quarter.

Write the procedure for them, operationalizing Occam's razor in a way that is faithful to what the theory actually says rather than to the slogan that simpler is better. It should be a sequence of steps an analyst can run in under an hour, and it needs to grapple with the following, which our lectures flagged as the places where naive operationalizations go wrong:

First, Solomonoff induction is provably uncomputable, and complexity depends on the choice of reference machine, so the procedure has to say which practically available surrogate for description length it uses (two-part MDL codes, parameter-count penalties like AIC and BIC, cross-validation) and when each surrogate is and isn't a defensible stand-in — in particular, when the constant terms that theory says can be ignored actually dominate at realistic sample sizes.

Second, the theory delivers a probability distribution over predictions, not a verdict that one model is the true or best one, so the procedure must say what it is legitimate to conclude at the end, and what to do when the lab's question is explanatory (which mechanism generated the data?) rather than predictive.

Third, which theoretical distinctions have to survive the translation into practice, and which can be dropped without much loss — I'd like you to defend your choices here rather than stipulate them.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) choosing between a cubic and a quartic polynomial on thirty noisy data points; (b) choosing between a well-motivated mechanistic model with six free parameters and an uninterpretable but slightly more compressive black-box model, where the analyst strongly prefers the mechanistic model and the procedure must be allowed to disappoint them — show what it actually outputs and how the analyst should report that result honestly; and (c) a case where the two candidate models are not nested and the sample size is small enough that the standard surrogates disagree.

Close with a frank section on what the procedure cannot do: where it degenerates into judgment dressed up as arithmetic, and whether anything in the framework itself justifies preferring simplicity, given the no-free-lunch-style worry that the universal prior is only privileged relative to a choice of language.
