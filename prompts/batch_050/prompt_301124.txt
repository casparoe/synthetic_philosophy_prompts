I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next week I'm interviewing the author of a new book arguing that Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE picture language should be revived as the standard medium for public statistics in the smartphone era: his claim is that a rigorously standardized pictorial grammar would make data on health, wages, and climate legible to everyone regardless of literacy, numeracy, or native language. Draft exactly six interview questions as a numbered list, with no introduction or conclusion, each item a sentence or two containing the question plus a follow-up keyed to the reply my guest is most likely to give. The questions should press where the position is genuinely vulnerable rather than invite him to restate it: whether pictograms are really less conventional than words or merely conventional in ways users notice less; how a picture language could express negation, modality, and uncertainty intervals rather than just quantities; whether a medium whose persuasiveness depends on concealing its own design choices can survive Neurath's scruples about transparency and democratic debate; who gets to sit on the standardization committee and why that isn't just technocracy by other means; what his system does with contested categories like unemployment or poverty, where the definition is the politics; and what empirical finding about real-world comprehension of pictorial charts would lead him to abandon the project.
