'Russell's insight was that definite descriptions — phrases like "the tallest building in Chicago" or his own example, the present King of France — only succeed in referring when there is exactly one object satisfying them. When nothing satisfies the description, any sentence containing it is neither true nor false but simply meaningless, since there is no object for the words to attach themselves to. Ordinary proper names, he argued, are in fact disguised definite descriptions: for most speakers, a name like "Aristotle" just abbreviates something along the lines of "the pupil of Plato who tutored Alexander", which explains how we can use names intelligibly even when we are wrong about whether their bearers existed.
