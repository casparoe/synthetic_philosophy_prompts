In my political philosophy seminar we reached Nozick’s entitlement theory and the Wilt Chamberlain example, and a classmate pressed what sounded like a decisive point: if a million people each freely hand over a small sum to watch Chamberlain play, then the resulting concentration of wealth is just, and any later attempt to tax part of it for schools, vaccines, or flood defenses is simply the state confiscating what arose from uncoerced choices. She added that complaints about bargaining power, inherited advantage, monopoly positions, or the fact that some people must sell labor while others merely collect payments are morally beside the point, because envy of a voluntarily produced pattern cannot turn an entitlement into a liability.

I am trying to formulate the exam question this raises: once Nozick has shown that liberty upsets patterns, why is he not committed to the stronger claim that every sequence of nominally voluntary transfers is self-ratifying, so that even a society drifting toward private government by a few heirs or celebrities would have to be called just provided each contract along the way was signed? In other words, does the Chamberlain case prove that end-state and patterned principles are not merely unstable but illegitimate in principle, or is there a mistake in moving from the innocence of individual transactions to the justice of the whole distribution and the institutions that grow out of it?
