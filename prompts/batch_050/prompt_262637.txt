A few months ago a doctor gave me a statistic, and the longer I have thought about it the less I understand what it meant. The number was supposed to be my chance of a particular medical outcome over the next ten years, but it changed depending on which facts about me were used. Count only my age and sex and you get one figure; add my family history and you get a second; add a genetic result and you get a third. Each figure, as far as I can tell, is an honest frequency drawn from real data. What I cannot find is any fact about which of them is my probability. Once I noticed this I started seeing the same shape of problem everywhere. An insurer prices my policy by placing me in a pool, but there are dozens of pools I belong to and they imply different prices. A judge considering bail reads that people with a certain record reoffend at a certain rate, but the defendant also belongs to narrower and broader groups with different rates. A forecaster says there is a 70 percent chance of rain tomorrow, yet tomorrow happens exactly once.

I understand that this difficulty has a name and a literature, but I do not want a survey of proposed answers yet. Before I read anyone's solution, I want to know what I should be demanding of one. Write the desiderata: what any adequate rule for selecting the relevant group — and hence the relevant frequency — for a single case must deliver. For each requirement, give the concrete case that motivates it. The list should cover at least: nested groups where each narrower one shifts the number; the narrowest group having almost no data behind it; two groups that overlap without either containing the other; the answer apparently depending on how the case happens to be described; and genuinely novel events that belong to no measured group at all. It should also say whether a solution must be usable by someone who actually has to act, and whether it must explain why adding more information sometimes makes an estimate worse rather than better.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really preferences of one school of thought. Flag any pair or subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a hunch that "use all the information you have" and "trust only frequencies from large samples" pull against each other, and if that tension is genuine I want it stated plainly. Say which single requirement eliminates the most naive answers. Then, briefly, assess the few candidate rules I have managed to articulate on my own against the list: always use the narrowest group you have data on; always use the broadest group with reliable statistics; use everything at once, weighted somehow; and the deflationary option that there is no fact of the matter and probability exists only relative to a chosen model. The standard I care about is whether the rule could justify a decision to the person the number is about — someone who, like me, was handed a figure and asked to live by it.
