Something that's been nagging me since a philosophy of mind seminar last term: the "problem of other minds" seems to have died rather than been solved. Like, in the first half of the twentieth century it was a live question -- Mill's argument from analogy, Russell taking it seriously, then Wittgenstein-adjacent people (Strawson with his point that you can't have the concept of a person without applying mental predicates to others on the same terms as yourself, Malcolm, Austin) arguing the skeptic's question was somehow malformed. And then... everyone just stopped writing about it? Nobody announced a solution. Now the same anxiety shows up dressed differently: zombies in the consciousness literature, "how would we even know" debates about whether animals or AI systems feel anything. So the topic is everywhere but the original debate is nowhere.

My half-baked theory is that the disagreement was never really one disagreement. The epistemic version (given that other people are minded, what justifies my belief in any particular case?) seems answerable -- analogy, inference to the best explanation, whatever, it's just induction with extra steps. But there's a conceptual version (how can I so much as *form* the thought of a mind that isn't mine, if my grip on mental concepts is essentially first-personal?) which is much weirder, and I suspect the Wittgensteinians "won" against the wrong opponent, or the two camps were talking past each other until everyone got bored. And now the empirical turn -- theory-theory vs simulation theory vs "direct perception" of others' emotions -- might be a third debate wearing the same name, one that cognitive science could actually settle, which would prove it was never the philosophical problem.

So: is my diagnosis right, and if not, what kind of disagreement was it actually? Verbal, substantive, a dissolved pseudo-problem, or a real one that got abandoned for sociological reasons rather than argumentative ones? I'd like you to ground it in what the sides actually said rather than textbook summaries, and tell me what would count as progress now -- a distinction, an experimental result, a worked-out account of how mental concepts are acquired, or an argument that nothing could count as progress because the question was ill-posed from the start. Also, tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis -- e.g. what's the strongest piece of evidence that the old debate *was* substantively resolved and I just haven't seen the resolution, or that the epistemic/conceptual split I'm leaning on doesn't survive contact with the texts? That's where I figure your reasoning is most likely to be wrong, so be honest about it rather than confident.
