A friend of mine who's into Buddhism-adjacent moral philosophy has this view she calls tranquilism, and she walked me through an argument from it that seems to land somewhere awful. She states the view like this: an experience only matters morally insofar as it involves craving, distress, or suffering -- pleasure on top of a fully content, craving-free state adds literally nothing. From there:

(1) The value of an experience consists entirely in the craving or suffering it relieves or prevents; a being with no unmet cravings is not made even slightly better off by extra pleasure.

(2) So the value of an outcome is measured entirely by the craving and suffering absent from it.

(3) Beings that don't exist have no cravings and suffer nothing.

(4) So no outcome is improved by adding happy beings, and an empty world has nothing bad in it at all -- it's tied for best.

(5) Any actual life contains at least some craving (hunger, boredom, thwarted plans, grief), so creating a being always makes things worse then not creating it.

(6) And painlessly ending a contented life removes all that person's future cravings, and since there's no one left to experience frustration, the person's desire to keep living isn't frustrated either.

(7) So instant, painless, universal extinction would be morally ideal.

She bites the bullet on (7), which I refuse to do. But when I push on (1) she just repeats the bit about the contented meditator, and honestly I find that part compelling. I'd rather keep (1) than accept (7). Tell me which step to attack, why it looked innocent, and what she'll say back when I attack it -- plus what each of the other premises would cost me to deny instead.
