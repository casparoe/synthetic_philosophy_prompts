You are a philosopher specialising in formal epistemology and the epistemology of financial markets, with a working command of the no-trade literature (Aumann on agreeing to disagree, the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade results, and the Grossman–Stiglitz observation that prices cannot be fully revealing if information is costly). I would like you to help me translate that body of theory into something a non-philosopher can actually use. Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming section identifying which theoretical distinctions genuinely matter for practice and which can be safely dropped, before you write the procedure itself.

I sit on a three-person pre-trade review panel at a mid-sized asset manager. Every week, analysts bring us roughly five trade proposals in which the central claim is, in one form or another, that the market has priced something wrongly: a share is undervalued, a bond spread is too wide, a currency will move when a consensus forecast says it will not. Our job is to approve, reject, or send back for more work. We have about thirty minutes per proposal, we are generalists rather than sector specialists, and our sign-off is required before capital is committed. At present we have no shared standard: some panellists ask whether the analysis is thorough, others ask whether they personally find the thesis persuasive, and we suspect both of these are the wrong questions.

Please write us a decision procedure, in words, that an analyst's proposal must pass through before approval, grounded in what the no-trade theorems and the surrounding epistemology of markets actually establish. In particular, the procedure should operationalise questions such as the following, in a sensible order: what information or model do we believe we have that is not already impounded in the price; who is on the other side of this trade and what might they know; would we still want the trade if we assumed the counterparty was at least as rational and at least as well informed as we are; is the apparent mispricing perhaps the price of the information itself (the Grossman–Stiglitz point) rather than a free lunch; and is the thesis in fact a disagreement about values, risk appetite, or time horizon masquerading as a disagreement about facts.

For each question in the sequence, state what would count as a sufficient answer to proceed, what constitutes a stopping point where the proposal is rejected outright, and at which junctures the procedure hands the decision back to human judgement because the question is one of values or risk tolerance rather than of epistemic standing. Be explicit about which distinctions from the theory you have preserved in translation and which you have deliberately sacrificed for usability, and say where you expect the procedure to misfire.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals:

1. An analyst argues that a large, heavily researched index constituent is overvalued by thirty per cent, on the basis of a discounted-cash-flow model whose assumptions differ from the sell-side consensus.
2. An analyst proposes buying a thinly traded small-cap share after the company published a routine regulatory filing which, the analyst argues, the market has not yet digested.
3. An analyst proposes shorting the bonds of a firm whose management our analyst has met privately and found evasive, on the grounds that other investors have not had this access.

Ensure that at least one of these applications ends in a rejection that the proposing desk will find unwelcome, and explain why the procedure bites there. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of trade thesis it will systematically mishandle, and what institutional safeguards you would place around it to compensate.
