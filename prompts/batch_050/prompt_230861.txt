A friend and I spent last night arguing about punishment: she thinks my skepticism about free will commits me to Gregg Caruso's position that criminals should be handled the way we handle carriers of dangerous diseases -- isolated while dangerous, never made to suffer because they deserve it -- while I think something like Dennett's stance, that desert-talk is mostly confused but that blame and sanction practices can still earn their keep, is genuinely different from hers and better. For our reading group next week, write a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on the justification of criminal punishment. Start from a commitment anyone in the room accepts (that the state may sometimes imprison people) and branch on diagnostic questions phrased so that no knowledge of the labels is needed: whether anyone could deserve suffering in itself, as opposed to suffering being a regrettable means; whether the answer to that changes if determinism is true; whether a known-harmless convict should still be punished, and why; whether the quarantine analogy -- detention of the dangerous-but-innocent -- really transfers to the guilty; and whether the practice's legitimacy turns on the truth of compatibilism. Each leaf should be a named position (retributivism, deterrence-based consequentialism, the quarantine or public-health model, Dennett-style revisionism, and abolitionism) with one sentence on its most uncomfortable implication. Where two leaves can't actually be separated by any question, flag the pair and say what further question would be needed. If some combination of answers is coherent but unoccupied, note it in a sentence -- we want the tree built from the logical space, not from the dramatis personae.
