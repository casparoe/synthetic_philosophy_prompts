I have no academic training in philosophy or logic, but a couple of years ago I picked up a well-known book that weaves together formal systems, art, and music, and I have been turning over one of its early puzzles ever since. In the puzzle, you are given the string MI and a handful of rules for producing new strings -- you may append a U to any string ending in I, double whatever follows an M, replace three consecutive I's with a U, and delete any double U -- and you are asked to derive MU. You can push strings around inside the system indefinitely and never arrive at MU, and the way you actually settle the matter is by stepping back and noticing that every derivable string contains a number of I's that is never divisible by three, while MU contains zero. The book's moral, as I understand it, is that minds can do what rule-bound systems cannot: jump out of the system and see it from above. I have drafted a short essay for an online forum pushing back on that moral, and I would like committed comments before I post it.

Here is the heart of the draft. The lesson of the MU-puzzle is usually stated too quickly, my essay says. Noticing the invariant about I's feels like transcendence, but it is itself a perfectly mechanical procedure: one could program a machine to count symbols, test candidate invariants, and halt when it finds one. So what looks like jumping out of the system is really just switching into a larger system whose rules one then follows every bit as slavishly. And, the essay continues, the deflation cannot stop halfway: if the first jump is just rule-following at a higher level, then so is the jump that observes the first jump, and there is no final perch outside all systems from which the choosing gets done. The essay concludes that the MU-puzzle teaches humility rather than transcendence -- the difference between a person and a string-pushing machine is not that the person can leave the system, but that neither can, and the person is simply better at not noticing this.

What I want from you is not a balanced survey of possible reactions but a verdict. Tell me plainly whether the essay's central inference holds: does it follow, from the fact that spotting the invariant can be mechanized, that there is no genuine outside-view? If it does not follow, identify the single weakest step in my reasoning and say what additional premise would be needed to save the conclusion -- or tell me honestly that the conclusion should be reversed, that something about the capacity to choose which system to inhabit survives the deflation, and defend that position against my regress argument. Also flag any place where I have misstated the mechanics of the puzzle itself, since I am working from memory and would rather be corrected now than in the comments section. Take a side and give me your reasons; I would rather rewrite the essay around a strong objection than publish it insulated from one.
