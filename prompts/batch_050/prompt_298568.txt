Hey, so I'm a PhD student in environmental ethics and I've been roped into running a session of our department's "philosophy for policy people" workshop series. It's aimed at Masters students from public policy, law, a couple of scientists, mostly people who have never taken a philosophy class. About 18 participants, 75 minutes, one room with movable tables.

I want to build the whole session around geoengineering, specifically the interplay between moral hazard and termination shock in stratospheric aerosol injection. Everyone in policy circles has heard these terms but I think they process them as abstract bullet points. I want them to *feel* it: the way cheap cooling quietly eats the political will to cut emissions, and the way a system can end up locked into spraying forever because stopping has become unsurvivable.

Act as an experienced facilitator of philosophy games and simulations -- someone who has run things like climate negotiation games and iterated on them many times. Think carefully and step by step: before giving me the final design, brainstorm 2-3 possible core mechanics (in a few sentences each) and say which you'd pick and why, then give the full design for your pick.

Concretely I need:

- A game/simulation the 18 of them actually play, with roles, rules, materials (I have index cards, tokens, a whiteboard, no budget), and a simple point or payoff structure that works without real money. They should not be told up front what the session is "about" beyond climate policy.
- The structure should force the trap to emerge from their own decisions: I want a moment where the group collectively realizes they've become dependent on the aerosol program and that whoever controls the off-switch now has enormous power over everyone else. Ideally someone at the table ends up *being* the person with their hand on the switch and feels how weird that is.
- A debrief plan (maybe 15 min?) that maps specific things that happened in the game onto moral hazard, termination shock, and the governance/"who decides" problem -- including questions to ask that surface what they did, not a lecture.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g. someone games the mechanics, the injection option is too obviously good or too obviously bad, one loud player dominates the negotiation) and how to fix or prevent each.
- An honest note on what the game *can't* capture -- I assume e.g. the real-world timescales, or the fact that real termination shock kills people rather than deducting points -- and what I should say explicitly in the debrief to fill that gap.

One more constraint: several participants are from countries that would plausibly be harmed or at least unevenly affected by real SAI deployment, so the design should make the distributional/unilateral-deployment angle do real work rather than treating the world as one undifferentiated player. Oh and keep the math simple enough that nobody needs a calculator, I definately don't want the session to turn into an accounting exercise.
