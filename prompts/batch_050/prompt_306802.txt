I'm a retired biochemist (enzyme kinetics, mostly) who picked up philosophy about two years ago, and I've landed on a position I want stress-tested before I get too attached to it.

Here's my position. I've read Fodor's old book on modularity (the early-80s one) and then some of the evolutionary psychology crowd, and I've ended up somewhere near massive modularity: the mind is mostly a collection of special-purpose computational systems, shaped by selection to handle recurrent problems — cheater detection, face recognition, folk physics, language acquisition, that kind of thing — and there isn't much of a general-purpose, content-neutral reasoner sitting on top of them. Central cognition, on my view, is far less "general intelligence" than the traditional picture assumes. The appeal for me is partly Fodor's own argument the other way: if central systems really were modular, we'd have to solve the frame problem, and nobody has, so maybe the search for a unified account of abductive reasoning is just misguided. It also rhymes with what I saw in biology my whole career: nature builds kludgy dedicated machinery, not elegant general solutions.

What I want is a map of what would count against this position. Specifically:

1. What evidence would actually be decisive? I keep hearing two lines from critics: neuroplasticity (kids recovering language after early left-hemisphere damage, say) and cultural variation in cognition. Are these genuinely damaging to massive modularity, or does the position have principled ways to absorb them — and if it absorbs everything, is that a warning sign in itself?
2. What would defenders themselves concede as a problem? I don't just want the critics' greatest hits, I want the admissions. E.g., would a serious massive modularity person agree that if it turned out there is a domain-general working memory system doing real inferential work, the thesis has to shrink? Where's the line between "shrink a bit" and "collapse into the old faculty-psychology picture with extra steps"?
3. What does the view predict that its rivals (one big general learner, or a few domain-general systems) don't? In my old field a theory that predicted nothing distinct was dead on arrival. Does massive modularity have risky predictions — say, about what kinds of cognitive deficits should dissociate, or what AI architectures built on pure domain-general learning should be incapable of?
4. And honestly: has the position been qualified into near-unfalsifiability? When every apparent counterexample gets handled by "the module is informationally encapsulated but its output is available centrally" or "the adaptation was for the ancestral environment, not this one," I start to get the same itch I got reading certain kinds of adaptationist just-so stories back in the day. If the view is insulated, is that actually a problem for a thesis of this kind, or is some insulation just what mature frameworks look like?

I'm inclined to think the position is right but overclaimed in its popular versions, so arguments that target the strong version while leaving a defensible core would move me most. Also, please tell me if I've muddled Fodor's actual position with the evolutionary psychologists' version — I suspect I've conflated at least one distinction there.
