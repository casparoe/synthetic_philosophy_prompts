I serve on the five-person grants committee of a family foundation, and at our winter meeting we have to decide whether to launch a "civilizational resilience" program — roughly a third of our endowment over ten years, aimed at reducing the risk of human extinction — or instead put that money into our existing global health grants, where it would reliably prevent a large number of deaths in the next decade. One of my fellow trustees has circulated a memo arguing that the choice is easy: extinction is simply a very large number of deaths plus the grief of survivors, so the only question is which program prevents more death per dollar, and on that metric our health grants win going away. I suspect something is wrong with that framing, and at dinner last week I pressed him with a scenario: imagine that humanity becomes universally infertile, so no one dies prematurely and everyone alive today gets to live out their full natural lifespan, but there will be no one after us. My intuition is that this would be a catastrophe — that people would find their projects, their life's work, even ordinary acts of care strangely drained of point — even though, by construction, not a single extra death occurs. He bit the bullet and said the scenario involves no loss of value at all beyond the foregone lives of people who, since they never exist, cannot be wronged. Please act as a philosopher retained to advise the committee and draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting.

The opinion should recommend a concrete course of action on the program (fund it, reject it, or fund a modified version — your call, but commit to one), and its reasoning should do three things. First, take a position on whether human extinction is bad in ways that go beyond the deaths and the non-existence it involves: consider separately the loss of the people who would have existed, the loss of everything humanity would have gone on to do, and the damage extinction does retrospectively to the meaning of what has already been done and built, and say which of these you think carries real weight and which are confused. Second, engage the infertility scenario directly: say what it does and does not show, since my colleague will fairly object that intuitions about a world with no future may reflect facts about human motivation rather than facts about value. Third, give my colleague's position its strongest run — the worry that "extinction is bad beyond the deaths" is an elusive claim that dissolves on inspection, and that even if it isn't, such abstract losses shouldn't outrank concrete suffering we can prevent now — and answer it rather than talking past it.

Think carefully through the argument before drafting. The opinion itself should be 700–900 words, written for intelligent non-philosophers, and it should lead with the recommendation in its first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating of the charge, no concluding throat-clearing. Avoid jargon, and where a technical notion is genuinely needed (the non-identity problem, for instance, if you think it matters here), explain it in a sentence as you go.
