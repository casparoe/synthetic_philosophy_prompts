So my partner roped me into this interfaith reading group a few months ago, and last week the topic was medieval Islamic thought and science. I wanted to not be totally lost, so beforehand I read a chapter on al-Ghazali in some popular history of philosophy book I grabbed at a used bookstore, focusing on the causation discussion in his Incoherence of the Philosophers. Now I keep repeating its claims at people (including, awkwardly, at the group, where one guy who actually studies this stuff got visibly annoyed but didn't explain why). Here's how the chapter put it:

'Al-Ghazali held that nothing in nature truly causes anything else. When fire meets cotton, the fire does not burn the cotton -- God burns it, directly, on the occasion of the contact. His reasoning anticipated Hume by centuries: we never observe causation itself, only one event following another, so belief in necessary connection has no rational foundation. And because no genuine causal necessities exist, absolutely anything could happen at any moment -- cotton could refuse to burn, the dead could rise. This is exactly what he wanted, since it makes miracles unproblematic, and it is why many historians hold him partly responsible for choking off scientific inquiry in the Islamic world. For al-Ghazali, God is the sole real agent; what we call causes are merely God's habits, which He may suspend at will.'

Something about the Hume comparison feels too neat to me, and the "anything could happen at any moment" bit seems like it would make everyday life unlivable rather than pious, but I can't articulate what's wrong with it. Can you audit this summary for me? Which parts are an accurate statement of al-Ghazali's position, which parts are the kind of simplification a specialist would grudgingly accept at a dinner party, and which parts are actually wrong or unfair? And separately: the book's big takeaway is that his view is basically proto-Humean skepticism deployed to defend miracles, with the decline of Islamic science as the downstream cost. Does that takeaway survive once the summary is corrected, or does it depend on the dodgy parts?
