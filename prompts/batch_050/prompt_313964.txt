After a long argument with a friend about when we are justified in crediting animals with understanding, I want to settle my own position by working through cases, in the spirit of Lloyd Morgan's canon from An Introduction to Comparative Psychology — roughly, the directive not to explain a behavior by appeal to higher mental faculties when a lower-faculty explanation will do. My gut verdicts: a border collie that reliably fetches the specific toy I name from another room — fine to say it knows which toy is which; a pigeon trained to peck a red key for food — not fine to say it understands the task, only that it learned a response; a crow that places nuts at a crosswalk and waits for the light to change — fine to call it planning; an octopus that opens a jar after watching a demonstrator do so — fine to say it learned by observation; my dog coming over when I cry — I call it genuine concern, though it could plausibly be conditioning; a parrot that says a phrase requesting a grape and receives grapes — not fine to call that language. The crow and the pigeon are what my friend and I fought over: both behaviors are learned contingencies, yet I treat one as planning and the other as mere conditioning. Is there a precise principle — specifying what kind of evidence licenses which cognitive attribution — that captures all of these verdicts? If not, state the simplest principle that captures most of them, identify which verdict it forces me to abandon, and say what it would predict for a case neither of us discussed: an AI-free robot that navigates home after being carried somewhere new.
