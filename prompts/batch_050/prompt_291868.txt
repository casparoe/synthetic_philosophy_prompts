Last month I sat in on a hospital ethics committee meeting (my partner's an oncologist, I mostly tag along for the free coffee and then get way too invested) and they've been arguing about whether to approve a trial randomizing patients between standard chemo and standard chemo plus a newer targeted drug. A couple of the senior physicians privately said they already think the combo is probably better for most patients, but since the field overall is still split, they figure enrollment is fine under "clinical equipoise." That struck me as weird. The justification for putting patients on a coin flip apparently isn't that *you* don't know what's best -- it's that the profession as a whole hasn't converged. I kept thinking: why does my moral permission to randomize depend on what some oncologist in another state happens to believe? And then someone pointed out the flip side -- if we waited for every individual doctor to be personally 50/50, almost no trial would ever run, and we'd never get the evidence that settles anything.

So here's what I want: your credence that the following claim is true -- "genuine disagreement or uncertainty in the relevant expert community is a necessary, non-redundant ethical condition for permissibly enrolling patients in a randomized trial; individual-physician uncertainty is not required, and mere net-positive expected value of the trial (to future patients) is not by itself sufficient." I'd like it decomposed, with a rough number and a sentence of justification for each sub-question: (a) how likely is it that a coherent state of "community uncertainty" actually exists, as opposed to being a fuzzy aggregate that's just an average of individuals who each lean one way; (b) how likely is it that the concept survives contact with a Bayesian picture, where uncertainty shrinks continuously as data accumulate and there's no natural point where equipoise "breaks" and the trial must stop; (c) how likely is it that equipoise is really just a proxy doing stand-in work for something else -- non-exploitation, acceptable risk to subjects -- such that if you built the trial rules directly on those, equipoise would add nothing; and (d) any other term you think the credence actually depends on. Then combine the terms, tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to, and tell me what evidence or argument (empirical or philosophical) would move that term most.

Please lead with your bottom-line credence in the first sentence, then the decomposition and reasoning -- no preamble, no restating my setup back to me.
