I've been arguing with a friend about when something outside your skull counts as part of your thinking, and I realized I should just write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and ask what principle, if any, unifies them. Here they are. My grandmother kept a small address book on her person at all times, flipped to it automatically whenever she needed a number, and trusted it completely; I want to say she genuinely knew those phone numbers in roughly the way she knew her own birthday, even between consultations. The same information sitting in a reference book at the library across town, which she could consult if she made a special trip, I don't count as something she knew. A calculator she used every day at her shop: I'd say the arithmetic happened partly in the calculator, not just in her head, but I don't feel the same pull toward saying she *believed* the numbers on its display before she looked. Facts she memorized in school but hadn't recalled in decades still seem like things she knew; entries in a notebook she owned but never once opened do not. And here's where I notice my own inconsistency: I'm inclined to say the contents of my smartphone, which is faster to consult and more accurate than her address book ever was, are *not* part of my mind in the way her book was part of hers, and I can't articulate why, beyond the fact that the information in it was written by strangers and she wrote hers herself -- though I also think that if she had copied an entry down wrong, she believed the wrong number she wrote, not the true one she meant to write.

What I'd like is a principle -- or a short list of candidate principles -- that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that each clause can be tied to the case that motivates it (something like conditions on availability, automaticity of use, prior endorsement, or authorship, but I want you to find the right conditions rather than adopt my guesses). Please be explicit about which of my verdicts your best principle has to overturn, and defend the overturning rather than hiding it -- I suspect the smartphone case is where I have to give something up, but I'd rather be shown that than have it smoothed over. Then apply the winning principle to three cases I haven't listed: a shared shopping list that my wife and I both edit, where I've never read some of her entries; a smartwatch that taps my wrist to remind me of appointments without my ever consciously rehearsing them; and a retired colleague who has donated his entire library but remembers exactly which book contained each piece of information. Finally, tell me what the principle implies about whether the boundary in question is one that can shift over the course of an afternoon -- say, when I leave my phone at home -- and whether that consequence should count against the principle or is actually a point in its favor.
