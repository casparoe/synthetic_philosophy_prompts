I'm in a decision theory reading group, and for three sessions now we've been stuck on the Death in Damascus problem, and I'd like a diagnosis of our impasse rather than another verdict on the case itself. The setup, as we use it: a man encounters Death in Damascus, who tells him they have an appointment tomorrow. Death is an extremely reliable predictor of where people will be, and the man can either stay in Damascus or flee overnight to Aleppo -- but whichever he chooses, he must reckon with the fact that Death wrote down his location yesterday, and Death is almost never wrong. Causal decision theory seems to recommend fleeing (or staying) on standard dominance-style grounds, yet the man knows, before he acts, that whatever he ends up choosing is very probably the choice Death anticipated -- so he knows in advance that the recommendation he follows will likely be the one that kills him. One faction in our group holds that this is a devastating structural defect in CDT: a theory whose advice the agent can know, ex ante, will turn out badly is self-effacing in a way no adequate theory should be. The other faction insists the case shows nothing about CDT at all -- the apparent pathology is entirely an artifact of building a perfect predictor into the example, and any theory short of outright fatalism will look "disappointed" in such an environment, so the right response is to refuse the intuition pump rather than revise the theory.

To make things worse, we introduced a variant, and it split the room further. In our variant, the man can pay a small fee to enter a "commitment registry": his choice is recorded and sealed before Death makes the prediction, and Death must then predict based on the registry entry. Half the group thinks this trivially dissolves the puzzle (of course you register; it converts the problem into Newcomb-with-transparent-boxes and everyone agrees what to do there), while the other half thinks the variant actually exposes the real issue in the original case -- namely, whether rational choice is about causing good outcomes or about being the kind of agent whose choices are good news -- and that the two factions in the original dispute were implicitly disagreeing about this all along without noticing.

My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the stall isn't really about decision rules at all: it's a disagreement about what a decision theory is for -- whether it's a standard that must guide deliberation from the inside (in which case ex ante foreseeability of failure is damning) or a criterion of rightness assessed from outside (in which case predictability is irrelevant) -- and that both sides keep producing arguments that are valid only under their own conception. Am I right about that, or is there a genuine substantive dispute underneath that could in principle be settled? I'd like a diagnosis grounded in what each position actually commits to: is this a verbal disagreement about "rationality," a disagreement about which features of a case are legitimate idealizations, or bedrock intuitions about agency that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress -- a formal result, a sharper distinction, a stipulated restriction on predictor cases, a change of question -- or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and explain why not.
