A friend in my philosophy of biology reading group ran this argument past me last week and it's been bugging me, because the conclusion seems nuts but I cant find the bad step. (1) A genuine explanation of some outcome has to cite something you could in principle identify or measure without already knowing that outcome -- otherwise you're in dormitive-power territory, where opium puts people to sleep because it has sleep-inducing power. (2) On the propensity interpretation, an organism's fitness just is its propensity to survive and reproduce in its environment. (3) But a propensity is only ever detectable through its manifestations: the only way to tell something has a propensity to do X is to see it do X, or to see things relevantly like it do X. (4) So the only evidence for an organism's fitness is actual survival and reproductive success, its own or that of organisms with the same traits. (5) So when we say the deeper-beaked finches survived the drought because they were fitter, we're citing something whose only evidence is the very survival being explained -- dormitive power all over again. (6) So on the propensity interpretation, fitness explanations are all circular, and the view was supposed to be the fix for the old tautology objection about survival of the fittest, not a repackaging of it. (7) So the propensity interpretation fails, and fitness has to be defined some other way. But (7) can't be right -- biologists use fitness to explain and predict things constantly, and the propensity reading is the standard answer to the tautology charge. Which step do I reject? And whatever you pick, tell me what rejecting it costs -- e.g. if I ditch (1) do I have to swallow the dormitive-power explanation as legitimate? Please actually commit to an answer: name the step, explain why it looked plausible, defend your rejection against the obvious comeback, and give a straight verdict on whether the propensity interpretation survives, rather than just laying out the menu of options and leaving me to choose.
