I have an old college friend who went on to become a metaphysician, and over the years I've kept a running record of her verdicts on whether famous philosophical disputes are merely verbal. Here is the track record as I have it:

1. The dispute between someone who says there is a table in the room and someone who says there are only simples arranged tablewise: merely verbal.
2. The dispute between two physicists over whether spacetime has ten dimensions or eleven: substantive, not verbal.
3. The dispute between a Platonist and a nominalist over whether the number two exists, where both parties fully agree about all of mathematics and its applications: merely verbal.
4. The dispute over whether a fetus is a person, between two parties who agree on every biological and psychological fact: substantive, not verbal.
5. The dispute over whether the statue and the lump of clay constituting it are one object or two: merely verbal.
6. The dispute over whether determinism is true: substantive, not verbal.

Now she has been presented with a new case. Two contemporary metaphysicians agree on every physical, chemical, and mathematical fact. Both explicitly decline to rest anything on ordinary English usage; each claims instead to be speaking a language whose quantifier expresses the one fundamental, joint-carving sense of "there is," and in that language one says there exist only mereological simples while the other says there also exist composite objects. Each insists the dispute is about what fundamentally exists, not about how anyone talks.

Predict her verdict on the new case and justify the prediction. In doing so: (a) lay out at least two rules that each fit all six of her earlier verdicts but diverge on this case -- for instance, a rule on which a dispute is verbal whenever the parties would accept a common dispute-free redescription (with perhaps a bedrock exception), versus a rule on which a dispute can be substantive without any first-order disagreement provided one side's vocabulary carves nature better; (b) say which of the earlier verdicts is the closest analog to the new case and why; (c) describe the further test case, or the further question you could put to her, that would most efficiently tell the competing rules apart. Finally, state how confident you are in your prediction overall, and single out the single point in your reasoning where you are least sure you have her view right.
