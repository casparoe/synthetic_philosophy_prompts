I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and I am grading responses to the following exam question. Below I reproduce the question, then one student's answer in full. Please read both carefully and grade the answer.

Exam question:

In "Quining Qualia" (1988), Daniel Dennett writes:

"My claim, then, is not just that the various technical or theoretical concepts of qualia are vague or equivocal, but that the source concept, the 'pretheoretical' notion of which the former are presumed to be refinements, is so thoroughly confused that even if we undertook to salvage some 'lowest common denominator' from the theoreticians' proposals, any acceptable version would have to be so radically unlike the ill-formed notions that are commonly appealed to that it would be tactically obtuse — not to say Pickwickian — to cling to the term. Far better, tactically, to declare that there simply are no qualia at all."

(a) State, in your own words, the exact scope of Dennett's claim: what is he denying, and what does he explicitly grant? (b) Explain what he means by calling continued use of the term "Pickwickian," and why he thinks even a salvaged lowest-common-denominator concept would not deserve the name "qualia." (c) Is Dennett's conclusion a substantive thesis about consciousness or only a tactical recommendation about vocabulary? Defend an answer, and say what turns on the distinction.

Student's answer:

(a) Dennett's target is broader than it might look. He is not merely saying that philosophers' technical definitions of qualia are muddled or that different theorists mean different things by the word; his claim concerns the everyday, pretheoretical idea of which all the technical definitions are supposed to be refinements. He grants that conscious experience is real and that it has properties — indeed, properties in virtue of which an experience has the content it does, so that experiencing something as red rather than green is true in virtue of something happening in the subject at the time. What he denies is only that any of these properties are "special" in the way qualia are supposed to be special. So the denial is narrower than a denial of experience itself, but wider than a quibble about jargon.

(b) "Pickwickian" means using a word in a sense so stretched or idiosyncratic that it survives only by the speaker's private charity rather than by any continuity with ordinary meaning. Dennett's point is that any cleaned-up concept one might rescue from the wreckage — the lowest common denominator of the theorists' proposals — would be so unlike what people actually mean when they talk about the way things seem to them that keeping the old word for it would trade on the very confusion being exposed. Declaring that there are no qualia is therefore more honest than redefining the term into respectability. Dennett's impatience with the opposing attitude comes through in the retort he quotes with approval from Louis Armstrong, on being asked what jazz is: if you have to ask, you are never going to know. For Dennett, the person who insists that everyone already knows perfectly well what qualia are has thereby conceded that the concept cannot bear examination.

(c) The conclusion is substantive as well as tactical. The recommendation to abandon the word is justified only by the prior claim that the source concept is thoroughly confused, and that claim is either true or false of our actual concept — it is not itself a matter of nomenclature. So the terminological advice rides on a substantive premise, and if the premise failed (if the everyday notion were merely vague but coherent) the advice would lapse. That said, the substantive premise cannot in the end be rationally established. Dennett himself concedes that rigorous arguments work only on well-defined materials, which is why he proceeds through intuition pumps such as the Chase and Sanborn coffee-tasters case rather than formal proof. But rigorous argument is the only thing that could settle a question of this generality, so the alleged thoroughgoing confusion of the pretheoretical notion remains, at bottom, one of Dennett's own intuitions — and the distinction between his substantive thesis and his tactical recommendation accordingly collapses.
