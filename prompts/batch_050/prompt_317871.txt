The controversy over computer-assisted proof has been fairly quiet for two decades. After the arguments around the four-colour theorem and, later, Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture, most mathematicians seem to have simply moved on: the Flyspeck project produced a fully machine-checked proof, referees continue to rely partly on trust, and journals continue to accept results that no human has surveyed end to end. Yet nobody I talk to thinks the underlying question was resolved. My hypothesis is that the debate stalled because it was really two disagreements wearing one label: an epistemological question about what a proof is for (certificate of truth versus vehicle of human understanding), and a sociological question about what the refereeing institution is supposed to guarantee. Please diagnose whether that is right, or whether there is a residual substantive disagreement underneath -- for instance, about whether a proof that no human can survey can ground mathematical knowledge in the same way a surveyable proof does. Give your answer in exactly five numbered items, each two or three sentences, covering: the diagnosis itself; the best evidence for it drawn from how the two disputes actually played out; what kind of disagreement (verbal, factual, about standards of warrant, or about the purpose of proof) each side was really having; what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis; and one reason the diagnosis might be wrong. No introduction or conclusion, and please write in British English.
