I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long piece on the ethics of scarcity — pandemic triage, grain reserves, climate migration — built around the lifeboat scenario associated with Garrett Hardin. One of my interviewees, a philosopher, pressed the following argument on me over coffee, and although each step seemed reasonable enough at the time, the conclusion now strikes me as proving far too much. I would like a proper diagnosis before I let it anywhere near print.

(1) A lifeboat rated for sixty carries fifty, with a hundred more in the water. (2) Taking aboard more than ten further people will swamp the boat and drown everyone, so admitting the eleventh swimmer foreseeably converts sixty survivors into none. (3) It is wrong knowingly to convert sixty survivors into none, however many of the hundred one wishes one could save; hence refusing further admissions is not merely permissible in the lifeboat but morally required. (4) The lifeboat differs from other allocation problems only in scale and setting, not in structure: wherever a resource has a maximum sustainable load, exceeding that load damages everyone the resource was sustaining. (5) Therefore, wherever demand exceeds capacity — an intensive care unit, a grain reserve in a famine, a state's infrastructure — refusing the excess is likewise required, not merely permitted. (6) And if refusing the excess is what is required, then the only remaining question is whom to refuse, and the choice of criterion — first come, lottery, prognosis, social usefulness — is morally optional window-dressing, since the duty is discharged by exclusion as such. (7) But (6) entails that a captain who overloaded the boat through his own negligence is just as required to turn swimmers away, and is morally blameless in choosing whom to save by personal whim; and that seems absurd.

What I want is not a general verdict on lifeboat ethics but a diagnosis of this particular chain of reasoning. Tell me which premise or inferential step I should reject and why; explain why that step looked innocent when my interviewee stated it; and price the alternatives — what I would have to give up, in ordinary triage or famine cases, if I rejected a different step instead. For what it is worth, I would rather keep (3), since I find the obligation of triage in the original case genuinely compelling, so if the rot is located there I want to know precisely what it costs me. Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
