In my philosophy of language course we've just covered presupposition, accommodation, and Lewis-style conversational scorekeeping, and after seminar two classmates got into an argument I've been asked to referee.

The case: at a meeting of our department's graduate colloquium committee, the chair said to a newer member, Ben, "Now that you've stopped circulating the reading notes late, could you take over the shared drive?" Ben paused, then simply said yes, and the meeting carried on. Three weeks later he asked that the minutes — which recorded that the committee "welcomed Ben's improved punctuality" — be corrected, and the chair refused, saying everyone had understood the remark as a throwaway.

Lena argues that Ben let the presupposition that he used to circulate the notes late onto the scoreboard. Accommodation, she says, is the default rule that keeps conversation running: silence functions as acceptance, the "hey, wait a minute" correction was right there and would have cost him a single sentence, and since he passed on it, the claim became common ground. The minutes merely record what the group was by then entitled to take for granted, and a practice in which every presupposition required explicit ratification couldn't function at all.

Marco replies that accommodation is a cooperative default, not a form of consent, and that exploiting it is a conversational foul. The burden lies on the speaker who smuggles in a reputation-damaging presupposition, not on the hearer who must interrupt, sound pedantic, and derail the meeting to block it. Ben's silence created at most an invitation to take the claim for granted, and the chair's later refusal to correct the minutes shows the harmless-default story was never honest.

Please adjudicate the dispute point by point: (1) what exactly happened to the common ground when Ben stayed silent; (2) who bears the burden of correction, and whether it matters that objecting carries social costs that accommodating does not; (3) whether the minutes episode changes the verdict or merely reveals what the verdict already was; and (4) what, if anything, both of them are missing.
