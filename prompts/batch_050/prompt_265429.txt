I sit on the programme committee of a mid-sized professional society. Three weeks ago we voted to rescind an invitation to a plenary speaker after a petition from members objected to things he had written years earlier. Since then, two colleagues on the committee have been arguing past each other in a way I can no longer mediate. One treats what we did as a punishment: an informal one, granted, but punishment all the same, and therefore subject to the disciplines punishment needs — proportionality, some notion of due process, a statute-of-limitations sensibility, and a route back. The other treats it as nothing of the kind: rescinding an invitation is simply the society declining to associate, no different in principle from a bookshop deciding what to stock or readers deciding what to buy, and demands for "proportionality" smuggle in a criminal-justice frame that has no application to people spending their attention and their presence as they please.

Each has privately accused the other of bad faith, so I want to try something more constructive: an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement the two of them could actually sign after a genuine attempt to reach common ground. For convenience call the first position the penal view and the second the associational view, but feel free to flag it if either label is slanted. I want the document to contain the following sections, in this order:

**1. Agreed claims (roughly 400 words).** A numbered list of propositions both signatories can endorse. I expect these to include genuinely substantive items, not only truces: for instance, claims about what social sanctions in fact do to their targets, about the difference between criticising and deplatforming, about what the petitioners were and were not owed by us, and about which costs of our decision fall on whom. Please also include, clearly marked, any pairs of claims that look like agreements but are actually the same words meaning different things to each side.

**2. Table of residual disagreements.** Each row should state one remaining disagreement in wording both sides would accept as a fair statement of the dispute — not wording that quietly concedes it to one of them. For each, give a one-sentence gloss of why the disagreement is real rather than verbal, or say so if you suspect it is verbal.

**3. Cruxes and tests (the longest section, roughly 500 words).** Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Two candidates I suspect matter: whether the penal view's proportionality demand presupposes an authority that a voluntary association does not have, and whether the associational view can account for the difference between choosing not to invite someone in the first place and publicly withdrawing an invitation already given. For each crux, specify a case, thought experiment, or observable pattern that both signatories would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each predicts about it. Invent a novel case if familiar ones (boycotts, no-platforming controversies, the usual celebrity pile-ons) are too freighted to test anything cleanly.

**4. Application to our case (roughly 250 words).** State what each signatory would conclude about our rescinded invitation, given the agreed claims, and what further facts about the case would have to be true for each to change that conclusion.

**5. Conditions of revision.** A short closing paragraph in which each signatory states, in their own voice, what evidence or argument would lead them to abandon their position. These statements should be falsifiable in spirit — not "if the other side persuaded me" but a description of what the world would have to look like.

Please write the whole document in the register of an actual joint statement rather than a referee's report, and do not take a side yourself anywhere in it.
