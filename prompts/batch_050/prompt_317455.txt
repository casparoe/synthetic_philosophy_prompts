I teach a first-year undergrad seminar on ethics and literature, 18 students, one 75-minute session. I want a session on moral learning from biography built around this passage from the opening of Plutarch's Life of Pericles (in the Dryden translation): "Such objects we find in the acts of virtue, which also produce in the minds of mere readers about them, an emulation and eagerness that may lead them on to imitation... But virtue, by the bare statement of its actions, can so affect men's minds as to create at once both admiration of the things done and desire to imitate the doers of them."

Design an activity where the students get to test this claim on themselves rather than just discuss it. My rough thought: they interview each other briefly and each writes a very short "Life" of a classmate, selecting one small telling detail rather than listing achievements, and the pieces get read aloud. Then, in a second round, the same facts get used to write the same person as a cautionary example instead of an admirable one. I want them to feel both halves of the problem: how a bare narrative of an action can produce the itch to imitate without any argument being made, and how much of that effect is actually the biographer's selection doing the work, not the virtue in the facts. They shouldn't hear Plutarch's name or see the passage until the end; open with the writing, no lecture up front.

Give me the full run of the session with timings, the debrief mapping what happened in the room back onto the passage (did the stimulus come from the virtue or from the writing, and does that support or embarrass Plutarch?), what will definately go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of his claim the activity can't reproduce and I have to supply by talking. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables -- like an email from a colleague.
