I am a sixth-form student preparing for our school debating society's annual ethics debate. This year's motion concerns white lies, and at last week's practice session something happened that I want to go into the debate ready for. I had argued that telling a friend her dreadful haircut looks fine, or telling a younger sibling that Father Christmas exists, is harmless and arguably kind. The opposing captain shut me down with a single line: honesty is the best policy. The room treated this as decisive, and I could not articulate on the spot why it should not be. The problem, as I have been turning it over since, is that the proverb seems to smuggle in several different claims at once, and I suspect that only some of them, if any, actually support the use my opponent made of it.

Here is what I would like you to do. Take the proverb "honesty is the best policy" and lay out the distinct, precise claims it could be making. I can already see a few candidates — for instance, that honesty is merely prudent (lies get found out and trust is expensive to rebuild), which is an empirical claim rather than a moral one; that honesty is a strict duty that admits no exceptions whatever the consequences; that deception is wrong even when one technically misleads without uttering a falsehood; and perhaps a claim about what kind of person one becomes through habitual small deceptions. There may be further readings I have not thought of. For each reading, I would like to know: whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something that is probably false as a generalisation; whether it is a claim about consequences, about duties, or about character; and — this is the part I most need — whether that particular reading would actually licence my opponent's move of treating the proverb as settling the question of white lies, rather than merely restating the very point under dispute.

A second layer, if you can manage it: the proverb seems designed to sidestep a distinction my opponent will not want drawn, between lying (saying what one believes to be false) and misleading (saying something true with the intention that the listener draw a false conclusion). If honesty is the best policy condemns the first, does it condemn the second? And does the answer change depending on which reading of the proverb is in play? I ask because my fallback strategy in the debate is to concede lying while defending strategic vagueness, and I want to know whether the proverb, on its strongest reading, blocks that retreat.

Please present the readings as a numbered menu I could plausibly read out from my notes mid-debate, each with a one- or two-sentence verdict, and finish with a short paragraph advising me which reading the proverb's everyday users most likely intend, and whether that intended reading survives contact with the white-lie cases that started all this. The answer should be written in British English throughout.
