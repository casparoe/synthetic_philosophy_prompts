I've been defending a principle about legal proof — verdicts must rest on evidence that ties the alleged fact to this defendant in particular, not on evidence merely showing that defendants of his kind are usually liable — and a colleague walked me into an argument that seems to prove too much from it.

(1) A verdict against a defendant is legitimate only if the evidence connects the alleged fact to that defendant individually, rather than to a class he happens to belong to. (2) This rules out the classic cases of naked statistical evidence: finding the bus company liable because it runs 80% of the buses on that route, or finding a randomly selected rodeo attendee liable because 501 of the 1,000 present hadn't paid. (3) Now consider an eyewitness: the factfinder credits her only because identifications by witnesses like her are usually accurate — absent that base rate, her testimony would carry no weight at all. (4) So reliance on testimony is reliance on a base rate, hence naked statistics by (1) and (2). (5) The same holds for fingerprints and DNA, whose entire force is a random-match probability. (6) So no verdict resting on testimony, fingerprints, or DNA alone is legitimate — which cannot be right.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If I have to choose, I'd rather give up the intuition about the gatecrasher than the possibility of DNA-based convictions. Name the step, explain why it looked fine, price the alternatives — and say what the diagnosis implies about what proof beyond reasonable doubt demands that the civil standard does not.
