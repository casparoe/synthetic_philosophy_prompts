I'm helping organize a small workshop on comparative cognition, and two of the participants have been circling each other for years. The first is a committed follower of Dennett's tower of generate-and-test: on her view, creatures sort into kinds by how their hypotheses get generated and filtered, from Darwinian creatures (hard-wired phenotypes tested by selection itself), to Skinnerian creatures (behaviors generated blindly and reinforced), to Popperian creatures (with inner environments in which candidate actions can be tested and die in the agent's stead), up to Gregorian creatures like us, whose inner environments are stocked with culturally transmitted mind-tools -- words, diagrams, numerals, argument forms, habits of self-interrogation -- that are themselves products of a second, faster evolutionary process. The second participant is a massive-modularity theorist in the tradition of evolutionary psychology, who thinks the tower picture is exactly backwards for humans: our flexibility is not a general-purpose inner simulation engine souped up by memes, but the aggregate behavior of a large set of domain-specific, informationally encapsulated adaptations, and the appearance of a unified, tool-using Gregorian mind is mostly hindsight and self-mythology. They have agreed to try an adversarial collaboration, and I've been asked to draft the joint statement they should emerge with.

Write that document. It should have four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can actually sign -- I suspect these include at least: that reinforcement learning is real but cannot alone account for human behavioral flexibility; that some human competences (face perception, folk physics in infancy, grammatical sensitivity) show signatures of domain-specific preparation; that cultural transmission demonstrably alters adult human competence in ways no module theorist should deny (numeracy and literacy being the obvious cases); and that no experiment to date cleanly separates the two architectures. Second, a disagreements table with three columns: the issue, the Dennettian's position phrased so she would sign it, and the modularist's position phrased so he would sign it. The disagreements should be stated precisely enough that it is clear whether each is empirical or verbal -- for instance, whether the dispute over the inner environment is about its existence or only about how modular versus general-purpose its contents are, and whether mind-tools is a load-bearing theoretical term or a redescription of facts both sides accept. If you judge that part of their quarrel is verbal, identify exactly which part and say why.

Third, a cruxes section: three or four sub-questions such that settling any one of them would oblige at least one side to retreat substantially, and for each crux a test, case, or consideration both parties accept as bearing on it. As test cases, use this battery of systems, which the two have stipulated in advance: the slime mold Physarum solving mazes; an octopus performing flexible foraging without anything like cultural transmission; an African grey parrot with a modest stock of referential vocal labels; a current large language model, whose training mixed something like selection over architectures, something like reinforcement, and ingestion of the textual residue of Gregorian culture; and a hypothetical child implanted at birth with a mature inner environment (vocabulary, arithmetic, argument schemas) acquired without any Skinnerian training history. For each crux, record in advance what the Dennettian predicts about the relevant members of the battery and what the modularist predicts, in wording each would endorse for their own side -- including, for the parrot and the language model, whether each camp counts them as Popperian creatures, Gregorian creatures, or as evidence that the tower's taxonomy breaks down, and what each camp would have to concede if the other camp's classification turned out to earn its keep explanatorily.

Fourth, a short closing section, written in the first person plural, stating the single point on which the two came closest to agreement during the collaboration and the single point that proved most resistant -- with both chosen so that both authors would recognize their own views in the description. The tone throughout should be that of a document both parties would actually sign: no strawmanning, no victories declared, and no false convergence.
