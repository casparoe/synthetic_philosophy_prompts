I help run a monthly "tech & society" reading group at my company, about a dozen people, mix of engineers and non-engineers, 90 minutes per session. Last month we did Frankfurt's On Bullshit, and the timing turned out to be perfect because two weeks ago one of our junior people got burned badly by an AI chatbot that confidently invented a regulatory requirement that doesn't exist, complete with a fake section number. At the next session I want to do something interactive instead of our usual discussion, and get at this question: when the chatbot does that, what exactly is the offense? It doesn't know the requirement is false, so "lying" seems off. But "honest mistake" also seems off, because it doesn't know anything, it just produces plausible-sounding text with zero concern for whether it's true. Which is weirdly close to what Frankfurt says the bullshitter does. Except Frankfurt's bullshitter is indifferent to truth, and indifference seems like an attitude, and people argue about whether the chatbot has attitudes at all.

So: design me an activity or game for the group that makes participants FEEL the difference between lying, bullshitting, and honest error from the inside, before anyone names the categories. Ideally the rules or incentives should be rigged so that at some point participants notice they've stopped caring whether what they're saying is true, and that this feels different from both trying to deceive and trying to be accurate. I want them to catch themselves in the act, basically. Then, in a second phase, they should somehow end up arguing about whether some agent without the relevant mental states could still be doing "that thing they just did" — which sets up the chatbot question without me lecturing. No philosophy vocabulary until the debrief, and definitely don't tell them upfront that the activity is about AI.

Give me concrete rules, roles, and timing, plus a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the lying/bullshitting/error distinction and then onto the hallucination debate. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this (e.g. I suspect some people will just say everything in the game is lying since they know they're making it up, and I need a move ready for that), and be honest about which feature of the real chatbot case the game can't reproduce and that I'll have to just supply in discussion.
