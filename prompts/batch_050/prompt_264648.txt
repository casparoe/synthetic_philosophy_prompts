I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about prediction markets and why people trade at all. While researching, I came across a passage in a popular book on rational choice that tries to explain the so-called no-trade theorems to lay readers, and I want to make sure I don't reproduce any errors if I paraphrase it. The passage runs roughly like this: "In the early 1980s, economists proved a startling result: if two traders are both rational and share the same prior beliefs about the world, then no trade between them can ever be mutually beneficial, because the very fact that someone is willing to sell you an asset tells you they know something you don't, and a rational buyer would always infer that the deal is bad for them. The conclusion is that the enormous trading volume we observe in real financial markets can only be explained by irrationality — people are overconfident, they disagree because they reason badly, and if everyone thought like a perfect Bayesian the markets would fall silent. This is closely related to Aumann's theorem that rational people cannot agree to disagree, which shows that any persistent disagreement between two honest people proves at least one of them is being irrational."

My instinct is that the passage captures something real — I understand there is a genuine result along these lines, attributed to Milgrom and Stokey among others — but several things make me uneasy. The leap from "no trade under these assumptions" to "real trading volume is evidence of irrationality" seems too quick, since people trade for reasons other than believing they have better information (hedging, liquidity, rebalancing a portfolio), and I'm not sure the book's version of the assumptions is the one the theorem actually needs — for instance, whether it requires merely that both traders be rational, or something stronger about each trader knowing that the other is rational, and knowing that the other knows, and so on. I also suspect the final sentence overstates what Aumann's result says, since I gather it concerns people with common priors whose beliefs are common knowledge, which is a much narrower claim than "honest people can't persistently disagree." But I'm out of my depth on where exactly the gloss goes wrong versus where it simplifies legitimately.

Please give me your assessment as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: which parts of the passage a careful economist would accept as accurate, which parts are defensible popular simplifications, which parts misstate the theorems or their assumptions (especially the rationality/common-knowledge point and the treatment of Aumann), whether the passage's conclusion about real markets being explained by irrationality follows from anything true in it, and what a corrected one-paragraph version suitable for my article would say.
