So I hang out on LW-adjacent discord and we got into it again last night about whether training RL agents is, like, morally fine by default or actually a huge potential s-risk factory, and I realized I don't even know what the standard practice *assumes*. Take how deep RL is actually done: agents get negative rewards all the time (die in an Atari game, -1; drop the block, penalty; bump the wall in a robotics sim, penalty), reward gets clipped to some fixed range, environments get reset mid-episode whenever the trajectory looks bad, and people run thousands of parallel copies of the same agent and keep only the good rollouts. Everyone's seen the "reward is not the optimization target" arguments, which I mostly buy for what they say about goals, but they don't obviously settle anything about whether getting the negative reward *feels like something*, or whether the agent's internal critic doing temporal-difference updates instantiates something with hedonic valence.

What I want from you: what theory does the standard engineering practice presuppose about what reward signals *are*? Like, does it only make sense if reward is a purely informational signal with no valence at all? Or does it actually presuppose something weaker, like "even if valence shows up somewhere in the network, it's the wrong kind to count as suffering"? Or does it presuppose nothing at all and just happen to be compatible with several views, in which case which ones? Please actually distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it's merely comfortable with -- my suspicion is that some parts (reward clipping, say) are philosophically innocent and others (resetting episodes when the agent wanders into a low-value region, or mass-spawning copies and culling the losers) commit you to something much stronger, and I want to know if that suspicion is right or if I'm just pattern-matching to stuff that would be horrifying if done to a rat.

Then the follow-up: suppose someone convinces you reward-as-implemented in current architectures does carry valence, not because of anything fancy but just because reinforcement and punishment in biological brains turned out to be one of the more conserved computations evolution found. What would minimally have to change in the practice? Would symmetric positive-only training work, does valence even track the sign of the reward or the sign of the TD error or neither, and is there any version of "we only use small penalties" that isn't just the trolley-problem version of hoping the badness is below some threshold you've never tried to measure? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, its weak points named, and the underdetermined parts flagged as underdetermined rather than quietly resolved in whichever direction feels safer.
