I coordinate a small charitable giving circle: eleven members, monthly meetings, a pooled fund we allocate by vote each quarter. Our members care about genuinely different things — malaria prevention, a local food bank, an animal sanctuary, disaster relief, an arts program — and at our last meeting someone proposed that instead of deadlocking or shaving the fund into ever-thinner equal portions, we trade support across quarters: you back my cause this time, I'll back yours next. Two members called this obvious common sense; two called it morally corrosive, a way of treating one's conscience as a bargaining chip. Reading around the question since then, I've learned that philosophers discuss such arrangements under the heading of moral trade, and I've become convinced the group needs to feel both the appeal and the queasiness before we vote, not just hear them argued.

Design a 75-minute activity for our next meeting, runnable with no prior reading and no introductory lecture, in which members encounter both sides themselves before anyone names the concept. I'd like assigned value profiles so that what is at stake differs sharply from member to member, and a two-phase structure: a first phase in which the fund is allocated under some symmetric non-trading rule, and a second with the same total resources in which binding promises across rounds are permitted, so that the aggregate gain from trading shows up in the numbers rather than in my summary. At least one round should put a member in the position where the efficient trade requires actively supporting a cause their profile counts as bad, not merely as unimportant. Scoring should use tokens rather than money, though if you can suggest a small real stake that sharpens things without creating actual resentment, include it. Give me complete rules, materials, a timeline in minutes, and instructions for keeping the vocabulary of moral trade out of the room until the end.

For the debrief, map specific moments the group will have lived through onto the relevant distinctions: how trading across issues differs from compromising within one, what the integrity or complicity objection actually objects to, and under what conditions the analogy to ordinary vote-trading breaks down. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to respond in the moment. Finally, be candid about which feature of real moral trade a token game cannot reproduce — I suspect the unverifiability of what others would have done, and the genuine cost to conscience, are candidates — and what I must therefore supply in discussion myself.
