I administer a small grant program for early-career researchers. Every application receives a composite score from 0 to 100, assembled from reviewer ratings, and our charter requires us to sort applications into three tiers: funded, declined, and a middle tier of "under further consideration," which goes to a live panel that debates each case and issues a final verdict. The trouble is that nobody can state a principled boundary for the middle tier, and this year two of my senior colleagues are pressing me to resolve the discomfort in opposite directions, and I need to decide before the next cycle opens.

The first colleague argues that the middle tier is a fiction we maintain because we cannot bear to admit that a line has to be drawn somewhere. On her view, any scoring rubric produces a continuum, and a continuum must be cut, so we should pick a threshold, force a binary verdict on every application, and accept the consequence: two applications differing by a fraction of a point will receive different outcomes, and there will be nothing to say to the applicant who asks why the line fell just above rather than just below her, except that it had to fall somewhere and it fell there. She thinks the existence of an actual boundary, even one no one can justify or even locate with confidence, is simply what administering a budget means.

The second colleague argues that this is exactly backwards. On his view, for a substantial range of applications there is no answer to the question "fund or decline" that the rubric, properly understood, delivers, and the honest response is to design the process so it never has to pretend otherwise: keep the middle tier, but reconceive the panel not as discovering the right answer but as legitimately settling a question the scoring system leaves open, the way a legislature settles questions a constitution leaves open.

Two further complications. First, whichever view I take, I cannot even locate the edges of the problem: I cannot say where "clearly fundable" stops and "arguable" begins, since any boundary I draw for the middle tier seems to inherit the same arbitrariness as the boundary it was meant to relieve. Second, the panel's deliberations do seem to change minds in ways that feel like discovery rather than coin-flipping, which sits awkwardly with my second colleague's picture but equally awkwardly with the first's, since if there were a discoverable answer, it is strange that only deliberation and never the rubric finds it.

Advise me. I want to understand whether the difference between my two colleagues is a real disagreement about what our scores mean or merely a disagreement about institutional etiquette; which picture, if either, better fits the felt structure of the hard cases, including the regress about the middle tier's own edges; and what concrete design features -- threshold policy, panel procedure, what we tell declined applicants -- actually follow from each picture, so that I am not choosing a philosophy and then quietly running the same process regardless.
