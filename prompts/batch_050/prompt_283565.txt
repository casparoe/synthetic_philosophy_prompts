I'm in a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of value, and this week we hit spectrum arguments against the transitivity of "better than." Consider the following spectrum: Case 1 is a week of moderate but constant pain; each subsequent case is slightly less intense but roughly twice as long, so that by Case 40 you have a barely perceptible ache lasting longer than a human lifetime, and by Case 80 a momentary twinge stretched across geological time. Each adjacent pair seems easy to compare -- the next case seems better than the previous -- and yet the endpoints seem clearly to compare the other way, with Case 1 better than Case 80. One of my classmates concludes that all-things-considered betterness is simply not transitive, and that we should accept intransitivity the way we accept other counterintuitive discoveries. Another insists this is impossible: she argues that "better than" is transitive as a matter of what the concept means, that a genuinely intransitive betterness relation would leave us unable to justify any sequence of choices and would invite exploitation, and that the spectrum really shows only that some adjacent pairs are incomparable or vaguely related -- "at least as good as" fails to be transitive, but that reflects gaps in comparability (rough equality between neighbors), not cycles of strict betterness.

I've asked both of them to stop arguing past each other and produce a joint statement as if they were conducting an adversarial collaboration. Draft that document for them. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered list of exactly six agreed claims -- claims both the intransitivist and the transitivity defender can sign, covering what the spectrum case actually establishes, what each side concedes about the phenomenology of the pairwise comparisons, and what each grants about the costs of the other's view. Follow this with a disagreements table containing exactly three rows, each row stating one residual disagreement in wording both parties would accept for their own position -- I expect one row to concern whether non-transitivity of "at least as good as" implies intransitive strict betterness or merely incompleteness, and another to concern whether intransitivity would actually expose an agent to diachronic exploitation or whether that worry conflates betterness with choice. Finally, for each of the three disagreements, give a crux: a concrete case, argument, or further question that both parties agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about how that crux resolves and what it would take for them to update. Write the document in a neutral register, with no introduction and no concluding remarks beyond the cruxes.
