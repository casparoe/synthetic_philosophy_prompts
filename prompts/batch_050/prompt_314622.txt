I've become preoccupied with a pattern after watching my own neighborhood housing cooperative tear itself apart last year. The co-op had an expulsion procedure that the founding members wrote to remove disruptive tenants, and the new board then used that very procedure, and the founders' own language about threats to community safety, to expel two of the founders. When I started reading about revolutions to make sense of it, I kept finding the same shape at larger scales. Danton helped construct the Revolutionary Tribunal and was then condemned by it as a conspirator against the revolution; the Girondins, who had spent 1792 denouncing hidden traitors and royalist plots, were themselves executed in October 1793 as hidden traitors and royalist plotters; in the Soviet Union, men who had built and staffed the machinery for unmasking enemies of the people were paraded through that machinery in the late 1930s on charges cast in their own vocabulary; after 1979 in Iran, the revolutionary courts turned on leftist allies of the uprising using the categories those allies had helped legitimize. What strikes me is not merely that revolutionaries end up destroyed by their own revolution -- there is the old line about the revolution devouring its children, and I know about that trope. It is something more specific: that a movement's self-created accusatory apparatus -- its purity tests, its special tribunals, its categories of enemy -- reliably gets turned inward, so that each wave of leaders is condemned in terms the condemned themselves authored, and yesterday's vanguard is reclassified, retroactively, as having been the enemy all along.

Does this specific mechanism have an established name in the historiography of revolutions, or in political science? I've considered some candidates and none seems to fit exactly. Thermidor, as I understand it, names the cooling-off phase when the revolution swings back toward moderation, but the inward turn I'm describing happens during radicalization, not only after it. The devouring-its-children line describes the outcome, not the mechanism of self-applied categories. Crane Brinton's stage model (which I only know secondhand) is a sequence of phases, not a name for this particular recycling of charges. And ordinary purge dynamics or scapegoating don't require that the weapons be ones the victims themselves forged. So: is there an established concept for the inward turning of a movement's own invented machinery of accusation, and if so, how is it delimited from the candidates above? If there isn't one, please coin a term, define it carefully, and mark the boundary cases -- for instance, whether it applies only where the accusatory categories are genuinely new creations of the movement rather than inherited, whether it counts when the outcome is expulsion or disgrace rather than death, whether the American Revolution's comparative restraint and my co-op's boardroom version are instances of the same thing or merely analogies, and whether the pattern is something genuinely recurrent in revolutions or a narrative historians impose afterward.

One more request about how you frame the answer: the historiography of the French Revolution is famously contested, so please distinguish clearly between what is broadly settled among historians -- the basic chronology and the existence of the pattern as a descriptive observation -- and what remains genuinely disputed, such as whether stage-models of revolution have any real explanatory standing after the revisionist critiques, and don't present any contested interpretive framework as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- either the established name and why it fits, or the verdict that none exists and your coined term -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
