Ok so I've been chewing on imaginative resistance -- why some morally deviant fictions are easy to sink into and others make me bounce off -- and I want to know if my gut reactions add up to anything. In Of the Standard of Taste, Hume remarks about the Odyssey that "The sage Ulysses in the Greek poet seems to delight in lies and fictions, and often employs them without any necessity or even advantage: But his more scrupulous son in the French epic writer exposes himself to the most imminent perils, rather than depart from the exactest line of truth and veracity." So Hume thinks Homer's hero is, by our lights, kind of a liar, and yet plainly nobody has trouble reading the Odyssey. Whatever principle I end up with has to explain why that one goes down easy.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A historical novel where the hero owns slaves and it's just unremarkable background, nobody comments on it: I can get fully absorbed. No resistance at all, even though I think slavery is about as wrong as anything gets.

2. Same book, but now the narrator in the novel's own voice repeatedly states that the slaves are slaves because they deserve to be, and that this arrangement is morally noble: I stall out. I start reading the narrator as ironic or unreliable even though nothing in the text supports that.

3. A grimdark fantasy where everyone betrays everyone and cruelty is simply how the world runs: fine, I can engage, even enjoy it.

4. A short story that flatly stipulates that torturing random innocents for fun is virtuous, and treats this as obviously true within the fiction: can't do it. I involuntarily read it as dystopia or satire.

5. A sci-fi story where aliens ritually eat their honored dead and this is presented as right, but I'm shown their reasons (the dead live on in the tribe, refusing to be eaten is the ultimate insult): after a few pages I can go along with it. The deviance dissolves once it's earned.

6. A story where exactly one moral verdict is flipped -- in this world, breaking a promise to save a life is monstrous, and the hero who does it is universally despised: I can imagine this, perversely enough, even though I think the verdict is wrong. But flip everything at once, like case 4 tries to, and I'm out.

The pair I can't reconcile is 1 and 2 -- the moral content of the world is identical, only the explicitness differs, and I have no idea why that should matter so much. And 4 versus 6 bugs me too: why is one flipped verdict digestible but a total inversion isn't?

So: is there a single principle that captures these verdicts and also makes sense of Hume's Ulysses going down easy while a modern novel with a lying hero might not (does the excuse we make for the ancients rest on anything more than prejudice about "the customs of their age")? State the principle precisely, tell me which of my six verdicts it has to sacrifice if any, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a comedy whose jokes only land if you find cruelty funny, and a puzzle story where the detective's genius consists in noticing moral facts that don't exist in our world.
