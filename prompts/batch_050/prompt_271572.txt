I'm a high school junior, and last month in my economics class we had a unit on incentives where my teacher wrote on the board, when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure, and treated it as a general law. It came up again during our debate tournament prep, where one of my teammates used it to shut down a proposal that schools should be ranked and funded partly by standardized test scores: the moment the scores carry funding, she said, they stop measuring learning. That sounded right to me in the moment, but the more I think about it, the less sure I am that the slogan is even one claim. Consider four cases that people gesture at with the same sentence: a student who gets a fitness tracker and starts walking in circles to hit a step goal; a hospital judged on surgical survival rates that quietly starts declining the riskiest patients; a city that judges its police department on reported crime and discovers officers reclassifying offenses; and a study that finds that the students admitted to a program because of the highest test scores do only slightly better than average later, simply because some of their scores were partly luck. These feel like different phenomena to me — deliberate cheating, strategic case selection, distortion of the recording process itself, and something purely statistical that involves no bad faith at all — and I suspect the slogan is doing different work in each.

So here is my request: treat when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure as a piece of language that needs disambiguating rather than as an established theorem, and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. For each candidate reading, state it formally enough that I could tell what would count as evidence for or against it (for example, does it claim something about incentives, about the correlation between proxy and goal, about the causal effect of optimizing the proxy on the goal itself, or about how selection on a noisy indicator behaves statistically), say whether the reading is trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and check it against my four cases. Then tell me which readings would actually support the use my teammate put the slogan to — that is, which ones, if true, would give a genuine argument against test-based school funding rather than just a caution to design the tests carefully — and whether the slogan, on its strongest reading, is a law about measurement at all or really a law about people responding to incentives, with the measurement language being misleading. I'd like the answer organized as a menu of readings I can bring back to my teammate, with your verdict on which one the people who repeat the slogan most likely have in mind.
