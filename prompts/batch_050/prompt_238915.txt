In my ancient political thought reading group someone reconstructed an argument out of Diodotus's speech in the Mytilene debate (Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 3 -- this is the Crawley translation we're using) and it's been bugging me because it seems to prove way more than Diodotus could have wanted. Here's the bit of the speech the argument leans on:

"All, states and individuals, are alike prone to err, and there is no law that will prevent them; or why should men have exhausted the list of punishments in search of enactments to protect them from evil-doers? It is probable that in early times the penalties for the greatest offences were less severe, and that, as these were disregarded, the penalty of death has been by degrees in most cases arrived at, which is itself disregarded in like manner. ... In fine, it is impossible to prevent, and only great simplicity can hope to prevent, human nature doing what it has once set its mind upon, by force of law or by any other deterrent force whatsoever."

And here's the argument as my friend wrote it up:

(1) Punishing the Mytilenians can only be justified by its future effects -- deterring other allies from revolting. The harm to Athens is already done, and nothing done to the prisoners now can undo it.

(2) But as Diodotus says, no penalty ever devised, death included, has actually stopped people from offending once hope, need, or ambition has taken hold of them. Every escalation of punishment in history has been disregarded in turn.

(3) If a penalty does not deter the people it is supposed to deter, imposing it serves no purpose.

(4) Everyone who commits an offence does so under some such motive -- necessity, ambition, hope of success -- since nobody knowingly walks into a penalty for no reason at all.

(5) Therefore punishment never deters the people it is aimed at, and by (3) it never serves a purpose.

(6) So punishment is never justified -- not for the Mytilenians, and not for anyone else either. The entire institution of criminal penalty is irrational, and Diodotus's speech, taken seriously, shows this.

The conclusion in (6) is obviously nuts -- it's one thing to spare Mytilene, another to abolish every criminal sanction anywhere. But each step looked plausible when we went through it, and when we pushed back on the speech itself my friend just said he was following Diodotus's own logic. Two of us want to keep something like Diodotus's actual verdict on Mytilene (don't execute them) while rejecting the general conclusion, if that's coherent; the third thinks the whole speech is just sophistry and we should reject (1) or (2) on those grounds.

So: which premise or step should we actually give up? I'd like you to name the specific point where the reasoning breaks, explain why that step looked fine when we read it (something about it clearly seduced my friend, and honestly seduced me for a while), and then price out the alternatives -- i.e., what it would cost to reject (1) instead, or to deny that (2) supports (5), and whether keeping Diodotus's Mytilene verdict while refusing the general abolitionist conclusion leaves you with a stable position or just an arbitrary one. Also say whether the flaw is in the argument itself or in how it uses the passage -- does Diodotus's own text commit him to (5), or is that my friends gloss on it?
