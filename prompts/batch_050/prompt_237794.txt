I TA for an undergraduate value-theory course, and next week I get one fifty-minute discussion section on spectrum arguments and the transitivity of "better than." The students have just finished a unit on the Repugnant Conclusion, and judging by their problem sets, most of them arrive thinking that spectrum arguments are just sorites puzzles dressed up in axiology — a few wrote nearly that sentence. A couple of the stronger ones have also gotten hold of the idea that anyone who denies the transitivity of betterness has simply left rationality behind, and they say it with great confidence. Please don't walk me through the arguments themselves; plan how I should teach them.

Specifically: in what order should I lay out the pieces so that the class comes to see the spectrum construction as a forced choice — every adjacent step is an improvement, "better than" is transitive, the endpoint is not better than the starting point; at least one of these goes — rather than as a sleight of hand? In particular, what do they need to already be comfortable with before the transitivity premise can even appear to them as a live candidate for rejection rather than an axiom nobody is allowed to touch? Then: which single example should carry the section — I'm torn between the pain-spectrum case (a short excruciating pain versus a slightly milder agony endured far longer, iterated stepwise) and a pure welfare spectrum with no pains at all — and what must that example contain and deliberately avoid? I have a feeling it must not rely on the sufferers' inability to notice the differences between adjacent steps, since that smuggles in exactly the perceptual confusion I want to exclude, but I'd like this worked out. Next, map the misconceptions I should expect, in roughly the order they'll surface — "the steps are too small to compare, so this is really about vagueness," "indistinguishable from means just as good as," "you can break the chain by introducing a third option," and "denying transitivity is obviously irrational, so the argument must fail somewhere upstream" — and tell me at exactly which point in the sequence I should head each one off, rather than letting it harden. Finally: what should I openly decline to settle at this level (I'm guessing the more technical machinery of essentially comparative accounts of goodness and the formal treatment of intransitive value belongs on that list), and what is one question I can pose in the last five minutes whose answer would show the students understand that the argument's bite is that some premise must be surrendered, each at a real cost — and not merely that they can recite the chain?
