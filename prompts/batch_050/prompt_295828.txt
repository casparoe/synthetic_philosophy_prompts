I chair the appointments committee for a small philosophy department. Three times a year we decide job offers by voting: each of the seven members votes separately on whether the candidate's research is strong, whether their teaching is strong, and then on the final question of whether to make the offer, where our standing rule is that an offer requires both criteria to be met. Last cycle we hit the classic problem: a majority judged the research strong, a majority judged the teaching strong, but when we voted on the offer itself, a majority voted no, because the two majorities were different people. The dean has asked me to write a standing procedure the committee will follow from now on so this can't produce an outcome nobody on the committee actually endorses.

Write that procedure. It should specify, in order: how to decide for a given vote whether members vote on the criteria first and let the verdict follow by rule, or vote on the verdict directly and treat criteria as discussion only (and by what features of the case this choice is made); how to detect in advance, from the pattern of criterion votes, whether the two methods would diverge; what to do about abstentions and about members who change their criterion votes after hearing colleagues, since both can manufacture or dissolve the divergence; and what to do when the divergence appears anyway, including whether revoting, deferral, or accepting the uncomfortable result is the right default. Then apply it to three cases I give you: last cycle's split-majority case; a case where six members think a candidate is strong on exactly one criterion each and the seventh thinks the candidate is strong on neither; and a case where one member votes strategically on the criteria to force the outcome they want on the verdict. At least one of these should produce a recommendation I'd prefer not to give the dean. State plainly which parts of the procedure rest on settled results about aggregation and which are judgment calls I could defend differently, and say what evidence or argument about how committees like mine actually behave would lead you to change the procedure's central recommendation.
