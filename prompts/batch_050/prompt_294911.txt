I am a novelist by trade, and for the past year I have co-hosted a modest podcast on the philosophy of fiction and storytelling. Our guest next month is a philosopher who has recently published a widely discussed paper arguing that the outputs of large language models are a near-pure instance of bullshit in Harry Frankfurt's technical sense: produced with indifference to how things actually stand, rather than with the liar's intent to deceive or the honest reasoner's intent to get things right. I find the argument compelling in outline, but I have two nagging concerns as a fiction writer. First, much of my own craft consists in inventing things with cheerful indifference to whether they are true, so if the Frankfurt analysis convicts chatbots, it seems to convict me as well, and I suspect there is a principled reply here that I want him to make explicit. Second, Frankfurt's account is built around a speaker's attitudes and aims, and it is contested whether a system that (on some views) has no intentions or beliefs at all can even be indifferent to truth in the relevant sense, rather than merely being unreliable.

Please draft nine to eleven interview questions for this guest. The questions should presuppose that I have read his paper and do not need the thesis restated; instead, press on its load-bearing joints. I would like at least one question on why he classifies confident LLM errors as bullshit rather than as mere error, given that error without any indifference to truth seems to fall outside Frankfurt's taxonomy entirely; at least one on the intentionality problem just mentioned, including whether his view commits him to the claim that a sufficiently sophisticated autocomplete could never lie; at least one on whether the term "hallucination" does quiet argumentative work that a more careful vocabulary would not; and at least one engaging my concern about novelists, ideally forcing him to say what distinguishes the fiction writer's sanctioned indifference to truth from the chatbot's objectionable kind, and whether the difference is in the agent, the audience's expectations, or the social practice surrounding the utterance. For each question, please include a follow-up calibrated to the reply he is most likely to give, so that I am not left flat-footed when he offers his stock response. I would like the sequence to build toward a closing question about what empirical or conceptual finding would make him abandon the Frankfurt framing altogether -- philosophers are rarely asked this and I find their answers to it the most illuminating part of any interview. The tone should be collegial but genuinely adversarial in the way good philosophical conversation is; I have no interest in questions that let him recite his abstract.
