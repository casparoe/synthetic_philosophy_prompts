Since I retired from my physics job last year I've been slowly working my way through the philosophy shelf at the used book store, and Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia has been the latest assignment in our little reading group (two engineers, a retired dentist, and me). Anyway, the Wilt Chamberlain business caused an actual fight at the pub last Thursday and I got drafted as referee, which I found I couldn't manage. Here's the exchange as best I remember it. Martin's line was that the example basically settles it: if everyone hands Wilt a quarter of their own free will, and every single step is clean -- nobody coerced, nobody defrauded -- then the big pile of money he ends up with can't be unjust, and any tax the state then takes out of it is just confiscation of what people legitimately chose to give him. Patterns, he said, can only be kept up by constant meddling. Sandra pushed back that the argument is rigged from the start, because it quietly builds Nozick's whole theory into the setup: you're told to picture the initial distribution D1 as just and the transfers as just, so of course the result is 'just' -- that's an assumption doing the work, not a conclusion. Martin answered that this misses the dialectical point: the argument isn't aimed at people who already reject entitlement theory, it's aimed at people who like patterned principles like equality, and it shows that *they* have to condemn a sequence of transactions nobody involved had any complaint about, which is absurd. Sandra's counter was twofold: first, that a patterned theorist doesn't have to condemn any individual transaction, only refuse to take step-by-step consent as the sole measure of the justice of the *outcome* distribution; and second, that taxation isn't the state storming in after the fact, it's a rule announced in advance that Wilt and the fans all know about when they make their choices, like a sales tax -- so the 'meddling' framing is misleading, nobody's liberty to hand over a quarter is being restricted, they just know 25 cents isn't the final number. Martin said that can't be right, because a rule announced in advance is still a taking, just a predictable one, and anyway the fans didn't consent to the tax, they consented to paying Wilt. Then Sandra said something I couldn't follow about the difference between consenting to a transaction and consenting to its total downstream consequences, and by then the dentist had ordered another round and it got dropped.

So can you adjudicate this properly? I'd like a move-by-move verdict -- who's right that the argument assumes what it tries to prove, who's right about whether the pattern-lover is forced into calling innocent transactions unjust, and who's right about the announced-in-advance taxation point. And if both of them are missing something, tell me what it is. I warn you, if your answer is just 'it depends on your prior views about property' I will be dissapointed, because I could have gotten that from the dentist.
