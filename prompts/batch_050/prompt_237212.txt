I'm the tech lead on a small applied ML team at a mid-size company, and I keep getting dragged into the same argument. Vendors pitch us optimizers and model architectures that are supposedly "just better, across the board," my own engineers fall in love with whatever won on the last benchmark someone ran, and every few months someone links a paper claiming their method dominates the field. I have a physics background, so I dimly remember the no-free-lunch theorems for optimization and supervised learning -- roughly, that averaged over *all* possible problems, every algorithm performs identically, so any edge has to come from the match between the algorithm's built-in assumptions and the structure of your actual problem. And behind that there's good ol' Hume's problem of induction: even on problems we've actually tried, past wins don't logically guarantee future ones. I feel like these two ideas together should add up to a way of deflating 90% of the hype claims I see, but I've never managed to turn them into something operational.

Please act as someone fluent in both learning theory and philosophy of science and build me a concrete evaluation procedure my team can actually run -- in under an hour, by engineers who haven't read Wolpert or Hume -- whenever someone claims that algorithm X is better than algorithm Y. Think carefully and brainstorm before settling on the final version. I want the steps in order: the question to ask at each step (e.g., "what inductive bias is this method actually baking in, and do we have reason to think our problems have that structure?"), what counts as a satisfactory answer, what to do when the answer is unavailable or unknowable (this will happen a lot -- benchmarks are always a finite, biased sample of "problems we care about"), and explicit flags for the steps that are judgment calls wearing a lab coat rather than real procedure. Also say honestly what the procedure can't settle, since I suspect the NFL theorems license less skepticism in practice than people think -- real problem distributions aren't uniform over all logically possible problems, and I want the procedure to reflect that rather than just chanting "no free lunch" at everything.

Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a vendor claims their new gradient-free optimizer beats Adam "on any loss landscape," offering benchmark results on 40 public functions as evidence; (2) an engineer on my team wants us to permanently switch our production models to an architecture that won on three public benchmarks in our broad domain -- note that I suspect the procedure will refuse to fully bless case (2), which will annoy my team, so show its work. Give me the procedure itself as a table with columns Step, Question, Sufficient_Answer, If_Unknowable, Judgment_Call (yes/no plus what's being judged), and then each case verdict as a JSON object with fields claim, steps_passed (list of step numbers), failing_step, verdict (one of "accept", "reject", "insufficient_evidence"), what_would_change_the_verdict, and confidence (low/medium/high) with a one-sentence rationale for that confidence rating.
