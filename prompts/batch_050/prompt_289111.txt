The students have solid logic and a semester of formal semantics but uneven probability theory, and most arrive having heard that the result is a technical footnote to the story they already know. Don't walk me through the proof; plan how I should teach it. What has to be in place before the equation P(A → C) = P(C | A) even registers as a substantive hypothesis rather than a definition, and how do I motivate why anyone wanted it to hold? Which single example should carry the session, and what must it have and lack (I'm guessing a simple chance setup, but tell me what goes wrong if the example is one where the conditional probability is obviously high)? Map the misconceptions I should expect — that the result merely restates the case against truth-functionality, that the material conditional satisfies the equation well enough, that treating 'if' as a restrictor is an obviously costless escape — and where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. Give me the closing question that separates students who grasp the argument's generality from those who memorized a counterexample, and tell me what to openly bracket at this level.
