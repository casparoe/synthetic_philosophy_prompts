I'm a journalist who covers the science-and-religion beat, and last month I moderated a panel that went sideways in a productive way. On one side, a psychologist who argues religion made large-scale cooperation possible -- the idea that watchful, moralizing gods let strangers trust each other, and that religious practice still does measurable good work today. On the other, a sociologist who thinks that's backwards: honest courts, functioning markets, and welfare states do the heavy lifting, religion is mostly along for the ride, and the healthiest, most trusting societies on earth are the most secular ones. They yelled for an hour, then agreed to co-write a joint statement. My editor wants me to draft the document they should come back with, so we can hold them to it.

Draft that joint statement as an adversarial collaboration. I want: a numbered section of claims both would sign -- including, please, an agreement on what "more moral" could even mean, since they spent twenty minutes discovering they meant different things (honesty with strangers? generosity? out-group tolerance? rule-following?), and I'd like the statement to disaggregate those instead of using one mushy word. Then the disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would actually accept -- for instance, whether the prosociality religion produces is real but mostly stops at the in-group boundary, whether secular societies are coasting on moral capital their religious ancestors built, and whether the active ingredient is belief in a watchful god at all or just the weekly communal practice. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp, and for each crux, a study, comparison, or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If some of their fight turns out to be about words rather than the world, say which part.

One more thing, since my fact-checker will ask: have the draft say how confident it is in each of the agreed claims -- some of these (like what the cross-national data on secular societies actually shows) are shakier than the panelists let on -- and have it single out the one point in the whole statement it's least sure of.
