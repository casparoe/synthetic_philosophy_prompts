As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I am wary of two opposite failure modes: treating falsifiability as a slogan that disqualifies anything historical or difficult to test, and treating evolutionary reasoning as so flexible that every observation can be explained after the fact. I would like a more disciplined middle position grounded in Popper’s treatment of Darwinism.

I am the methods editor for an interdisciplinary journal that publishes evolutionary explanations in medicine, psychology, and anthropology. I review about six submissions a month. For each, I have roughly ninety minutes for an initial methods assessment, no access to the authors’ unpublished data, and no authority to require new fieldwork before making a recommendation. The handling editor makes the final decision, but my recommendation determines whether a paper is rejected, sent for specialist review, or returned with mandatory revisions. The stakes are practical: our medical papers can influence clinical advice, and our psychology papers are often amplified by journalists.

Please convert Popper’s distinction between Darwinism as a broad research programme and particular evolutionary hypotheses as testable scientific claims into a reusable review procedure I can apply to submissions. The procedure should be specific enough for a competent editor who is not an evolutionary biologist to follow. Include:

1. The questions to ask, in order.
2. What evidence would count as a sufficient answer at each stage.
3. The points at which the procedure must stop with rejection or referral.
4. The points where specialist judgment remains unavoidable and should be explicitly flagged rather than hidden behind a checklist.
5. Rules for distinguishing an explanatory hypothesis from a speculative possibility, while allowing speculative sections when they are clearly labeled as hypothesis generation.
6. A treatment of adaptation, byproduct, drift, developmental constraint, cultural transmission, and exaptation that does not let authors switch between these explanations whenever one becomes difficult to test.
7. A distinction between merely fitting known observations and making a comparatively risky prediction, including what would count as risky in historical or cross-cultural research where experiments are impossible.
8. Guidance on whether simulations, comparative phylogenies, fossils, genomic data, natural experiments, and agent-based models can supply severity rather than only plausibility.

The procedure must handle a common bad case: authors define fitness, adaptation, or ancestral environment so loosely that either the presence or absence of a trait would confirm their account. It must also handle the opposite bad case: reviewers demanding direct observation of ancestral selection even when several independent lines of evidence converge.

After giving the general procedure, apply it step by step to these hypothetical submissions:

- A medical paper argues that pregnancy nausea is an adaptation protecting embryos from dietary toxins, citing its timing, characteristic food aversions, and associations with pregnancy outcomes.
- A psychology paper argues that a present-day preference for socially dominant partners evolved because dominant mates provided resources in ancestral environments, with questionnaire evidence from one wealthy country.
- A microbiology paper proposes a specific antibiotic-cycling policy intended to slow resistance, supported by genomic measurements from hospitals and by a model predicting which resistance mutations should appear under each cycling schedule.

For each case, state the recommendation, the evidence that would change it, and any revisions required. At least one worked application should produce a recommendation that an initially sympathetic reader is unlikely to like. Finally, state what this procedure cannot accomplish: where Popperian standards cease to settle a dispute, what kinds of evolutionary claims it will unfairly penalize, and what additional philosophical or empirical considerations would then be needed. Do not provide a bibliography; make the procedure usable on its own.
