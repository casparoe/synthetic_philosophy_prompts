Write an essay on the following dilemma in animal ethics. The "scale argument" for insect welfare runs: even if the probability that insects are sentient is low (say, 1–10%), insects exist in such vast numbers — on the order of 10^18 alive at any time, with perhaps 10^12 farmed annually for food and feed — that the expected moral weight of insect suffering dwarfs that of all vertebrate farming combined, where sentience is near-certain but headcounts are far smaller. Now consider the dilemma. Either we accept straightforward expected-value reasoning here, in which case the same logic seems to force us to take seriously the welfare of entities with far lower credences of sentience — soil nematodes, and perhaps large reinforcement-learning systems or other candidates where the probability is tiny but the numbers are astronomical — until our moral attention is consumed by ever more speculative beings. Or we impose some cutoff below which low-probability sentience stops counting, but every proposed cutoff (a probability floor, a neuroanatomical criterion, a "sufficiently complex valence" threshold) looks ad hoc, and arguably smuggles in the conclusion it was meant to justify. Take a definite position on how this dilemma should be resolved: either defend a principled cutoff and show why it is not ad hoc, or defend the fanatical conclusion that low-probability, high-scale cases should dominate our priorities and explain what follows for how a person convinced by your argument should actually live and give. Your essay should commit to one of these horns — not merely survey both — and it should address the strongest objection to the horn you choose.
