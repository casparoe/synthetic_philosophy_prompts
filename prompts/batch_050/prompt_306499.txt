I work in the policy unit of a German Land government (one of the bigger western states), and part of my job is reviewing draft proposals coming down from Brussels, across from Berlin, and up from our municipalities, and giving a view on which level of government should actually be handling the thing. My director keeps invoking subsidiarity, but at the moment our assessments are vibes-based: someone writes two paragraphs gesturing at scale effects and democratic proximity and calls it an analysis. I've read up on the principle myself — roughly, decisions should sit at the lowest level that can genuinely achieve the aim, and higher levels should only step in where the goal can't be sufficiently achieved lower down and would be better achieved at scale — but I can't work out how to turn that into something my five-person team can apply consistantly on a Tuesday afternoon.

Please write us a decision procedure we could run on any proposal in about half a day, using only information we can realistically get hold of (no commissioning studies — we have no budget for that), and which a senior civil servant has to be able to sign off at the end. I want the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure halts with a recommendation that the lower level keeps the matter; and where it explicitly hands over to political judgement because the question is one of values (how much divergence between regions we're prepared to tolerate, say) rather than of competence. Be careful with the distinction between "could in principle do it" and "can actually do it now" — our municipalities could in principle do almost anything if you threw enough money at them — and with cross-border spillovers, which is where most honest arguments for upward referral actually live. Also say where you think the procedure will misfire, because no checklist survives contact with a genuinely novel policy area, and I'd rather know the failure modes in advance.

Then run the procedure on three concrete proposals: (1) binding limits on fertiliser run-off into rivers that cross state borders, (2) standardising the secondary-school curriculum across the whole federation, and (3) a harmonised EU-wide permitting regime for wind turbines. Assume for the sake of the exercise that at least one of these should come out as "leave it local", even though that answer will annoy a colleague of mine who thinks standardisation is always tidier. If none of them comes out that way, tell me whether the procedure is broken or whether my instinct was.
