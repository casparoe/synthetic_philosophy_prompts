I TA for an intro epistemology course and last week two of my stronger students stayed after section and got into it over G.E. Moore, and I realized I couldn't cleanly settle it. Some background: we'd just read Moore's "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925, the one reprinted in his Philosophical Papers), where he lists his famous truisms -- that he has a body, that the earth existed long before he was born, etc. The part they kept circling back to wasn't the list itself but the sentence that introduces it:

"I am going to begin by enunciating, under the heading (1), a whole long list of propositions, which may seem, at first sight, such obvious truisms as not to be worth stating: they are, in fact, a set of propositions, every one of which (in my own opinion) I know, with certainty, to be true."

Maya's opening move was that this sentence gives the game away. Moore claims certainty and then immediately hedges with "in my own opinion," and her point was that you cannot answer the external-world skeptic by announcing that, in your opinion, you're certain the skeptic is wrong. Declaring certainty is just asserting the thing under dispute with extra confidence, so the whole "defense" begs the question, and the parenthetical shows Moore half-knows it. Daniel pushed back that the hedge is the opposite of a concession: Moore is being scrupulous that he's reporting his own epistemic state, and the real argument isn't "I'm certain, therefore you're wrong" but a burden-reversal -- any premise the skeptic uses (say, that you can't know you're not dreaming) is itself *less* certain than the truisms it's supposed to undermine, so a skeptical argument can never be more rationally compelling than simply denying its conclusion. Maya said that's just a mirror-image stalemate: the skeptic can run the exact same move in reverse, ranking the principle "knowledge requires ruling out the dream hypothesis" above any particular truism, and Moore offers no neutral scale for weighing certainties, so the whole thing bottoms out in both sides thumping the table. She also pointed out -- and I checked, she's right about the text -- that the list itself includes "I have had dreams," so Moore concedes the very phenomenon the skeptic builds on. Daniel's reply was that this misreads what's being conceded: having dreamt sometimes doesn't show you can't know you're awake now, and more importantly Moore's point is that the skeptic's demand -- prove the truisms without relying on anything the skeptic questions -- is a demand no proof of anything could meet, including the skeptic's own arguments, so the skeptic is operating a double standard. Maya's final shot was that if that's the move, Moore hasn't refuted the skeptic, he's just redefined what counts as an acceptable standard for proof and declined to play, which the skeptic can happily accept as a draw.

So, referee this for me, because they both come to office hours and I have to say something. Go through it point by point: is Maya right that "in my own opinion" is a damaging hedge, or is Daniel right that it's careful bookkeeping? Does the burden-reversal actually do work against the skeptic, or does it collapse into the stalemate Maya describes? And does the "I have had dreams" bit matter at all, or is it a red herring? Say who's right about each specific move and who's more right overall, and if there's something both of them are missing about what Moore is up to in that essay, tell me what it is.
