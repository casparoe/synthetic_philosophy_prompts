My roommate is a quant at a trading firm and we got into it last weekend over whether Dutch book arguments actually show anything deep. His view, roughly: once you grant the logic of the Dutch book, you're forced into risk neutrality, and basically every normal human financial habit becomes a pathology. He wrote it up for me as a numbered argument and I've been stuck on it for days, so here it is:

(1) If a set of bets, each of which you're willing to accept, jointly guarantee that you lose money no matter how things turn out, then your willingness pattern is irrational. (This is just the Dutch book idea, no?)
(2) Whatever Dutch-book reasoning convicts of irrationality is irrational simpliciter — the argument condemns the agent, not the situation the agent happens to be in.
(3) So your credences have to behave like probabilities, since anything else is Dutch-bookable. (Standard, I'm fine with this one.)
(4) But the same logic applies to your attitudes toward money itself. If you refuse bets that are fair-or-better in expected monetary value — say you'd take a sure $45 over a coin flip between $0 and $100, or you happily pay an insurance premium that's higher than your expected loss — then a counterparty who deals with you repeatedly locks in a guaranteed margin off of you. That's a Dutch book, just executed slowly, with you voluntarily handing over money as a sure thing in exchange for avoiding variance.
(5) So risk aversion is irrational in exactly the way incoherent credences are: buying insurance is the same kind of mistake as assigning probability 0.7 to p and 0.5 to not-p.
(6) Hence the only fully rational agent is risk-neutral, and insurance, diversification, emergency funds, etc. are all just sophisticated ways of getting Dutch booked.

(6) seems nuts to me — it puts my grandmother's life insurance on par with flat-out incoherent credences. But each step looks... fine? I suspect something hides in the move from (3) to (4), since the classic setup sort of forces your credences into bets while nobody forces you to buy insurance, but I can't say precisely why that difference should matter, and my roommate says "willingness is willingness." Which step do I reject, and why did it look compelling in the first place? And here's the part that worries me: if the fix is to say Dutch books only reveal inconsistency rather than any substantive mistake, what does that cost the standard use of the argument to establish probabilism back in (3)? It would be a shame to save my grandmother by throwing out the whole theorem. Bonus: does the diagnosis change if the bookie can only offer each bet once, or if the agent knows the counterparty is adversarial rather than just a fair casino?
