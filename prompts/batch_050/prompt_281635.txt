At a workshop on decision theory, I watched two participants argue past each other about a colleague's valuation of two gambles, and I'd like a referee. The setup: an urn contains 100 balls, each either red or black, in completely unknown proportion. The colleague, Dev, says he would pay at most $20 for a ticket that pays $100 if a drawn ball is red, and likewise at most $20 for one that pays $100 if it is black. Anya argued this is straightforwardly irrational: the two tickets together are equivalent to a guaranteed $100, so whatever probability p he assigns to red, his valuations should sum to $100; valuing each at $20 means he implicitly treats red as 20% likely and 80% likely at once, which no single probability assignment can represent. Dev replied that this just assumes what is in dispute: he assigns no precise probability to red, his rational state is a range of credences, and valuing each gamble at its worst-case expected value over that range is a coherent policy under theories of imprecise probability -- caution under genuine ignorance is not inconsistency. Anya countered that the worst-case rule is exploitable: after the draw but before the reveal, Dev would pay $5 to swap his red ticket for the black ticket (worst-case value $25 beats $20), and would then pay another $5 to swap back, leaving him $10 poorer with the identical ticket, so his policy runs a money pump against himself. Dev answered that the pump only works if he is forced to evaluate each offer myopically; an agent who foresees the sequence refuses the first swap, and the right lesson is that ambiguity aversion requires resolute choice, not that it requires precise credences. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying who is right about each specific point, and then say who has the better of the overall dispute -- or whether it turns on a question neither of them has addressed.
