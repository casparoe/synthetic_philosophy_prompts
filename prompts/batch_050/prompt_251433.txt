So this has been bugging me for a while and I don't know if it's a thing anyone has studied or if I'm just noticing noise. I keep catching people doing a specific move in conversation: instead of describing something, they name a famous member of the category it belongs to and let you work out the rest. A friend says a café in our town is "very Brooklyn," and everyone at the table immediately pictures the same thing, even though none of us could list the rules for what makes something Brooklyn. Or someone calls a person "the dad of the group," or a gadget gets reviewed as "the Rolls-Royce of toasters." Nobody ever defines anything; they just point at the most famous example and trust you to figure out which features are supposed to carry over (and which ones, like the actual Brooklyn's geography, obviously aren't).

The weird part to me is that it also runs in the other direction. Ask someone what counts as a sandwich and they won't give you a definition either -- they go "hot dog? no, that's too far" or "a wrap? borderline," like they're measuring distance from a center rather than checking rules. So it feels like the categories in our heads might be organized around star examples rather than definitions, and the "very Brooklyn" move is just that structure showing itself in speech.

Is there a name for this phenomenon, or for the move of borrowing the star example as a descriptor? I know linguists talk about metaphor, but this feels different from calling someone a lion or a snake, though I can't articulate why. If it fits some established concept only partly, tell me what the nearest ones are and where each one falls short. And please say how confident you are in whatever mapping you land on, and flag the single claim you're least sure of.
