I’m a senior software engineer at a mid-sized tech company, and I sit on our eight-person Workforce Policy Committee. I have no formal philosophy background, so please treat me as an intelligent layperson: define any technical terms you use and don’t assume familiarity with the literature.

You are a professional philosopher with expertise in nineteenth-century liberal political philosophy and in the philosophy of social science. Please think carefully and methodically. Before drafting, spend a short labeled section brainstorming the strongest arguments on each side; then write the deliverable.

The situation. Our company runs two targeted programs aimed at increasing the share of women in our infrastructure and platform engineering teams: a sponsored recruiting pipeline and a funded mentorship program. A senior engineering director has circulated an internal white paper proposing that both programs be sunset at the end of the fiscal year. His argument, in essence: (1) the proportion of women in infrastructure roles is low and strikingly consistent across many companies and countries, (2) no one is barred from applying, so current outcomes reflect free self-selection and, ultimately, differences in what men and women naturally prefer, and (3) the targeted programs are therefore a "disparity of privilege" favoring one group over another, and the burden of proof lies on anyone who wants to keep them to demonstrate actual discrimination, which he says has not been shown. Three committee members find this persuasive; four of us are skeptical; one is undecided.

In preparing for the vote I came across John Stuart Mill’s The Subjection of Women (1869), and one passage from Chapter 1 struck me as directly relevant, though I’m not sure I’m reading it correctly:

"Again, in practical matters, the burthen of proof is supposed to be with those who are against liberty; who contend for any restriction or prohibition; either any limitation of the general freedom of human action, or any disqualification or disparity of privilege affecting one person or kind of persons, as compared with others. The à priori presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality."

What troubles me is that the director seems to be claiming this exact presumption for his own side: he casts the mentorship and pipeline programs as the "disparity of privilege" that must be justified or removed. My instinct is that Mill would locate the restriction elsewhere — in whatever social machinery produces such uniform "choices" in the first place — and would deny that choices made inside that machinery tell us anything about anyone’s nature. But I can’t tell whether that instinct is a defensible reading of the passage or just my own view projected onto it, and I can’t tell whether settling that interpretive question should actually change our vote.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee before our meeting. The memo should:

- State a clear, actionable recommendation (sunset the programs, retain them, or retain with specified modifications and a review date) — pick one and commit to it.
- Interpret the quoted passage: explain what Mill means by placing the burden of proof on those "against liberty," and adjudicate the dispute over which side of our case is in fact "contending for a disparity of privilege." Note that the director’s white paper leans heavily on framing our programs as partiality, so this interpretive question is doing real work in the debate.
- Apply the passage to the director’s central inference — from consistent observed self-selection to natural preference. Assess whether, on a Millian reading, that inference can carry the evidentiary weight he places on it, and what the "presumption in favour of freedom and impartiality" concretely requires of a company in our position when both sides claim it.
- Give an honest, steelmanned treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, including the possibility that targeted programs can be genuinely unfair to individuals even if the director’s inference is flawed.
- Close with the specific conditions or evidence the committee should require before revisiting the question.

The memo itself should be roughly 600–800 words, written in plain professional English suitable for an audience of engineers and HR executives. The Mill passage above does not count toward that length, and please do not pad the memo with citations or reading lists.
