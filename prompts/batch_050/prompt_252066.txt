So a few of us from the old SSC/LW diaspora run a small philosophy Discord, and we have this running series of "which position do you actually hold" flowcharts (we did one for consciousness theories that caused a minor schism). My assignment is the ontology of ordinary objects — statue-and-clay territory. The reason I want a proper decision tree and not just a list of positions is that I've watched people confidently declare "the statue obviously just is the clay" and then completely fold the moment you hit them with a case they hadn't pre-chewed, so the tree needs to sort people by their verdicts on cases, not by which label sounds sophisticated. Please don't include any question like "do you believe in temporal parts?" — our crowd mostly hasn't read the literature, and that's the point.

Here's the spec. Start everyone at the same concrete setup: a sculptor forms a lump of clay into a statue at noon, displays it, and squashes it flat at midnight — and the tree's branches should be further cases/questions that split people from there. The leaves have to include at least: the deflationist one-object view (the statue just is the clay, and all the talk of different persistence conditions is loose modal talk, not ontology); the two-coincident-things view (two objects in the same spot, different persistence conditions); the four-dimensional temporal-parts view; the view that identity is sortal-relative so "same" is ambiguous without a covering concept; and the eliminativist option that strictly speaking there are no statues at all, only fundamental stuff arranged the way statues are. Along the way, route people through these four cases I'm insisting on, because they're the ones that actually broke our server: (1) an ice sculpture whose artist declares in advance that the work survives melting as long as the puddle stays in its display tray — does stipulation decide which persistence conditions govern?; (2) a statue restored over 30 years by swapping the clay for resin one gram at a time, where a rival museum buys up all the discarded clay and reassembles it into the original shape — which museum has the statue?; (3) an untouched lump exhibited with a plaque declaring it a monument, where someone sneaks in and moves the plaque to a different lump — did the artwork move?; (4) the statue sliced cleanly in two and each half finished into a smaller copy of the original (the personal-identity fission people in our server will overthink this one, which is fine, that's the fun). Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the answer-combinations they permit — then locate the positions in that space, mark any unoccupied combinations with a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody's there because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually seperate. Aim for 6–9 branch nodes, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, because that's the part people argue about in the comments anyway.

One more ask: at the end, tell me how confident you are that the leaves you've used cover the positions people actually defend (not just the famous textbook ones), and single out the single branching question or case you're least sure about — the one where you suspect it either doesn't do real diagnostic work or sorts people into the wrong leaf. I'd rather know where the tree is weakest than get a confident-looking flowchart.
