So I'm a PhD student working on wellbeing/axiology and I've become increasingly sympathetic to a view I keep defending in our reading group: basically, that nothing is ever good in itself, at least not in a way that could ever justify bad things. On this view, pleasure, joy, wonder, love etc. aren't positive goods that get added to the ledger -- they're at best the absence or relief of various frustrated states (cravings, discomforts, boredom, longing). What matters, morally speaking, is only that there be no suffering and no unmet craving. A life with zero desire and zero contentment-of-any-kind isn't missing anything. A perfectly tranquil mind that wants nothing and suffers nothing is, on this view, just as good as a mind full of ecstatic happiness, and a world with nobody in it is no worse than a world full of thrilled people, assuming the thrilled people also have the usual frustrations.

I've stress-tested this against the standard classroom objections (the "so a pinprick outweighs all joy?" line, the "you'd prefer being a rock?" line) and I find them mostly rhetorical. But my supervisor keeps pressing me on a question I realised I can't answer well: what would actually count *against* this view, in my own eyes? Not what its critics find distasteful, but what would genuinely trouble me if it turned out to be true.

So here's what I want. Lay out the map of this view's exposure:

1. Which observations, cases, or arguments would be genuinely decisive against it (if any)? For each, spell out what the world or our intuitions would have to be like for it to land.
2. Which objections are merely awkward -- things the view has to bite a bullet on, but where biting is survivable? I suspect a lot of the standard stuff lives here, but tell me if I'm wrong about any of them.
3. What do I, as a defender, need to concede would count against it? Like, what's the equivalent of a falsification condition for a theory of wellbeing -- or is asking for that already a category mistake for normative views?
4. Relatedly: has this view quietly insulated itself? I worry that because it reinterprets every apparent good as the relief of some prior lack, it can absorb any counterexample by redescribing it ("you only enjoyed the concert because you were bored before" etc). If a view can always do that move, is that a strength or a symptom?
5. One specific thing that would personally hit me hardest: cases where suffering seems worth it *from the inside*, not as a means -- people looking back on grief or struggle and endorsing it, not because it bought them something later, but as part of a life they affirm. Does the view have an honest answer to that, or does it have to say all those people are confused about their own lives? And how bad is it if it does?

Be honest about whether the view is actually exposed to anything at all. If you think it isn't, say so, and say whether that's a problem for it or just what a correct theory looks like.
