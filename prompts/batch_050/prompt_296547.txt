I teach a second-year political philosophy seminar, about 14 students, and I've got one 75-minute session on what makes consent valid. I want a game or structured activity where they actually consent to things and then find out their consent was defective -- not a discussion of hypothetical cases, but real choices in the room where they signed off and then feel the pull to take it back.

The specific thing I want them to bump into is the asymmetry between two kinds of deception: being lied to about what the deal is (the terms, the object) versus being lied to about why you might want it (the background facts, the other party's motives). Everyone agrees the first kills consent; the second is genuinely murky, and I want them to discover that murkiness by disagreeing about real cases that just happened to them, not by me lecturing. Ideally the activity also produces a coercion case on its own -- a situation where someone's consent is formally free but something about the setup makes them feel it wasn't, so we can get at the offers-vs-threats baseline question too.

Give me rules, materials, a timeline, and any roles. No real money, no actual embarassment of students, but the choices should have some small real stake so the revocation impulse is genuine. Then a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the deception/coercion distinction and the standard conditions on valid consent (information, competence, voluntariness), tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g. students feeling the game itself was deceptive and refusing to play along -- how do I handle that?), and one honest paragraph on which feature of real consent cases the activity can't reproduce and I have to just supply by talking.
