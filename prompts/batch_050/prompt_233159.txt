I am a PhD student in machine learning, and my dissertation touches on safe reinforcement learning. I keep running into the following gap in the side-effect-avoidance literature, and I want the conceptual specification written out before I commit to a framework.

The motivating problem: we would like an agent that does its assigned task while changing as little else about the world as possible, and several approaches (penalties on departures from a counterfactual baseline, preservation of attainable utilities, restricted reachability of states) try to cash this out without the designer having to specify in advance which states of the world are good or bad. The recurring worry is that any such criterion either smuggles value judgments back in through the choice of what counts as "an effect," or fails in characteristic ways — the agent hides its effects rather than avoiding them, or learns to interfere with whatever does the measuring, or treats the entire causal future as an effect of its actions and so refuses to act at all.

Write the desiderata that a criterion of "low impact" or "side-effect avoidance" would have to satisfy in order to count as a genuine partial substitute for a full specification of human value. For each desideratum, give the case or failure mode that motivates it (for example: irreversible changes to objects the task does not concern; effects of merely carrying out the task, which must not be penalized; actions taken to cancel or disguise an earlier effect; effects that propagate far downstream through chains no one can trace; the agent's incentive to alter or deceive the sensors on which impact is computed; the way more capable agents produce more side effects and so need a criterion that does not scale its demands with capability). Distinguish desiderata you take to be non-negotiable from those that look like artifacts of one particular research lineage, and say what separates the two. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — my suspicion is that penalizing downstream effects without limit and permitting normal task performance are in tension, and that any baseline-based approach invites exactly the offsetting behavior it was meant to prevent — then say so explicitly and treat that as a result in its own right, not a defect of the list. A table is acceptable if it helps.

Finally, and this matters to me as much as the list itself: state what would change your assessment. Which single desideratum is most likely to be doing too much work, what empirical or formal result would show that a value-free criterion of this kind is achievable after all, and where is your own reasoning most exposed?
