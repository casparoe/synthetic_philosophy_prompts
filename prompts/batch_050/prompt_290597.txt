Last weekend a friend and I got into an argument that started over dinner and ended, four hours later, exactly where it began. The question: why are some countries rich and others poor? He is convinced the answer is geography and its long shadows — tropical disease burdens, landlocked coastlines, whether a region happened to get the crops and animals that could support dense settlement centuries ago. I argued that institutions do the real work: property rights, constraints on rulers, whether ordinary people can keep what they produce. We both had our stock examples. He pointed at the correlation between latitude and income; I pointed at divided places — the two Koreas, East and West Germany, towns split by old colonial boundaries — where geography is held fixed and outcomes diverge wildly. He replied that such cases are cherry-picked and that institutions are themselves downstream of historical conditions, so I'm just renaming the puzzle. I replied that "downstream" is doing all the work in his theory. A third friend kept trying to settle it with culture — work ethic, trust, religion — which satisfied neither of us.

What struck me afterward is that this argument is not peculiar to us. Professional economists and historians have been running the same three-way dispute for decades with far better data, and it hasn't converged either. My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than accepted: the stall isn't because one side is stupid or ideological, but because the question demands causal inference from exactly one run of history. There are no experiments, every candidate cause (climate, institutions, culture, trade access) is entangled with every other, and each variable plausibly causes and is caused by development itself — so each side keeps finding instruments and natural experiments that the other side dismisses as not really exogenous, and there's no shared standard for when such a dismissal is legitimate. In other words, I suspect the disagreement is really about the epistemology of macro-historical causation wearing the costume of a factual dispute about national income.

Tell me whether my diagnosis is right. Is this a genuinely factual disagreement that better data or cleverer identification could settle, a verbal disagreement where each side defines the "cause" of prosperity differently (ultimate vs. proximate, necessary vs. merely accelerating), or a clash of standards of evidence in a domain where no standard can be met? Support the diagnosis with what each camp actually argues, and tell me concretely what would count as progress given your answer — a distinction, a kind of evidence, a better question — or argue honestly that none should be expected. Begin with your diagnosis stated in a single sentence, and keep the whole response under 500 words.
