My grandmother was recently diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's, and because she had the foresight to sign an advance directive years ago -- refusing all life-prolonging treatment should she ever reach a state of severe dementia -- my family is now trying to figure out what authority that document should have over the woman she will become. This has sent me down a rabbit hole, and I've discovered I have strong but possibly incoherent gut verdicts about a range of cases. I'd like you to act as a bioethicist with expertise in personal identity and autonomy, and help me work out whether there's one principle that captures my intuitions, or whether I have to give one of them up. Please think carefully, and structure your answer as: first, your candidate principle (or two, if one won't do), stated precisely; second, a verdict-by-verdict check against my cases; third, an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate; fourth, its prediction on the new case I describe at the end. No more than 500 words total.

Here are my cases and gut verdicts. (1) My grandmother's case: she becomes severely demented, appears content, develops pneumonia, and has no current expressed wishes either way. I think the directive should be followed -- no antibiotics. (2) A competent adult carries a signed card refusing blood transfusions on religious grounds; he's brought in unconscious after an accident. I think the card must be honored, even at the cost of his life. (3) A bipolar patient in a stable phase signs a "Ulysses contract" instructing that she be hospitalized if she later shows specific manic symptoms, and that any mid-episode revocation be disregarded; the episode comes, and she loudly revokes. I think the contract should hold. (4) Same as (1), except the demented grandmother actively and repeatedly says "I want to be treated, I don't want to die," whenever the question arises -- though she can't remember the conversation an hour later. My gut says we should treat her, directive or not, and I notice this sits badly with (1) and (3). (5) A healthy 25-year-old writes, in a fit of romantic despair, that if he is ever demented he wishes to be killed rather than cared for; he never reaffirms it. I think this binds no one. (6) A directive refusing treatment written by someone already moderately demented at the time of signing. I think it carries much less weight than one written in full competence, even if the present wishes of the signers in (1) and (6) would now be identical.

I have a tentative principle of my own -- something like "the competent earlier self governs the incompetent later self, unless the later self is actively protesting" -- but I suspect it's ad hoc, and I'd rather you test it against the cases and repair or replace it than simply accept it. I'm aware that philosophers in this area distinguish between what some call critical or precedent autonomy and the current experiential interests of the patient, and that there's a real dispute about whether a severely demented person is even the same person who signed the directive; feel free to draw on that, but don't assume it settles anything. Finally, apply whatever principle you land on to a case I haven't listed: a woman whose directive refuses artificial feeding, who in her demented state eagerly eats puréed food by hand and becomes distressed when food is withheld -- should the spoon be offered?
