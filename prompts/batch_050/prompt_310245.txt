I co-organize a monthly journal club that sits between the hospital's pain medicine service and the university's philosophy department; the attendees are mostly neurology residents and one anesthesiologist, plus me, a philosophy PhD candidate who has never held a reflex hammer. Next month's session is mine, and I've chosen pain asymbolia as the entry point into the question of why pain is bad. The clinical side will know the literature far better than I do, so the session only works if the philosophy does the organizing. Here's the dialectic I want them to see: patients with certain insular and parietal lesions still report that pinpricks hurt, and can locate and grade the pain, yet they no longer seem bothered by it — no grimacing, no withdrawal, no request for analgesia — and a similar dissociation is familiar to this audience from morphine, under which patients say the pain is present but they don't mind it. The natural philosophical use of these cases is to argue that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the painful sensation itself but a separate attitude we take toward it; the natural countermove is to say that what's damaged in these patients is the motivational system, so the unpleasantness remains intact but is simply disconnected from care and action. I want the session to end with the audience genuinely unsure whether the neurology settles the philosophy or merely poses it.

Please plan my session rather than deliver it. What I need is the explanatory arc: which ideas have to be in place before the central question even makes sense to a room that thinks in lesion localizations, and in what order, with the dependencies made explicit. In particular, I'm torn on the carrying example. My instinct is to build the whole thing around morphine rather than the lesion cases, because half the room has administered it, several have presumably received it, and it's the dissociation they can verify from the inside — but I worry that morphine's muddy mechanism makes it a worse philosophical instrument than a clean focal lesion. Tell me which example should carry the session and what it must have and must lack to do that job, or propose a third I haven't considered.

I'd also like the misconception map, in the order the misconceptions will actually surface, with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. My guesses: someone will open by insisting the asymbolia patients simply don't feel pain, full stop; someone else will conclude too quickly that the badness of pain is "just an attitude" and therefore somehow unreal or optional; and the anesthesiologist will want to reduce the whole thing to receptor pharmacology. Correct my list, order it, and show me where each defusal belongs so that the objections arrive as refinements rather than derailments. Finally, give me one question I can pose in the last five minutes whose answer will tell me whether they've grasped the philosophical problem rather than merely retained the neurology, and tell me what I should openly decline to cover in a single session — I suspect the formal theories of pain's representational content belong on that list, but I want that decision argued, not assumed.
