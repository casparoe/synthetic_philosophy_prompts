I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind course and want a final-exam essay question on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness that students cannot answer by retelling the standard commissurotomy experiments or by shouting one word, emergence, modularity, or self. Build the question around this hypothetical, which you should treat as fully specified rather than as a cue to search for literature: engineers fit healthy volunteers with a temporary bilateral relay that can, for ten-minute intervals, make each hemisphere unable to influence the other while leaving eyes, hands, speech, and bodily control intact. In the triadic report task, the left visual field sees a red tool, the right visual field sees a green animal, and the participant is asked to name aloud what is common to both, point with either hand to the odd one out, and later give a sincere verbal account of why they pointed as they did. Define in the exam three candidate marks of unity that students must keep apart: phenomenal co-presence, meaning there is one field in which the tool and animal are experienced together; access unity, meaning either side can in principle broadcast the information for reasoning, speech, and choice; and practical unity, meaning the episode issues in one endorsable agent-level intention rather than two merely coordinated policies. The essay question should force students to argue which of these, if any, the ordinary split-brain results and the relay case actually trouble, and to say whether the deepest threat is simultaneous disunity, diachronic stitching, over-privileging the speaking hemisphere, or an illusion produced by treating reportability as metaphysics.

Write the question so a strong answer must do at least five things: state a precise criterion for when two simultaneous mental events belong to one subject rather than to one organism; explain why left-hand success plus verbal confabulation underdetermines whether there were two experiences or one experience with uneven access; compare a two-subjects reading, a one-subject-with-firewalls reading, and a one-field-with-defective-narration reading without assuming that whichever reading best predicts behavior is therefore phenomenologically true; use the relay case to test whether their criterion is about consciousness itself or only about post-hoc rationalization; and identify one observation that would make them abandon their view, with attention to the difference between a subject who cannot integrate information and a subject who is integrated only for purposes of speech. After the question, provide a model answer of about 700 words that takes a defensible position rather than surveying everything, and make it explicit where the model answer is making a risky metaphysical commitment. Then give a 40-mark marking scheme: five must-hit moves worth 5 marks each, 8 marks for handling the relay case as a genuinely new test rather than as a synonym for familiar split-brain findings, 4 marks for the falsifiability or defeat condition, and 3 marks for clarity. The scheme must say, in one line per move, how an A answer differs from a B answer and name the mistake that should cap an answer at C, such as equating unity with verbal report, inferring two persons from two hand competencies, treating coordination as sufficient for co-consciousness, or answering only with labels. No citations, bibliography, or appeals to real passages; the exam should be self-contained and reward reasoning about the cases as stated.
