I direct a small "economic possibilities" unit inside a national finance ministry. About once a week a line department sends us a proposal justified on wellbeing grounds — statutory workweek reductions, a GDP-plus growth target, taxes on conspicuous consumption, a citizens' leisure dividend from automation — and my three analysts must triage it within ten working days before a deputy minister signs off. We have no agreed test for what belongs in our remit.

I want the unit's framework to be Richard Jeffrey–style operational Keynes. Act as an expert on Keynes's economic essays and on institutional design. Early in "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930), Keynes writes: "We are suffering, not from the rheumatics of old age, but from the growing-pains of over-rapid changes, from the painfulness of readjustment between one economic period and another." As I read the essay, this diagnosis does real work: elsewhere in it Keynes distinguishes needs that are absolute (felt regardless of what others have) from relative ones (felt only insofar as satisfying them lifts us above others), and he treats much modern discontent in rich societies not as evidence of decline or of an unsolved economic problem, but as the pain of habits and institutions lagging behind material abundance — a transitional problem that growth alone cannot cure.

Please write a triage procedure my analysts can run on any incoming proposal: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient, evidence-based answer to each; the tests for classifying the underlying problem as (a) an unsolved economic problem of absolute needs, (b) a positional problem of relative needs, or (c) a growing-pains problem in the sense of the quoted passage — and which instruments each classification licenses (growth and redistribution, positional de-escalation, or institutional experimentation with leisure and purpose); the points where the procedure stops with a "not our remit"; and the points where it must hand the decision to the deputy minister because the question is one of values, not facts. Say explicitly which of Keynes's distinctions the procedure preserves and which it deliberately drops, and what it cannot do.

Begin by brainstorming the ways each classification could be misapplied, then give the procedure. Finally, run it on two proposals: a four-day statutory workweek for a wealthy region, and a permanent 0.5-percentage-point GDP growth target justified as funding future wellbeing. For at least one of these, let the procedure deliver the verdict my growth-oriented deputy minister will not want to hear, and say so.
