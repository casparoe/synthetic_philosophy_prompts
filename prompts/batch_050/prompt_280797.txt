I'm a high school senior in an independent study on paradoxes of rational choice, and my advisor has asked me to write down my current positions on the self-torturer puzzle so she can see whether my views actually cohere. I'd like your help stress-testing them before I submit.

For context, here is the case as I understand it. A person has a device implanted that can deliver pain across a long series of settings, say 1 to 1000. The difference between any two adjacent settings is completely imperceptible to him — he cannot feel any difference between setting n and setting n+1. He is offered a deal: each time he advances the dial by one setting, he receives a large sum of money, but he can never turn it back. At low settings the pain is trivial; at setting 1000 it is constant agony that no amount of money could compensate for. He advances one step at a time, collects the money each time, and ends up in agony.

Here are my commitments:

1. Rational preferences must be transitive. The money-pump argument convinces me: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, you can be induced to pay repeatedly to cycle through options and end up back where you started, poorer. Being exploitable in that way is a mark of irrationality.

2. A choice is rational when, by the agent's own lights at the moment of choosing, it is a strict improvement over the alternative. Each single turn of the dial qualifies: the agent gains a large sum and cannot detect any increase in pain, so by his own lights each step is a clear win.

3. Ending up at setting 1000 is a catastrophe that a rational agent must avoid. So the rational thing is to stop at some intermediate point — where exactly is a matter of personal taste in weighing money against pain, and different people could rationally stop at different settings.

4. If two options are genuinely indistinguishable to an agent in every respect he can detect, then rationality cannot require him to choose one over the other, and he should be indifferent between them.

5. What it is rational to do cannot depend on distinctions the agent could not possibly perceive. Imperceptible differences should make no difference to rational choice.

6. There is no irrationality in the agent's preferences themselves: preferring more money at imperceptibly greater pain, step by step, is not like preferring A over B over C over A.
