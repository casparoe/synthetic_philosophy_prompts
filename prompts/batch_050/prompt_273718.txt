I have no formal training in political philosophy, but I have been turning over a question in my head for some time: when a collective decision is made by vote, who is entitled to cast one? I have noticed that my own answers vary by case in ways I cannot currently justify, and I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle underneath them. Here are my verdicts, stated as honestly as I can manage.

A sixteen-year-old who works part-time and pays taxes should, I think, be allowed to vote in national elections. A foreign national who has lived in the country legally for fifteen years, raised children there, and paid every tax asked of her should also be allowed to vote, at least in local elections -- though I notice I said "local" almost automatically, and I am less sure about national ones, which already feels like an inconsistency. A citizen who emigrated thirty years ago, has no plans to return, and holds another citizenship should, in my view, lose the vote eventually, though I could not tell you when or why. I do not think tourists or short-term business visitors deserve any say, however much a policy affects their trip. I do think the residents of a territory governed by a country -- people subject to its laws but without full citizenship -- deserve a say, and I feel this one strongly.

The harder cases are these. When a region votes on secession, my gut says only the region's residents should vote, not the whole country; but when I ask myself why, the best I can come up with is that they are the ones most affected -- and that same reasoning, applied elsewhere, seems to imply that citizens of neighboring countries affected by a trade policy, or indeed everyone on Earth affected by a country's carbon emissions, should get some say in decisions currently reserved for that country's citizens, and I find I do not believe that at all. Relatedly: I feel future generations deserve representation somehow, but I cannot picture what a vote for a person who does not yet exist would even mean, so I am not sure this feeling amounts to a verdict or just a sentiment.

My tentative principle, if I have one, is that everyone whose interests are significantly affected by a decision should have a say in it. But as the secession case shows, this principle seems to endorse conclusions I reject. The obvious rival -- that everyone subject to the laws should have a say -- handles the foreign resident and the territory cases well but seems to say nothing about the emigrant, the secessionists, or the sixteen-year-old, and I am reluctant to give up on those.

Is there a single principle, or a principled combination, that captures most of these verdicts without the absurd consequence that virtually everyone gets a say in virtually everything? If achieving that requires dropping one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why. And whatever account you arrive at, please apply it to a case I have not mentioned and have been unable to settle myself: whether long-term prisoners should vote.
