I'm putting together a six-episode podcast miniseries on the Meno problem — the question of why knowledge seems more valuable than merely having a true belief — and I need to lock in the episode list by Friday. Here's the angle that's got me stuck. A friend of mine (a statistics guy, not a philosopher) insists the whole puzzle basically dissolves once you notice that knowledge and true belief produce identical outputs in any single case: if I truly believe the bridge is safe and you *know* it's safe, we both cross it, we both get to the other side, end of story. His claim is that knowledge's extra value is purely statistical — it only shows up across many repeated cases, as reliability — and once you see that, there's nothing deeper left to explain. I suspect he's missing something, but I can't quite articulate what, and I don't want to build the series around dunking on a strawman either.

So, taking his hypothesis seriously as a live option: which two or three open questions in this neighborhood actually matter most, and in what order? Candidates I have in mind: (1) whether the extra value of knowledge can be fully explained instrumentally — reliability, stability in the face of new misleading evidence, and so on — or whether there's something non-instrumental going on; (2) whether "credit" accounts (the idea that in knowledge the truth is to the believer's credit, like a skilled archer rather than a lucky one) survive cases where the agent's competence is outsourced or scaffolded, like relying on a calculator, GPS, or a search engine; and (3) whether the entire framing changes if the "believer" is an AI system that tracks truth reliably without anything like human justification, since that seems to pit the reliability answer against the credit answer directly.

Give me a defended ranking, not a survey. I want to know which questions are upstream of which others, which are tractable for a smart general audience without heavy formal machinery, and — just as important — which one you'd cut from the series entirely as overrated, and why dropping it wouldn't lose much. If your ordering depends on whether my friend's statistical deflation works, say so and show how the ranking shifts. Please write in American English.
