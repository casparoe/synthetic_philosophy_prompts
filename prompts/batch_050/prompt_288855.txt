I write a science-and-ideas column, and I'm working on a piece about consciousness research -- specifically the ongoing fight between "higher-order" theorists (a mental state is conscious only if you have some representation OF that state) and "first-order" folks (a state is conscious just in virtue of representing the world in the right way, no meta-representation needed). My editor wants a sidebar with a vivid thought experiment that shows readers what's actually at stake, and I tried drafting one over the weekend but it kept collapsing into "imagine a zombie," which is boring and my philosopher friend told me it begs the question.

So here's what I need from you: construct a thought experiment where higher-order and first-order theories give opposite verdicts about whether the subject is having a conscious experience. Constraints: (1) the scenario should be at least vaguely neurologically or psychologically plausible -- think a brain-injury patient, a split-brain subject, or an experimentally induced situation, not a teleportation machine or a Cartesian demon; (2) you can't stipulate the verdicts, i.e. you can't say "and she really was seeing red consciously" -- the theories have to generate the answers from the setup, and a reader should be able to see why; (3) the subject's observable behavior must be fully specified and must NOT settle the question (otherwise the experiment is doing nothing).

After presenting the scenario, do the legwork: state precisely what the higher-order theory says about the subject and why, then what the first-order theory says and why. Then identify the single structural feature of your case that's making the two theories peel apart -- is it the existence of a higher-order representation with no first-order target, a first-order state with no higher-order representation, or something subtler? -- and tell me what happens to the case if you remove that feature (does it stop being a disagreement case at all?). Finally, since my readers love a comeback, give me the best objection each side would raise against your scenario: how would the first-order theorist deny that your setup really instantiates what the higher-order theory needs, and vice versa?
