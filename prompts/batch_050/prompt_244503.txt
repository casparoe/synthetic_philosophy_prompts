I just spent all evening arguing with my roomate about whether special relativity really forces us into a block universe, and now I have to grade practice exams on exactly that. I don’t want our argument to bias me, so I need an outside assessment.

Act as a demanding but fair philosophy-of-physics examiner. Think carefully through both the physics and the metaphysics before grading; don’t just reward confident terminology. Exam question:

Reconstruct a Rietdijk–Putnam-style argument from the relativity of simultaneity to the conclusion that past, present, and future events are equally real. Use two observers moving relative to one another and a suitably distant event, and make the main steps explicit. Identify the strongest metaphysical assumption the argument needs. Does saying that reality is frame-relative provide a satisfactory way to resist the conclusion? Finally, distinguish the block-universe conclusion from determinism.

Student answer:

Special relativity says that simultaneity is frame-dependent. Suppose Maya and Noor briefly meet but are moving rapidly in opposite directions. Using the standard synchronization procedure for their respective inertial frames, an event E in a distant galaxy may be simultaneous with their meeting according to Maya, while a later event F in that galaxy is simultaneous with the meeting according to Noor. If what is simultaneous with us is what is real now, then E belongs to Maya’s present while F belongs to Noor’s. Since neither frame is privileged, there is no physical basis for saying that one of these events exists while the other does not. By considering further velocities, every event can be placed in somebody’s present. Thus all events are equally real and spacetime is a block.

The argument does not depend on light from E or F having already reached either observer. The observers can correct for light-travel time and calculate which distant event is simultaneous with their meeting. The real weak point is therefore not the relativity of simultaneity but the identification of an observer’s present with what is real. A presentist can deny this identification and say that only the observer’s immediate here-now is real, treating simultaneity as a coordinate convention. The argument also seems to require a transitivity principle: if E is real relative to Maya, and Maya’s and Noor’s frames are equally legitimate, then E cannot simply fail to be real relative to Noor.

A relativist can resist the argument by saying that reality itself is frame-relative: E is real for Maya, F is real for Noor, and there is no fact about what exists simpliciter. This preserves the equality of frames, but it has the strange result that two observers at the same location disagree about what exists. This is stronger than ordinary relativism about simultaneity, since it makes existence itself frame-dependent rather than merely making temporal ordering frame-dependent.

The block conclusion should not be confused with determinism. A fixed four-dimensional spacetime might contain laws that do not determine earlier states from later ones. The argument also does not directly show that temporal passage is an illusion; at most, it shows that passage cannot be understood as the successive coming into existence of global simultaneity slices.
