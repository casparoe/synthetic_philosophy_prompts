I'm part of a small reading group that has been working through the philosophy of rules — we recently spent an evening on Peter Suber's game Nomic and on the old puzzle of whether a constitution's amendment clause can be used to amend itself, and the conversation got stuck in the way conversations do when everyone agrees there's a paradox but nobody can make it vivid. I want a piece of fiction that does the work our discussion couldn't.

Write a short story set aboard a generation ship, three centuries into its voyage, that is governed entirely by a founding rulebook with two tiers of rules: a large body of ordinary rules, which any citizen assembly may change by majority vote, and a short list of "entrenched" rules — including the amendment procedure itself — which by their own text may never be altered. The plot should turn on a character (perhaps a junior archivist who actually reads the rulebook closely, unlike everyone else) who notices that the rule declaring the entrenched rules unchangeable is not itself on the entrenched list, and files a formal motion to amend it by ordinary majority — not out of malice, but for some sympathetic reason, like extending the franchise to the ship-born generation. The story should dramatize the conceptual stakes rather than lecture about them: what the assembly's lawyers argue, whether "the rulebook" is the same rulebook after the vote, whether a majority can legitimately vote away the conditions that made its own majority meaningful, and what it feels like for the characters to live inside a legal system whose foundations are being tugged at in real time.

I'd like an actual story — scenes, characters, a resolution or an intentionally unresolved ending that earns its openness — not a philosophical dialogue with fiction-flavored padding. Roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words. You needn't name-drop philosophers or cite legal theory inside the story; if the ideas about self-reference, legal identity over time, and bootstrapping authority come through in the events and the arguments the characters actually make, that's success. Feel free to invent the rulebook's other contents and the ship's social texture as you see fit, as long as the self-amendment puzzle stays at the center.
