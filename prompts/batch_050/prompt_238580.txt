I've been a reader of Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, and the surrounding blogs for well over a decade, and one pattern I've learned to watch for is a dispute that looks substantive but is actually two people using the same word to do different jobs. Last week I watched two friends of mine — call them Mara and Jonas, both software people with some philosophy exposure — have an argument that I think may be of exactly that kind, but I can't settle it myself. I'd like you to adjudicate it carefully, move by move.

The setup: their team runs a small prediction platform. A contractor, Dev, was supposed to run a particular validation test before a scheduled model update. He didn't — he was busy, figured the change was minor, and skipped it. The update went out, contained a subtle bug, and produced garbage outputs for a weekend before anyone noticed. In the post-mortem, Mara said that Dev's skipping the test *caused* the bad outputs. Jonas objected, and they went back and forth roughly as follows:

(1) Mara: The cause of the bad outputs was Dev not running the test. Everything else was business as usual; the one thing that departed from the procedure was his omission. If we want the post-mortem to name a cause, it's that.

(2) Jonas: Omissions can't be causes. Causation is a relation between things that actually happen — a bug gets deployed, code executes, servers respond. "Dev not running the test" isn't an event; there's nothing there to stand in a causal relation. The deploy caused the bad outputs. Dev merely failed to *prevent* them, which is a different notion, and conflating the two is how you end up blaming people for physics.

(3) Mara: We have a perfectly standard test for causation: counterfactual dependence. Had Dev run the test, the bug would have been caught and the bad outputs wouldn't have happened. That's the same but-for reasoning we use everywhere, including in the cases Jonas accepts. If it licenses "the deploy caused the outputs," it licenses "the skipped test caused the outputs."

(4) Jonas: But-for reasoning overgenerates catastrophically for omissions. Had I flown over and run the test myself, the outputs wouldn't have happened either. Had Dev's mother raised him to be more diligent, same result. By Mara's standard, I caused the bad outputs, and so did roughly everyone alive. A test that makes everyone a cause of everything is not a test; it's a license to blame whoever's convenient. Positive causes don't overgenerate this way because they're anchored in actual events.

(5) Mara: The fix is obvious and we use it constantly: among all the counterfactually relevant conditions, we cite the one that *deviates from what's normal or expected*. Dev had a standing responsibility to run the test; the rest of the world didn't. That's why his omission is the cause and my failure to intervene isn't. And note this isn't special pleading for omissions — we do the identical thing for positive events. The whole room full of oxygen was counterfactually necessary for the fire, but we say the spark caused it. Causal selection has always been partly about expectations and salience, for acts and omissions alike.

(6) Jonas: Two problems. First, the spark-and-oxygen move selects *among actual events* using pragmatic criteria; Mara is using it to conjure an event out of an absence, which is a much bigger metaphysical ask. Second, look at what her "normal or expected" criterion actually is: it's a norm. Dev had a *duty*. So her analysis amounts to saying that an omission is a cause when the omitter is blameworthy — which means "X caused the harm" has become a disguised way of saying "X is at fault," and the word is no longer doing any independent work. Fine for assigning responsibility in a retro, but let's not pretend we've said something about the causal structure of the world.

(7) Mara: Jonas is assuming there's some pristine, norm-free notion of causation we're defiling, but causal talk is a tool — it exists to tell us where to intervene and who should change their behavior. A notion of cause that can't distinguish Dev from the Pope is useless for running a team, and a notion of cause that refuses to count omissions can't explain why control groups, safety checks, and dead-man's switches matter. If his metaphysics can't make those distinctions, so much the worse for the metaphysics.

They both turned to me, and I honestly couldn't give them a verdict. Here's what I'd like from you:

- Go through moves (1) through (7) individually. For each, say whether it's sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and identify precisely where it goes wrong if it does. In particular: does the but-for test really overgenerate, and does the spark/oxygen analogy actually survive Jonas's "selecting among actual events" reply?
- Say who's right *about what*: is there a defensible sense in which omissions are causes, a defensible sense in which they aren't, and are Mara and Jonas even disagreeing about the same question?
- Apply the standard rationalist diagnostic: if we tabooed the word "cause," what claims would each of them actually be asserting, and how much of the dispute survives tabooing? Is the residual disagreement empirical, metaphysical, normative, or purely about linguistic convention?
- Finally: who is more right overall, and what is each of them missing about the other's strongest point?
