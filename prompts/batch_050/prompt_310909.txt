I teach a first-year undergraduate philosophy seminar of 24 students in a 75-minute session. Design a classroom activity on the social construction of kinds, using an in-class currency as the central case. Do not lecture beforehand or tell students that the activity concerns socially constructed kinds.

The activity should make students experience the difference among a physical object, a status that object has because people collectively recognize and enforce it, and the practical power that status acquires. Ideally, they should initially treat the tokens as worthless, gradually come to depend on them, and then discover that neither an individual declaration nor a unanimous verbal agreement is enough by itself to create or abolish their value. Build in a moment when the currency remains physically unchanged but its status becomes uncertain because issuers, users, and enforcers no longer coordinate.

Please provide:

1. A complete setup and rules for three or four timed phases, including materials, student roles, a scarce privilege or grade-neutral reward that gives the tokens practical significance, and a ledger or enforcement mechanism.
2. At least one controlled rule change or institutional breakdown that forces students to negotiate what makes the tokens count, who has authority over them, and whether possession, recognition, or enforceability matters most.
3. Incentives that encourage genuine strategic behavior without using money or grades, plus safeguards against one confident student simply persuading everyone to adopt a new rule by majority vote.
4. A debrief that names the target idea only after students describe what happened. Map particular moments in the activity onto claims about money: its dependence on collective acceptance, its reality as a source of constraints and opportunities, the distinction between being socially constructed and being imaginary, and the difficulty of reforming a status that people continue relying on even while criticizing it.
5. A careful final extension from money to race and gender. The debrief should make clear that these are not all constructed in the same way: money is more directly created by institutional assignment, while race and gender involve bodies, inherited classifications, personal identity, social expectations, law, and long histories of unequal treatment. Explain how the activity can illuminate the reality and persistence of socially maintained categories without suggesting that they are merely voluntary, easily revised, or unreal.
6. A list of predictable ways the activity will fail the first time—such as students treating it as ordinary barter, ignoring the ledger, obeying the instructor rather than the institution, or turning the debrief into a claim that social kinds are fake—and concrete ways to handle each.
7. An honest statement of which aspects of social construction the activity cannot reproduce and what the instructor must supply through discussion afterward.
