I've been teaching myself the population ethics literature this term -- Parfit's ladder of populations in Reasons and Persons, the mere addition paradox, and the subsequent impossibility results suggesting that no axiology can avoid at least one bullet-biting conclusion -- and for the final meeting of our reading group I'd like to contribute something less dreary than another handout of lettered populations. Write me a villanelle about the repugnant conclusion in which the two refrains are, respectively, the seemingly innocent principle that mere addition can never make an outcome worse and the conclusion toward which the argument keeps steering us, a world of vast numbers of lives barely worth living. The villanelle form should do philosophical work, not merely decorate: the refrains' recurrence should mimic the way the same apparently harmless move -- add some worthwhile lives, then smooth out the inequality with a small drop in average quality -- iterates down the ladder from the A-world to the Z-world, and the final quatrain, where the two refrains are forced to stand side by side for the first time, should stage the moment the reader realizes that rejecting the conclusion means either denying transitivity of better-than or refusing one of the tiny steps they found unobjectionable a stanza earlier.

Someone who knows the argument should be able to tell which refrain is which without being told, and should recognize in the intervening lines at least gesturing acquaintance with the real structure of the debate: the sense that each step is locally acceptable while the terminus is not, the temptation of appealing to average versus total views and why each fails, and the lurking thought that the lives added are lives that would not otherwise exist, so no one is wronged by their addition. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher who works on population axiology would find it right rather than merely allusive, but it should also work as a poem -- the repetitions should feel like accumulating weight rather than padding, and the close should land on the genuine vertigo of the problem rather than a tidy resolution, since the whole point of the literature is that tidy resolutions are precisely what we do not have.
