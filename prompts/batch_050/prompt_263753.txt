Exam question: State Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, explain what conclusion it is meant to establish about meaning and extension, and discuss whether the argument depends on the division of linguistic labor.

Student's answer: Putnam imagines Twin Earth, a planet exactly like Earth except that the liquid called water there is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ, which is indistinguishable from water in everyday life. He sets the case in 1750, before anyone on either planet had the chemistry to tell H2O and XYZ apart. Oscar on Earth and his molecule-for-molecule duplicate Twin Oscar are in identical internal states, yet when Oscar says water his word refers to H2O, while Twin Oscar's word refers to XYZ. Since their psychological states are the same but the extensions of their words differ, Putnam concludes that meaning is not in the head: two speakers can be internally identical while their words mean different things, because the reference of a natural kind term is fixed by the actual chemical structure of the samples in the speaker's environment. The argument clearly depends on the division of linguistic labor: ordinary speakers defer to experts, and it is the expert chemists who fix the reference of water as H2O, so without them there would be no fact of the matter that Oscar's word meant H2O rather than XYZ. Putnam's argument also establishes that water is a rigid designator, picking out H2O in every possible world. One might object that Twin Oscar's word is simply not translatable as water, but this misses Putnam's point that the two communities use phonetically identical words in all the same ordinary contexts.
