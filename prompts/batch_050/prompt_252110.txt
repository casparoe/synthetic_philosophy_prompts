My wife's biopsy results arrive next Friday, and our families have begun offering the consolation I find philosophically sinister: whatever will be, will be. I keep hearing Aristotle's sea battle in it — if it is already true today that there will be a battle tomorrow, or already true that there will not, then the outcome is fixed and deliberation is pointless. Write me a villanelle about that argument, set at my kitchen table rather than on any shore, that I can live with this week, and do not name Aristotle or the sea battle in the poem itself. One refrain should carry the fatalist's move, from the present truth of the prediction to the fixity of what it predicts; the other should carry the rejoinder that truth never made anything happen, that the result grounds the prediction rather than the prediction the result. The tercets should admit how persuasive the first refrain sounds at three in the morning, and the closing quatrain, where the refrains finally meet, should land on the exact point of the slide: from the harmless necessity of "if it will be, it will be" to the paralyzing conclusion that nothing we do matters. A reader who knows De Interpretatione 9 should be able to point to the line where the slide occurs; my wife, who has never read it, should simply want to keep the poem on the refrigerator.
