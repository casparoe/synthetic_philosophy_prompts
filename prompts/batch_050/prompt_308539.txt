A friend of mine who did a philosophy minor years ago was explaining epiphenomenalism to me at dinner, and then hit me with what he called the paradox of phenomenal judgment, which he said he remembered from Chalmers. His summary went something like this:

"Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences are real, but completely disconnected from the physical world -- they float free of the causal order entirely, neither causing nor being caused by anything physical. Now here's the paradox. Suppose it's true. Then when you say 'I'm experiencing red right now' or 'this headache is awful,' that speech act is a physical event, so it must have purely physical causes. Your experience itself plays no role in producing your judgment about the experience. But then a zombie version of you -- physically identical, no experiences at all -- would say all the same things with exactly the same conviction. So your belief that you're conscious has nothing to do with your actually being conscious, which means the belief is unjustified. And that's a straight-up self-contradiction: the epiphenomenalist asserts a view which, if true, would mean they have no reason to assert it. So epiphenomenalism is logically refuted, and this is basically an argument for physicalism, since only a view where consciousness is physical can explain how we manage to talk about it."

I nodded along but something felt off to me, I just couldnt put my finger on what. A few specific things I'm unsure about:

1. Is the "completely disconnected, neither causing nor caused" bit right? I had a vague memory that epiphenomenalists think the brain does produce experiences, just not the other way around, but maybe I'm confusing it with something else.
2. Is the zombie-replica point actually a fair statement of the puzzle, or is he garbling it?
3. The step from "our judgments aren't caused by our experiences" to "so the view is self-contradictory and refuted" seems too quick. Would a defender of epiphenomenalism (or even Chalmers himself, who I gather isnt a physicalist) just accept that? Or is there a difference between the view being literally inconsistent and it having an awkward consequence about how we know we're conscious?
4. And does any of this actually support physicalism, or would the same puzzle about how we know about consciousness come back for other views too?

Basically: go through his summary, tell me which parts a careful philosopher of mind would accept, which parts are subtly wrong or overstated, and whether his final "so epiphenomenalism is dead and physicalism wins" conclusion survives once the summary is corrected.
