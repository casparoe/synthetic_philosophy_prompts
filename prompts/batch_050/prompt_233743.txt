Two months ago my phone was stolen, and the strangest part of the aftermath was how much of my own past seemed to go missing with it: photographs I can no longer summon, appointments I no longer know I have, numbers I once 'knew' in the way one knows things without ever having memorised them. A philosopher friend assures me this is no metaphor and pointed me to the extended mind thesis — the case of Otto and his notebook, the parity principle, the claim that a resource constantly carried, automatically trusted and effortlessly consulted can be part of one's memory in the literal sense. My insurer disagrees: my claim for cognitive rehabilitation was rejected on the grounds that a stolen handset is property damage, not a head injury.

Write my letter of appeal, in the voice of an extremely reasonable and quietly overreaching policyholder who has done his reading. The letter should press the parity principle against the insurer's skin-and-skull boundary, answer their objection that the phone's contents were merely the causes of my remembering rather than my remembering itself, and then — this is where the satire lives — keep going: my sense of direction, kept in the maps app; my wife, custodian of our shared diary; the search engine holding everything I have ever looked up, until the letter has rebuilt the cognitive-bloat objection out of the claimant's own mouth. Deadpan throughout; the comedy should come from the philosophy being applied correctly and carried one step past where it should be. No more than 500 words, in British English.
