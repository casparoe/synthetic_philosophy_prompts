I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on scientific progress, and my students keep conflating two things: how well confirmed a theory is, and how close to the truth it is. I want to pry those apart by giving them a decision tree that sorts the main accounts of verisimilitude (truthlikeness) by the questions that actually separate them, so that a student who answers the questions honestly lands on a position without ever having been asked "are you a Popperian?" I already know Popper's original definition — more true consequences and no new false ones — was shown by Miller and Tichý to trivialize: on it, no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other false theory. So Popper's account should appear as a dead-end leaf with that failure marked on it, not as a live option.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the diagnostic questions, then the positions located at the combinations of answers they allow. The questions I want on the tree, though you may add or reorder them if the logic demands it: whether closeness to truth is a matter of a theory's consequences (content) or of which atomic states of affairs it gets right (structure); whether all consequences count or only "relevant" ones (the worry being that any truth p also yields irrelevant weakenings like "p or it rains in Tokyo"); whether the measure must be invariant under re-axiomatization and under switching to a logically equivalent language with different primitive predicates (this is Miller's language-dependence objection, and I want a node where it bites); and whether the relation is merely comparative (A is closer than B) or quantitative (a numerical distance from a target truth). The leaves must include: the relevant-consequence repair of Popper associated with Schurz and Weingartner; the likeness or average-distance program associated with Tichý and Oddie, where a theory's truthlikeness averages how its constituent claims score against the fully true constituent; Niiniluoto's quantitative estimated-truthlikeness approach, which combines distance with degrees of confirmation (this is the one leaf where my students' conflation is licensed rather than corrected, so say so); a structuralist leaf in the style of Kuipers, where theories are regions in a space of structures and closeness is measured by how those regions sit relative to the true structure; and a pessimist leaf for the view that no language-invariant objective notion of truthlikeness exists. Gloss each leaf in one sentence so a student who reached it by answering questions can recognize it.

Phrase every node as a question a student could answer using only toy cases — e.g., theories stated over three atomic facts, one of them the true world — and not by knowing the literature. For any combination of answers with no named position, note whether the gap looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate by these questions, and say what further question would separate them. End with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} naming the one diagnostic question that does the most sorting work in your tree.
