I've spent a semester in a mind-and-language reading group going back and forth on metaphor, and I want a diagnosis of why the central fight never resolves. On one side, Davidson: a metaphor means nothing beyond what its words literally mean, there's no hidden second meaning to decode, and what a metaphor does is get you to notice things rather than say anything. On the other side, conceptual metaphor theory: metaphor is a system of cross-domain mappings (argument understood through war, time through space) that structure ordinary thought, with the systematicity of everyday talk as the evidence. My hunch is that the two are mostly answering different questions -- Davidson about what utterances mean and how communication works, the mapping theorists about how cognition is organized -- so a conceptual metaphor theorist could grant Davidson's point about meaning and lose nothing essential, and a Davidsonian could grant the mappings as psychology while denying they generate meanings. Am I right that the dispute is (mostly) not a real one? Diagnose it using what each side actually commits to, and find out whether there's any single question they'd answer differently. The best candidate I can see is novel metaphors: mapping theory seems to predict which novel metaphors are instantly comprehensible and which arent, while Davidson offers no account of how we get a new metaphor at all and maybe thinks none is needed. If that is a genuine divergence, say what would count as progress -- a conceptual distinction, an experimental result on comprehension, something else -- and whether the loser could just redescribe any result so the debate survives anyway.
