I took a philosophy of math seminar last year and got into a running argument with one of my friends about Gödel's incompleteness theorems and what they mean outside of logic. He's on the Penrose-ish side, thinks the theorems show minds can't be machines, maybe something about truth outrunning proof in general. I've landed on a pretty deflationary position and I want to stress-test it properly.

Here's the position: the incompleteness theorems are results about formal systems -- roughly, consistent, recursively axiomatizable theories that can represent a certain amount of arithmetic. Any attempt to export them to minds, truth, AI, theology, whatever, has to cross a bridge, and the bridge always turns out to be an extra premise doing all the work. The classic one is the slide from "if the system is consistent, its Gödel sentence is unprovable in it" to "we can see that the Gödel sentence is true" -- which requires assuming the system is sound or at least consistent in a way we supposedly can't assume for ourselves. Similarly, the anti-mechanist argument needs the premise that human mathematical reasoning is, in principle, captured by one single fixed formal system, and I don't see why anyone should grant that rather than thinking of us as a messy, fallible, possibly inconsistent set of dispositions that no single theory characterizes. So my view is: the theorems constrain formal theories of arithmetic, full stop, and every famous "implication" beyond that evaporates when you make the bridging premise explicit.

What would count against this? I want the strongest possible case that the deflationary reading is wrong or at least incomplete, and I want it sorted from fatal to merely awkward. In particular: is there any version of a Gödelian argument that doesn't rely on the soundness/idealization moves I just complained about, or that justifies those moves in a way I should accept? Could it turn out that my "no single fixed system" response actually concedes the interesting point (e.g. if any plausible idealization of human reasoning ends up Gödel-incomplete anyway)? Are there non-mathematical uses of incompleteness -- not the Lucas/Penrose stuff, but anything in epistemology, law, computer science, theories of rationality -- that genuinely inherit force from the theorems rather than just borrowing the vibe? And finally: has the deflationary position itself been hedged into triviality, i.e. is it only safe because I've defined "export" narrowly enough that nothing counts? If so, tell me what a real test case would look like. The thing I'd find most persuasive is a concrete argument where making the bridging premise fully explicit leaves it still standing.
