The philosopher Daniel Dennett has argued that the traditional question of whether free will exists is badly posed, and that we should instead ask which varieties of free will are worth wanting: which capacities — for instance, being responsive to reasons, anticipating the consequences of one's actions, restraining one's impulses, deliberating without being coerced, or being the sort of agent whose future behavior can be shaped by blame and praise — are both compatible with a thoroughly naturalistic, deterministic picture of the mind and sufficient to underwrite our practices of moral responsibility. On Dennett's view, the libertarian ability to have done otherwise in some absolute, causally unconditioned sense is neither real nor especially desirable, whereas other capacities that have historically traveled under the name of free will are real, valuable, and worth cultivating.

Friedrich Nietzsche, writing roughly a century earlier, attacked free will as a fiction invented so that human beings could be made accountable, guilty, and punishable — a conceptual tool, he suggested, of priests and moralists. Yet Nietzsche also celebrated self-mastery, the strength to keep one's promises over long stretches of time, and the rare individual who has earned the right to make commitments; and he scorned the picture of a person as a mere plaything of whichever drive happens to be strongest at the moment.

Suppose Nietzsche were presented with Dennett's framework, explained carefully and on its own terms. Which varieties of free will, if any, would he concede are worth wanting, and which would he dismiss as leftovers of the moralized conception he despised? In your answer, reason from Nietzsche's known commitments — his critique of responsibility and punishment, his naturalistic account of agency as an interplay of drives, and his admiration for self-overcoming — rather than merely classifying him as a compatibilist or incompatibilist. Where would his agreement with Dennett run deepest, and where would he insist that Dennett's project still smuggles in moralistic assumptions about accountability that a consistent naturalist should abandon?
