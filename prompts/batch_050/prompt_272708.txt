Last week at a pub quiz night I got stuck listening to two friends, Priya and Marcus, go at it about machine translation for like forty minutes, and I want a referee. Act as a philosopher of mind and language who also actually knows how modern translation systems work -- think carefully, maybe sketch the strongest version of each side before you start ruling on anything.

Some context: Marcus had just read Douglas Hofstadter's piece "The Shallowness of Google Translate" (The Atlantic, 2018) and was waving it around. The bit he kept quoting was this: "To my mind, translation is an incredibly subtle art that draws constantly on one's many years of life experience, and on one's creative imagination." The argument went roughly like this:

Round 1. Priya said Google Translate plainly understands language in the only sense that matters, because it reliably produces correct translations of sentences nobody has ever written before, and if a human did that we'd credit them with understanding without a second thought. Marcus read out the Hofstadter passage and said the system has neither life experience nor creative imagination, it's just very fancy pattern matching, so it doesn't understand anything.

Round 2. Priya said the passage is an assertion dressed up as an argument, and that "life experience" is doing no real work -- the system was trained on billions of sentences produced by people with life experience, so the experience is baked into the data whether the machine lived it or not. Marcus said a recording of experience isn't experience, any more than a thermostat responding to the cold feels chilly.

Round 3. Priya said the thermostat comparison is the real cheat here, because the system actually does the things we use as tests for understanding: it resolves ambiguous words like "bank" using context, it gets pronouns right in ways that require knowing how the world usually goes, it handles implicature-ish stuff. Marcus said it resolves "bank" purely from co-occurrence statistics and has no model of any situation, only of texts, which is why it breaks in ways no bilingual human ever would -- and that Hofstadter's whole point is that this difference shows up empirically, in the *kind* of errors.

Round 4. Priya said fine, but "understand" has no precise defintion anyway, so the whole fight is verbal and they should just agree to use "understands*" with an asterisk. Marcus said no, there's a real stake: whether the system's future failures will look like human slips or like something alien, and that's a testable prediction, not a matter of words.

I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, and I don't have a dog in this. Can you go through the four rounds one by one and say who's right about each specific move -- including whether the Hofstadter passage actually supports what Marcus wants it to support, whether Priya's "baked into the data" reply lands, and whether her final "it's all verbal" retreat is a draw or a loss? Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
