I'm in a phil of religion seminar this semester and we're on the problem of evil, mostly Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion. In Part XI Philo runs through what he calls four circumstances that generate pretty much all the suffering in the world. From my notes, roughly: first, animals are spurred to action by pain rather than just by diminished pleasure; second, the world runs on general laws instead of God tweaking things case by case; third, creatures are given pretty stingy powers relative to their needs (we tire, hunger, sicken); fourth, nature's machinery is sloppily put together, so it overshoots into droughts, floods, disease. The bit that matters for my question: Philo concedes it's not impossible that all four are necessary and unavoidable, but insists none of them appear necessary to human reason, since we can picture a world just as good or better without each one. (That's paraphrase, not an exact quote, I don't have the book on me.)

My roommate, who has never taken a philosophy class and it shows, turned this into an arguement over breakfast and now claims Hume refuted way more than he meant to:

(1) If some feature of the world produces a lot of suffering, and the world would be just as good or better without it, then no perfectly good designer would include that feature.
(2) Philo's four circumstances each produce a lot of suffering, and per the passage none of them is necessary; a world without each is conceivably just as good or better.
(3) So no perfectly good designer made this world.
(4) But nothing in (1) required the designer to be perfectly good. A designer with even a slight preference for good over suffering would leave out features that cause suffering for no gain. Costless improvements get made by anyone who cares even a little.
(5) So no designer with any preference for the good at all, however weak, made this world.
(6) The only designers left are ones totally indifferent to good and evil (or ones that actively like suffering, which is the same problem flipped).
(7) But a designer indifferent to good and evil is, for explanatory purposes, just chance with extra steps; it predicts nothing a blind process wouldn't.
(8) So Hume's four-circumstances argument really shows the world wasn't designed at all, by anyone.

Here's my problem. Philo's own conclusion in the Dialogues is way more modest than (8), something like the cause of the universe probably has no moral character we can grasp, not that there is no designer, full stop. So the argument seems to prove too much, and it must break somewhere. But I went through it twice and every step looked fine in the moment.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look okay? A few ground rules for the answer:

- Start from the passage. Does Philo actually hand my roommate premise (2)? I'm suspicious of the slide from the circumstances not appearing necessary to human reason to their flat-out not being necessary, but I can't tell if that's the fatal move or just a premise that needs a small repair.
- Price the alternatives. If I keep (3) but reject (4)–(5), what does it cost? Does Hume's case against a benevolent God still stand if the four circumstances might be secretly necessary after all? And what should someone who believes in an indifferent or morally mixed deity (Manichean, lazy demiurge, whatever) actually say to (6) and (7), because my roommate's parenthetical there feels like he's waving at a hole in his own argument?
- I'd rather lose the later steps than (1)–(3), since I want the argument against perfect benevolence to survive. But if the honest diagnosis kills (1) or (2), say so.
- Don't just tell me a theist would deny premise (1). I want to know where the reasoning slips as a piece of reasoning, and what each rescue attempt costs.
