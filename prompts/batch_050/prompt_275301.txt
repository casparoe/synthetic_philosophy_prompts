In 2001 Portugal decriminalised the possession of all drugs for personal use, defined as quantities of up to ten days' average supply. Trafficking remained a crime, but users caught with small amounts were no longer prosecuted; instead they were summoned before regional 'Commissions for the Dissuasion of Drug Addiction' — panels typically comprising a legal official, a doctor and a social worker — with authority to order treatment, impose small fines or community obligations, or, as often happened, to suspend proceedings altogether, particularly where the person was judged not to be addicted. Over the following two decades drug-related deaths and HIV infections among users fell sharply and overall consumption did not rise, though how much of this is attributable to the reform itself, as opposed to the accompanying expansion of treatment provision and a declining heroin market, is contested.

Analyse this episode through Foucault's account of power, knowledge and discipline. On the face of it, the reform is a move away from the juridical-sovereign mode of power — the prohibition, the trial, the prison term — towards something Foucault would recognise as the disciplinary-administrative management of bodies: the user is no longer condemned but examined, classified and, where deemed necessary, normalised through treatment; and the dissuasion commission looks very much like an examination in his technical sense, a ritual of surveillance that simultaneously constitutes the 'drug user' as an object of medical and social knowledge. Set out this reading carefully. What, on it, has genuinely changed and what has merely been redistributed? Who now exercises power over the user, and in virtue of what claims to knowledge? And what, given Foucault's insistence that power is productive rather than merely repressive, do the falling death rates actually show — are they evidence that power has relaxed its grip, or that it has found a more efficient form?

Then turn the analysis around. A standing criticism of Foucault's framework is that it cannot register differences between more and less oppressive arrangements: if every regime of care is also a regime of control, then replacing prison with a commission whose proceedings are usually suspended and which most users attend without coercion looks, from within the theory, like no improvement at all. So which is it? Does the Portuguese case vindicate Foucault — power did not retreat, it relocated into public health, epidemiology and the dossier? Does it embarrass him — a reform that plainly reduced suffering is illegible as an improvement in his categories, which suggests the categories are defective? Or does it bring out a distinction his framework can in fact draw, between power that totalises and power that leaves room for refusal, even if Foucault himself rarely drew it? I am less interested in a verdict on Portuguese drug policy than in what the episode does to the theory, so be disciplined about the mapping: say exactly which features of the reform fit which concepts, and where the fit is forced rather than natural.
