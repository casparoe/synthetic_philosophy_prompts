I'm putting together a reading for a small undergrad seminar and could use help with this one: read Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice through Aristotle's views on money and exchange in the Politics and Nicomachean Ethics. Specifically I'm thinking of Aristotle's distinction between natural household acquisition (getting what you need, with limits set by the good life) and unlimited money-making, his claim that usury is the most unnatural way of getting wealth because money born from money perverts what coinage is for, and his account of exchange as needing some kind of proportionate reciprocity to hold a community together.

Shylock is the obvious test case -- a man whose whole livelihood is money breeding money, and whose bond literalizes the perversion: interest paid in flesh instead of coin. But don't stop at the easy reading. Antonio's wealth is tied up in risky overseas ventures, which Aristotle would presumably have its own worries about (pure gain from trade at a distance, exposure to fortune), yet the play codes him as generous and Shylock as monstrous. And Belmont runs on inherited wealth, lavish gifts, and the ring plot, which is arguably a parody of contract and exchange. So: does the play actually vindicate Aristotle's framework, or does it expose something the framework can't see -- say, that the line between "natural" and "unnatural" acquisition conveniently maps onto who's inside the community and who isn't? Where does the play resist the Aristotelian reading and an honest critic has to admit it? Which scenes would you have the seminar actually sit with?
