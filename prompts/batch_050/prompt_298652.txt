A colleague in my department circulated the following argument, which he built directly on a passage from Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, and I have been unable to locate the fault in it — though I am confident there is one, because the conclusion cannot be right. First the passage:

"The wise man does not deprecate life nor does he fear the cessation of life. The thought of life is no offence to him, nor is the cessation of life regarded as an evil. And even as men choose of food not merely and simply the larger portion, but the more pleasant, so the wise seek to enjoy the time which is most pleasant and not merely that which is longest."

Now the argument:

(1) Good and evil imply sentience: nothing can be good or bad for a person except by entering, in some way, into what that person experiences.

(2) Death is the privation of all sentience; the dead experience nothing.

(3) Therefore death itself is neither good nor bad for the one who dies.

(4) As the passage indicates, the value of a life, like the value of a meal, lies in how pleasant it is and not in how long it runs; duration as such contributes nothing of value.

(5) An event can be bad for a person only in virtue of producing some state that is bad for that person; badness must terminate somewhere, in a bad condition of the subject.

(6) So an event that merely hastens death — a fatal diagnosis, a mortal accident — is not bad for the person insofar as it kills; it is bad only to the extent that it occasions pain or distress before death.

(7) Hence preferring a longer life to a shorter but equally pleasant one is irrational, and so is devoting resources to safety, preventive medicine, or life extension beyond what pre-death comfort requires.

(8) The same reasoning covers everything that follows death: since one will not exist, nothing posthumous — one's reputation, the fate of one's projects, promises made to one — can harm or benefit one.

(9) And since murder simply brings about the victim's death, and death harms no one, murder does not harm its victim; whatever is objectionable about it must consist entirely in its effects on other people.

(10) But murder plainly does harm its victim — it is close to the paradigm of harming someone — so at least one step above must be rejected.

I take it that (1) through (4) are simply Epicurus's own position, so if the reasoning is sound, Epicurus is committed to (9), and if (9) is false, either Epicurus's hedonism about welfare falls with it or one of the inferential steps fails. I cannot tell which. Which step do you reject, and why does it look so plausible at first glance? If more than one step is vulnerable, I would like them ranked by how much each costs to abandon — in particular, whether resisting (7) requires only a more careful reading of the passage about food and time, while resisting (9) requires something philosophically larger, such as an account of how a person can be harmed by what they never experience. If forced to choose, I would keep (10) and give up whatever blocks it, but I want to know what that choice actually commits me to — whether I can still accept the core Epicurean thought that death is nothing to us, or whether rejecting (9) drags (3) down with it.
