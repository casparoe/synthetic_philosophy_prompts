Two colleagues of mine, Anika and Dev, were debating Mīmāṃsā epistemology over lunch, and their argument spilled into a case they invented on the spot. For context: the Mīmāṃsā school holds that validity is intrinsic to cognition (svataḥ prāmāṇya) — a belief or utterance is presumed valid as it arises, and only specific defeaters can overturn it — and that the Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya), which is precisely why no author's defects can taint it. Their case: a research lab trains a language model on millions of texts and lets it generate a long, coherent treatise on soil chemistry; no human wrote or checked any particular sentence. The question was whether this treatise counts as testimony, and whether its claims carry any presumptive warrant.

(1) Anika argued the Mīmāṃsā framework vindicates the treatise: validity doesn't need to be earned by auditing an author's reliability; since cognition is intrinsically valid, the sentences are warranted unless we find a defeater, and Mīmāṃsā was right that the demand to establish warrant positively from the speaker's credentials is misguided.

(2) Dev replied that this equivocates on testimony. Testimony is essentially an interpersonal act — a speaker vouching. An authorless text isn't testimony at all; it's evidence, like tree rings or sediment layers, and tree rings aren't "presumed valid," they're used as premises in inferences from their known causal origins. Authorlessness doesn't purify a text, as Mīmāṃsā thought; it removes it from the testimonial category altogether.

(3) Anika answered that the tree-ring comparison fails: the treatise consists of grammatical, meaningful sentences, and you understand them as assertions before you know anything about their provenance — understanding and presumptive uptake come together. Moreover, Dev's own practice is Mīmāṃsā-like: when a stranger gives him directions or he reads an anonymous note, he believes it unless something seems off. Auditing every speaker's reliability first is impossible, since you'd typically need further testimony to do the auditing, generating a regress.

(4) Dev conceded the regress point has force but distinguished two things he accused Mīmāṃsā (and Anika) of running together: a practical presumption (believe first, check later) versus the actual source of warrant (which is still the speaker's known or presumed reliability — the presumption is just our policy for applying it cheaply). He also pressed a disanalogy: the model was trained on human writing, so the treatise has derivative authorship, like a compilation, whereas the Mīmāṃsā Veda is eternally unauthored, not merely author-unknown. So even if Anika's view works for the Veda on its own terms, the AI case is different.

(5) Anika granted the disanalogy partially but said it cuts against Dev too: if the treatise is a compilation of human testimony, then on Dev's own view it should inherit testimony's presumptive warrant, yet Dev's framework gives no verdict here because no individual's reliability is checkable — a case intrinsic validity handles easily and his view can't.

(6) Dev's final move: intrinsic validity entails that even a known hallucination-prone system's output is presumptively valid until defeated. But when we know a source errs often, the presumption rationally flips to suspicion — which shows the presumption was always tracking known reliability, and was therefore extrinsic all along.

Neither would yield, and I stayed out of it. Please adjudicate: assess each of the six moves individually — which are sound, which rest on a confusion, and which concede more or less than the speaker realizes — then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing about the Mīmāṃsā position itself.
