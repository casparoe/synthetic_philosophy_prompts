So I've been chewing on loss aversion and the endowment effect for a while now. The Sequences basically trained me to file both under "bias, debias it," but the more actual cases I run, the less I believe that flat verdict. Here are my gut calls on a bunch of cases, and what I want is the principle (if there is one) that's generating them -- or to be told which of my verdicts has to die.

1. I won concert tickets in a work raffle. I would never have paid $300 to see this band, but when a coworker offered me $400 for them I said no, and honestly I don't think that was irrational. Once the tickets were mine, selling felt like losing something, not like turning down $400.

2. A friend is holding a stock that's down 50% because selling would "make the loss real." That feels like pure bias. The money is gone either way; the sell button doesn't change that.

3. The classic mug experiments, where people get handed a mug and five minutes later demand twice what they'd have paid for it. Verdict: bias. Come on. You've owned the mug for five minutes.

4. My grandmother's ring. You could offer me triple the market price and I wouldn't sell, and I'd say that's not just excusable, it's obviously correct behavior, and anyone who sold would be doing something worse than making a math error.

5. I'd refuse a 50-50 bet where I win $120 or lose $100, and I count that refusal as a genuine mistake on my part -- positive EV, tiny stakes, textbook risk aversion doing me no good. But I'd also refuse a 50-50 bet where I win $120k or lose my entire $100k of savings, and that refusal I endorse wholeheartedly. Not sure those two sit comfortably together given that they're the same shape of decision.

6. I stayed in a mediocre apartment for three extra years mostly out of inertia when a cheaper, better one was available. Verdict: bias, and an expensive one.

The obvious problem: cases 1 and 3 look structurally identical (won't sell a thing for more than you'd pay for the thing) and I split them. Cases 2 and 4 both involve "it's mine and letting go hurts" and I split those too, in the opposite direction. So either there's some variable doing work that I haven't articulated -- time of possession? whether the thing is a store of value vs. a vessel of meaning? whether the loss is "real" in the sense of being irreversible? -- or one of my verdicts in each pair is just wrong.

So: give me the best candidate principle (or two, if the field is genuinely split), stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is motivated by which case. Tell me which of my six verdicts it saves and which it overturns, and don't flinch if the answer is that I'm wrong about the ring or wrong about the tickets. Then run the principle on two cases I haven't listed: (a) someone who refuses a free phone upgrade becuase they'd have to relearn the interface, and (b) deliberately exploiting your own loss aversion as a commitment device -- Beeminder-style, staking money you forfeit if you don't hit a goal. If loss aversion is a bug, is weaponizing the bug against yourself coherent, or is it a sign the "bug" framing was always too glib?
