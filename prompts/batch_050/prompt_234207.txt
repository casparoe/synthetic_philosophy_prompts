Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day political philosophy workshop, and I want to be ready for the discussion period. The paper defends the following thesis: employment in developing-country export factories is, in the typical case, mutually beneficial exploitation. That is, the jobs leave workers better off than their actual alternatives (a point I grant the economists), yet the wage bargain is still wrongful, and the wrongness does not consist in harm. My positive claim is that the exploitation lies in how the surplus from the employment relationship is divided: the fairness of a bargain should be assessed not against the wage a competitive market would generate, nor against the worker's pre-transaction baseline, but against the wage that would obtain under background institutions that justice actually requires — institutions under which workers' outside options would be far better, and therefore their bargaining power far greater, than they in fact are. Because the actual wage reflects bargaining power generated by an unjust status quo, consent does not launder the transaction. The policy upshot I defend is that this grounds neither boycotts nor laissez-faire but a duty on multinational employers and their home states to pay a wage premium above the local market rate, calibrated to the counterfactual just-baseline wage, even though paying it is supererogatory relative to what the workers can demand.

The audience is small and I know its composition: a labor economist who works on garment-sector wages and is deeply suspicious of counterfactual baselines she cannot estimate; a Kantian ethicist who thinks my framework still treats workers as means because it prices the wrong rather than prohibiting it; a Marxist political theorist who will say my account individualizes and moralizes what is a structural feature of capital accumulation, and that a "fair wage" under capitalism is a contradiction he has no interest in rescuing; and a handful of graduate students, most of whom have read the standard literature on mutually beneficial exploitation and will have its usual moves memorized.

Prepare me. Give me the objections I will actually receive from this audience, roughly ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does if I handle it badly. Phrase each objection the way the person raising it would phrase it, in their own vocabulary — the economist's worries about identifying the counterfactual baseline and about disemployment effects should sound like an econometrician's, not a philosopher's parody of one. For each objection, tell me what I should concede immediately (I would rather give ground early than look evasive), what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in two minutes, with an honest note on where that reply is soft. Identify the single weakest joint in the argument as I have stated it — I suspect it is the move from "the baseline is unjust" to "this particular employer owes the premium," but I want your own assessment rather than confirmation of mine — and tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Finally, be calibrated: for each predicted objection, indicate how confident you are that this audience will in fact raise it and in roughly that form, and single out the prediction or recommended reply you are least sure of, so I know which part of the prep sheet to double-check against my own judgment.
