My reading group has been circling an argument that seems to dissolve the concept of path dependence, and none of us can find the move to reject. I'd like a diagnosis.

(1) An outcome is path-dependent only if, had small early events gone differently, a different outcome would have been selected; that contrast with the merely inevitable is what makes the notion do explanatory work.
(2) The canonical cases qualify: the QWERTY layout, VHS over Betamax, narrow-gauge railways all trace back to contingent early choices amplified by increasing returns.
(3) But amplification of small differences is ubiquitous: given enough time, arbitrarily minor changes in initial conditions -- a postponed meeting, a different teacher, a ship that sails a day late -- ripple outward and alter which firms, technologies, and institutions end up existing at all.
(4) So virtually every large-scale outcome would have been different had small early events gone differently.
(5) Hence virtually every outcome is path-dependent by the standard stated in (1).
(6) A predicate that applies to everything distinguishes nothing and explains nothing.
(7) Therefore path dependence is an empty concept; historians who invoke it are merely redescribing contingency in technical dress.

I would like to keep (2) -- I am not prepared to surrender the canonical cases -- and (3) strikes me as plain causality rather than a contestable thesis. Which premise or step should go, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? If your answer is to tighten the definition offered in (1), I want the tightened version to do real work: name some actual outcome that would count as not path-dependent under it, and explain how we could ever tell the difference empirically rather than by stipulation.
