So I've been thinking about conceivability arguments in philosophy of mind (zombies, disembodied minds, that whole genre) and I've landed on a position I want stress-tested. Roughly: conceivability is a decent guide to possibility, but with a distance limit. I call it modal locality -- our imagination was calibrated (by actual experience, by fiction, by everyday counterfactual reasoning) on possibilities close to home, so it's trustworthy for nearby possibilities, ones that differ from the actual world along a few familiar axes, and it degrades the further out you go. Swapping the outcome of a coin flip, imagining a chair in an empty room, even imagining slightly different laws of traffic: fine, conceivability works there. But conceiving of a zombie world or a disembodied mind is a far-field extrapolation, and my imagination just isn't licensed out there. So Chalmers-style conceivability arguments fail not because conceivability never tracks possibility, but because they're using the instrument way outside its range.

What would count against this? I want a map of the position's exposure, not a polite summary of "both sides." Are there cases where it clearly fails close to home, which would be even more damaging?
