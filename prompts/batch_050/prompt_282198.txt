So I sit on my university's animal research ethics committee, and we've been asked to rule on a proposed policy change that's causing real friction. Background: we oversee a lot of protocols, and currently we evaluate harm roughly per-protocol without any systematic way of comparing, say, a study on 60 mice against one on 8 crows against one on 3 macaques. A new member, a computational neuroscientist, has proposed we adopt what she calls "neuron-weighted welfare accounting": when we assess the moral cost of a protocol, we multiply the number of animals by the animal's cortical neuron count, so that the aggregate score tracks the total amount of "experience-capable tissue" being subjected to distress. Under this scheme 60 mice and 3 macaques come out surprisingly comparable, while 8 crows get scored on a special bird-adjusted column because corvids pack a huge number of neurons into a small forebrain. She argues this is the only non-arbitrary, non-speciesist metric on the table, and points out that our current practice implicitly just weights by how cute or humanlike the animal seems, which is worse.

The pushback, mostly from a philosopher on the committee and a couple of the vets, is that brain size is simply morally irrelevant: suffering is suffered by individuals, one at a time, and a mouse's distress isn't a fraction of a macaque's just because its cortex is smaller. There's also a fight about what happens at the margins of the policy: under pure neuron-weighting, you could in principle justify unlimited harm to insects or fish as long as the per-animal neuron count is tiny, and conversely a single large-brained animal would effectively get veto power over any protocol. The committee chair wants a written decision memo from me before the next meeting. Please draft it: recommend either adopting the neuron-weighted policy, rejecting it, or adopting some modified version of it (you'll probably want to invent the modification), give the reasoning in terms a mixed committee of scientists and non-scientists can follow, and include a genuinely fair statement of the strongest argument on whichever side loses -- not a strawman, since the loser will be in the room when this is read out. Also flag, briefly, any point where the memo's recommendation depends on an empirical claim about animal minds that we might just be wrong about, because I suspect that's where the real fight will happen.
