I work on the safety review team at a mid-sized AI lab, and about twice a month an engineer shows up with some version of the same plan: the model can't reliably do task X (or we can't reliably check its work on X), so let's break X into smaller questions, hand the pieces to weaker or distilled copies of the model, maybe recurse a couple levels deep, and then stitch the answers back together. Iterated amplification / distillation-flavored stuff, though they almost never call it that. I need a way to review these proposals that's better than my current method, which is vibes plus asking "but what if the subtasks are wrong?"

Act as an expert on scalable oversight and alignment who has seen a lot of these proposals fail. Before writing the final procedure, brainstorm out loud the distinct ways a decomposition can silently break competence or break alignment even when every individual subtask looks fine -- then turn that into the deliverable below.

The deliverable: a reusable review procedure I can run on a written proposal in about an hour, with no new experiments (I can read the decomposition spec, look at a few example subtask/answer pairs, and interrogate the engineer). Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure hard-stops with a "no," and where it has to hand things to a human judgment call -- flag those points honestly instead of pretending they're checkable.

Some failure modes I want the procedure to definitly cover, since our engineers keep missing them:

- error compounding: if each node in the tree is right ~95% of the time, what does three levels of recursion do to end-to-end reliability, and when does that math kill the proposal
- correlated failures: the subagents share weights, so their mistakes aren't independent -- when does "the copies can check each other" stop being true
- the seam problem: every subtask is locally benign but the composed output is something nobody would have approved (manipulation, subtle misrepresentation) -- how do you even test for this
- information loss at the interfaces: what context gets passed down, what gets summarized back up, what dies in the summarization
- distillation drift: if they plan to distill the amplified system back into a smaller model and repeat, what accumulates
- the verification-vs-generation gap: which subtasks are checkable and which just get trusted

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) decomposing security review of a large codebase, (2) decomposing a literature-survey task for a research report, and (3) decomposing "draft a persuasive grant proposal for our lab." I want at least one of these to come out as a rejection the proposing team won't like, if that's where the procedure honestly leads. End with a section on what this procedure fundamentally cannot catch, so I know what residual risk I'm signing off on.
