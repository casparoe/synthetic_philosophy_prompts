I'm in a graduate seminar on early modern rationalism, and as a final exercise each of us has to submit a one-page statement of our own "Spinozist profile" — not what Spinoza held, but which of his commitments we'd actually endorse — and defend it in discussion. A friend who read my draft told me I've assembled a cafeteria Spinozism that can't all be true at once, but she wouldn't say where the problem is, and I want to find it before the seminar does. Here is what I hold. (1) Strict necessitarianism: everything follows from the divine nature with the same necessity by which it follows from the nature of a triangle that its angles equal two right angles, and nothing could have been otherwise than it is. (2) Human freedom is nonetheless real and comes in degrees: the person who acts from adequate ideas is more free than the one pushed around by passive affects, and someone can genuinely become more free over a lifetime. (3) Nothing is good or bad in itself; we do not desire a thing because we judge it good, we judge it good because we desire it. (4) Knowledge of God is the mind's greatest good and the intellectual love of God is blessedness itself — and this holds universally, for every human being, not merely for those of us who happen to be temperamentally drawn to understanding. (5) God does not love or hate anyone; affects of that kind belong only to finite minds, and wanting God to love you back is a confusion about what God is. (6) Repentance and self-contempt are not virtues but passions that diminish our power of acting, so the free person simply does not indulge in them. (7) Moral exhortation genuinely works, and the Ethics itself is worth writing, teaching, and pressing on students precisely because reading it can change what people do.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me on Spinoza. For each pair of commitments, tell me whether there is a real tension, an apparent tension that dissolves on inspection (and how it dissolves), or no interaction at all. I suspect at least one of my friend's complaints is that I've combined a thoroughly subjectivist picture of value with a perfectionist claim about what is good for everyone, but I don't want to prejudge whether that's the worst problem, so rank the tensions you find by severity rather than treating them all as equal. For each genuine tension, identify what would have to give — which commitment I'd have to revise or weaken, and what the revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. If the cheapest overall fix requires giving up something I seem to care about a lot, say so plainly. One constraint: I'd rather you reason from the content of the commitments than appeal to scholarly interpretive debates, since I can't verify citations and this exercise is about my views, not about reconstructing Spinoza's.
