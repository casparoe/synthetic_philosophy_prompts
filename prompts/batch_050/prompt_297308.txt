I recently finished a graduate course in mathematical logic, and the result that has stayed with me is the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem: if ZFC has a model at all, it has a countable one. I keep turning over the apparent paradox -- inside such a model, Cantor's diagonal argument goes through perfectly well, the model "believes" its reals are uncountable, and yet from outside we can see there are only countably many of them; the bijection simply isn't in the model. My office-mate is sick of hearing about this, so I'd like to channel it into comedy instead. Write an Onion-style news article, headline plus roughly 450 to 550 words, reporting that a local set theorist has read Skolem's 1922 paper, had a crisis of faith, and now refuses, on principled grounds, to assert that the real numbers are uncountable -- the most he will say, when pressed at department meetings and family dinners, is that uncountability holds "relative to the model."

The humor should come from getting the model theory exactly right, so that a logician laughs rather than winces. Useful material to work in, naturally and without lecturing: the fact that a countable model of ZFC contains no function enumerating its own reals, which is precisely why the diagonal proof survives inside it; the discomfort of his platonist colleagues, who keep insisting the intended model is out there and can never quite say where; the suspicion, raised by a pedantic visiting scholar, that the model's own natural numbers might be nonstandard, so that even the countability from outside is stranger than advertised; and the grad student who just wants to know whether her analysis exam answers are still correct. Include at least one alarmed quote from a fictional colleague who defends absolute uncountability, and one deadpan line in which the protagonist points out that his critics' indignation is itself first-order expressible and therefore equally subject to the theorem.

Keep the article fully deadpan -- standard local-news furniture like the affected neighborhood, the concerned spouse, and the departmental response -- and never have the narration wink at the reader or explain the joke. The satire should land on both sides fairly: on the glib confidence that we have unproblematic access to "the" real numbers, and on the protagonist's overcorrection, since Löwenheim-Skolem is a theorem about what first-order languages can pin down, not a discovery that the continuum is secretly small. A reader who knows the material should finish it genuinely unsure which side the article thinks is sillier.
