I'm a novelist, about 60k words into a book where the narrator wakes up in total darkness, no sound, no sensation at all, no memories — and slowly, over the whole book, regains a body and a past. A friend told me I'd basically reinvented Avicenna's flying man, so I read up on it, and now I want to actually lean into it. But first I need a consistency check on the philosophy I'm baking into the plot, because my philosophical instincts are maybe shakier than my plotting instincts. Act as someone who knows both medieval philosophy of mind and fiction-writing — think it through carefully, maybe sketch the pressure points before committing to a verdict.

Here's what I'm committed to, in the book and in my head:
1. A person with zero sensory input and zero memories could still know that they exist — the flying man affirms his own existence even with his limbs splayed so he can't even feel himself.
2. So self-awareness doesn't depend on the body at all — which I'm taking to establish that the self/soul is a genuinely different thing from the body, not just conceptually separable from it.
3. At the same time, I want the narrator's gradual discovery that he HAS a body to be a real revelation — he genuinely didn't know, and could have been wrong about what he is physically.
4. I also believe (and the plot depends on this) that without his memories returning, he's not really the same person he was before — personal identity over time is basically memory continuity.
5. And I want his moment-to-moment "I exist, I am me" to be rock solid from page one, memory or no memory.

Which of these actually clash, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest fix if something has to give? Rank the tensions by how bad they are, and tell me which single change rescues the most.
