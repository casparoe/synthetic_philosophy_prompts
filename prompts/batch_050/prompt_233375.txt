In Part I, Section I of Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Smith argues that much of the pleasure of society lies in an answering harmony between one person's feelings and another's: we delight when someone enters into our joys and griefs, we are stung when they cannot, and he compares this concord of minds to instruments tuned to play in concert. In Part III he turns the thought inward, describing how each of us comes to judge our own conduct through the eyes of an imagined impartial spectator, lowering the pitch of our passions toward what such a spectator could share, while the spectator strains upward toward what we actually feel.

Write a Shakespearean sonnet that dramatizes this double movement. The first quatrain should render the pleasure of mutual sympathy as the speaker first experiences it; the second should render the loneliness of its failure, when grief or joy meets only blankness or excess. Let the volta open the third quatrain with the recognition that sympathy disciplines both parties: the sufferer must compose his passion to a pitch others can enter, and the onlooker must work to rise toward his. Reserve the closing couplet for the impartial spectator itself, which must emerge as an internalized, imagined standpoint rather than a real bystander, a majority, or a god. Let the rhyme scheme enact the argument: only in the couplet do adjacent lines rhyme, so that full formal agreement arrives only where the spectator's concord is achieved. Do not equate Smithian sympathy with pity or benevolence; it is fellow-feeling with any passion whatever, including resentment and joy. The sonnet should be exact enough that a scholar of Smith would recognize the argument rather than a greeting-card notion of empathy, yet moving enough that a reader who has never opened Smith would still find it a good poem. The Smith material above is context and does not count toward the fourteen-line limit.
