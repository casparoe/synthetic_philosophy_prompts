I am a retired molecular biologist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been reading my way into philosophy, lately into the literature on artificial intelligence and so-called specification gaming. In that literature one encounters, with great frequency and very little unpacking, the dictum that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. I gather it descends from remarks by the economist Charles Goodhart about monetary policy in the 1970s, though the writers who invoke it rarely say what he originally claimed, and they deploy it to do heavy work: specifically, to argue that any attempt to train an AI system to optimize a measurable proxy for what we actually want is bound to produce a system that satisfies the proxy while frustrating the intent.

I would like to understand what that one sentence could precisely mean, because it seems to me the argument about AI needs the strongest version of it, while only weaker versions may be true. So please lay out, as a list I can think through one by one, the distinct theses the slogan might be expressing. I can imagine several candidates and would like you to supply the ones I have missed: that it is merely a reminder that correlation between proxy and goal is imperfect; that it is a statistical observation, along the lines of regression to the mean, about what happens when one selects on a noisy correlate; that it is a claim about incentive pressure -- that people, or optimizing algorithms, distort the measure itself once rewards hang on it, whether by gaming, by resource diversion, or by erosion of whatever the measure used to track; that it is a claim about causal structure, to the effect that a regularity observed in a system changes once the system is deliberately steered by it (which I believe is closer to what Goodhart himself said, and resembles what I know of the Lucas critique in economics); and perhaps a still stronger claim, that optimization pressure on any proxy for a complex goal will, given enough power behind the optimization, actively drive the goal away, rather than merely decouple from it.

For each reading, I would like three things: a statement of it precise enough that I could say what evidence would count for or against it; a verdict on its plausibility (is it near-trivial, is it substantive and well supported, is it contested, is it simply false as stated); and an assessment of whether it is strong enough to underwrite the conclusion the AI writers draw from it, namely that proxy optimization fails by default and not merely sometimes. If the readings differ in what they imply about remedies -- selecting better proxies, rotating among several measures, keeping optimization pressure low, and so forth -- I would like that noted as well.

Finally, a request about calibration. Having spent a career in an empirical field, I am sensitive to the difference between what a discipline has established and what its loudest members assert. Please indicate, for each thesis, whether it is a settled point (in economics, in statistics, in the study of organizations, wherever the relevant expertise lives), a reasonably supported generalization with known exceptions, or a genuinely contested extrapolation -- and please do not present the strong claim needed by the AI argument as received wisdom if it is not.
