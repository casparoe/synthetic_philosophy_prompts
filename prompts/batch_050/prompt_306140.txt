I am the operations director of a network of nine regional hospitals, roughly 14,000 staff, and our board has caught centralization fever. Every few months a new proposal arrives to move some class of decision -- purchasing, staffing, scheduling, maintenance, IT -- from the individual hospitals to the head office, always justified by economies of scale and consistency. Some of these have worked well; others have been slow-motion disasters that took years to undo, and I have no framework for telling which is which in advance.

In trying to build one, I have been reading about the socialist calculation debate: Mises's argument that planners without market prices lack a common denominator for comparing alternative uses of resources, Hayek's argument that much of the economically relevant knowledge is local, transient, and inarticulable -- knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place that cannot be transmitted to a center as statistics -- and Lange's rejoinder that planners could mimic the market by posting accounting prices and adjusting them by trial and error in response to shortages and surpluses. It strikes me that a hospital network is, internally, a small planned economy: most resources move between units without prices, and the head office decides in roughly the position of Lange's planning board. So the debate ought to have something to say about which decisions can safely be centralized and which cannot. But it was conducted at the level of whole economies, and I cannot see on my own how to translate it into questions I could put to a proposal sitting on my desk.

Here is what I would like from you. Adopt the role of an expert on the socialist calculation debate who also has practical experience advising large organizations on the design of decision rights. Think carefully about this, and begin by briefly brainstorming which distinctions the debate actually offers -- the calculation problem versus the knowledge problem, articulable versus tacit information, genuine versus simulated prices, the speed of local change versus the speed of reporting cycles -- and which of them can survive translation into an internal, non-market setting where no real prices exist at all. Then give me a decision procedure my office can run on any centralization proposal in under a day: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each, including what to do when we cannot obtain the information; the points at which the procedure stops with a recommendation against centralizing; the conditions under which a Lange-style fix -- internal accounting prices or metrics adjusted by trial and error -- would answer the knowledge objection, and when that fix itself merely reproduces the problem in miniature; and the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to a person because the trade-off is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit about which distinctions from the original debate you kept, which you dropped, and where the procedure will misfire. In particular, tell me what it does with proposals whose benefits (scale, standardization, compliance) are easy to quantify in advance and whose costs (lost local knowledge, slower adaptation) are not, because that asymmetry is exactly what keeps fooling our board.

Finally, run the procedure on three proposals currently in front of us: centralizing all purchasing of standard pharmaceuticals and consumables; moving nurse scheduling from ward managers to a central algorithmic system; and consolidating all decisions about building maintenance, including when a ward is taken out of service, in the head office. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict our board will not like, and close by stating plainly what a procedure of this kind cannot settle.
