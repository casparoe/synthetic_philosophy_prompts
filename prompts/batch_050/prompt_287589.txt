The Mind's Distension: A Defense of the Reductionist Reading of Confessions XI

[1] Few texts wear their perplexity as openly as Book XI of Augustine's Confessions. What is time? Augustine's celebrated answer is that so long as no one asks him, he knows; as soon as someone asks and he tries to explain, he no longer knows (XI.14). This essay asks what positive doctrine lies behind the confession of ignorance. Readers have tended toward one of two views. On what I will call the chronometric reading, Augustine's aim is the limited one of explaining how finite minds manage to measure time, and his remarks about memory and expectation belong strictly to that project. On the reductionist reading, which I defend here, Augustine means something stronger: time as such has no existence outside the soul. It is, quite simply, the distension of the mind.

[2] Begin with creation. Asked what God was doing before he made heaven and earth, Augustine replies — with a sharpness he plainly relished — that God was preparing hell for those who pry into deep things. The barb is usually repeated as a joke at the questioner's expense, but its philosophical point should not be missed: Augustine treats any question of the form "what happened before time began?" as malformed, since before and after have application only within the created order. Time is not a stage on which creation happens; it arrives with creation, and so belongs wholly to the creaturely side of the line.

[3] Consider next the ontological inventory of XI.15–18. The past no longer exists; the future does not yet exist; and the present, Augustine argues, shrinks to a knife-edge: a minimal interval too brief to be cut into parts. From these premises it follows that long times have no foothold in the world. A hundred years cannot reside in the past, which is gone; nor in the future, which is not yet; nor in the evanescent sliver of the present. If long times exist anywhere, they must exist in the one place where past, present, and future are gathered together — the mind.

[4] This is precisely what the doctrine of the threefold present supplies (XI.20). There is, Augustine says, a present of things past, which is memory; a present of things present, which is attention; and a present of things future, which is expectation. Whatever being the past and future possess, they possess as present contents of a mind. The grammar of "was" and "will be" is thereby explained without positing nonexistent times.

[5] It is worth noting where Augustine stands relative to the ancients. Aristotle had defined time as the measure of motion with respect to before and after, and Augustine endorses the essential point: time is bound up with the movement of bodies, and in particular with the revolution of the heavens, whose regular circuits furnish the units by which we count days and years. His distinctive contribution is to add that without a mind to register and retain it, motion would have no temporal aspect at all. Time, on this picture, is motion as held in a soul.

[6] The decisive text is XI.26, where Augustine draws his conclusion: time, he says, is a certain distension — and of the mind itself. The Latin distentio carries the image of something stretched, and the metaphor does real philosophical work. Just as a line's length consists in its being extended between two points, a duration consists in the soul's being stretched between what it remembers and what it awaits. Time is not something the mind measures from without; it is the mind's own stretchedness.

[7] The measuring passages of XI.27–28 confirm this. When I recite a hymn I know by heart, what do I actually measure? Not the syllables already sung, which are nothing, nor the syllables still to come, which are nothing, but the impression that the passing sounds leave in me, which abides present while the sounds themselves perish. We measure not things but the traces of things, and traces exist only where they are retained. Hence the long and the short of time are features of mental content, full stop.

[8] One objection deserves mention, since it seems to me the strongest available to the chronometric reading. Call it the regress of measurement. Measuring is itself a temporal activity: it takes time to attend to an impression and pronounce it long or short. If the duration of the first impression is fixed only by a measuring act, then the duration of that act must be fixed by a second measuring, and so on. Either the regress is vicious, or at some level duration is simply given, unmeasured — and in that case there is duration in the world prior to all mind, which is exactly what the reductionist denies. My reply is that the objection equivocates: the measuring of an impression does not itself stand in need of a further metric, any more than a ruler requires a second ruler in order to have the length it has. I concede, however, that if the objection works it tells against the reductionist reading only, and not against Augustine — which would itself be some evidence that the chronometric reading is the truer one.

[9] I conclude that the reductionist reading best unifies the text: the creation argument removes time from God's eternity, the ontological inventory removes long times from the world, the threefold present relocates them in the soul, and the distension doctrine names the result. Augustine's perplexity at the start of Book XI is thus not an admission that he lacks a theory. It is the appropriate response to a theory whose object is as strange as this one: a thing that is real, measurable, and intimately known, yet exists only in being stretched across a mind.
