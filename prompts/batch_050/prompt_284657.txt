I'm a novelist, and for a book I'm drafting I need a set piece: an advice column from the local paper in a world where expected value fanaticism -- the view that you should always maximize expected value, no matter how tiny the probability or how absurdly huge the payoff -- is just plain common sense, the way "save for retirement" is here. The columnist is a minor character in the novel and he's totally sincere. Write the column, maybe 500-600 words: a brief intro and three or four reader letters with his replies. The comedy has to come from the real mechanics of the view: everytime someone asks something ordinary (should I visit my sick mother, take the safer job, bother with a seatbelt), the answer gets steamrolled by some infinitesimal chance of an astronomically large payoff, and he never blinks. Fold in the standard pressure points without ever naming them -- probabilities basically invented on the spot, the fact that any smooth talker can hijack him by promising a big enough prize, the question of whether a human being could actually live this way. One letter writer should be visibly cracking under the strain, and the reply should treat the cracking itself as just another expected-value calculation. Deadpan all the way through, no winking at the reader. By the end it should be obvious the column works as a reductio, but only because he followed the logic faithfully -- not because he slipped up anywhere.
