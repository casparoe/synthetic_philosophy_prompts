Two friends of mine got into a long argument at the pub and I want a neutral referee. Setup: one of them had just read a pseudohistory book — the kind that claims the early middle ages were largely fabricated, that the surviving chronicles are later forgeries, and that eclipse records and carbon dating have been systematically misread. (Think the ancient-aliens/Atlantis genre, but aimed at the historical record.)

Friend A: it's pseudoscience because it's unfalsifiable. Every counterexample gets absorbed — the chronicles were forged, the labs are sloppy, the experts are stuck in a concensus bubble. A theory that no evidence could touch isn't a theory.

Friend B: that's backwards. The book makes piles of falsifiable claims — it predicts certain eclipses won't line up, certain tree-ring sequences won't correlate. The claims are false, sure, but falsified isn't unfalsifiable. If anything it's the mainstream chronologists who absorb anomalies — they calibrate their dating against the very documents the book questions, which is exactly the protective-belt move A is complaining about.

A: the difference is what the protective belt does. When mainstream history patches an anomaly, the patch generates new tests and sometimes new finds; the book's patches only ever explain away. Degenerating research programme vs. progressive one.

B: so a young fringe programme loses by default because it hasn't had centuries of institutional support to rack up confirmed predictions? By that standard continental drift was pseudoscience in 1915 — no mechanism, explaining away objections, out-predicted by the establishment. And flip it around: if the pseudohistory crowd ran preregistered eclipse predictions and honestly updated on their failures, they'd count as "science" overnight even though the underlying claim is still bonkers. That can't be right either.

A: honestly, maybe it is right. Science is a method, not a claim.

B: then "pseudoscience" tells us nothing about whether a claim is false, which seems like the one thing that word should do.

Neither would budge. Who's right about which specific points? And is there something both of them are missing about what demarcation is even for?
