Write a dialogue between two philosophers debating the following case. In 2045, a woman named Iris undergoes a neural prosthesis procedure in which her biological neurons are replaced, one per hour, by synthetic units that take over each replaced neuron's exact input-output role within the intact, continuously functioning brain. The replacement takes about eleven years. Throughout, Iris reports no discontinuity, her personality and memories are stable to every test, and the discarded biological neurons are incinerated. One philosopher argues that the person who walks out of the clinic is numerically identical to the Iris who walked in, on grounds of functional and psychological continuity; the other argues that the original Iris died sometime during the procedure and that the person who walked out is a successor who merely resembles her perfectly.

The dialogue should exhibit the following features. First, both interlocutors must be equally competent: neither may rely on the other missing an obvious point, and each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than to caricatures of them. Second, at some point they should explicitly test whether their dispute concerns any empirically or logically checkable fact about the case, or whether it is purely a disagreement about what personal identity consists in—and they should not simply settle for the easy conclusion that the disagreement is merely verbal. Third, the second philosopher must press the gradualist on where, exactly, identity is supposed to be lost if not at any particular neuron's replacement, and the first philosopher must press the skeptic on why a process that never once interrupts the flow of consciousness should count as death when ordinary neuronal turnover and sleep do not. Fourth, introduce a complication of your own devising partway through—for instance, new evidence about the synthetic neurons, or a variant of the procedure—and have the characters reason about whether it changes anything.

End the dialogue with each character stating, in their own voice, the single consideration or argument that, if established, would lead them to concede the other's position—and, if a character suspects no such consideration exists, have them say so candidly and reflect on what that implies about the nature of the dispute.
