I'm a bankruptcy and restructuring attorney with about fifteen years of practice, mostly Chapter 11 and creditor-side workouts, and no formal philosophy training beyond a college survey course. Lately I've been struck by the fact that "forgiveness" is a term of art in my world -- forgiveness of debt, discharge, release of claims -- and I want to know how far that parallel runs.

Take the standard philosophical debate about forgiveness -- roughly: the account on which forgiving centrally involves forswearing resentment toward the wrongdoer while still holding the wrong to have been wrong; the controversy over whether forgiveness should be conditional on the offender's repentance, apology, or changed ways, or whether unconditional forgiveness is possible and perhaps even more admirable; and the question of whether forgiveness can ever be obligatory, i.e., whether a victim who refuses to forgive is doing something morally wrong rather than merely declining a gift. Restate these positions and disputes in the vocabulary I use professionally: principal and accrued claims, discharge in bankruptcy, settlement for less than face value, releases and covenants not to sue, conditions precedent, statutes of limitations, non-dischargeable debts, guarantors and third-party obligors, judgment-proof defendants, and the court's power to discharge over a creditor's objection. Be as precise as you can about the mapping -- for instance, whether repentance-as-a-condition works like a condition precedent to a release, and whether non-dischargeable debts (say, those incurred by fraud) correspond to the category of the unforgivable.

Then give me the residue report. Which elements of the philosophical debate translate cleanly into debtor-creditor terms, which require distortion, and which have no expression at all? I'm particularly interested in: (a) whether the emotional core of forgiveness -- the giving up of resentment -- has any counterpart, given that a discharged creditor may remain furious; (b) the standing question, since in my field only the creditor of record can release a claim, yet courts can extinguish one -- does that illuminate or obscure the philosophical dispute about whether anyone but the victim can forgive; and (c) whether the obligatoriness question survives, given that the law can compel a creditor to accept discharge but, I'd think, no one thinks the law thereby compels forgiveness.

Finally, I want your judgment on the residue: does what fails to translate show that my professional vocabulary is missing something real about moral life, or does it suggest that parts of the philosophical debate are less substantive than they appear -- disputes that look deep only because ordinary language doesn't force the distinctions a term sheet would? Please don't flatten the philosophy to flatter the legal framework; if a translation requires me to import an assumption my field makes (e.g., that all claims are fungible into money), flag that assumption rather than letting it ride along silently.
