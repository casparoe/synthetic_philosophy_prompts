I got into a surprisingly heated argument with a friend last weekend (she does field ecology, I mostly read philosophy) about a view I've been quietly holding for a while: that the lives of most wild animals are, on balance, not worth living, because the vast majority of animals are small, short-lived r-strategists whose offspring die in enormous numbers, mostly young and mostly badly, and the few survivors still deal with hunger, parasites, cold, fear, and being eaten. The upshot I defend is that wild animal suffering is probably net-negative at a staggering scale, and so we have at least a defeasible moral reason to develop and eventually use interventions to reduce it — not just leave nature alone. She kept saying "you have no idea what the population dynamics would do," and I kept saying that's a research problem, not a refutation, and we didn't get anywhere.

So here's what I want: act as a philosopher who works on animal ethics and population ethics and has thought hard about welfare biology, and steelman the case against my view rather than patting me on the back. Think carefully and brainstorm widely before you settle on the list. I'd like a map of what would count against the position, sorted roughly from fatal to merely awkward. Cover the empirical defeaters (what would actual measurements of wild animal welfare have to show?), the conceptual ones (e.g., if premature death isn't very bad for beings without life plans, does the whole r-strategist argument collapse?), the normative ones (the "don't play God in ecosystems" worry, the risk that every intervention backfires at population level), and the meta one I secretly worry about most: has this view quietly become unfalsifiable, in that every piece of contrary evidence gets absorbed as "a reason for more research" rather than as a defeater? For each item, say whether a thoughtful defender of the view would concede it counts against them or brush it off, and be honest about which ones *I* would find uncomfortable rather than which ones my ecologist friend would find decisive. If the honest answer is that the view is extremely well insulated and nothing could really dislodge it, say that and tell me whether that's a vice or just what a structurally sound position looks like.
