I am a retired geochemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has developed a somewhat belated interest in political philosophy and the ethics of public policy. A reading group at our local library has been working through the literature on famines, and our starting point was Amartya Sen's work on entitlement and starvation, which I encountered not in the original but through a chapter in a popular history of twentieth-century hunger that someone in the group lent me. The chapter summarizes Sen's position as follows: "Sen's entitlement thesis holds that famines are never really about food. In every major famine of the modern era — Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, and China after the Great Leap Forward — there was ample food available within the affected country; the problem was purely one of distribution, because particular groups lost their legal entitlements to food while merchants hoarded it or governments exported it. Sen demonstrated this statistically by showing that food production did not decline in any of these cases, which proves that nature had nothing to do with any of them. His conclusion is that famine is therefore a political crime rather than a natural disaster, and he seals the argument with his most famous result: no famine has ever occurred in a democracy, since wherever there are elections and newspapers, mass starvation is simply impossible."

Something about this summary sets off my old professional instincts — in my own field, any claim containing "never," "every," or "simply impossible" tended not to survive peer review — but I do not know the primary material well enough to locate the problem precisely. The chapter is written by a competent and well-regarded popularizer, so I hesitate to dismiss it. What I would like is an accuracy audit of this summary against Sen's actual position, so far as it can be reconstructed without reference to page numbers. Which parts would Sen himself endorse? Which parts overstate, compress, or distort his argument — for instance, is his notion of "entitlement" really a matter of legal rights, and is his claim about food availability really as sweeping as stated, particularly for the Chinese case? Is "political crime" his framing or the chapter's editorial gloss? And regarding the democracy claim: what exactly did Sen assert, what is the mechanism he takes to explain it, and does the chapter's use of it as a conclusive proof survive a more careful statement of the thesis? Finally, if the misrepresentations are corrected, does the chapter's headline conclusion — that famines are crimes rather than natural events — still follow from what remains, or does it need to be weakened?

Please write your answer in American English, and feel free to go through the summary sentence by sentence if that is the most orderly approach.
