Here is roughly where I have landed on panpsychism and its ethical consequences, and I would like to know whether the package hangs together. (1) I am a Russellian monist: physics describes only the relational structure of matter, and the intrinsic nature realizing that structure is experiential; part of what draws me to the view is that it avoids positing a brute, radical emergence of consciousness out of the wholly non-conscious. (2) Axiologically, I hold that only valenced states — suffering and enjoyment — carry moral weight; experience without valence is ethically inert. (3) I believe valence requires functional organization of a particular kind — integration, a self-model, something in that family — so electrons, rocks, and thermal gradients do not suffer, and nearly all moral weight resides in brains and perhaps in some artificial systems. (4) I am scope-sensitive under moral uncertainty: small credences in vast amounts of suffering, as with insects or as with suffering subroutines inside large AI systems, deserve genuine attention rather than a shrug. (5) I dismiss the speculation, which some panpsychists take seriously, that fundamental physical processes might contain astronomical quantities of suffering; I treat it as unfalsifiable and therefore as something that should not drive priorities.

Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection? In particular, I would like to know whether the anti-emergence motivation behind (1) sits badly with the sharp valence threshold in (3), whether (4) forces me to take the scenario in (5) seriously rather than dismiss it, and — if the set is inconsistent — which commitment is cheapest to abandon and what giving it up would cost the rest.
