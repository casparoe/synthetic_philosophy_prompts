So there's this German case from 2002 that I keep thinking about. An 11 year old boy, Jakob von Metzler, gets kidnapped, the police catch the guy (Magnus Gäfgen) pretty quickly, but he won't say where the boy is and they think the kid might still be alive and running out of time. The deputy police chief in Frankfurt, Wolfgang Daschner, orders his officers to threaten Gäfgen with severe pain if he doesn't talk. Gäfgen immediately gives up the location. Twist: the boy was already dead, had been the whole time. Daschner gets convicted (though barely punished), and years later the European Court of Human Rights says the threat violated the absolute ban on torture and inhuman treatment, no exceptions, even to save a child.

Ok so here's my question. This looks like the real world version of the ticking time bomb scenario that people use to argue the absolute prohibition on torture has to give. Except everything about it seems slightly off compared to the textbook version: it was a threat not actual torture, the info came instantly, and the "bomb" turned out to have already gone off since the boy was dead. So analyze the case as a ticking bomb case -- what would the pro-torture-warrant people (the ones who want judges to be able to authorize this stuff) say Daschner should have done, and what would absolutists say he should have done? Does the fact that the boy was already dead make the case count *against* ticking bomb arguments (the scenario never actually obtains in real life, you can't verify the "ticking"), or is that just a red herring since the police reasonably believed he was alive? And what do you make of the argument that even if you think the threat was justified, the right legal setup is still an absolute ban where the cop acts illegally and then throws himself on the mercy of the court afterwards, rather than building torture into the rules? Rank those three positions (warrants, absolute ban, ban-plus-disobedience) by how well they handle this case, and say where each one breaks.
