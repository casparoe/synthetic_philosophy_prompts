I am grading final-exam answers for an undergraduate course on justice and political philosophy. I need your help with one of them.

The exam question, as printed on the paper, reads:

"In the chapter on justice in The Methods of Ethics (1874), Henry Sidgwick argues that the common-sense ideal of distribution according to desert divides into two standards that can pull apart: rewarding people in proportion to their moral worth — the goodness of their intentions and efforts — and rewarding them in proportion to the actual benefit of the services they render. He also suggests that the first standard runs into difficulty once we ask how far the will is free. (This is a paraphrase of his position, not a quotation.) First, explain the difference between the two standards and why they can conflict. Second, use the distinction to assess the claim that highly paid professionals deserve their salaries because their work is highly valuable."

Here is the student's answer, about 250 words:

"Sidgwick's two standards of desert correspond to two different things we praise. On the first standard, a person deserves good fortune in proportion to moral worth: how virtuous their intentions are and how hard they try. On the second, desert tracks services: the actual good that a person's work does for others, regardless of whether their motives were admirable. The two can conflict because a conscientious but incompetent surgeon ranks high on the first standard and low on the second, while a selfish scientist who develops a vaccine is the reverse.

Sidgwick's conclusion is that justice ultimately requires the second standard: since we can observe services but not hearts, distribution according to services is the coherent ideal, and the moral-worth standard collapses once we see that our intentions are not fully up to us.

Applied to the salary claim, the professional's case is strongest on the services standard. A high salary is the market's measure of the service rendered — economists call it the marginal product — so a high earner's pay does reflect the size of their contribution, which vindicates the desert claim. On the moral-worth standard the claim fares worse, because high earners are not obviously more virtuous, and because their effort rests on native talents they did nothing to deserve. But since Sidgwick sides with the services standard, the salary claim survives his analysis, at least in outline."
