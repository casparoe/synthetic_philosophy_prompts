I'm in an ethics seminar this term and an argument broke out in our discussion section last week that I still can't settle, so I want a verdict.

We were given a case about two hospital residents who, on the same night, each skipped a mandatory second check on a medication dose because they were exhausted and figured it was probably fine. One got lucky: the dose was correct, nothing happened. The other's patient nearly died and ended up with permanent kidney damage.

Maya argued the two residents are exactly equally blameworthy. They made the same choice, with the same information and the same reasoning, and everything after that point was out of their control -- so if the outcomes change how much blame they deserve, then blame basically tracks luck. Jordan said that's absurd: only one of them actually hurt somebody, and "she injured a patient" is a fact about what the second resident did, not just a fact about the world. He said refusing to blame her more is kind of an insult to the patient, and he pointed out that the law punishes failed attempts less than completed crimes and everyone accepts that. Maya replied that this just shows the law cares about deterrence and about victims' feelings, which are real considerations but aren't reasons about blameworthiness itself. Jordan then came back with: on her view, the resident whose patient was fine should feel exactly as guilty as the other one, and the fact that the second resident does and should feel worse tracks something real, not just superstition. Maya said that's just moral emotions lagging behind the argument.

Neither budged and I honestly couldn't referee it. Who's right about which points, who is more right overall, and is there something they're both missing?
