I work in the licensing team of a district council, and much of my job amounts to deciding whether residents and businesses may impose small risks on their neighbours without asking them: a charity fireworks display over terraced housing, a fell race whose support vehicles occasionally end up in a ditch, a farmer wanting to spray near a public footpath, a drone photography firm hoping to fly over the high street. Hardly anyone is ever hurt, but now and again something goes wrong, and I have become uneasy that we have no principled way of deciding in advance which impositions would have been permissible even if nothing bad had come of them — and, conversely, which were impermissible even though, as luck had it, nobody was harmed. A colleague who has read some moral philosophy tells me there is a literature on the ethics of imposing risk without consent: on whether such impositions can be justified by the benefits they bring, by reciprocity (we all drive past other people's houses), or by the sheer fact that ordinary life is saturated with small consentless risks; and on the awkward suggestion that a wrong can be done by the imposition itself, before and regardless of any harm — her example being the drunk driver who arrives home safely and has nonetheless wronged everyone he passed. I have not read this literature and will not have time to, but I want its insights built into something my team can actually use.

Please write a decision procedure we could run on any application of this kind: the questions to ask, in order; what would count as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure stops with a refusal; and the points at which it must hand the question to the committee because what remains is a judgement of value rather than of fact. It must preserve whatever distinctions genuinely matter — I would guess these include the difference between imposing a risk and merely failing to reduce one, the difference between the affected people consenting and merely not objecting, and the question of who benefits relative to who bears the risk — but it may discard whatever does not survive translation into practice. Some constraints: I see perhaps twenty such applications a year and can spend at most two hours on each; applicants are mostly charities and small firms who cannot supply quantitative risk models, so the procedure must say what to do with rough estimates and with risks nobody can quantify; and every decision has to be defensible afterwards to elected members and, in the worst case, to the ombudsman. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire — I suspect the accumulation of many individually trivial permissions is one such place, and risks borne by children another — and say plainly what it cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on three applications. First, the hospice charity's bonfire night: professional display, genuine fire risk to nearby roofs, large benefit to a good cause, several thousand attendees. Secondly, a courier firm seeking a six-month trial of parcel drones over a residential estate: small but real crash risk falling on people who mostly gain nothing, the benefits accruing to the firm and its customers. Thirdly, the council's own annual road race, which I help to organise and would be sorry to refuse: if the procedure is honest it should here produce an answer I will not like, and I would rather you gave it to me than soften it. Where the procedure issues in a refusal or an escalation, give the reasoning in the form I could paste into a decision letter. Please reply in British English throughout.
