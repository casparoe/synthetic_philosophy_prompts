I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on power and historical knowledge (18 students, one 80-minute session), and I want an in-class simulation that makes students feel, from the inside, how archives produce silences rather than merely reflecting them. The standard reading on this leaves students able to say that the powerful get recorded and the marginal get erased, but it stays abstract. I want them to experience the specific mechanisms: how silences enter at the moment a record is created, at the moment someone decides what is worth keeping, and at the moment someone describes what was kept -- and how these three moments have different culprits, different incentives, and different remedies.

Here is the rough skeleton I have in mind; your job is to turn it into a runnable activity, improving or replacing my skeleton where it won't work. Phase one: the class is given a simulated event -- I'm imagining something like a dispute at a boarding house, or an accident at a workshop, involving perhaps six characters with sharply unequal standing (owner, manager, tenants, a servant, a passing neighbor) -- along with a pile of raw material: letters, a ledger, testimony, a photograph, a torn note. Phase two: the class, acting as an archive, must reduce this pile under a hard constraint (they may keep only a small fraction, and must write a one-line catalog description for each item they keep). Phase three: the retained items and their descriptions are handed to a group of "future historians" who must reconstruct what happened, knowing nothing else.

Please specify: the exact roles and rules for each phase, including who in the room gets decision rights over what (I want the power asymmetries of the simulated event to leak into the archiving decisions themselves, not just into the documents); the raw materials list, concrete enough that I can fabricate it; the constraint numbers (how many items survive, how long the catalog lines may be); a minute-by-minute timeline for 80 minutes; and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the three points of silencing above, so that I can show students which of their own choices did the damage.

Three further requirements. First, the cataloging step matters to me: I want students to discover that a finding aid is not a neutral summary but a decision about what the item is *of* -- that describing a letter as a tenancy matter versus a domestic matter pre-decides who will ever find it. Build that discovery into the rules rather than into my lecture. Second, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- I anticipate, for example, students archiving strategically to "tell the true story" rather than as a real institution would, and historians reconstructing from their knowledge of the setup rather than from the documents alone -- and how to handle each failure mode. Third, be honest about which feature of real archival silence this activity cannot reproduce: my suspicion is that a one-session simulation with aware participants cannot capture silences that arise from things never being written down at all, and I'd like your judgment on that, plus what I should supply in discussion to compensate.
