Ok so I'm putting together a ten-week grad seminar for next spring on counterfactual history and the philosophy of historical explanation, and I keep coming back to the Hitler question as the natural centerpiece: would there have been a Nazi Germany (or something close enough) without Hitler? The problem is that the debate as it usually gets run -- "great man" versus "structural conditions" -- feels stalled to me. Both sides just keep pointing at their favorite evidence and nothing ever resolves. So I want to structure the seminar around the open questions that would actually need answering to move this debate somewhere, and I'd like your help deciding which ones deserve the weeks.

Here's the framing I want you to work with. Suppose we grant, as most historians would, that Weimar Germany had a bunch of structural pressures pushing toward some kind of authoritarian nationalist regime: Versailles resentment, the Depression, a weak democratic tradition, an anti-republican military and judiciary. The interesting dispute is over what I'll call the *fungibility thesis*: the claim that within those structural constraints, the particular individual was basically interchangeable -- someone else (a Strasser, a Schleicher, some figure we never heard of) would have occupied roughly the same role and driven roughly the same outcomes, so Hitler explains little beyond timing and detail. The counter-claim is that specific features of this one person -- his particular obsessions, his risk tolerance, his skill set -- explain outcomes that weren't in the structural attractor at all (the speed of consolidation, the specific form of the racial state, the decision to invade the USSR, maybe the Holocaust itself).

Given that framing, what are the main open questions this debate actually turns on, and which two or three would you prioritize for the seminar? I'm thinking of things like: (a) the metaphysics of the counterfactual itself -- what similarity relation between possible worlds even makes "would there have been a Nazi Germany" evaluable, and whether we need some Lewis-style closeness ordering or whether that's the wrong tool; (b) how to individuate outcomes -- whether "Nazi Germany" in the counterfactual has to match on ideology, on foreign policy, on death toll, or on something else for the fungibility thesis to come out true; (c) whether the debate is even empirically tractable or whether it's condemned to be underdetermined forever, and what would count as evidence either way; (d) what, if anything, hangs on the answer morally -- collective responsibility, how we judge Weimar elites, how we think about our own institutions now. But you should feel free to restructure or add.

For each question you prioritize: tell me why it's upstream of the others (i.e., which other questions can't be settled until this one is), how tractable it is with philosophical tools rather than requiring more historiography -- my students are philosophers, not Germanists -- and which questions you'd deliberately deprioritize because their answer wouldn't change anything downstream. I'm less interested in the list than in the defense of the ordering. And if you think the whole great-man/structure framing is the wrong way to carve this up, say so, but then tell me what the seminar should be about instead and why that's better.
