I'm writing the final for my upper-level undergrad seminar on classical Islamic theology (kalam), and I need one essay question on the Mu'tazila vs. the Ash'arites about whether good and evil are known by reason or fixed by God's command. Students get 75 minutes and it's out of 20 marks.

The problem with every question I've tried so far is that students can coast on the slogan version: Mu'tazilites think reason discovers moral truths, Ash'arites think revelation creates them, done. So I want the question built around a specific pressure test they haven't seen, and here's the one I've come up with. Call it the reversed-decree test: imagine God reveals (through an unimpeachable prophet, in an unambiguous text) that torturing an innocent child for an afternoon is now obligatory and will be rewarded. No hidden wisdom clause allowed in the setup. The question should force students to say what each school must say about this case, and then -- this is the part I really care about -- make them connect the ethics debate to the free will debate. My hunch is that the Mu'tazilite commitment to human free will (qadar) isn't a separate doctrine but the same commitment showing up twice: God can't be unjust, so He can't punish us for acts He determined, just as He can't command what reason sees as evil. I want students to either defend that "stand or fall together" reading or show the Ash'arite can pry the two debates apart.

So: write the exam question itself (framed around the reversed-decree test, which you should present to students as a stipulated thought experiment), then a model answer of roughly 600-700 words, then a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, and for each point a one-line note on the way students typically fumble it.

The five points I'm imagining a top answer hits are something like: (1) the Ash'arite has to bite the bullet on the reversed decree in some sense, but the interesting question is whether that bullet is "it's impossible because God wouldn't do that" -- which smuggles rational moral facts back in -- versus "it's coherent but won't happen," and whether that distinction even survives scrutiny; (2) the praise problem for the Ash'arite, i.e., if justice just is whatever God wills, then calling God just looks empty, and whether there's a non-trivial reading left; (3) the mirror problem for the Mu'tazilite: if moral truths are independent of God's will, is God now subject to something external, and is that a real threat to omnipotence or a confusion about what omnipotence requires; (4) the actual argument that the ethics and free will positions share a crux for the Mu'tazila (divine justice as the fixed point generating both); (5) a verdict on whether the Ash'arite pairing of voluntarist ethics with divine determination is more internally stable than the Mu'tazilite pairing, or just stable at a higher price. Feel free to restructure these if you think the marking scheme works better carved differently, but the model answer has to actually take a position on (5), not just survey the options.

Also, keep the model answer at the level of a strong student who's done the reading -- it shouldn't name-drop obscure figures or texts, just al-Ash'ari, the Mu'tazila, and the standard positions. And make sure the question as written is answerable by someone who only knows the standard positions but unanswerable by someone who's only memorized them.
