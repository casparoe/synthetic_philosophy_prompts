In debates over welfare reform, the metaphor of the safety net is nearly universal — but participants pull it in opposite directions. Defenders of unconditional benefits take the net literally: it exists to catch anyone who falls, no questions asked, because falling itself is harm enough. Critics of generous provision counter that a net too comfortable becomes a hammock, rewarding idleness and dulling the incentive to climb back up. A third camp prefers the trampoline: support should be conditional on active effort, designed to bounce recipients back into work rather than merely hold them. Audit this family of analogies. First, give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of gravity, of the fall, of the height from which one falls, of the acrobat's own skill, and of the person who decides whether the net is deployed. Second, identify where each variant breaks — considering, for instance, that poverty is often a chronic condition rather than a fall from a prior height, that a net performs no behavioral test before catching, and that a trampoline only returns energy the jumper already supplied. Third, say what each metaphor quietly imports about the causes of poverty, the agency of recipients, and the moral logic of conditionality that its users may not intend. Finally: can any member of this family actually support a position on workfare or benefit conditionality, and if the whole frame is misleading, what better comparison would you put in its place?
