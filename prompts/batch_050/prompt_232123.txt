I'm a software engineer at a company that builds contract-automation tooling, and a recent project pushed me into legal philosophy for the first time. We've been experimenting with encoding regulatory requirements as machine-checkable rules, and during a design review a colleague argued that a rule which can't be publicly stated, applied prospectively, and kept internally consistent simply isn't a rule at all, no matter what authority issued it. Someone pointed me to the Hart–Fuller debate, and as far as I can tell the core exchange happened decades ago, generated a mountain of commentary, and never resolved: Hart's side holds that a norm counts as law if it was produced through the system's accepted procedures, however wicked its content; Fuller's side holds that certain formal features -- publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, practicability, stability, and congruence between announced rules and official action -- are partly constitutive of law itself, so that a regime violating them badly enough fails to have a legal system at all. The usual test case is something like the postwar prosecution of people who denounced others under Nazi-era statutes: was punishing them retroactive legislation in disguise, or the refusal to treat morally rotten directives as law?

Here's my hypothesis, offered as an engineer's intuition rather than a defended position. The debate looks to me like a dispute over whether "law" is more like "valid program" or "working system." Hart is saying validity is determined by the build process -- the recognized sources and procedures -- and a law that compiles is law even if it's malware. Fuller is saying a system that fails basic operability constraints badly enough doesn't count as a running system in the first place, whatever was deployed. If that's the right analogy, then the disagreement is substantially verbal: the two sides are answering different questions (what was validly enacted? what deserves the cooperation and obedience that law characteristically claims?) under one label, and the sixty years of commentary were mostly people talking past each other. But I can also see an argument that it's a real disagreement about a hidden empirical or functional question -- whether gross violations of Fuller's principles in fact destroy the guidance-of-conduct function that makes a practice count as law -- in which case the sides should be able to converge if pressed on that function.

So my request: diagnose the structure of the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Using what Hart's and Fuller's positions actually commit them to, tell me whether this is a verbal dispute about the extension of a word, a substantive dispute about the concept's function, or two research programs answering different questions. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress -- a conceptual distinction, a better account of what law is for, a testable claim about how legal systems degrade -- or whether no progress should be expected and why. Finally, as a genuinely selfish question from my day job: does the arrival of machine-executable rules (smart contracts, automated administrative decision-making, compliance-as-code systems like ours) sharpen the debate, dissolve it, or reveal that both sides were implicitly assuming a human reader all along? I'd rather have your honest structural assessment than a survey of the literature.
