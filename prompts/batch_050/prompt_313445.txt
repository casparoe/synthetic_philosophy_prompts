For a seminar on the foundations of probability, write a dialogue between two philosophers debating what Bertrand's chord paradox really shows. The setup: a chord of a circle is drawn "at random"; depending on whether one parametrizes the chord by its midpoint's radial distance, its angular separation of endpoints, or the position of its midpoint in the disk's area, the principle of indifference yields 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 as the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle.

One character defends the principle of indifference by appeal to invariance: the correct distribution is the one invariant under the symmetries of the problem (rotation, translation, and scale of the circle), and Bertrand's conflicting answers merely reflect carelessly chosen parametrizations that fail to respect those symmetries. The other character argues that this response relocates rather than resolves the difficulty: nothing in the bare statement of the problem fixes which symmetries are the relevant ones, and the only principled way to select a distribution is to specify the physical mechanism by which the chord is generated — so the principle of indifference does no independent epistemic work.

To push the disagreement into sharper territory, have them turn to a harder case: a sealed machine produces chords of a circle by an unknown internal mechanism, and the two philosophers must decide what a rational agent's credence about chord length should be given only that fact. The invariance defender should argue that the symmetry of the agent's epistemic situation, rather than of any physical process, determines the answer; the skeptic should press whether this makes indifference claims true at all, or merely conventional, and whether "the agent's epistemic situation" is itself too indeterminate to fix a unique distribution.

Both characters should be equally competent, and neither should be a strawman: the invariance defender should concede genuine difficulties where they arise rather than retreating to dogma, and the skeptic should acknowledge that the invariance approach has real successes rather than dismissing it wholesale. The dialogue should feature genuine back-and-forth, with each character engaging the other's actual arguments, and should end without an artificial resolution — though it should be clear by the end precisely where the residual disagreement lies.
