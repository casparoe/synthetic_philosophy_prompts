I'm a junior in high school, and my school offers a one-semester philosophy elective that I've been taking this year. Last month our teacher introduced the symbol grounding problem: the worry that a system manipulating symbols purely on the basis of their relations to other symbols -- the way a dictionary defines every word using only more words, or the way a language model learns from text alone -- never actually connects those symbols to anything in the world, and so never genuinely understands them. I found the idea compelling, maybe too compelling, because I sat down to write it up for our debate club and produced an argument that seems to prove that nobody understands anything, which can't be right. Here it is. (1) Understanding a symbol requires knowing what it means, not merely how it relates to other symbols. (2) A system whose entire access to meaning consists of symbol-to-symbol relations -- definitions, translations, patterns of co-occurrence -- never reaches what any symbol means, since every step just hands it more ungrounded symbols. (3) Such a system can at best simulate understanding: it can produce behavior indistinguishable from understanding without possessing it. (4) A human brain's entire access to the world consists of electrochemical signals arriving from the sensory organs, which are themselves just internal events standing in relations to further internal events. (5) Those signals are symbols in the relevant sense -- they represent states of the world, they are systematically related to one another, and they can misrepresent, as in illusions and hallucinations -- so a brain is a system whose entire access to meaning consists of symbol-to-symbol relations. (6) Therefore a brain, too, can at best simulate understanding. (7) But (6) is absurd: we obviously do understand words, and the symbol grounding problem was supposed to mark a difference between us and machines, not to erase one. So somewhere between (1) and (6) something went wrong.

My debate club is split. Half of us think the argument is valid and that one of the early premises has to go; the other half think the flaw is in the middle, somewhere around (4) and (5), and that the brain case and the dictionary case aren't actually parallel in the way the argument needs. I personally would rather keep (1) than (5) if forced to choose, because (1) strikes me as almost definitional, but I can't articulate where the parallel breaks down -- when I try, I just end up saying that our signals are "connected to the world" and theirs aren't, and then I can't explain why that connection isn't itself just more internal events.

Could you tell me which step you'd reject, why that step looked plausible in the first place, and what it costs to reject it? I'd also like to know whether the step you reject is the same one a defender of the original symbol grounding problem would reject, or whether saving human understanding here requires giving up something the original problem depended on. If the argument turns out to be valid after all, I'd like an honest accounting of which premise is cheapest to surrender and what position that leaves me defending at the debate.
