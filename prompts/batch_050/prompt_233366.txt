Having spent my career in the natural sciences, I have long been struck by how often the same discovery is made independently at nearly the same moment: Newton and Leibniz with the calculus, Darwin and Wallace with natural selection, multiple claimants for the telephone and for the isolation of oxygen. Now that I am reading philosophy of science in retirement, I find a standing dispute over what these "multiples" show. One camp takes them as evidence that discoveries are more or less inevitable given the state of a field -- that if not Darwin, then Wallace -- and that the individual genius is replaceable. The other camp replies that the cases are cherry-picked, that simultaneous inventions frequently differ in depth and downstream consequences, and that the contingency of who got there first can matter enormously for what a field becomes. This argument seems to have circulated for decades without either side conceding anything. My question is not which side is right but what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it an empirical dispute about the base rate and distribution of multiples, which better historical bookkeeping could in principle settle? Is it a conceptual dispute about what counts as "the same" discovery, given that two independent versions of a theory are rarely identical? Or is it a clash of intuitions about contingency and necessity that no amount of historical data could move, because each side already interprets every case through its own lens? Please diagnose the structure of the impasse, drawing on how each side actually argues, and tell me what -- a distinction, a body of evidence, a reframed question -- would count as progress given your diagnosis, or else explain why none should be expected.
