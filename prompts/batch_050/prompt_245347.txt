ok so this is going to sound like a stoner dorm-room thought but I promise its actually practical for me. I make beats and I sample old soul and jazz records, and last month I got a scary email from a rights holder about one of my tracks, which sent me down a total rabbit hole that ended, weirdly, in the philosophy of music.

So apparently there's this view -- Platonism about musical works, I think its called -- that a musical work is an abstract type that exists timelessly. Beethoven didn't create the Fifth Symphony, he discovered it, the way a mathematician discovers a theorem rather than inventing it. And no performance or recording IS the work, they're just instances or tokens of it, the work itself floats around in abstract-object-land.

Here's where I think this gets me off the hook:

1. If musical works are eternal abstract objects, then nobody created them, and you cant own something nobody made. At most composers discovered them first, like discovering a comet, and finding something first doesn't make it yours.

2. Even if I'm wrong about that, when I sample I'm not touching the WORK at all. I'm copying a recording, which on this view is just a token, one particular instance. The actual work stays completely untouched up in platonic heaven. So at absolute most I stole a performance of something, not the song itself.

I ran this by a friend of mine who's a paralegal at an entertainment firm and she literally laughed in my face, but when I asked her WHERE the argument fails she just said "that's not how any of this works" and couldn't really say why. So -- does the philosophy actually give me this and the law is just operating on a cruder picture? Or am I conflating things? If sampling really does only copy a token and never the type, then why does everyone (courts included) talk as if I've copied the song? I'd genuinely like to know where my reasoning breaks, because if it doesn't break I feel like the whole legal setup around sampling rests on a philosophical mistake nobody's noticed.
