So I was re-reading the old Hofstadter careenium setup — for anyone rusty: picture a huge vat of tiny marbles ("simms") banging into each other according to dumb low-level dynamics, and much bigger balls ("simmballs") whose positions are completely slaved to the simms crashing around underneath them. The point is that a creature watching only the simmballs would see stable macro-patterns that behave like symbols: they come in repeatable types, they get shuffled in rule-governed ways, and at that level the system can even look like it's "thinking." I tried to turn the obvious moral of the thing into a crisp argument, and now I think it proves too much, but I can't find the leak.

(1) Everything that happens in the careenium is fully fixed by what the simms do: given the simm-level state and the low-level rules, the simmball positions are determined, no residue, no wiggle room.
(2) If the simm level already fully fixes what happens, then the symbol level does no causal work of its own — there is nothing left for a symbol, qua symbol, to cause. Any effect you might credit to "symbol S got shuffled" is already fully credited to some swarm of simms.
(3) So symbols never push the substrate around, and never push anything else around either; the symbol-level story is a handy description, not a cause.
(4) The careenium was supposed to be a model of us: simms are neurons or whatever, simmball-patterns are thoughts. So by parity, no thought ever causes anything — nobody ever acts *because* they understood an arguement; the understanding just rides along on low-level processes that would have run identically whatever the thoughts meant.
(5) But this eats itself: if nobody is ever moved by the content of an argument, then this argument can't move anyone either, and the whole symbol level — reasons, meanings, the very book explaining the careenium — turns into causally inert decoration. A theory that renders meanings inert while plainly meaning to convince me of something is pulling its own plug.
(6) So something in (1)-(4) has to go.

I'm most reluctant to drop (1), since the setup really does look closed at the low level, and I'd also hate to just swallow epiphenomenalism about meaning and call it a day. My hunch is that (2) smuggles in some contestable assumption about what it would be for a higher level to "add" causal work, but I can't make the hunch precise. Diagnose it for me: which step do you reject, why does it look fine anyway, and what does rejecting it cost you? Also tell me what would change your verdict — what consideration or counterargument would push you to indict a different step, and where is your own diagnosis most likely to fall apart?
