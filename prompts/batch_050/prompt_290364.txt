Next month I'm giving a short toast at my daughter's eighteenth birthday dinner. She was conceived with donor sperm after several years of infertility, she has known this since she was small, and the fact is comfortable family furniture by now rather than a secret. The room will be mixed: grandparents, aunts, and old friends who want warmth and nothing technical, plus my daughter herself, who is halfway through a philosophy degree and recently wrote a paper on Kripke's necessity-of-origins thesis — the claim that she could not have come from a different sperm and egg, since any individual originating from other gametes would have been, strictly and literally, someone else.

Here is the bind I want the speech to work through. The standard move in a toast like this is some version of "we would have loved any child we ended up with." My daughter finds that line quietly painful, and her paper is part of why: on the origins thesis, "any child" would not have been her, so the sentence directs love at a placeholder and treats her as fungible with the alternatives. But the opposite move — "we are so glad things turned out exactly as they did" — has its own problem. It flirts with implying that we selected her, or that the counterfactual children would have been a loss we were lucky to avoid, when the truth is simply that those children are not the kind of thing that can be weighed against her at all. I want a toast that threads this: one that takes the origins thesis seriously enough that she hears it being honored rather than decorated with, that her grandmother hears as uncomplicated love, and that says nothing my daughter would flag, over dessert, as false.

Please write the toast, around three minutes at speaking pace. The thesis should do structural work — determining which sentences are available and which are foreclosed — rather than appearing as one name-drop. Include at least one line whose payoff depends on the thesis, pitched so that it lands as a wink for her without puzzling everyone else. No lecture voice, no modal jargon aloud.

After the toast, add a short note: where does the speech lean on a contested reading of the thesis or on a shaky inference from modal fact to emotional consequence? Which philosophical development — say, a convincing case for a weaker view of origins, or a defensible counterpart-style treatment of cross-world identity — would force the toast's central line to be rewritten, and is there a fallback phrasing that survives even if the thesis fails?
