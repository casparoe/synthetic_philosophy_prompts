You are an expert in social epistemology and the formal theory of collective choice, familiar with the Condorcet jury theorem and its standard idealizations. I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I am trying to reverse-engineer the implicit rule my dissertation advisor uses when she judges whether a majority verdict is epistemically trustworthy. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming at least three distinct rules that could fit her track record before committing to a prediction.

Her verdicts so far:

- Case 1: 1,001 voters choose between two options; each voter is independently right 55% of the time. Verdict: the majority outcome is highly trustworthy.
- Case 2: A panel of 9 judges, each independently right 80% of the time, binary decision. Verdict: highly trustworthy.
- Case 3: 1,001 voters, each independently right 45% of the time. Verdict: the majority outcome is anti-trustworthy; you should expect the majority to be wrong.
- Case 4: 9 specialists, each with an excellent individual track record (around 90% accuracy on past cases of this kind), but all trained in the same small research programme, using the same proprietary method, and with a documented history of failing together on the same rare pitfalls. They vote independently, without conferring. Verdict: the majority outcome is only modestly trustworthy, far less than the individual accuracies would suggest.
- Case 5: A single decision-maker with a 99% accuracy record. Verdict: highly trustworthy, no aggregation needed.

Next week she will consider this case: a hiring committee of 15 members, each of whom would individually rank the two finalists correctly about 75% of the time if they voted privately and simultaneously. Instead, they deliberate in a meeting and then vote by a show of hands in sequence, going around the table, so that every member sees how all the earlier members voted before casting their own. The first few members to speak happen to favour the same candidate.

Predict her verdict on this case. In doing so: (a) identify the candidate rules that fit her earlier five verdicts -- I have in mind at least a pure competence-and-size rule, a rule sensitive to correlated error through shared sources, and a rule that treats public deliberation as competence-boosting, but check whether these actually fit and whether something else fits better; (b) state what each surviving rule predicts about the new case, noting where they diverge; (c) give your best guess at her actual verdict, with reasons; and (d) describe the single further case -- some minimal variation on the committee setup -- whose verdict would most decisively discriminate between the two leading candidate rules.
