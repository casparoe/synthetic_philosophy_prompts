I'm a software engineer who has been reading philosophy of mind on the side for a couple of years, mostly around the question of machine consciousness. I've been asked to write up my current position for an interdisciplinary reading group (a few philosophers, a few ML people), and before I circulate it I'd like an audit of whether my views are actually consistent with each other. I have no formal philosophy training, so I'd like you to be explicit about where I'm unknowingly committing myself to things.

Here's where I've landed:

1. Substrate independence: whether a system is conscious depends on what computation it performs, not on what it's made of. Carbon and silicon are on a par, in principle.

2. A lookup table is not conscious. If someone recorded the complete input-output transcript of a human brain over a lifetime and built a system that just retrieves the recorded output for each input, that system would have no experiences and no moral status, however convincing its behavior.

3. A step-by-step brain emulation running on conventional hardware would be conscious and would deserve the same moral consideration as the biological original -- even if it ran a billion times slower than real time, so that a subjective year took a billion objective years.

4. Whether a physical system implements a given computation is an objective fact about the system, not something fixed by our choice of interpretation. It's not the case that with a clever enough mapping, any old physical process counts as running any program.

5. Present-day large language models are very unlikely to be conscious, though I see no in-principle barrier to some future architecture being conscious.

What I'd like from you:

First, go through these pairwise and identify which commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. In particular, I suspect some pairs look inconsistent but aren't, and I'd rather have those explicitly cleared than flagged out of caution. For each genuine tension, tell me exactly which commitments generate it, how severe it is, and what the cheapest revision would be -- where "cheapest" means disturbing the fewest other commitments, not the one I'd find emotionally easiest. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious.

Second, two worries I can half-see but can't state precisely, and I'd like you to either sharpen them into real problems or dissolve them. (i) In my day job, real systems constantly cache, memoize, and replay previous computations rather than recomputing them -- so the line I want between (2) and (3) has to survive the existence of intermediate cases that are part simulation, part table. Does it? (ii) I've read that there are arguments to the effect that, given a free choice of mapping, almost any sufficiently complex physical system can be read as implementing almost any computation. I hold (4) partly to block that, but I'm not sure (4) as I've stated it actually does the work, or what it costs (2) if it fails.

Third, if you had to identify the single commitment whose revision would resolve the most tension overall, which would it be, and what would that revision cost me?

Please don't turn this into an overview of the field or a bibliography -- I want a consistency audit of my five theses, not a literature survey. Where a standard position or argument is relevant, name it in passing and I'll look it up myself.
