I'm preparing a graduate seminar session on Jeremy Bentham's attack on natural rights in Anarchical Fallacies, and I want to stress-test my own sympathies. Bentham's core move, as I read him, is to treat talk of rights as legitimate only where there is actual law, actual legislators, and actual enforcement behind it: real law generates real rights, whereas appeals to natural or imprescriptible rights that supposedly exist prior to and independently of any legal order are, on his view, empty rhetoric that mistakes fervent moral assertion for a description of something in the world, and worse, invites people to treat whatever they intensely desire as a claim that no government may ever override. My seminar students mostly think this is obviously wrong, and I suspect they're too quick about it, so I'm collecting objections to Bentham's position in order to find the strongest one.

Here are the objections I already have on my list, so please don't repeat these or close variants of them: (1) the side-constraint objection, that rights are needed precisely as trumps or limits against the kind of utility aggregation Bentham endorses, so his framework can't accommodate their function; (2) the conflation objection, that Bentham collapses the distinction between legal rights and moral rights, and that the entire point of natural-rights talk is to provide a standpoint from which existing law can be criticized as unjust; (3) the proves-too-much objection, that his argument would equally undermine talk of moral duties that no law backs, leaving him unable to say that a legally permitted atrocity is morally wrong; (4) the modern-theories objection, that interest-based or choice-based accounts of rights give rights a respectable home without any commitment to pre-legal metaphysical entities, so Bentham is attacking a strawman; and (5) the fiction objection, that Bentham himself relies on fictions elsewhere in his jurisprudence when they are useful, so the mere fact that natural rights are not real entities can't be the decisive issue — the question is whether the fiction does good or harm.

I want one further objection to Bentham's critique that is not on this list and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of items already on it — in particular, if your point depends on rights serving as limits on utility maximization, on the moral/legal distinction, or on the idea that talk of nonexistent things can still be useful, it probably counts as a variant, so push further. State the objection clearly, explain briefly how it engages Bentham's actual argument rather than a caricature, and then tell me honestly whether you think it succeeds or where it runs into trouble. I'd rather get one genuinely considered objection than a list of five weak ones.
