Act as a professional philosopher who regularly helps people without academic training turn rough ideas into feasible research projects. Think carefully and prioritize conceptual precision over impressive terminology.

I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been thinking about situations in which a person or community tries to bind its future self: a patient requests that certain treatment preferences be honored during a future psychiatric crisis, while a constitution makes some legal changes difficult for future majorities. In both cases, the later individual or community may competently and sincerely reject the earlier decision.

I am applying for a three-month independent research grant and need a proposal of about 1,500 words. My half-formed idea is to shift the debate away from the familiar question of whether the earlier self has authority over the later self and toward the question of how that authority is maintained. A precommitment is not made only once: it must later be interpreted, applied, reaffirmed, revised, or overridden. I suspect that the important distinction is not simply between binding and nonbinding commitments, but among different maintenance designs, such as:

- hard locks, which strongly resist later change;
- scheduled revision windows, which require the commitment to be reaffirmed periodically;
- emergency brakes, which permit override under carefully specified conditions.

My tentative thought is that authorization can decay even when the original consent was fully voluntary and informed. It might decay because circumstances change, because the later person’s values or understanding change, because a political community’s membership changes, or because the commitment was never given a meaningful procedure for reconsideration. On the other hand, perhaps some commitments retain force despite later disagreement because other people have reasonably relied on them, because the later self is predictably biased, or because easy revision would defeat the purpose of precommitment. I would like to call the resulting approach a consent-maintenance account, although you may propose a better name.

Please turn this into a research proposal. Begin with a brief brainstorming section comparing three possible ways of framing the project, explaining which is most tractable and why. Then provide the proposal itself, including:

3. A manageable scope: compare one individual case, a psychiatric Ulysses contract, with one collective case, constitutional entrenchment through demanding amendment procedures. Do not try to cover every kind of promise, contract, law, or advance directive.
