You are a philosopher who also has years of experience designing seminar activities that make abstract positions in normative ethics viscerally felt rather than merely stated. Think carefully, and brainstorm two or three candidate mechanics before settling on a final design; show me the alternatives you rejected and why, briefly, before the full design.

The setting: an upper-level undergraduate seminar on distributive justice, 16 students, one 90-minute session. The students have already read standard material on utilitarianism, egalitarianism, and prioritarianism, and they can recite the leveling-down objection -- the claim that if equality is valuable in itself, then making the better-off worse off without helping anyone is in at least one respect an improvement, which seems absurd -- but in my experience they recite it without feeling it. They nod along and then, in discussion, keep sliding back into treating equality and priority to the worse-off as the same thing.

Design a structured activity, staged with real small stakes (I can offer chocolates, or grade-free participation points they genuinely care about), that makes participants run into the distinction themselves before it is named. Specifically, I want the following to happen in the room, in this order: first, the group should unanimously endorse lossy transfers from the better-off to the worse-off (so they discover that both egalitarians and prioritarians agree in the standard cases); second, they should be confronted with an option to equalize downward -- destroying some of the better-off participants' holdings with no gain to anyone -- and the room should actually split, with some participants reaching for it and others recoiling; third, they should have to articulate, under pressure from their peers, what exactly they were valuing in round one if they recoil in round two.

One design constraint you must address explicitly: any mechanic using diminishing marginal returns (e.g., a concave conversion of tokens to welfare) risks making prioritarianism indistinguishable from simple risk aversion, which would teach the wrong lesson. Your design must include a move that separates giving priority to the worse-off from being cautious under uncertainty.

Give me: the full rules and payoff structure (with concrete numbers), any roles or information asymmetries, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact point at which the facilitator is allowed to name the concepts, and a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the session onto the leveling-down objection and the prioritarian reply. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when this is run -- students gaming the stakes, a unanimous round two, someone invoking fairness-as-procedure -- and how to handle each. End with an honest note on which feature of the philosophical dispute the activity structurally cannot reproduce and the facilitator must simply supply.

Close your response with a single line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, what the activity makes participants feel and what it cannot make them feel.
