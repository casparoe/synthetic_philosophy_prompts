I'm organizing a public colloquium at my university and will be moderating the Q&A with the invited speaker, a philosopher of physics whose new book defends a modern Machian relationism about space and motion. Her core claim, developed through a technical appendix on best-matching and shape dynamics, is that only relative configurations of bodies and their changes are real, and that Newton's bucket establishes at most that a theory needs more structure than bare instantaneous configurations -- not that it needs absolute space. I've read enough of the literature to know the standard dialectic on both sides, and I expect the audience to include several substantivalists who will ask the obvious questions badly. I'd like my own questions to do better.

She has two habitual deflections I'm specifically trying to neutralize. The first: when pressed on rotation, she says that rotation relative to the total matter distribution of the universe is all the rotation there is, and that an empty-universe scenario is not one her view is committed to making sense of. The second: when pressed on general relativity, she says GR is only imperfectly Machian and that the existence of non-Machian solutions (rotating cosmologies, matter-free spacetimes with global rotation) shows a mismatch between GR and the correct fundamental theory, not a problem for relationism as such. I want a sequence of six to eight questions that progressively closes off these two exits: starting from the twin-globes-in-empty-space case, moving through the question of what grounds the privilege of inertial frames if not spacetime structure, and pressing on whether her view can explain, rather than merely redescribe, why the surface of the water in the bucket curves only when the water rotates relative to the distant stars -- including whether she owes us an account of what the correlation between local rotation and the fixed stars consists in, and whether a world with different distant matter but the same local physics is coherent on her view.

Please write the questions themselves, and for each one give me a one- or two-sentence note on the reply I'm most likely to get and the follow-up that keeps the pressure on, so I can adapt in real time. The final question should push on what would have to be true, empirically or conceptually, for her to concede that spacetime has structure beyond the relations between bodies. Avoid anything biographical or soft; every question should turn on the actual mechanics of her position.
