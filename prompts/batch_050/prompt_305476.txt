Two philosophers of medicine have spent a semester arguing past each other: one defends a naturalist, value-free account of health and disease on which disease is a matter of species-typical biological functioning falling below some statistically anchored level (in the spirit of Boorse's biostatistical theory), while the other defends a normativist account on which calling something a disease is ineliminably a value judgment, since no purely descriptive facts about function or statistics can settle which conditions count as disorders. Write the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The statement must contain the following, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should not be vacuous: include, at minimum, points of agreement about what any adequate concept of disease must do (e.g., its clinical and classificatory roles), about what the statistical facts alone can and cannot establish, about the historical record of contested classifications (such as the past medicalization of homosexuality) and what both sides take it to show, and about which stock objections each side concedes it must answer (for the naturalist: the reference-class problem and the fact that not every suboptimal function is a disease; for the normativist: the risk of making disease attributions hostage to shifting social values).

2. A disagreements section in which each remaining dispute is stated in wording each side would accept as a fair description of its own position. Explicitly flag any disagreement that, on inspection, turns out to be partly verbal -- for instance, if the two sides are using "disease," "disorder," or "illness" differently, say so, and restate the residual non-verbal disagreement underneath.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. Candidate cruxes you should consider (keep whichever survive scrutiny, add your own): whether a notion of biological function can be fixed -- via etiological, selected-effects, or systemic-capacity accounts -- without smuggling in values; whether a harm or badness component is built into the concept of disease or attaches only downstream, in decisions about treatment; whether the choice of reference class (age group, sex, species, environment) can be made non-arbitrarily without normative input.

4. For each crux, at least one concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's verdict recorded in advance. Include at least the following constructed cases among those you adjudicate: (a) an engineered human variant whose livers, by design, cease functioning at age forty in every member of the population, with no felt distress but a shortened lifespan -- is this disease, normal design, or neither, and where do the two accounts diverge; (b) a statistically universal age-related decline (e.g., presbyopia) that everyone develops and everyone dislikes; (c) a statistically rare condition that impairs no species-typical function but is profoundly unwanted by those who have it. For each case, state what the naturalist must say, what the normativist must say, and whether the case forces either side into a verdict it should find uncomfortable.

Close the statement with a short joint assessment: which parts of the dispute, if any, both parties now regard as semantic; which crux, if resolved in the naturalist's favor, would most weaken the normativist position, and conversely; and one empirical or conceptual result -- from biology, linguistics, or clinical practice -- that both agree would be evidence against their own view.

Format: headings matching the four numbered sections above; the agreed claims numbered individually; each disagreement and crux labeled; tone that of a document both authors would actually sign, i.e., no strawmanning and no claims so hedged that neither side could object to anything.
