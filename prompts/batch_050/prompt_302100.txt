So here's a thing that's been bugging me. In the US (and most other countries) it's completely legal, even celebrated, for someone to donate a kidney to a total stranger -- there are non-directed donors who just show up at a hospital and give a kidney to whoever's next on the list, and transplant centers run these elaborate kidney chains where one altruistic donor kicks off a whole sequence of swaps. But if that same person offered the same kidney to the same recipient for, say, $40,000, everyone involved could go to prison. Meanwhile, you can legally be *paid* to donate plasma (lots of people do it regulary for a couple hundred bucks a month), you can be paid to enroll in a clinical trial with real medical risks, and in many states you can be paid tens of thousands of dollars to be a surrogate, which is a far bigger medical ordeal than a nephrectomy. So the line the law draws isn't just "no selling body stuff" -- it's something more specific.

My question: what theory of commodification does this whole setup presuppose? Does it only make sense if money inherently corrupts certain kinds of transactions (in which case why is paid plasma fine?), or if irreversible harm plus payment equals exploitation in a way that reversible or risky paid stuff doesn't (in which case what's the principled line between a kidney and a dangerous phase-1 trial?), or if it's really about protecting poor people from themselves (in which case why do we trust the same people to donate for free -- is the worry that money makes the choice *less* voluntary rather than more?). Reconstruct what the practice actually commits us to, tell me which parts are strictly presupposed versus just consistent with several different theories, and then say what would have to change if we swapped the implicit theory for a flat-out welfarist one where a voluntary deal that leaves both sides better off should be allowed. Also curious whether the crowding-out worry (paid kidneys driving out altruistic donation) is doing real work here or is just a post-hoc patch.
