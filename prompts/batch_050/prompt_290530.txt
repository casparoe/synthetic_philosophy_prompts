I'm teaching a seminar on political philosophy to advanced undergraduates, and next week we reach Hobbes. I want to pair Leviathan with Golding's Lord of the Flies, which most of my students read in school and which they tend to remember as straightforward proof of Hobbes's view: remove the adults, and life on the island becomes nasty, brutish, and short. I'm suspicious of that memory, and I want them to be suspicious too. Please write a careful reading of the novel through the lens of Hobbes's account of the state of nature and the logic of the social contract.

A few things I'd like the reading to grapple with directly. First, the island is arguably not a true state of nature in Hobbes's sense: the boys arrive already formed by civilized habits, institutions, and norms, so is the novel really testing what humans are like without government, or only what happens when government is withdrawn from people who have known it? Second, consider how well the novel's factions map onto Hobbes's theory of sovereignty: Ralph's assembly and the conch as a kind of covenant without a sword, and Jack's tribe, which seems to acquire obedience precisely through fear, feasting, and protection from the beast — arguably a darkly successful Leviathan. Third, take seriously what Hobbes would make of the ending: the naval officer arrives and the war simply stops. Is that the contract theorist's point made in one scene, or does it expose something Hobbes's framework can't easily explain, since the officer's authority rests on a larger order the boys did not consent to and cannot see? Fourth, where does the novel resist Hobbes altogether — for instance in Simon, whose goodness seems to survive the absence of law, or in the role of the beast, which is not an external enemy but something the boys carry in themselves?

I don't want a reading that reduces Golding to an illustration of Leviathan, nor one that distorts Hobbes into a cartoon pessimist to make the fit easier. If the novel complicates the theory, say where and how; if the theory illuminates a scene that students usually read naively (the killing of Piggy and the shattering of the conch seems like a natural case), show it. Finally, I'd like the reading to end with what the novel can show my students about the state of nature that Hobbes's own argument cannot — something about the psychology of fear, perhaps, or about how quickly covenant unravels when no one enforces it — and, conversely, what the philosophical argument establishes that no story, however good, ever could.
