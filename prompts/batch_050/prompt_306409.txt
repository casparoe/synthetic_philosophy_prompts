I'm in a reading group with some friends (mix of history and econ people) and we just did Marx's 1859 Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy. Afterwards one of them, who tends to be very confident, sent this to the group chat, and I want to know whether he's actually being fair to the text: "Marx's view is basically that economics determines everything else. Law, religion, art, philosophy -- none of it has any independent content, it's all just the economic base wearing a costume, usually a costume designed to disguise who's exploiting whom. Since ideas are just products of material conditions, you never need to engage with what anyone actually says: you just identify their class position and that tells you why they believe it and why they're wrong. And because the economic side of things is the only part that can be studied rigorously, like a natural science, history is fundamentally predictable in a way the history of ideas never could be. So historians who take debates about religion or political theory seriously as causes are missing the point."

Here's the chunk of the Preface we focused on, which I think he might be leaning on too hard, or maybe reading correctly and I'm wrong:

"In studying such transformations it is always necessary to distinguish between the material transformation of the economic conditions of production, which can be determined with the precision of natural science, and the legal, political, religious, artistic or philosophic – in short, ideological forms in which men become conscious of this conflict and fight it out. Just as one does not judge an individual by what he thinks about himself, so one cannot judge such a period of transformation by its consciousness, but, on the contrary, this consciousness must be explained from the contradictions of material life, from the conflict existing between the social forces of production and the relations of production."

Can you go through his message claim by claim and tell me which parts Marx would actually sign, which parts are a defensible-but-strong reading, and which parts contradict or at least aren't supported by this passage? A few specific things bugging me: does explaining consciousness in material terms really license dismissing arguments without engaging them (the analogy to not judging an individual by what he thinks of himself seems to cut in a weird direction here?), does the passage say anything about ideas being deliberate disguises as opposed to just materially explained, and does "fight it out" in ideological forms imply ideas have some real role after all? Also, if his last claim about predictability doesn't follow from the passage, is there anything in the distinction Marx draws that he's maybe half-misremembering?
