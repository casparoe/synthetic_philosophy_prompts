So I'm in this early modern philosophy seminar and we just did Montaigne's "Of Cannibals" (in the old Cotton translation). Everyone in class wanted to talk about the famous bit later in the essay where he says we call barbarous whatever isn't our own custom, and the professor kept saying the standard reading of Montaigne as a cultural relativist gets the essay wrong. Fine. But what actually confused ME is the opening, which nobody wanted to discuss. The essay starts like this:

"When King Pyrrhus invaded Italy, having viewed and considered the order of the army the Romans sent out to meet him; 'I know not,' said he, 'what kind of barbarians' (for so the Greeks called all other nations) 'these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it.' [...] By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report."

And then a couple paragraphs later, introducing his source about the New World, he writes: "I am afraid our eyes are bigger than our bellies, and that we have more curiosity than capacity; for we grasp at all, but catch nothing but wind."

Here's my problem. The Pyrrhus anecdote seems to set up a lesson about distrusting hearsay and secondhand reports -- judge by the eye of reason, not by common report. But then the entire rest of the essay is Montaigne trusting hearsay! His evidence about the Tupinamba comes from a servant who lived in Brazil, plus Plato, Aristotle, Virgil, and other books. And the "grasp at all, catch nothing but wind" line sounds almost like he's admitting his own essay is a case of eyes-bigger-than-belly curiosity. So I have this half-formed idea that the opening isn't really a relativist move at all (Pyrrhus doesn't conclude the Romans are fine *by their own standards*, he concludes they were misdescribed) -- it's an epistemology-of-testimony move, and the essay is deliberately performing the failure of the very standard it announces.
