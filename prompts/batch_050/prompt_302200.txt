The puzzle is that in equal-stakes cases (each of the five dies if not reached, same as the one), contractualists clearly want to save the five. But if the number of equal claims is allowed to break the tie, what's stopping numbers from doing more — why doesn't a billion headaches aggregate up to outvote one death?

Give me your actual credence in this claim: Scanlon-style contractualism can justify saving the greater number in equal-stakes rescue cases without either abandoning the individualist restriction or being forced to aggregate many trivial harms against one serious one. Decompose it, roughly: the probability the individualist restriction is even coherent on its own; the probability that tie-breaking among equal claims can be motivated without smuggling aggregation in the back door; the probability that any workable patch for the rescue case ends up collapsing into aggregation anyway; the probability that contractualism can afford to just stay silent on these cases and remain a viable theory of right and wrong. A number and one line of justification for each, how they combine, then which term your final number is most sensitive to and what argument would move it most.
