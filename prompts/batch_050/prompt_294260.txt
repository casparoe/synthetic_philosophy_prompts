I'm a philosophy undergrad and I've gotten myself properly tangled up in the Dennett–Searle fight over original vs. derived intentionality. In seminar we did the Chinese Room and I mostly sided with Searle, but then my roommate, a total Dennett partisan, hit me with the two-bitser: a vending machine built to sort quarters, where the state that "detects" quarters only means quarter because its designers and users treat it that way, so its meaning is borrowed from ours. Fine, granted. But then he ran the following argument on me, which seems to prove way too much, and I can't figure out where to push back:

(1) The two-bitser's sorting-state means quarter only in virtue of the intentions of the people who built and use it; strip those away and it's just a lump of metal going click.
(2) What goes for the two-bitser goes for anything: if a system's organization was produced by some process that selected it for a function, then whatever meaning its states have is conferred by that process, not intrinsic to the system.
(3) Human brains got their organization from natural selection, which selected various neural states for their functions (detecting predators, food, mates, and so on).
(4) So if human thoughts mean anything at all, their meaning derives from natural selection.
(5) But natural selection has no intentions. It's not an agent, it doesn't have purposes, and it "selects for" things only metaphorically.
(6) Derived intentionality can only exist where there's a source with intentionality of its own to lend -- that's what "derived" means.
(7) So human thoughts have no derived intentionality, and given (4), no intentionality of any other kind either.
(8) But my thought that there's pizza in the fridge is obviously about pizza. Contradiction, so one of the premises has to die.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Two constraints: the two-bitser intuition in (1) seems rock solid to me and I'd rather not give it up, and I'm obviously not going to accept (8)'s eliminativist conclusion that my thoughts aren't about anything. Walk me through the main exits -- Searle-style intrinsic intentionality, Dennett's it's-derived-all-the-way-down view, and the teleosemantics move of grounding meaning in selection history -- and what each one has to pay to block the argument. Also please be honest about what's actually settled here versus what's still a live dispute among philosophers of mind; I don't want Dennett's or Searle's spin presented to me as the consensus. Keep the whole reply under 600 words.
