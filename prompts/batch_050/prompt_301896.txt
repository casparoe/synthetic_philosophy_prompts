My mother has been on dialysis for three years and is on the transplant waiting list, which is how I ended up in the ethics-of-organ-markets literature at 1 a.m. A widely shared explainer article I read last week summarizes the case against legalizing kidney sales as follows.

The article claims, first, that opponents of kidney markets object because selling an organ treats the human body as a commodity, and that this objection is essentially religious in origin and so carries no weight in secular policy debate. Second, it says that economists have shown that a legal market would increase the total supply of kidneys, because payment gives people a reason to sell who currently have no reason to donate, and that the only serious disagreement is over the size of the increase. Third, it presents the concern that sellers would be disproportionately poor as a concern that poor people would be coerced, and dismisses it on the grounds that offering someone money cannot coerce them, since coercion requires a threat. Fourth, it cites the classic comparison of voluntary and paid blood-donation systems as proof that introducing payment crowds out altruistic donation and lowers quality, and treats this as the settled empirical verdict on organ markets too.

I want to believe most of this, because it points toward a policy that might save my mother's life, and that motive is exactly why I don't trust my own reading of it. My amateur sense is that the article is setting up weak versions of the objections to knock them down, but I can't tell which parts a careful market advocate would actually endorse and which parts misstate the opposing side.
