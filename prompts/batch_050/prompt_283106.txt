So I got into a dumb argument with a couple of friends after a department talk last week and I want to settle it properly. The talk mentioned in passing that both the calculus and the theory of evolution by natural selection were arrived at independently by two people at roughly the same time, and apparently this happens alot -- the telephone was patented by Bell literally hours before another guy filed, and there's that whole list of "multiple discoveries" historians of science keep. My friend said this shows the whole credit system in science is basically arbitrary, and I couldn't quite say why he was wrong.

Here's the practice I want analyzed. Science (and patent law, and prize culture generally) is organized almost entirely around priority: whoever gets there first gets the Nobel (capped at three people, so even big collaborations have to pick winners), gets their name attached to the thing, gets the patent -- and most patent systems now don't even care who invented first, only who filed first, which seems to make it even more nakedly a race. Meanwhile the person who publishes six months later gets nothing, even if their work was completely independent and just as good. My question: what does this whole setup presuppose about what a discovery *is*? Does it only make sense if discoveries are contingent achievements -- things that would not have happened, or not happened when they did, without that particular person -- so that priority credit tracks real counterfactual credit? Because the simultaneous-invention pattern seems to push the other way: if calculus and natural selection were more or less in the air, inevitable given the state of the field, then rewarding the first discoverer starts to look like giving a medal to whoever happened to be standing where the lightning was going to strike anyway.

And then the follow-ups. Can the practice be defended on purely incentive-based grounds even if the inevitability view is right -- i.e., it presupposes nothing about the metaphysics of discovery, it's just a coordination device and a bounty system -- or does that defense quietly collapse, since you don't need to incentivize what would happen regardless? Is the practice even internally consistent: we treat "independent rediscovery" as worth nothing in science but in patent law an independent inventor still infringes, which seems to presuppose two different theories of what the reward is *for*. Finally: if we fully took the inevitability view seriously, what would actually have to change -- do we fund and honor fields or problems instead of people, and would anything of real value be lost if who made a discovery genuinely stopped mattering?
