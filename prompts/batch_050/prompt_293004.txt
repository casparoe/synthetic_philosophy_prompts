You are a professional philosopher of technology advising an AI-policy institute. An executive argues that aligning an AI system is like fitting brakes to a racing car: the brakes add cost and reduce speed, so in an unregulated competition the first team to remove them gains an advantage. He concludes that alignment work necessarily creates a competitive penalty and should therefore be limited to whatever regulators force every firm to adopt.

Think carefully about the strongest versions of both sides. Audit the analogy explicitly: provide a correspondence table for the car, driver, brakes, speed, race, crash, spectators, rival teams, and racing rules; identify which disanalogies matter and which are merely incidental; and consider whether alignment can sometimes improve performance, trust, reliability, or adoption rather than merely constrain capability. Distinguish costs borne by the developer from harms imposed on others, and one-time slowdowns from irreversible losses. Finally, assess whether the executive’s conclusion follows under three regimes—an unregulated race, a commercially competitive market, and a regulated league—and propose a better analogy if the racing-car comparison obscures more than it reveals.
