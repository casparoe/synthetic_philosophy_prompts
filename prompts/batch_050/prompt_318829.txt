A friend of mine from my graduate cohort — we both did our coursework in decision theory and have been needling each other about this ever since — has very settled intuitions about when it is rational to "cooperate with people who reason the way you do," in the vicinity of what Hofstadter called superrationality, and I want to figure out what rule she is actually running. Here is her track record, which I've compiled from years of arguments. (1) In an anonymous one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger, moderate stakes, she defects, and says any other answer confuses wishing with choosing. (2) Against a perfect psychological copy of herself, where both parties are told the other is an exact copy who will deliberate identically, she cooperates without hesitation. (3) Against a close friend of hers who shares her values, has read the same game theory, and whom she can predict fairly well in ordinary life, she still defects — her comment was that being able to guess how someone will reason is not the same as their reasoning being tied to yours. (4) In the Platonia-style setup — twenty players, each may privately submit their name, the prize is awarded only if exactly one name is submitted — she says the rational move is to enter with probability 1/20 using a genuine randomizer. (5) In a Luring-Lottery-style setup — a fixed prize is divided among all entries received, unlimited entries per person, and one entry is drawn at random to determine the winner — she says the rational move is to randomize so that her expected number of entries is small, and she describes people who flood such lotteries with thousands of entries as defectors wrecking a commons. Now the new case: a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, no communication, against someone she will never meet, but the organizers tell both players that they screened ten thousand volunteers on a battery of a hundred calibration dilemmas and paired the two people whose response patterns matched most closely — her counterpart's answers agreed with hers on ninety-seven of the hundred. There is no causal connection between them and no guarantee of identical reasoning; the only thing she has is this striking statistical evidence that whatever they are, they are the same kind of decider. Will she cooperate or defect?

I'd like you to answer as a philosopher specializing in rational choice theory who is equally at home with causal, evidential, and "superrational" approaches to correlated decision-making. Please think carefully rather than pattern-matching to a textbook: the interesting work here is that her five verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one rule. For instance, she might be running a strict rule on which cooperation is warranted only when some mechanism *guarantees* that the counterpart's choice is correlated with hers (a copy, a shared source of deliberation), with everything else — including the lottery cases — handled by ordinary equilibrium and expected-value reasoning. Or she might be running a graded evidential rule on which sufficiently strong *evidence* of correlated reasoning is enough, with the close-friend case explained by the weakness of that particular evidence rather than by any requirement of a guarantee. These two rules agree on her entire track record and diverge on the matched-pair case, and there may be a third rule I've missed that also fits. Note where each candidate rule strains — for example, the graded evidential rule has to work to explain why ninety-seven-out-of-a-hundred concordance counts but years of friendship doesn't.

Structure your answer as follows. Begin with two or three sentences of prose giving your best-guess prediction of her verdict in the matched-pair case and your degree of confidence. Then provide a table with exactly these columns: "Candidate rule" (state the rule precisely enough that it could be applied mechanically), "Fit with verdicts (1)–(5)" (note any verdict the rule accommodates only with strain, and say what the strain is), "Prediction for the matched-pair case" (cooperate, defect, or genuinely indeterminate, with one sentence of justification), and "Discriminating case" (describe a further scenario on which this rule and the others come apart, so that her verdict there would tell them apart). Close with one short paragraph saying which rule you find most plausible as her actual rule and why, and conceding honestly if you think the track record underdetermines the answer — but even then, commit to the prediction you'd bet on.
