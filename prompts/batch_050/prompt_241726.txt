I recently got into a debate with a friend who is training as a lawyer, and I realized I could not articulate the philosophical basis of a distinction she takes entirely for granted. In Anglo-American criminal law, the line between acts and omissions does enormous work. A stranger who watches a child drown in a shallow pond, doing nothing, commits no crime, whereas someone who pushes the child in commits murder -- even if the bystander's intervention would have been trivially easy and the outcome just as foreseeable. Yet the same legal system happily convicts a parent, a lifeguard, or a contractual caregiver for standing idly by in the very same pond scenario, and it treats withdrawing life support from a terminally ill patient as permissible "allowing to die" while treating a lethal injection as a criminal causing of death. Courts and commentators habitually say that the defendant in the active cases "caused" the death, while the mere bystander "let it happen" -- but then the special-relationship cases seem to concede that an omission can cause a death after all, provided the right duty is in place. Meanwhile, in many civil-law jurisdictions, general Good Samaritan statutes impose a duty of easy rescue on everyone, suggesting the whole arrangement is contingent.

Please answer as a philosopher with genuine expertise in the metaphysics of causation and in legal theory, and work carefully: first map out the candidate positions, then evaluate them. My question is what this body of legal practice presupposes about causation by omission. Does the act/omission asymmetry in liability presuppose that omissions are not causes at all -- that causation requires the transfer of some physical magnitude, or a genuine connection between events, which an absence cannot provide? Or does it presuppose only that omissions are a *different kind* of cause, parasitic on norms or expectations about what was supposed to happen? Consider whether a simple counterfactual theory of causation can even be squared with the practice: if the bystander's inaction is such that, had he acted, the child would have lived, then on a counterfactual view his omission caused the death just as much as the pusher's act did -- so does the legal distinction force us to reject counterfactual accounts, or can they be patched with defaults-and-deviants machinery to recover the asymmetry?

Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly *presupposes* and what it merely *fits with*: perhaps the law's asymmetry is really tracking something other than causation -- moral responsibility, the costs of enforcing duties, or a liberty interest in not being conscripted into rescues -- in which case the causal talk in the cases would be a façade. If so, say what would license that diagnosis. Finally, tell me what would have to change in legal doctrine if we adopted a thoroughgoing counterfactual account on which every outcome that counterfactually depends on an omission is caused by it: would the act/omission distinction survive as a doctrine about duty rather than about causation, and would anything be lost in that translation? Begin with a brief inventory of the main positions before settling on your assessment.
