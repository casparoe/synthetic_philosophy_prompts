I coordinate a monthly philosophy reading group for adults without formal philosophy training -- mostly lawyers, engineers, and one retired schoolteacher -- and next quarter we are reading selections from Wittgenstein's On Certainty. I have forty-five minutes at the start of the first session to give them an orientation to hinge epistemology before they read a word of the text, and I want a plan for that orientation rather than the orientation itself. My worry is that this is an unusually easy idea to half-learn: my group will arrive knowing the skeptical problem from Descartes or the Matrix, they will expect Wittgenstein to defeat the skeptic on the skeptic's own terms, and I suspect several of them will read the material as either dogmatic foundationalism or shrugging relativism depending on their temperament.

Plan the forty-five minutes as a teaching design problem. What has to be in place before the hinge idea can even be stated -- I am guessing they first need to see doubt as an activity with preconditions rather than a free-floating attitude, but tell me if I have the dependency wrong -- and in what order the components should be introduced, with the reason for each ordering decision. Identify the one concrete example that should carry the whole session (something from ordinary life, not Moore's hand and not a brain in a vat, since I want them off familiar skeptical terrain), and say what that example must have and must lack to do the work. Then give me the misconception map: the predictable misreadings -- that hinges are beliefs Wittgenstein thinks are obviously true, that he is offering a new anti-skeptical proof, that hinges can never change, that the riverbed metaphor licenses relativism, that he is just Moore with better prose -- and for each one, the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off and how, including whether any of them should be allowed to form briefly before being corrected because the correction lands better that way.

Please be decisive throughout: commit to one carrying example, one ordering, and one handling per misconception, and defend your choices against the runner-up in each case, rather than handing me a menu of options to choose among. Close the plan with a single question I can put to the group at the end of the session whose answer would show me whether they grasped hinges as a structural feature of epistemic practice rather than as a list of indubitable propositions, plus a short note on what I should openly decline to explain at this level (the later scholarly disputes about whether hinges are propositional at all, for instance) and how to say so without making the material feel withheld. The whole response should be in American English.
