A Casablanca rewatch with friends turned into an actual argument and I want ammunition, so: read the film through the nationalism vs cosmopolitanism debate, the one about whether compatriots (or co-members of some community or cause) have special moral claims on us that strangers don't.

My read going in: for the first two thirds Rick Blaine is basically the ideal cosmopolitan. He's lived everywhere, belongs nowhere, sticks his neck out for nobody, and his cafe is a little league of nations where he deals with refugees, Vichy cops, and Nazis on the same coolly equal terms. The only thing that moves him is a private attachment (Ilsa), which is partiality in the purely personal register, not the national one. Then the ending "converts" him: he gives up personal happiness and signs on to a cause. So the fight we had was: what actually happens at the airport?

Option A: the film is a vindication of partiality -- it's saying the rootless impartial stance was always either hollow or a mask for selfishness and cynicism, and moral maturity means letting particular ties (to a cause, a side, a moment in history) make claims on you that override both self-interest and neutral fair-dealing. Option B: the film is actually making the cosmopolitan's best move -- Rick never acquires special obligations to France or the Allies as such, he just finally notices that his impartial principles (anti-fascism isn't a national cause, it's a universal one) require sacrifice, which makes him a *better* cosmopolitan, not a convert to partiality. Fight this out for me, but actually adjudicate it, dont just split the difference.

A few scenes I think should do work in the answer: the roulette scene with the young Bulgarian couple (is Rick's rigging the game for them impartial benevolence, or favoritism toward the people who happen to be sympathetic and in front of him -- and if the latter, was the film's "cosmopolitan" phase ever impartial to begin with?), the Germans singing at the club and Laszlo ordering the band to play the Marseillaise (everyone in the room *cries* -- what does the film think national feeling *does*, motivationally, that universal principle can't?), and the fact that Rick's neutrality was always propped up by exit options and money that the refugees in his bar dont have. Does the film show neutrality to be a luxury good, available only to people with a plane to catch or a bar to own?

Also complicate things for me: the film was made in 1942 explicitly to argue Americans out of isolationism. Does that propagandist origin distort the philosophy -- does it smuggle in a conflation between fighting fascism (a universal cause any impartialist could sign up for) and loyalty to one's own side (a genuinely partial commitment)? And end by telling me what, if anything, the film dramatizes about partiality that the philosophy papers tend to miss -- my hunch is something about how detachment *feels* from the inside and what it costs to maintain, but convince me or talk me out of it.
