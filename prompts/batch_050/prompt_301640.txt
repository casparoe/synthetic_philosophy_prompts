My friend Samira runs a mid-sized animal welfare nonprofit, and over the years she's developed a pretty consistent instinct for which deals to strike with people who don't share her values and which to walk away from. We've argued about it enough that I've started keeping mental score. Her track record, as far as I can tell:

- A wealthy donor who trophy-hunts offered to fully fund a spay-and-neuter campaign, no strings attached beyond his name on the website. She took it.
- A coalition of religious groups she disagrees with about nearly everything (abortion, euthanasia, you name it) wanted to co-sponsor an anti-factory-farming ballot initiative. She campaigned alongside them happily.
- A fur company offered a huge donation if she'd publish a statement saying the fur industry had reformed. The money would almost certainly have saved more animals than the statement would cost. She refused.
- A grocery chain offered to fund her flagship program for five years if she'd quietly stop criticizing them -- no statements required, just silence. She refused that too.
- A restaurant group wanted to sign her welfare certification but only met the weaker "cage-free" standard, not the "pasture-raised" one she actually believes in. She certified them at cage-free rather than hold out and get nothing.
- A politician offered a straight trade: her organization's endorsement on an agriculture bill she mildly opposed, in exchange for his vote on a funding bill she cared about more. She took the trade.

Now there's a new one on her desk. A fast-food chain, whose welfare practices are genuinely somewhat better than the industry average (she's verified this herself), offers to bankroll her entire flagship program for three years if she'll appear in one of their ads. She wouldn't have to say anything false -- the script has her saying, sincerely, that their standards exceed the industry norm. But obviously appearing in the ad functions as an endorsement of the company as a whole, which she does not in fact endorse.

Predict what she'll do, and more importantly: what rule is she running? I can see at least two or three rules that fit all six past verdicts (one about never compromising her own voice, one about only trading things rather than endorsing things, maybe one about whether the deal is a genuine mutual concession vs. hush money), and I think they diverge on this case. Lay out the candidate rules, say which verdict each one predicts here, give me your best guess at her actual principle with the closest precedent from the list, and tell me one further hypothetical deal that would cleanly separate the two rules you find most plausible.
