I sit on the ethics advisory board of a midsize environmental foundation, and we've been asked to develop a position on solar geoengineering before our next round of grantmaking. At our last meeting I realized that my colleagues and I were talking past each other because none of us had articulated the principle behind our gut reactions, so I tried to write down mine as a list of verdicts. I'd like you to help me figure out what principle, if any, unifies them -- or to tell me honestly that they don't cohere and which one I should abandon. Here they are. (1) A single country unilaterally pursuing large-scale carbon removal -- direct air capture, enhanced weathering, afforestation -- is fine, even admirable, with no one's permission needed. (2) A private individual releasing small quantities of sulfur dioxide from balloons, at a scale with negligible climatic effect, purely as a commercial stunt, is not fine, and I feel this strongly even though the physical impact is a rounding error compared to one transatlantic flight. (3) A consortium of nations running a properly governed stratospheric aerosol injection program, with affected states genuinely consenting and an agreed ramp-down protocol, is permissible in principle. (4) The identical injection program carried out alone by a single climate-vulnerable island nation, after decades of being ignored in international negotiations, feels permissible to me too, or at least far harder to condemn than (2), despite having vastly greater physical impact and no one's consent. (5) Pure modeling and lab research on injection should be unrestricted, but I balk at the first outdoor field test, even a tiny one -- and I can't tell whether that's a principled line or squeamishness. (6) Once an injection program has been running for twenty years, I think the operators acquire an obligation to continue it (or wind it down very gradually) because of the termination shock an abrupt stop would cause -- and the fact that starting creates this lock-in strikes me as a serious argument against starting, which seems to sit oddly with my verdict in (3).

I have a tentative principle of my own, which is that what matters is consent: unilateral action is wrong in proportion to how many people it affects without their agreement. But I can already see it struggles -- it condemns (1) if the country is big enough, can't explain why (2) bothers me more than (4) when (2) affects almost no one, and says nothing about (5) or (6). So: is there a principle, or a small set of principles, stated precisely, that captures all or most of my verdicts? Please identify which of my cases motivates each clause of your proposal, and if the best candidate forces me to give up one of the six, tell me which and why. Then test your answer on two cases I haven't listed: first, a coalition of wealthy northern states proposes marine cloud brightening whose side effect, on the best models, is a modest shift in monsoon rainfall over South Asia; second, a scenario in which the mere prospect that injection might someday be available measurably reduces a government's willingness to cut emissions today -- does your principle treat that as an argument against researching injection, deploying it, talking about it, or none of these? I'm not looking for citations or a literature survey -- I can supply those -- just the conceptual analysis, done carefully enough that I can defend it at our next board meeting.
