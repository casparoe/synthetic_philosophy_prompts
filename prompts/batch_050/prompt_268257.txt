Construct a thought experiment in which an "easy knowledge" inference is intuitively legitimate: a subject forms beliefs through some process, and then infers, solely from the deliverances of that very process, that the process is reliable — and this time the inference strikes an ordinary, unprejudiced judge as perfectly good grounds for the conclusion, rather than as obviously epistemically worthless. The literature on bootstrapping and easy knowledge is stocked almost entirely with cases engineered to make such reasoning look disreputable (fuel gauges certified by their own readings, coloured walls checked under the same lighting); I want a case engineered in the opposite direction.

Constraints: (1) the setting must be ordinary life — no gauges, dials, or measuring instruments, no coloured objects viewed under special lighting, and no scenarios stipulated to be sceptical nightmares; (2) the belief-forming process must in fact be reliable, but the subject must possess no antecedent, process-independent evidence that it is, otherwise the conclusion isn't reached by bootstrapping at all; (3) the scenario should be one paragraph, with enough concrete detail that the intuitive verdict can be elicited from someone who has never heard of epistemic circularity; (4) you may not build the verdict in by stipulation — the legitimacy of the inference has to fall out of features of the situation that a third party could inspect.

After presenting the scenario, do the analytical work. First, state precisely the premises the subject is entitled to, the inferential step at issue, and the conclusion about reliability that results, and explain why a theorist committed to closure and basic sources (perception, memory, testimony, or whichever source your case uses) is under pressure to call the conclusion knowledge. Second, identify the feature of your case that does the work — the difference between your scenario and the standard disreputable ones — and say whether that feature is epistemically principled or merely psychological (i.e., whether it marks a real difference in warrant or only in how the case feels). Third, explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed: does it collapse back into a paradigmatic bad bootstrap, or does some residual legitimacy survive? Fourth, give the strongest response available to the bootstrapping-sceptic who wants to show your case only *seems* legitimate — for example, by arguing that the subject has smuggled in independent evidence of reliability, or that the conclusion reached is thinner than "my process is reliable" — and say whether you think that response succeeds.

The point of the exercise is to locate, by construction, the boundary between vicious and benign epistemic circularity: if a clean case of good bootstrapping can be built, the standard objections to easy knowledge must be targeting something more specific than circularity as such. Say, at the end, what your case implies that something is.

Lead with the thought experiment itself in the first sentence, then give the analysis, with no preamble and no restating of these instructions. Write in British English.
