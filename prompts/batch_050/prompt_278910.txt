You are a professional philosopher of art with particular expertise in theories of humour. Think carefully before drafting. Begin with no more than 150 words of planning notes identifying the strongest version of each position and the precise point at which they conflict; then write the dialogue.

I am a novelist revising a scene in which mourners gather at a hotel after a funeral. The deceased woman’s elderly brother approaches a newly installed glass door, which repeatedly slides shut just as he reaches it and tells him to step away even though he is standing still. The mourners initially suppress their laughter, but laugh openly when the door readily admits a catering trolley while continuing to obstruct him. The dead woman’s daughter laughs hardest and is immediately ashamed of herself.

The exchange should help me decide what, exactly, makes the scene comic. It should turn on the following passage from Henri Bergson’s *Laughter: An Essay on the Meaning of the Comic*:

“The first point to which attention should be called is that the comic does not exist outside the pale of what is strictly HUMAN. A landscape may be beautiful, charming and sublime, or insignificant and ugly; it will never be laughable. You may laugh at an animal, but only because you have detected in it some human attitude or expression. You may laugh at a hat, but what you are making fun of, in this case, is not the piece of felt or straw, but the shape that men have given it,—the human caprice whose mould it has assumed.”

Write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers:

- Dr. Vale, who defends an incongruity theory: laughter arises when an event violates the pattern or expectations through which we understand it.
- Dr. Sorel, who defends a superiority theory: laughter expresses a felt, if temporary, elevation over some person, thing, or human failing.

Neither should be a strawman. Vale and Sorel must respond to each other’s actual arguments rather than merely restate their theories. Each should interpret Bergson’s claim that the comic is “strictly HUMAN”: does this mean that every comic object must be human, that it must be humanly made or interpreted, or that laughter always has a human target? Each should also propose at least two modifications to my scene that would test the other’s view—for example, removing the elderly brother, replacing him with an animal, changing the machine’s origin, or making someone responsible for its behaviour.

At some point, they should consider whether relief theory or benign-violation theory better explains the daughter’s laughter and subsequent shame, but neither may escape the disagreement by saying that all the theories are partly right unless they identify precisely what each contributes and which mechanism is indispensable. Each character should concede at least one genuine difficulty for their own view.

End the dialogue with each philosopher recommending one concrete revision to the scene that would make it a stronger test of their theory. Do not resolve their disagreement artificially. The dialogue should be approximately 1,200–1,600 words, excluding the planning notes and the quoted passage.
