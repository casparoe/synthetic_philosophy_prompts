People keep mentioning Wittgenstein's family resemblance and prototype theory (the Rosch-style stuff from cognitive psych) in the same breath, as if they're basically the same idea wearing different hats. Please compare them properly, because I suspect they aren't the same claim at all. Family resemblance is usually read as an anti-essentialist thesis: for words like 'game' or 'language' there's no set of necessary and sufficient conditions, just overlapping strands of similarity. Prototype theory, by contrast, seems to be an empirical claim about how people actually categorize: graded typicality, central exemplars, fuzzy boundaries. Do these two even answer the same question? Here's what I want you to chew on: could prototype theory be true while family resemblance (as the anti-essentialist claim) is false -- say a category has a hidden strict definition that nobody consciously consults but everyone somehow tracks? And could family resemblance be right about meaning while the psychological story about typicality ratings is wrong? Use this test case in your answer: suppose linguists find a speech community where 'game' turns out to have a strict three-condition definition that speakers apply flawlessly under time pressure, yet the same speakers still produce typicality gradients (chess feels more 'game-y' than catch). Which theory does this vindicate, which does it embarass? Finally, take an actual position: does one of them give a better account of word meaning specifically, and does the other one explain anything about meaning at all, or only about cognition? No bibliography please, just sharp analysis.
