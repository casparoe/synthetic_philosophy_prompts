I'm putting together a taxonomy for a talk on why deepfakes are epistemically bad, and I want to make sure I'm not missing a mechanism. So far my list has: (1) straightforward deception -- synthetic video gets people to believe specific falsehoods (candidate X said the racist thing, here's the clip); (2) the liar's dividend -- once fakes are cheap to make, anyone caught on real tape can just claim its a deepfake, so genuine evidence loses its force even when it's authentic; (3) corrosion of the backstop function -- video used to be the thing that settled disputes, the 'pics or it didn't happen' standard, and once video stops playing that role we don't just lose a tool, we lose the tiebreaker that kept factual disagreements resolvable at all; (4) epistemic learned helplessness -- people conclude that verifying anything is hopeless and retreat to just trusting whatever their in-group says, so the harm is a change in habits rather than any particular false belief.

Is there a fifth mechanism I'm missing? It should be a genuinely distinct way that synthetic media does epistemic damage, not a special case or remix of these four (so 'deepfake audio of your CEO authorizing a wire transfer' doesn't count, that's just (1) in another medium). For whatever you propose, also say whether it's really a harm of the technology itself or more of a downstream social adaptation, and how it interacts with the ones already on my list -- does it amplify any of them, or is it independent? If you think my four actually collapse into fewer than four distinct mechanisms, I'm open to hearing that too.
