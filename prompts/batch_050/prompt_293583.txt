I teach a second-year undergraduate political philosophy seminar (24 students, one 75-minute session, no prior knowledge of American constitutional law assumed). We are in a unit on constitutionalism and democracy, and I want an in-class simulation that makes students feel the tension at the heart of judicial review before anyone names it. The session is built around this passage from Alexander Hamilton, Federalist No. 78:

"The complete independence of the courts of justice is peculiarly essential in a limited Constitution. By a limited Constitution, I understand one which contains certain specified exceptions to the legislative authority; such, for instance, as that it shall pass no bills of attainder, no ex-post-facto laws, and the like. Limitations of this kind can be preserved in practice no other way than through the medium of courts of justice, whose duty it must be to declare all acts contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution void. Without this, all the reservations of particular rights or privileges would amount to nothing."

Design the full activity, in about 900 words, structured as follows:

1. Setup and rules. Students should run a small fictional polity with a short written constitution containing two or three deliberately vague rights clauses, an elected legislature whose members face periodic re-election by the rest of the class, and a small court appointed for the duration of the exercise. Include the exact text of the constitutional clauses, the role assignments, the sequence of rounds, and a scoring or points scheme (no money) that gives legislators a real incentive to pass popular statutes and gives judges no electoral incentive at all. The game should generate at least two cases in which a broadly popular statute collides with one of the vague clauses, so that the court must decide whether the statute is contrary to the constitution -- and the class must watch what "manifest tenor" actually looks like when the tenor is not manifest.

2. Built-in pressure. Describe how the design makes students feel both horns of the problem from the inside: first, how quickly the parchment limits evaporate if no one is empowered (or willing) to enforce them against a majority; second, how the court's power to void legislation slides into something that looks like unelected legislation, especially once the losing side of a policy dispute learns to reframe it as a constitutional claim. The twist should emerge from play, not from me announcing it.

3. Debrief plan. Only after the game do I hand out the Hamilton passage. Give me a debrief script that maps specific moments of the session onto Hamilton's argument: the claim that a limited constitution needs an enforcement mechanism, the phrase "manifest tenor" and the interpretive latitude it smuggles in, and the objection (which Hamilton addresses elsewhere in the essay) that a power to declare legislative acts void seems to make the judiciary superior to the legislature. For each, tell me which moment of play to point back to and what question to ask the room.

4. Failure modes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- for instance, judges who rubber-stamp everything, legislators who stop legislating, or a class that treats the vague clauses as obviously meaning one thing -- and how to adjust the clauses, the incentives, or my facilitation to prevent or exploit each failure.

5. Honest limits. End with a candid note on which features of real judicial review the simulation cannot reproduce (the stakes of actual rights, precedent, appointment politics, the court's dependence on other branches to enforce its judgments) and what I must supply in discussion so the game doesn't leave students with a distorted picture.

The passage above does not count toward the 900 words.
