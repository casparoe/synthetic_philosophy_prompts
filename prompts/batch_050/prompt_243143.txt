I've landed on a position about probability in Everett and I want it stress-tested. My view: the Born rule problem is best dissolved rather than derived. Once you grant that an agent can be genuinely uncertain about which post-measurement branch they're on (the self-locating uncertainty move, roughly Sebens–Carroll style), the usual "probability is meaningless if every outcome happens" objection loses its bite, and Born weights fall out of a symmetry principle over equi-amplitude branches plus continuity arguments — no need for the full Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic machinery, which I reckon is overkill and smuggles in too much rationality theory. So: what would count against this? Give me a map of the view's exposure — which formal results, cases, or intuitions would actually sink it versus merely embarass it. Think about, e.g., branching structures with no natural amplitude symmetry, branches of exactly equal weight where the Born rule would force unequal credences, or agents who never learn the outcome. Also: which of these would a committed self-locating-uncertainty defender concede as real threats versus brush off, and is the dissolution at risk of unfalsifiability — does it predict anything its rivals don't, or just retro-fit the Born rule? Rank the threats from fatal to merely awkward.
