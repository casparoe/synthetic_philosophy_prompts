I'm trying to work out my position on whether immorality can be an artistic defect, and I'd like you to help me find the principle behind my case judgments. Here are my verdicts:

(1) Triumph of the Will is a great film, and its greatness is bound up with its propaganda power — the same staging that makes it artistically effective makes it morally repugnant. Verdict: great art, full stop.

(2) A competently made documentary with impeccable moral aims but flat cinematography and a plodding structure is mediocre art. Moral virtue contributes nothing to artistic value on its own.

(3) Wagner's vicious anti-Semitism, being a fact about the man rather than anything demanded of the listener, leaves the Ring's value untouched.

(4) A hypothetical novel whose suspense only works if the reader takes sadistic pleasure in a character's suffering — put the sadism aside and the book is inert — strikes me as artistically worse *because of* the response it requires, not just morally worse.

(5) A painter's beautiful landscapes are unaffected by the discovery that he committed terrible crimes in his private life, unconnected to the paintings.

Cases (1) and (4) seem to pull against each other: appreciating Riefenstahl's film arguably requires some uptake of a fascist aesthetic, just as the novel requires sadism, yet I want to convict only the novel. Is there a principle that captures all five verdicts — presumably by distinguishing *how* a work solicits an immoral response, or what the response is a response to? State it precisely, identify which clause of it each case motivates, and tell me honestly which verdict you'd overturn if forced to simplify. Finally, apply your principle to two cases I haven't given: a stand-up routine that is only funny if you share the comedian's contempt for a vulnerable group, and a love poem written by the poet to manipulate its addressee.
