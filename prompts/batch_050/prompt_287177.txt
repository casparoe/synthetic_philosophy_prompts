So I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend Dan about famines and we got nowhere, mostly because we couldn't agree on what would even settle it. His position is basically: a famine is a crime when the state could have stopped it and didn't. Mine is that that standard is way too crude -- it convicts every poor incompetent government in history and acquits some genuinely monstrous ones that just happened to be lucky. We kept circling back to the usual three cases: Bengal 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, and China during the Great Leap Forward, and we kept reaching for Sen's line about famines being entitlement failures rather than food shortages, but neither of us could turn that into an actual test.

So: write me the desiderata. What would a theory of famine responsibility -- a standard for deciding when mass starvation is a political crime (or at least a political wrong) versus a natural disaster the authorities merely failed to manage -- actually have to do to count as adequate? I want the full requirements list, with the case or consideration motivating each one. Things I suspect belong on it, but tell me if I'm wrong:

- it has to handle the fact that Bengal had food around while people starved, i.e. it can't just be about supply
- it has to say something non-hand-wavy about the intent problem: the Great Leap Forward involved no desire to starve anyone, arguably, but involved catastrophic policy plus suppression of the information that would have corrected it. Does the theory treat that as crime, negligence, or tragedy, and can it justify the answer?
- it has to draw a line between a government that fails to prevent starvation and one that causes it, without just collapsing into the old acts/omissions distinction, since laissez-faire Ireland is exactly the case where that line is most contested
- it has to not convict every pre-modern state that lacked the capacity to move grain at all (a standard that only makes sense for 20th century states would be a problem, or maybe it wouldn't -- argue it)
- it should say whether foreseeability matters, and whose knowledge counts, given that officials in these cases were often lied to by their own subordinates

For each requirement, give the case behind it and say whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp (legal-minded people vs. political economists vs. moral philosophers, roughly). Then the two things I really want: which single requirement knocks out the most currently popular positions, and whether any pair of desiderata on the list can't be jointly satisfied -- because Dan and I both suspect the list itself is inconsistent somewhere and that would be the most interesting result. Skip scoring Sen or anyone else against it, I'll do that part myself. Oh and fix the purpose first -- are we building a standard for assigning blame after the fact, or a standard a government could use to know its duty in real time? Those might need different lists and I hadn't thought of that until typing this.
