So I'm a science/ideas journalist working on a feature about the "Rashomon effect" in machine learning -- the fact that you can often train a bunch of totally different models (different architectures, different features they lean on, different internal logic) that all perform equally well on the same dataset, with no statistical test to pick between them. A researcher I interviewed said, kind of offhand, "oh yeah, this is just Duhem-Quine underdetermination, philosophers figured this out a century ago." Another researcher I talked to pushed back hard and said ML multiplicity is a completely different beast and conflating them is sloppy.

I want to write the centerpiece of the article as a dialogue, and I'd like you to draft it for me. Write a dialogue between two equally sharp characters -- say, a philosopher of science who thinks modern ML genuinely instantiates the classic underdetermination problem (and that the old debates, e.g. about whether underdetermination is merely transient or radical, map neatly onto the ML case) and an ML researcher who thinks the apparent parallel dissolves once you look at the details. 

A few things I need for journalistic purposes:

- Neither of them should be a strawman. The philosopher should know what the Rashomon effect actually is and shouldn't just recite Quine at the person; the ML person should actually know the difference between transient and radical underdetermination and shouldn't dismiss philosophy out of hand.
- They should really engage each other -- respond to the actual last point, concede when a point lands, press when it doesnt. I hate dialogues where two people just take turns giving speeches.
- At some point they should hash out whether the disagreement comes down to this: classic underdetermination is about *theories of the world* being equally supported by all possible evidence, whereas ML multiplicity might "just" be about predictive models where the underlying truth might still be recoverable -- or whether that distinction doesnt actually hold up, since many scientific theories are arguably predictive instruments too.
- I'd also like them to touch on whether Occam-y tiebreakers (simplicity priors, parsimony) do real work in either case, or whether that's cope in both cases.
- End it the way a good interview ends: not with a hug and agreement, but with each of them stating clearly what would change their mind. That gives me a natural kicker for the article.

Length-wise, maybe 700-1000 words. You can give them names so I have placeholders.
