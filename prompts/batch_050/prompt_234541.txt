For some years now, a small but serious literature has argued that wild animal suffering is among the largest moral problems on Earth: most wild animals die young, often painfully, and natural selection rewards gene transmission, not welfare, so the default state of nature plausibly contains vastly more suffering than flourishing. Conservationists and environmental ethicists have pushed back on several grounds at once: what matters morally is the integrity of species and ecological processes rather than the experiences of individuals; predation and starvation are not harms in the same morally relevant sense as human-caused harms; and any large-scale intervention would likely backfire because ecosystems are too complex to manage. Both camps are naturalists, neither romanticizes wilderness, and yet the debate seems stuck, with each side mostly re-deriving its own conclusion from premises the other never granted. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is not a straightforward values disagreement but a fight over which naturalized unit of moral accounting — individual welfare or ecological process — gets to claim that nature itself vindicates it, with the empirical and epistemic objections serving mainly as proxies for that deeper contest. Diagnose the disagreement: is it axiological, empirical, about who bears the burden of proof, or several distinct questions running under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result about when intervention is net-positive, a shared empirical test, a principled account of why the two currencies cannot be traded against each other, or something else?
