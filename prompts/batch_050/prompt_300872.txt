I'm preparing a seminar unit on bounded rationality for an undergraduate course in judgment and decision-making, and I want a worked case study that forces students to actually apply Herbert Simon's ideas rather than just recite the definitions. I've settled on the Challenger launch decision of January 1986, and I'd like your analysis to be disciplined about the mapping. To make sure we're working from the same facts: the night before the launch, NASA and Morton Thiokol held a teleconference about the unusually cold forecast for the next morning. Thiokol's engineers, most prominently Roger Boisjoly, recommended against launching, arguing that the data on O-ring performance at low temperatures was too thin to be confident the joint seals would hold. NASA managers pushed back on that recommendation, Thiokol's management caucused privately without the engineers present and reversed the company's position, and the launch went ahead the next morning in near-freezing weather. The right booster's field joint failed and the orbiter broke apart a little over a minute into the flight.

Please analyze the reversal of Thiokol's recommendation as an instance of satisficing under bounded rationality. Be precise about who the boundedly rational agent is here — Thiokol's management team, NASA's launch-decision hierarchy, or the organization as a whole, since the answer changes the analysis considerably. Specify what the operative aspiration level was (what would have counted as a good-enough reason to scrub, versus a good-enough reason to launch), describe the search process the decision-makers actually ran, and explain what the well-documented inversion of the burden of proof that night — the shift from "prove it is safe" to "prove it will fail" — does to a satisficing model, given that satisficing is normally defined by stopping search once an option clears the aspiration threshold. Then tell me where the model earns its keep and where it merely relabels what happened. In particular, distinguish the genuinely cognitive bounds (the sparse cold-temperature data, the ambiguous engineering evidence, the impossibility of running the relevant statistics on a handful of flights) from the motivational and structural pressures (launch schedule, contract stakes, career incentives, and the gradual acceptance of O-ring damage on prior flights that Diane Vaughan later called the normalization of deviance). Does satisficing have anything substantive to say about the second category, or does folding those pressures into bounded rationality drain the concept of its content? Note too that the engineers fit the model as well: they could not prove danger from the data they had, only assert risk.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode show about satisficing as a theory? You could read it as a vindication — agents under pressure settled for the first option that met their (shifted) aspiration level — or as an embarrassment, in that nearly any decision can be redescribed post hoc as satisficing once you get to choose the aspiration level to fit. Say which reading you find stronger and what, if anything, would falsify the satisficing account of a case like this. Throughout, please keep what is broadly settled among people who study this episode separate from what is genuinely contested: for example, the Rogers Commission's individual-decision framing and Vaughan's sociological reframing are not the same claim, and Edward Tufte's well-known argument that the engineers' own charts doomed their case is much more disputed among engineers and historians than popular retellings suggest. Don't present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
