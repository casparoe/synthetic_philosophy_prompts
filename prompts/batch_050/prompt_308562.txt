You are a professional philosopher of science with particular expertise in the metaphysics of classification. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the space of possible objections and test cases before organizing your response.

I've come to accept a broadly realist position about natural kinds, of the homeostatic property cluster variety. My version, stated precisely: (1) a category is a natural kind to the degree that its membership conditions are held together by some mind-independent causal mechanism that makes the clustering of properties stable enough to support induction and explanation; (2) this makes kind-hood come in degrees, so chemical elements sit near the top, biological species and disease categories somewhere in the middle, and categories like "weed" or "gemstone" near the bottom; (3) the interest-relativity of how we draw the boundaries between clusters is confined to the margins and doesn't undermine the reality of the clusters themselves; and (4) cases that look like trouble -- jade splitting into jadeite and nephrite, the demotion of Pluto, the heterogeneity of psychiatric diagnoses -- are actually successes of the view, since in each case better causal knowledge revised or dissolved a category, exactly as the view predicts should happen.

What I want is a map of this position's exposure, not a defense of it. What would count against it? Please address at least the following, but go beyond them: which possible empirical results in the special sciences (say, in taxonomy or psychiatry) would genuinely embarrass the view rather than merely complicate it; whether the view is vulnerable to the charge that successful sciences sometimes work with gerrymandered or disjunctive categories and sometimes ignore genuine causal clusters; what the view is committed to saying about cases where two equally successful sciences classify the same domain in cross-cutting ways, and whether either answer costs it something; and whether clause (4) is a legitimate prediction or a sign that the view has insulated itself against refutation by counting every revision as vindication. For each candidate defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of this view would themselves concede it as damaging or dismiss it as compatible, and why. Finally, rank the defeaters from potentially fatal to merely awkward, and say plainly if you think the position ends up so well insulated that its empirical content is in doubt -- and whether you consider that a problem for it or a virtue.
