I'm putting together a graduate seminar on naturalistic theories of mental content, and I need a purpose-built case to stress-test the main fault lines. Construct a thought experiment in which three broadly teleological theories of content assign three different contents to one and the same internal state of a single system:

1. An indication- or learning-based theory (in the spirit of Dretske), on which the state's content is fixed by what it has acquired the function of carrying information about.
2. A producer-oriented selectionist theory, on which content is fixed by what it was the biological function of the representation-producing mechanism to represent, where function is grounded in the selection history of the producers.
3. A consumer-oriented selectionist theory (in the spirit of Millikan), on which content is fixed by what the state's consumers needed to correspond to it in order to perform their own proper functions under historically normal conditions.

The three verdicts should come apart cleanly -- ideally three distinct determinate contents, though one of them coming out indeterminate while the other two diverge is acceptable if you argue it is a genuine output of the theory rather than vagueness in your setup.

Constraints on the scenario:
- Do not use frogs snapping at BBs, magnetotactic bacteria, or swamp creatures. Build the case from something else. You may, for instance, exploit a lineage with an unusual selection history -- exaptation, a shift in ecological niche, horizontal transfer of a mechanism, or a signal produced by one lineage and consumed by another (a symbiotic or inter-species arrangement is one promising route, since it lets producer and consumer selection histories genuinely diverge) -- but the construction is yours.
- The scenario must be physically and biologically possible; no stipulating that the state "means" something. The content facts have to fall out of the selectional, informational, and functional facts you describe.
- The scenario itself should take no more than a few paragraphs.

Then demonstrate that the case works. For each of the three theories, walk through which facts in your scenario it latches onto and why it delivers its particular verdict. Identify the single structural feature of the case that is doing the work of separating the three theories, and say what happens if you remove it: does the case collapse back into a familiar setup where all three agree? Finally, take whichever theory issues the verdict you find least intuitive, and present the strongest defense available to its proponent -- how would they redescribe the case so that their theory gives a more palatable answer, or so that the stipulated divergence never arises?

Close by stating how confident you are that the case as constructed actually meets each of the specifications, and single out the one point -- in the scenario itself or in your analysis of what a given theory says about it -- that you are least sure of.
