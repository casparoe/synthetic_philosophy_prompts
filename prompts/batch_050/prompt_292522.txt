I’ve just come back from the pub after a stupidly long arguement with my friend Dan about whether the collapse of the USSR was basically an Ibn Khaldun story. I don’t want you to take sides with either of us; I want the episode itself worked through.

Take the final years of the Soviet Union, roughly 1985 to 1991: economic stagnation, the war in Afghanistan, Chernobyl, falling oil revenue, Gorbachev’s glasnost and perestroika, the failed August coup, and the republics breaking away. Analyse the episode through Ibn Khaldun’s cyclical account of political rise and decline: asabiyyah, the move from founding solidarity to bureaucratic routine, complacency and luxury, and the weakening of legitimacy across later generations. Then compare that with a more linear modernisation reading, on which the USSR failed because a command economy and closed political system could not cope with an increasingly complex technological society.

Please be disciplined about the mapping rather than just name-dropping concepts. Who or what is supposed to possess asabiyyah here: the Communist Party, the security services, the army, the national movements, ordinary citizens, or nobody? What would “luxury” or complacency mean in a state with queues and shortages? Which developments are evidence of declining solidarity rather than merely bad policy? Where does Ibn Khaldun explain the timing of the collapse, and where does he explain only a general mood of exhaustion?

Also test Dan’s pet idea, which he calls succession load: regimes can survive mistakes while the founders’ myths and personal networks still carry them, but each transfer of power adds the burden of convincing a generation that did not experience the founding that obedience is still worthwhile. Is that a useful extension of Ibn Khaldun, a disguised linear theory, or just another word for declining legitimacy?

End with a verdict on which lens gets more of 1985–91 right, and identify the single fact most awkward for each. Please separate broadly settled historical facts from contested historical interpretations and from more speculative theorising; don’t present any particular reading as the consensus if specialists would genuinely dispute it.
