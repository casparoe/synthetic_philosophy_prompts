In debates over solar geoengineering, a widely deployed analogy casts stratospheric aerosol injection as a powerful painkiller — sometimes as an addictive one: it masks the symptom (warming) while the disease (accumulating atmospheric carbon) progresses untreated, it dulls the motivation to pursue the real cure (emissions cuts), it creates dependency, and quitting abruptly means a withdrawal more violent than the original condition. Op-ed writers use this to argue that even researching such technology is irresponsible; more moderate voices use it to relegate geoengineering to a last-resort palliative. Audit the analogy. Give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the patient, the drug, the pain, the underlying pathology, tolerance and escalating doses, dependency, and withdrawal — and then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular: pain is a signal that guides an organism's own repair, whereas global mean temperature is not; a patient's body heals on its own timescale, whereas the climate does not; and a patient consents to their own prescription, whereas here one set of parties would be dosing a planet whose other inhabitants never agreed. Which of these disanalogies matter for the conclusions the analogy is used to draw — the strong one (do not even research it) and the weak one (treat it as end-stage palliative care only) — and which are merely decorative? Does the analogy quietly smuggle in a single unified decision-maker where the real problem is precisely that none exists, and does that help or hurt the anti-geoengineering case once it is made explicit? Finally: if the painkiller framing cannot carry its intended conclusions, propose the closest analogy that could — whether that is blood-pressure medication taken indefinitely in place of lifestyle change, an experimental surgery, or something else — and say which conclusions the better analogy actually licenses.
