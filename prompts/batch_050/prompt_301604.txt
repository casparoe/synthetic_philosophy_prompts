A close friend and I have a running disagreement about history that neither of us is trained in -- everything we know comes from popular books and documentaries, and I suspect our argument is really about what the word collapse means rather than about the past. Her verdicts so far are consistent, or so she claims: the late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, with its burned palaces and vanished scribal culture around 1200 BC, was a genuine collapse; the Classic Maya abandonment of the southern lowland cities was a genuine collapse; Cahokia's rapid emptying was a genuine collapse. On the other side, she insists that Rapa Nui was not a collapse, since on the revisionist account the population catastrophe followed European contact and what came before was adaptation; that the Western Roman Empire's fifth century was a transformation, not a collapse, since people, institutions, and ways of life carried on in altered form; and that the Soviet Union in 1991 was not a collapse at all, since a state dissolved while its personnel, structures, and ordinary daily life mostly continued. Now I want to put a new case to her: the Indus Valley civilization, which after roughly 1900 BC lost its cities, its writing system, and its long-distance trade networks permanently, yet by most accounts without any sudden population crash -- people seem to have dispersed into smaller villages and carried much of their culture with them. Predict her verdict and say which precedent is the closest analog. More importantly: identify the rule she seems to be running, state at least one other rule that fits her track record equally well but delivers the opposite verdict on the Indus case, and name a further historical episode that would best distinguish between the two rules.
