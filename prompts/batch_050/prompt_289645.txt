I teach an undergrad seminar on freedom and social choice, about 14 students, one 75-minute session. I want an activity where they stumble into the collision between "your own business is your call" and "if literally everyone prefers it, we should do it" -- Sen's liberal paradox, though they must not hear that name or the word Pareto until the very end. Don't use the usual Lewd-and-Prude book setup; instead stage it as a shared house with three housemates, where each person has one decision that's plainly theirs alone (their own room decor, what they eat at the table, whether they burn incense in their own space) but everyones preferences are a little bit nosy, so the unanimous-preference rankings cut straight across the private domains. What I need from you: role cards with full preference orderings over the outcome combinations, each ordering perfectly sensible and sympathetic on its own; a voting procedure that mechanically implements both rules (decisive say over your own turf, plus adoption of anything the whole house prefers to the status quo); and a sequence of pairwise votes that walks the group into a cycle or into an outcome everyone dislikes before anyone spots the structure. Then the debrief: map each vote onto minimal liberalism, weak Pareto, and unrestricted domain, show them the cycle they produced with their own ballots, and walk through what it costs to give up each condition. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I suspect students will start negotiating and trading votes instead of just following their cards -- say how to handle that and anything else), and give an honest note on what a paper game can't capture: my guess is the gap between an inconsistent *social* ranking and an actual chooser who has to pick something, but tell me what you think the real shortfall is.
