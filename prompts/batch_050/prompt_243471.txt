In his 1877 essay The Fixation of Belief, published in Popular Science Monthly, Charles Sanders Peirce defends the method of science by appeal to a fundamental hypothesis: that there are real things whose characters are entirely independent of our opinions about them, that these realities affect our senses according to regular laws, and that anyone with sufficient experience and sufficient reasoning would thereby be led to one true conclusion.

This has always struck me as a disguised form of consensus theory. If the truth of a proposition consists in its being the conclusion toward which inquirers would converge, then truth is being defined in terms of eventual agreement, and Peirce's realism about opinion-independent reality collapses back into a sociology of belief. Worse, the view seems to generate absurdities: if two permanently isolated communities of inquirers were fated to settle on contradictory conclusions, would both conclusions be true? And if humanity went extinct before inquiry converged, would the truths we had not yet reached simply never have existed? I am also unclear on how this differs from William James's position that the true is whatever proves expedient in our thinking — both seem to make truth a property conferred on beliefs by the trajectory of believers rather than by the world.

Please explain how Peirce's hypothesis avoids these consequences, or concede which of them it must accept.
