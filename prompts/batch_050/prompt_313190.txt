You're a philosopher who works on normative ethics and moral uncertainty, and you're good at writing both sides of a dispute without putting your thumb on the scale. Before writing, jot down (just for yourself, dont include it) the two strongest moves each side has, then write the dialogue.

Here's the setup. Sam and Riley are on their company's charitable-giving commitee, and they have to decide together how to split this year's $60,000 between a global health charity and a corporate animal welfare campaign. Riley thinks the animal campaign is vastly more important per dollar; Sam thinks the human health charity is. A 50/50 split strikes both as fine but uninspired.

Sam is a believer in "moral trade": when people with different values cooperate, each should concede more on the issues they care less about in exchange for concessions on what they care most about, so both sides end up with more of what they value than a plain compromise gives them. Sam wants to propose exactly this kind of deal. Riley thinks the whole idea of trading moral commitments like goods at a market is confused, maybe even corrupting: a conviction youd swap away for the right price was never really a conviction, and agreeing to let something you believe is seriously wrong go ahead in exchange for goodies elsewhere isnt humility, its complicity.

Have them actually argue, not speechify. Riley should press at least two distinct objections (e.g. the "values aren't fungible currency" worry and the complicity worry) and Sam should answer them on their merits, not dodge. Sam should lean at some point on moral uncertainty -- neither of them is 100% sure they're right about which cause matters more, and Sam thinks that changes what rational cooperation looks like. Riley needs a real reply to that, not just a shrug. Each of them should concede or refine something at least once. Neither side wins; end on the sharpest point where they're still stuck.

Hard limits: the dialogue itself is at most 14 exchanges and 650 words. Then, after the dialogue, add a short coda (max 100 words, not part of the dialogue) that says which single objection each character left genuinely unanswered, and what consideration, if it were developed, would flip your assessment of who came out ahead -- i.e. what would change your mind about the verdict.
