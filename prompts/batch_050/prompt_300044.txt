I teach an environmental ethics unit to first-year undergrads, about 18 students, and I have one 80-minute session on Garrett Hardin's "The Tragedy of the Commons" (Science, 1968). Everyone fixates on the pasture parable, but I want them to wrestle with a less-quoted bit instead:

"A technical solution may be defined as one that requires a change only in the techniques of the natural sciences, demanding little or nothing in the way of change in human values or ideas of morality."

Design an in-class game where this claim does the work. My rough thought: they play several rounds of harvesting from a shared renewable pool, and between rounds the group can spend earned points on interventions from a menu you design -- where the "technical" options (more efficient gear, faster regeneration, better forecasting) only delay or even accelerate the crash, while the options that demand something of them (quotas, monitors, sanctions, giving up harvest rights) are the only ones that stabilize anything. I want them to feel the pull toward the technical fixes and the sting of realizing why those don't count as solutions on Hardin's definition, before anyone names it. Give me the full rules, the payoff structure (no real money), a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific rounds onto the passage. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g. students negotiating a side agreement that breaks the setup), and which part of Hardin's claim the game can't actually reproduce -- my guess is the "change in human values" bit, since game cooperation is still just self-interest, but tell me what you think.
