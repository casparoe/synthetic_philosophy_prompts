I teach a seminar on Marx and the philosophy of technology, and this term I want to press my students beyond the lazy equation of Luddism with technophobia. Here is the episode as I would frame it. Between roughly 1811 and 1816, organized bands of workers in Nottinghamshire, Yorkshire, and Lancashire — stockingers, croppers, and later hand-loom weavers — conducted nocturnal raids destroying knitting frames, gig mills, and shearing frames, operating under oath-bound secrecy and issuing threatening letters signed by a mythical captain, Ned Ludd. The state responded with military force, made machine-breaking a capital offense in 1812 (over Lord Byron's maiden speech in the Lords protesting the bill), and executed participants after mass trials at York. The textbook reading is that these workers irrationally attacked progress. I want something more careful.

Analyze this rising through the lens of Marx's treatment of machinery in Capital, especially the famous distinction, developed in the chapter on machinery and modern industry, between the machine itself and its capitalist employment — Marx's point being that it took workers time and bitter experience to learn that their enemy was not the material instrument of labor but the social mode in which capital wielded it, and that machinery under capital is the chief lever for extracting relative surplus value, deskilling labor, and swelling the reserve army. Do the mapping rigorously: on Marx's account, what exactly did the Luddites misidentify, and what in their situation does his framework capture well — the wage suppression via cheap frame-knit goods, the obliteration of craft skill, the state's response as an expression of class power? Then tell me where the mapping strains. Three pressure points interest me in particular: first, whether the machine/employment distinction survives the observation that these specific machines were designed from the start for capitalist use, so that smashing the frame was arguably smashing the social relation in its material form; second, whether Marx's attack on the compensation doctrine — the optimistic claim that displaced workers are reabsorbed — is vindicated or embarrassed by the actual decades-long immiseration of the hand-loom weavers, who had plenty of time to acquire the "correct" consciousness and were crushed anyway; and third, whether the episode is a case Marx's theory explains or one it merely redescribes, given that the Luddites were often quite strategic, targeting only the frames of employers who undercut wages while sparing others.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory — confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? And as a coda, test the framework against our present moment: when generative AI systems are trained on the accumulated text and images of the very writers and artists whose livelihoods they now threaten, is the machine/employment distinction still doing work, or has the instrument and its capitalist use fused in a way Marx's categories cannot cleanly separate? Where the theory is genuinely silent on any of these questions, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
