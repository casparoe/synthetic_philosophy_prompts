"Explain what Ian Hacking means by the 'looping effect of human kinds' and how it distinguishes human kinds from natural kinds. Does the looping effect give us reason to think psychiatric classifications like depression or PTSD cannot pick out anything real? Defend an answer."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

---

Hacking's looping effect is the idea that when we classify people, the classification changes the people classified, which then changes the classification, and so on. He calls this dynamic nominalism: kinds of people are made up as we classify them, rather than sitting there waiting to be discovered. His main example is multiple personality disorder, which ballooned from a handful of cases to thousands once the diagnosis became famous -- people (and their therapists) started producing the symptoms the category described.

Natural kinds don't do this. Classifying quarks doesn't change quarks; the classification and the classified are indifferent to each other. Human kinds, by contrast, are what Hacking calls interactive kinds.

So the looping effect is basically the self-fulfilling prophecy mechanism sociologists had already described as labeling theory: once someone is tagged as, say, schizophrenic, they internalize the label and act accordingly, which confirms the label. The person has to be aware of their classification for the loop to close.

Because of this, psychiatric kinds can't be real kinds. Every classification intervenes on the very thing it tries to describe, so the science of human kinds is chasing its own tail -- a moving target of its own making. Hacking's conclusion is that these categories are constructed, full stop, and constructed things aren't real, any more than money or national borders are real. This doesn't mean psychiatry is useless, just that we shouldn't pretend its categories carve nature at its joints.
