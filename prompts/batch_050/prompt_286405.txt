I'm a PhD student in philosophy of medicine and I'm stuck on a paper section about the placebo-controlled perinatal HIV trials in the mid-90s, hoping you can help me think it through.

Quick recap of the episode in case its fuzzy: in 1994 a big US trial (the 076 study) showed that a long, expensive course of AZT given to pregnant women cut mother-to-child HIV transmission by about two thirds, and it immediately became standard of care in wealthy countries. Then, in the following years, researchers ran trials of a much shorter, cheaper AZT course in Thailand, Côte d'Ivoire and elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa -- and these trials had placebo arms, meaning women were randomized to sugar pills even though everyone already knew the full regimen worked. The defense was roughly: the full regimen was never going to be affordable or deliverable in these settings, so the only policy-relevant question was whether a short cheap course beat nothing, and placebo controls answered that fastest and cleanest. There was a huge public fight about whether this was a Tuskegee-style scandal or perfectly good science responsive to local conditions.

What I want is an analysis of the episode through clinical equipoise -- the idea that a trial is only ethical when there's genuine uncertainty in the expert community about which arm is better. Concretely:

1. Does equipoise condemn these trials, permit them, or does it depend entirely on how you define the comparison? Work through both readings carefully.
2. Here's the part I keep going in circles on: imagine the exact same short-course trial, placebo arm and all, proposed in Boston in 1996. Obviously it's dead on arrival. So it seems like equipoise is being treated as having a *location parameter* -- as if "the standard of care" can differ by zip code. Is there a coherent version of equipoise that makes room for that, or does allowing it quietly transform equipoise from an epistemic principle (about what we know) into a political one (about what people can afford)? Give me the best steelman of the local-standard-of-care move, then tell me if it survives.
3. Where does equipoise just go silent on this episode? My sense is it has nothing to say about things like what researchers owe the host community afterwards, or whether it's exploitative to run a trial whose benefits will mostly flow elsewhere, and those turn out to be the morally load-bearing issues here. Tell me whether you agree, and what that implies: is the right conclusion that these trials were fine *by equipoise's lights* and that's exactly the problem with equipoise, or that equipoise was violated and the framework survives?

End with your verdict: does this episode call for patching equipoise (with something like a non-exploitation or fair-benefits condition), replacing it, or leaving it alone and accepting that trial ethics needs more than one principle?
