Between 1945 and 1963, the United States, the Soviet Union, and the United Kingdom conducted hundreds of nuclear tests in the atmosphere, distributing radioactive fallout across the entire planet. No one outside those governments consented to this exposure; most of the people affected were never even informed, and the decision was, by its nature, one that could not have been referred to those it touched. Analyze this episode through the ethics of imposing risk on others without their consent. Begin with the strongest version of the consent-based position -- that knowingly imposing a risk of harm on someone without their consent is a wrong in itself, over and above whatever harm actually eventuates -- and work out exactly what it convicts the testing states of: whether the minuteness of each individual's added risk matters to the verdict, whether the fact that the testers exposed their own populations alongside everyone else's mitigates or aggravates the wrong, and whether the impossibility of obtaining global consent excuses the imposition or is precisely what condemns it. Then run the analysis in the other direction. If unconsented risk imposition is inherently wrongful, then operating coal plants, building highways, and driving to work appear to be wrongful in the same way, and the testing episode threatens to make the theory prove too much. Use the case to determine where the theory must introduce a threshold, a standard of justifiability to those affected, or a distinction between kinds of imposable risk -- and say which repair best survives the episode, or whether the episode instead shows that consent was the wrong frame for this class of harms from the start.
