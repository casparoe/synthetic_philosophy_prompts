I teach a second-year undergraduate political theory course, 20 students, and I have one 75-minute session to spend on Arrow's impossibility theorem before we read it. Design an in-class activity that makes the students run into the impossibility themselves before they ever hear the theorem's name. I want the following, in this order:

1. A warm-up vote (10 minutes). The class must produce a single collective ranking of three options by pairwise majority vote, with the agenda arranged so that the pairwise votes cycle. I don't want to rely on luck: give me a concrete mechanism for seeding the cycle (e.g., preference cards dealt to subgroups whose contents I control) while keeping the options themselves something the students genuinely care about a little — a real choice for the class, not toy letters — so they don't suspect the setup. Tell me how to handle the case where the real rankings, once revealed, fail to cycle.

2. The main exercise (35 minutes). Split the class into four teams. Each team is handed a short list of desiderata for a collective decision rule — write these desiderata in plain undergraduate language, without the technical names, but they should correspond to unrestricted domain, unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship — and tasked with designing a voting rule that satisfies all of them, which they must then defend against challenges from the other teams, who get to construct preference profiles as counterexamples. Tell me how to structure the challenge phase (time limits, what counts as a successful counterexample, how I referee disputes) so that the teams keep failing for systematically different reasons, and so that at least one team gravitates toward a positional rule like Borda and gets defeated specifically by an irrelevant-alternatives counterexample rather than a cycle.

3. A debrief (20 minutes) that maps each team's failure onto one of the conditions of the theorem, names the theorem only now, and draws the distinction the activity is really about: the difference between "we haven't found a rule yet" and "no rule can exist." Include three or four debrief questions I can pose, and for each, the wrong answer I should expect and how to push past it.

4. Practicalities: exact materials, how I should record votes visibly, a timeline, and a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time — ties with an even headcount, students voting strategically once they sense a game, the IIA counterexample landing flat because students don't see why dropping a loser shouldn't change things — and how to handle each. Also tell me honestly which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce (I suspect the universality over all possible profiles and all possible rules is the weak point, but tell me if I'm wrong) and what I must supply in lecture to close that gap.

Where the design rests on a conjecture about classroom dynamics rather than on logic, say so and give your confidence level; and flag the single design element you are least sure will work as intended. End with a one-sentence summary of the activity's core mechanic as the last line.
