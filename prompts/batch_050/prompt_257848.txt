I am a novelist at work on a book whose plot turns on a promise made to someone who never learns it was made, and I have been trying to get the moral machinery right. An essay in a literary magazine recently summarized T. M. Scanlon's account of promissory obligation for me roughly as follows: "A promise binds because of the expectations it produces. Once the promisee has come to rely on your word, breaking it harms them, and it is this reliance-based harm that explains the duty to keep one's word. It follows that a promise which creates no reliance binds only weakly or not at all, and that Scanlon's theory reduces promissory obligation to a case-by-case calculation of welfare, differing from act-utilitarianism only in presentation." The essay adds that this is why philosophers find Scanlon's view unable to handle deathbed promises and promises to the dead.

I am inclined to think the conclusion I want—a secret, unrelied-upon promise that nonetheless binds—is available on Scanlon's view, but I cannot tell whether the summary permits it. Which parts of this characterization would Scanlon accept, which misrepresent the position, and does the essay's verdict about my plot device survive the correction?
