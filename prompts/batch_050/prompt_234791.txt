I am writing a survey of proposed impact measures for low-impact artificial agents: attempts to penalize unwanted side effects without giving the agent a full specification of everything we value. The proposals I have catalogued so far are the following:

1. Relative reachability. Penalize actions in proportion to how much they reduce the reachability of states relative to a baseline, where the baseline is either the starting state or the state that would have obtained had the agent done nothing.
2. Attainable utility preservation. Penalize shifts in the optimal value achievable under a sample of auxiliary reward functions, so that the agent avoids actions that change what any plausible objective could attain, in either direction.
3. Empowerment regularization. Penalize changes to the agent's own future control over the environment, on the thought that having an impact tracks shrinking or swelling the agent's option set rather than any particular state of the world.
4. Whitelisting. Permit effects only on a pre-specified set of variables; effects on anything outside the whitelist are penalized or forbidden outright.

As far as I can tell, these four exhaust the standard proposals. I would like one more proposal whose core mechanism is genuinely different from all of them. In particular, it must not be a new choice of baseline for a reachability-style measure, a new sampling scheme or weighting for the auxiliary rewards in an attainable-utility-style measure, a new statistic computed over the agent's option set, or a new way of drawing the whitelist boundary. Two hybrid combinations of the above also do not count.

Please describe the penalty precisely enough that I could work out, in a small gridworld with a vase, a human, and a time limit, which transitions it would penalize. Then do two things. First, state one concrete scenario where your measure behaves better than everything on my list, and one where it behaves worse. Second, say how it fares on the two classic difficulties for this literature: offsetting, where the agent takes a large disruptive action and then expends effort to undo or compensate for it in order to shrink its measured footprint, and the symmetry problem, where an agent that is penalized for all large effects is thereby also prevented from taking large helpful ones, such as stopping a disaster it did not cause.
