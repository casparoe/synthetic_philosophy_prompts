So this came up because I'm finishing a book manuscript (a local history thing, self-published, don't judge) and I wrote a preface where I thanked people and then wrote that despite everyone's help, errors surely remain. My brother read it and said that sentence is weird -- if I'm so sure errors remain, why don't I just find them and fix them before printing? I said that's not how it works, I've checked every claim as hard as I can, each one individually I'm confident in, but I've done this before and I know how it goes with a 300-page book. He wasn't convinced, and honestly the more we talked the less convinced I was myself, especially once he brought up the scratch tickets he buys at the gas station.

Here's where I've landed on the various cases, going by gut. Each claim in the book: I've fact-checked it, I believe it, and I think I'm rational to believe it. The claim that at least one claim in the book is wrong: I believe that too, flat out, not just as a probability -- I'd bet on it at bad odds. My brother's scratch ticket: he asked if I believe it will lose, and I found I don't want to say I believe it, even though the odds are way worse for him than my odds on any single sentence in the book. I'd say I'm confident it'll lose, but I won't say I believe it, and that feels right, not just like squeamishness. His ten tickets: I also won't say I believe all ten lose, even though I've never seen anyone win anything on those. My car keys, which I'm 99% sure are on the hook by the door: I straight-up believe they're there, and I'd be weirded out if someone said I merely have high confidence. The weather forecast saying 90% chance of rain: I don't believe it will rain, I just bring the umbrella. My friend saying the bridge ahead is closed based on one text from her cousin: I believe her, even though texts from cousins are maybe 85% reliable on road stuff.

So my question is: what principle is doing the work here, if any? The book case and the ticket case look structurally identical to me when I stare at them -- in both I have a bunch of highly probable claims and the question is what I get to believe. My gut delivers opposite answers, and I'd like to know if that's a defensible difference or just a prejudice. Also: is there a principled line between the keys case (believe it) and the rain case (don't), when the probabilities are similar? Feel free to tell me which of my verdicts has to go if the honest answer is that no single principle gets all of them -- I suspect the preface one or the keys one is the weak link but I can't tell which. And tell me what your principle says about the case I genuinely can't make up my mind on: the committee that reviewed 50 grant applications, checked each one carefully, and wants to state in its report that at least one of the applications it approved probably shouldn't have been.
