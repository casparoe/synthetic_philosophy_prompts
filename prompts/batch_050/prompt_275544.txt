Okay so I'm a grad student and in a few weeks I'm presenting a paper at our department's moral philosophy work-in-progress seminar, and I need to not get destroyed in the Q&A. The paper's claim, roughly: Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason is less deep than everyone takes it to be. The standoff between rational egoism and utilitarianism only *looks* like a clash between two equally valid principles of reason because Sidgwick slides between two different notions of what's "reasonable." Once you hold fixed the logic of his own axiom of prudence — equal concern for all stages of one's life, no normative weight to mere temporal location — the egoist can't give a principled reason for stopping at the boundary of the person rather than the time-slice without quietly leaning on a metaphysical thesis about the separateness of persons doing real normative work. So the dualism isn't a discovery about the structure of practical reason at all, it's hostage to a contested view in the metaphysics of personal identity. Take away the metaphysics and the egoist's position either collapses into the utilitarian one or shrinks to a claim about one's own good that's too weak to generate the famous conflict.

The room: maybe 12-15 people. Two faculty who actually work on Sidgwick and the 19th century utilitarians and have read everything, including the edition-to-edition changes. A couple of Kantians who think the whole project is rotten from the axioms up. One or two Parfit people. The rest are generalist ethicists who know the Methods mostly from grad school and will follow whatever looks like the smart objection. No decision theorists, no psychologists.

Prep me. I want the objections I'm actually going to get from this particular room, ordered roughly by how likely they are to come up, each phrased the way that person would phrase it — the Sidgwick scholars will push on what Sidgwick actually says and what he explicitly refused to concede, the Kantians will try to flip my argument into a reductio of the axiom of prudence rather than a dissolultion of the dualism, etc. For each one: how worried I should be, what I should concede immediately (so I don't look like I haven't read the book), what I should hold the line on, and a tight 2-3 sentence version of my best reply. Also point out where my argument leans on premises the audience is more likely to reject than I am — I tend to treat the broadly Parfitian picture of persons as the default and I'm aware it isn't in that room. And give me the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, so I at least have something half-decent ready if it comes.
