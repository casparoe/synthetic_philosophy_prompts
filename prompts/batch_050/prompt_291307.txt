I'm a novelist at work on a book about an elderly composer and the wealthy collector who becomes obsessed with his music. In the plot as I've drafted it, the collector purchases both the original handwritten score of the composer's sole symphony and the master tapes of its only studio recording, after which he announces that he now owns the symphony itself, full stop. He reasons that since he possesses the definitive physical sources of the work, any future performance by an orchestra would be an unauthorized reproduction of his property, and he petitions to have such performances enjoined. The composer's ghost (a somewhat pedantic spirit) haunts the collector precisely over this claim.

My difficulty is that I want the philosophical stakes of their quarrel to be sound rather than merely atmospheric. It seems intuitively right to me that the collector's reasoning holds: if a musical work is a thing that exists, then surely acquiring every physical embodiment of it, the score and the master recording, amounts to acquiring the work, and subsequent performances really would be copies made without the owner's leave. After all, if I bought every copy of a rare book and the author's manuscript, wouldn't I in some meaningful sense possess the novel itself?

Could you confirm that my collector's position is philosophically defensible, and help me articulate it more rigorously? Specifically, I'd like to understand in what sense owning the master recording and the autograph score constitutes ownership of the symphony as such, so that I can write the ghost's rebuttal only after I've given the collector the strongest possible case.
