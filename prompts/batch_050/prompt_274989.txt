I'm part of a small reading group that has spent the term on future contingents, beginning with Aristotle's sea-battle argument and working through the modern options. Our organizer, a philosopher of logic, has refused to tell us which formal semantics she actually endorses; instead, she issues verdicts on sample sentences and lets us reverse-engineer the view. Her track record so far: of "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" (we stipulate the matter is genuinely open), she says it is not true as things stand -- and when pressed on whether it is therefore false, she replies only that she did not say that. Of "Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not be," she says it is simply true, today, full stop. Of "The sun will rise tomorrow," she says true, and adds that what matters is that the future is settled on this point rather than that anyone knows it: she would say the same about a deterministic process no one is in a position to predict, while withholding "true" from a genuinely chancy outcome even if some perfectly reliable oracle foretold it. And of "It is inevitable that there will be a sea battle tomorrow," she says false.

At our next meeting she has promised verdicts on two further sentences, and I want to work out my own predictions beforehand. First: "There will be no sea battle tomorrow." Second, a retrospective case: yesterday an admiral asserted "There will be a sea battle tomorrow," and today the fleets are in fact engaged -- was his assertion true at the time he made it? Please tell me what she will say about each, identify the two or three candidate rules her earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with (for instance a supervaluationist treatment, a Peircean branching-time treatment on which "will" behaves like a necessity operator, an assessment-relativist treatment, or something else), show how at least two of those rules diverge on the new sentences, and give your best guess about which rule she is actually running with your reasons for preferring it. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so, and name one further sentence -- not among the ones listed -- whose verdict would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving candidates.

Open your reply with your two predictions, one line each, then give the analysis. Keep the whole response under 400 words.
