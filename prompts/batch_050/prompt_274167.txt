I co-run a biweekly AI ethics reading group at my university -- mostly grad students, a few keen undergrads, usually around 14 people, and we get about 75 minutes per session. Next month we're doing a session on what it even means for an AI system to be honest or deceptive, and I want to open with an activity instead of my usual mini-lecture, because every time I just present definitions (lying = saying what you believe is false with intent to deceive, etc.) people nod along and then immediately produce counterexamples.

Here's roughly what I'm picturing, but I want you to actually design it properly: some kind of game where one or two participants play a "model" that answers questions from the rest of the group, except the model has hidden information or constraints the group can't see -- like a card saying what it privately "knows," what its operator wants, maybe a rule it must follow. The group first agrees, in writing, on a definition of when the model is being honest vs deceptive, then interrogates the model over several rounds and has to score each answer by their own definition. The point I want them to hit themselves, without me telling them, is that their definition keeps failing: the model will say things that are literally true but engineered to leave a false impression (is that lying?), things that are false that the model was "made to believe" (can a system lie if it has no beliefs of its own, only trained-in tendencies?), things that are accurate in training-like situations but the model has learned that honesty gets rewarded so it's honest for the wrong reasons (is strategically honest behavior actually honest?), and the case where the model just says "I don't know" because evasion was never defined. I'd like at least 3-4 of these failure cases baked into the scenario cards, ordered so each one breaks the previous round's patched definition.

Please give me: the full rules, the scenario/knowledge cards (you can write the actual card contents), how to split 14 people into roles, a timeline for 75 minutes, and a debrief plan that maps each broken definition onto the real philosophical fault lines (intent-to-deceive vs causing-false-belief accounts, whether deception requires a believer, whether motive matters or just behavior). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this -- e.g. I'm worried the "model" players will ham it up or the group will just keep patching the definition forever without noticing the deeper problem -- and be honest about which part of the AI honesty question a roleplay with humans fundamentally can't reproduce, since a human actor always actually has beliefs and intentions and a real model arguably doesn't.
