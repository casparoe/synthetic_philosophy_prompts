I am a retired comparative physiologist who spent four decades studying nervous systems in mammals and birds, and since retiring I have joined a local philosophy society, where I have been making my way through Descartes — the Discourse on Method, the Meditations, and some of the correspondence. Our society runs an occasional evening in which a member role-plays a historical philosopher and submits to interview, and I have volunteered to conduct the Descartes session. What unsettles me most, and where I want to concentrate, is his treatment of animals in Part V of the Discourse: the claim that an animal is a kind of automaton, and his two proposed tests — that no beast arranges words or signs to express genuine thought, and that no machine could display the versatility of reason across novel situations rather than the fixed dispositions suited to particular tasks. My professional life makes this hard to swallow. I have watched corvids solve multi-step problems they could not have been trained for, and I know how thin the line looks between "mere mechanism" and "thought" once you actually study the physiology. But I also find Descartes's position more elusive than its caricature: in his letters he seems willing to grant animals something — passion, sensation, a corporeal analogue of feeling — while withholding thought proper, and I cannot pin down what work that concession is doing.

Please draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions I can put to our "Descartes," ordered so that each builds on the last. I do not want openers that invite him to restate the beast-machine doctrine at length; I want questions that press where the position is genuinely strained. The sequence should include, at minimum: a question on whether the two Discourse tests are criteria for thought or merely evidence of it, and what follows if an animal could in principle pass one; a question pinning him down on exactly what, if anything, an animal experiences when it screams under the scalpel — since he can hardly deny the bodily motions are there, the question is what it is like from the inside, if there is an inside at all; a question on why the same mechanistic physiology he celebrates in humans should count as evidence against mentality in animals rather than as its substrate; and a closing question on the moral consequences — whether, on his view, cruelty to an animal is wrong at all, and if so on what grounds, given that the wrong cannot lie in the animal's suffering. For each question, give the reply Descartes would most plausibly offer based on his texts and letters, and then a follow-up question that closes off the obvious escape route.

Above all, I would like the questions designed to force commitment rather than permit hedging. Descartes's defenders tend to retreat into ambiguity at precisely the interesting moments — animals have no thought but perhaps something like sensation, the automaton claim is methodological rather than metaphysical, and so on. Wherever a question admits both a hard and a soft reading of his position, please say which reading the question should compel him to adopt, and why you judge that the more damaging or more revealing answer. I would rather have a sequence with a definite point of view about where his position breaks than a neutral survey of the interpretive possibilities.
