I'm a second-year cognitive science student, and I'm writing this after a frustrating discussion section in my seminar on concepts and categorization. We spent the last two weeks on prototype theory and exemplar theory. As I understand it, prototype theory says a concept is a summary representation -- roughly the central tendency or feature-weighted average of the category members you've encountered -- and you categorize a new item by comparing it to that summary. Exemplar theory, by contrast, denies that any abstraction happens at all: a concept is just your stored memory traces of individual instances, and you categorize a new item by summing its similarity to all of them. So far, so clear.

Here's my trouble. In section, I argued that this isn't a real dispute at all. If categorization under exemplar theory is driven by summed similarity to stored instances, then -- at least to a first approximation -- it should just reduce to similarity to the average of those instances, which is exactly what the prototype is. The exemplar model keeps more raw data around, sure, but the behavioral output should come out the same either way: graded typicality effects, faster responses to typical members, and so on. Both theories predict all of that, and my textbook admits as much. So I claimed the two are empirically equivalent, differing only in implementation details, and that running experiments to "distinguish" them is a waste of effort -- cognitive science should just pick whichever formalism is more convenient for modeling and move on. My TA pushed back but didn't convince me, and honestly I think she was defending the distinction out of disciplinary loyalty rather than because there's a real difference at stake.

Could you explain what, if anything, actually separates these theories -- either in their commitments about mental representation or in the predictions they make? If my reduction argument goes through, I'd like to understand why the literature treats them as rivals rather than notational variants. And if my argument doesn't go through, please say so plainly rather than meeting me halfway: I'd rather be told directly that I've conflated two genuinely different proposals, and be shown exactly where the equivocation sneaks in, than receive a diplomatic answer that papers over the mistake.
