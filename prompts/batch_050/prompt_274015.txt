I'm TAing for an intro philosophy of mind course this semester and the prof has me grading the midterm essays. One of the questions was: "Both higher-order and first-order representationalist theories agree that mental states are representational, but they draw the line between conscious and unconscious states in different places. Explain how each view draws that line, state one consequence the higher-order view has for the consciousness of creatures like dogs or fish, and briefly assess whether that consequence counts for or against the view." I want a second opinion on one of the answers before I finalize grades, so here it is verbatim:

"Higher-order theories hold that what makes a mental state conscious is that the subject has a higher-order representation of being in that state. On the higher-order thought version defended by Rosenthal, a pain is conscious only if the subject has a thought to the effect that they are in that pain state, and this higher-order thought must itself be a conscious thought, since an unconscious thought could hardly confer consciousness on anything else. First-order representationalism, by contrast, holds that a state is conscious if it has the right kind of content and that content is poised to be available for reasoning, verbal report, and the control of action. No representation of the state itself is needed, only the state's representation of the world. This is why first-order theorists like Tye can say that simple creatures are conscious so long as they perceptually represent their environment. Higher-order theories instead imply that creatures lacking the conceptual sophistication to think about their own states have no conscious experiences at all, so on this view a dog or a fish presumably feels nothing, even when injured. Defenders of the higher-order approach, such as Rosenthal with his inner-sense model, bite this bullet and argue that our intuition that animals are conscious is just a prejudice, and anyway we attribute consciousness to them only because their behavior resembles ours. I think the animal consequence is a genuine cost of the view, but not a decisive one, since first-order theories have their own troubles, for instance explaining why some poised representations seem to remain unconscious."
