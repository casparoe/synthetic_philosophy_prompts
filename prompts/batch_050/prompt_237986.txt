I coordinate the volunteer mentors at a lay meditation center (think: 40 volunteers, each fielding maybe 2-3 "what should I do about X" conversations with students a week, everything from divorce to whether to quit a job to whether to report a coworker). None of them are philosophers, most have been meditating a long time, and the de facto framework everyone uses is the reflection instructions the Buddha gives his son in the Ambalatthika-Rahulovada Sutta (MN 61, Thanissaro translation):

"Whenever you want to do a bodily action, you should reflect on it: 'This bodily action I want to do — would it lead to self-affliction, to the affliction of others, or to both? Would it be an unskillful bodily action, with painful consequences, painful results?' If, on reflection, you know that it would lead to self-affliction, to the affliction of others, or to both; it would be an unskillful bodily action with painful consequences, painful results, then any bodily action of that sort is absolutely unfit for you to do."

The sutta then repeats this for during and after the act, which is great, it's basically a three-stage procedure already. But two things keep breaking it in practice. First, the same sutta has the Buddha saying you should train yourself "I will not tell a deliberate lie even in jest" -- no reflection, no consequences test, just no. So is the reflection test the master rule and the lying ban a special case, or the other way around? What does a mentor say when the reflection test clearly green-lights a lie (protecting a friend from an abusive ex who asks where she is)? Second, the test assumes you can know the consequences, and our people often can't.

Write me the one-page procedure I'd hand to mentors: the steps in order, what counts as a settled answer at each step, how to handle genuine uncertainty about outcomes, what to do when the consequence test and an absolute precept collide, and where the mentor must stop advising and hand it back to the person. Then run it on three real cases we've had: the abusive-ex lie above, a student deciding whether to quit a stable job that funds her sick mother's care to ordain, and a student who cheated on his taxes years ago and wants to know whether confession now (with real penalties) is required. At least one of these should come out somewhere I'm not going to like, and tell me plainly what the procedure can't settle.
