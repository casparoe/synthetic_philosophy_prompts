**The Self-Torturer, Inc.**

Warren Quinn's self-torturer puzzle, roughly as I remember it: a madman implants a device in you with a dial from 1 to 1000. You can't remove it, but you can turn it up. Each notch pays you $10,000 on the spot, and also slightly increases a painful shock you'll recieve regularly from then on. The catch is that the pain difference between any two adjacent settings is completely indiscriminable — you cannot feel the difference between 45 and 46. But the difference between 1 and 900 is the difference between nothing and agony.

The puzzle: at any given setting it seems rational to turn the dial one more notch. Ten grand for pain you literally cannot perceive. But keep acting on that reasoning and you end up at 950, in constant misery, holding a pile of money you'd give back in a heartbeat to be at setting 10 again. Quinn's own take, as I understand it, was that a rational agent just stops somewhere in the safe zone — and that this shows the rationality of a whole course of action doesn't reduce to the rationality of each step. Some people read the puzzle as showing preferences can rationally be intransitive; others say it's more about how rationality can fail to be preserved across sequences of individually rational choices.

Now my analogy. Think of the engagement team at a big video platform. Every week they A/B test a tweak: autoplay timing, thumbnail contrast, a slightly better ranking model. Each tweak increases average watch time by a tiny amount, and the effect on any individual user's life is genuinely indiscriminable — nobody feels the difference between 43 and 45 extra minutes a week. But the cumulative effect of hundreds of such tweaks over years is a big chunk of people's waking hours spent on content that those same users, in reflective surveys, say they regret watching.

I want to argue this is the self-torturer run by committee. The platform is the dial, the engagement team is the hand, and the marginal payout is real: revenue, promotions, bonuses. Each individual PM faces a choice problem that looks just like the self-torturer's — ship a tweak no user will notice, get rewarded for it. And just like the self-torturer, at no point along the way is there a single step where stopping seems required.

Conclusion: if Quinn is right that the rational agent should stop somewhere in the middle even without a principled stopping point, then maybe institutional rationality works the same way — the team ought to stop optimizing even though no individual tweak is objectionable. And conversely, the puzzle might explain why regulation by nudges keeps failing: any rule that only forbids "noticeable harms" structurally cannot touch any single step.
