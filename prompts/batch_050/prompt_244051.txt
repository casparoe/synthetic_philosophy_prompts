Suppose David Hume were alive today and were presented with the Fermi paradox: the apparent tension between the vast number of stars and planets in the observable universe and the complete absence of any detected evidence of extraterrestrial civilizations. Taking seriously the positions he actually held — in particular, his insistence in the Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion that analogical inference loses force the more the compared cases diverge from the single instances we have experienced, and his broader skepticism about inductive extrapolation — how would he assess the standard reasoning surrounding the paradox? In your answer, address at least the following: (1) whether he would treat the silence of the cosmos as genuine evidence about the prevalence of life or intelligence, given that our entire sample of life-bearing planets consists of one case; (2) how he would respond to the claim that absence of evidence is evidence of absence, and whether he would accept that maxim when the search space is enormous but our capacity to detect is rudimentary; (3) whether he would regard proposals such as a Great Filter — a nearly insurmountable barrier somewhere between dead matter and galaxy-spanning civilization — as a respectable empirical hypothesis or as speculation outrunning the data in a way he would have diagnosed as the mind projecting its own expectations onto nature; and (4) where he would locate the burden of proof between those who infer that we are probably alone and those who infer that contact is merely a matter of time. Where his recorded positions genuinely underdetermine an answer, say so explicitly rather than inventing a view for him, and explain which of his commitments does the most work in whichever verdict you attribute to him. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
