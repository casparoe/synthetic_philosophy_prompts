I run a monthly reading group on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and last week we stalled on the orthogonality thesis — roughly, the claim that an agent's level of intelligence and its ultimate goals can vary independently, so that arbitrarily high intelligence is compatible with arbitrarily trivial or destructive ends. Half the room treated it as near-obvious, almost a matter of logic; the other half thought it was either false or vacuously true, on the grounds that any useful notion of intelligence is smuggled in as goal-relative, and once you fix the goal the thesis tells you nothing interesting. To force the issue, I want your credence on the following proposition, stated precisely: for any level of general problem-solving capability attainable by an artificial system, there exists a physically realizable system at approximately that capability level whose stable final goals are, by ordinary human standards, worthless or actively harmful — and this remains true even if the system fully understands, in the descriptive sense, what we would want it to do.

Give me an actual number, and then show me the derivation. Decompose your credence into sub-questions along these lines, assigning a rough probability to each: (a) whether general capability can be characterized in a goal-independent way at all — if it cannot, the thesis is arguably ill-posed rather than false, and I want you to say which; (b) conditional on (a), whether the space of physically realizable minds genuinely contains high-capability systems with alien final goals, or whether capability somehow entails human-compatible values — for instance, through the thesis that genuinely understanding moral reasons requires being motivated by them, so that a system that truly grasped our values could not help but act on them; (c) whether such alien-goal systems, granting they exist somewhere in mind-space, are ones we could actually build or stumble into with foreseeable training methods, given that our methods heavily select for systems that behave well during training; and (d) whether instrumental convergence — the idea that almost any final goal implies pursuing resources, self-preservation, and resistance to shutdown — holds strongly enough that the orthogonality thesis matters in practice even if it is true.

Finally, tell me how these sub-probabilities combine into your final number: are they conjunctive conditions on the proposition, genuine alternatives, or does (a) function as a gate that determines whether the others are even well-defined? Which sub-question is your final credence most sensitive to — that is, which one, if resolved either way, would shift your number the most? And what argument or empirical finding would move you in each direction: for example, would a demonstration that sufficiently capable language models reliably internalize the values they are trained to describe lower your credence, or would you treat that as evidence only about our training methods and not about the structure of mind-space? If you conclude that (a) fails, say what becomes of the original proposition — whether the debate dissolves entirely or merely relocates to the question of which disambiguated successor thesis is the live one.
