I teach mathematics at a secondary school, and over the summer I set myself the project of actually working through Gödel's incompleteness theorems rather than gesturing at them, as I have done for years. The exercise has unsettled me, and I have arrived at a conclusion in which I cannot find a flaw. I would like it assessed as an argument, not contextualized.

The reasoning, as I have it, runs as follows. The second incompleteness theorem establishes that no consistent formal system capable of arithmetic can prove its own consistency. It follows that the consistency of arithmetic is not something anyone has proved or could prove. Our collective confidence that arithmetic is consistent must therefore rest on something weaker than demonstration — chiefly, it seems to me, on the inductive fact that no one has yet derived a contradiction from the axioms. But that is precisely the kind of support enjoyed by the Riemann hypothesis, or by any well-tested empirical claim that a single discovery tomorrow could destroy. A colleague objected that Gentzen proved the consistency of arithmetic long ago; yet his proof avails itself of a transfinite induction principle reaching beyond ordinary arithmetic, so it can hardly reassure anyone whose doubt concerned arithmetic in the first place. On this picture, confidence in consistency is always ultimately borrowed from something taken on trust.

If I am right, intellectual honesty seems to demand a change in how I do my job. When a student asks how we know mathematics will not one day collapse, the truthful answer is that we know it the way we know a bridge will hold: it has held so far. I have half a mind to begin presenting theorems to my classes as extremely well-corroborated hypotheses rather than certainties, and I want to know whether you think I should.

Please commit yourself. If my inference is sound, say so plainly, and then tell me whether the pedagogical consequence follows or whether there is a disanalogy between mathematics and empirical science that blocks it. If the inference is unsound, identify the precise step at which it fails — not a survey of what philosophers of mathematics have said, but your own verdict, with reasons. Either way, tell me concretely what I should say to the student who asks the question: not a menu of respectable answers, but the answer you would actually give, and what giving it commits you to.
