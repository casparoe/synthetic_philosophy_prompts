I am a novelist, and I would like your help with a problem in my current manuscript. Please take on the role of an expert in game theory and epistemic logic — someone who also has experience advising writers on the accurate use of technical material in fiction. Think carefully before answering; I would rather have a measured, well-organized assessment than a quick verdict. Please write in American English.

The situation is this. My novel is a literary thriller built, without my having intended it, around a centipede structure. A dying shipping magnate arranges his estate so that his eldest child may claim a modest sum at once, or pass; if she passes, the sum grows and the choice falls to the next sibling, and so on down the line — each heir able to take a growing pot or defer, letting it grow further for the next in line. The emotional arc of the book depends on several of them passing at moments when, on the surface, grabbing looks like the smart move.

A mathematician friend read the draft, told me the plot is broken, and summarized the relevant result as follows:

"Backward induction settles any finite game of perfect information, and your inheritance scheme is one. The last heir in line has nobody left to pass to, so of course they take; the second-to-last heir, knowing this, takes a step earlier; and the reasoning unravels all the way back to the opening move, so any rational player takes the pot at the first opportunity. That is why, in what game theorists call the centipede game, rational play ends on move one. Your characters only pass because none of them has thought it through; the instant one of them reasons correctly, your plot collapses. And the experiments showing that real people pass deep into these games just confirm that real people are irrational — so unless your novel is meant to be about fools, you need a different mechanism."

I suspect my friend is broadly right about the mathematics but wrong about what it does to the book — though I could not defend that suspicion under cross-examination, and some of his phrasing ('settles any finite game,' 'rational play ends on move one') strikes me as more sweeping than what I dimly remember of the subject. What I want from you is an accuracy audit I can act on. Please:

1. Begin with a brief, free-form brainstorming pass — a short paragraph of your raw reactions to the summary — before organizing your final assessment.
2. Then go through my friend's claims one by one, separating what a careful game theorist would accept, what is a common simplification that hides a substantive assumption, and what is actually wrong. In particular, tell me exactly which premises about the players' knowledge and rationality the backward-induction argument needs, and what happens to the argument when one of my characters observes a sibling pass — does that observation reinforce the conclusion or destabilize it?
3. Address the empirical point: what, if anything, do the laboratory results on centipede games show, and is 'real people are irrational' a fair gloss on them or a standard objection dressed up as a refutation?
4. Examine the assumption my friend never states: that the heirs care only about the size of their own payout. If my characters care about one another — as, being family, they plausibly do — does the backward-induction conclusion still go through, or has he smuggled in a claim about my characters' motivations and presented it as a theorem about the plot's logic?
5. Finally, give me your verdict on the only question I ultimately care about: does my plot collapse on grounds of internal rationality, as he claims, or is the unraveling something my more perceptive characters could themselves see and reason their way out of — and if the latter, what would that reasoning look like in a form I can dramatize without a single equation on the page?
