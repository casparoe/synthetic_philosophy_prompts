At a math department dinner last week I got into a low-key argument with a logician. Someone mentioned the four-colour theorem, and he said -- pretty confidently, like it was obvious -- that a proof no human being can actually check isn't really a proof, and then extended it to the Kepler conjecture stuff too, as if that settled the matter. I've heard this line a bunch of times, sometimes aimed at computer case-enumeration, sometimes at anything too long for one person to survey, and I realized I couldn't tell whether he was saying something deep or something trivially true dressed up as deep.

So: what are the distinct precise claims that slogan could actually be making? Lay them out as a menu, from the most modest (something like: we should be less confident in results whose derivations nobody has personally verified) to the most radical (something like: proof is essentially a surveyable, human-to-human object, so computer-assisted proofs literally don't count as mathematics, whatever their epistemic virtues). For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a live and defensible position, or just false, and which readings would actually justify my dinner companion's dismissive attitude versus which would leave the four-colour theorem standing. One wrinkle I'd like the answer to handle: the original four-colour proof and the later machine-*checked* formal verification of the Kepler conjecture seem to trip the slogan in very different ways -- one is unsurveyable because a computer did the work, the other arguably became *more* certain than most human proofs precisely because a machine checked every step. Does the slogan even apply to both cases, and if not, which cases does the strongest defensible reading of it actually cover?
