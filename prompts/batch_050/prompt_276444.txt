I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately been reading philosophy, and I keep circling one question: when a discovery was going to be made by somebody soon anyway, how much does the actual discoverer deserve? Ogburn and Thomas's 1922 paper "Are Inventions Inevitable?" catalogued well over a hundred independent duplicate inventions and argued, as I understand them, that once the requisite cultural materials have accumulated, the invention follows almost of necessity, and the individual is more occasion than cause. I want to test that thesis against my own verdicts on cases, which do not seem to sit on one principle:

(1) Newton and Leibniz: both deserve full credit; the shared name is right. (2) Darwin and Wallace: the theory is rightly called Darwin's, because his decades of evidential labor did the real work, even though Wallace hit the mechanism independently — co-announcement was just, but co-naming would be wrong. (3) Bell and Gray filing at the patent office on the same day: the simultaneity proves the telephone was inevitable, so the immense personal glory that fell to Bell is mostly an accident of a clerk's timestamp — and yet I still feel the patent itself was rightly his. (4) In my own field, a rival group once published a result first with thin data while we held back to confirm it properly; priority rightly went to them, though I confess it still rankles decades later. (5) A discovery nobody else was anywhere near — say a lone, serendipitous one with no parallel thread in the literature — strikes me as more creditable to its author than one five groups were racing toward. (6) Yet by my own case (2), I refuse to discount Darwin on inevitability grounds, though Wallace's existence makes natural selection as inevitable as the telephone.

Is there one principle about credit, priority, and inevitability that captures all six verdicts, or close? State it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and tell me which verdict you would overturn if forced to simplify. Also: what does your principle say about a case I keep flip-flopping on — the scientist who publishes first but understands the result less deeply than the one who publishes second?
