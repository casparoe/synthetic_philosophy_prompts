I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, third book, due to my editor in the fall -- and I've run into a problem that my writing group and I have been argueing about for months, and it turns out philosophers actually have a name for it: imaginative resistance. My novel is set in an invented theocracy where the narrator, a sincere and thoughtful man, treats certain things as obviously good that my real readers will find appalling -- let's say, the public shaming of disabled children as a form of moral instruction, presented by him as kindness. Here's the weird part: I can get readers to accept dragons, a plague that kills only adults, a city that floats. No one blinks. But the moment the narration states, straight-faced, that the shaming is good, half my beta readers report being thrown right out of the story, even though they know perfectly well it's fiction. Some of my writing group thinks that recoil is the book working as intended. My editor thinks it's the book failing, and I honestly can't tell who's right.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run, every time this comes up in revision -- and in this book it comes up constantly -- to decide whether a given reader's balking is the deep phenomenon (the morally deviant claim resisting being imagined as true-in-the-fiction, no matter how well I write it) or just a craft failure on my end (underprepared worldbuilding, a broken genre contract, a narrator whose voice hasn't earned the claim yet). Walk me through the tests in order. One I've been toying with is a swap test: replace the deviant moral claim with a deviant factual claim of comparable strangeness and see whether readers still balk -- tell me whether that test actually diagnoses anything or whether it confuses two different problems. Give me the decision points in plain language a novelist with no philosophy background can work through in an afternoon with a manuscript and a pot of coffee. Then show the procedure running on two or three cases -- you can use mine: the shaming-as-kindness narration, plus, say, a world where cruelty to animals is genuinely harmless because the animals can't suffer, and a subtler case where the deviant value is only slightly off from ours. Include at least one verdict I won't enjoy, like the procedure telling me to cut or rewrite a passage I love, and mark the spots where no procedure can decide for me and it's simply my call as the author.

Two last things. Where the philosophers who work on this stuff genuinely disagree -- like whether the resistance is about what can be imagined at all versus what readers are willing to imagine, or whether the moral/non-moral asymmetry is even the right way to carve it up -- say so plainly and don't present one camp's story as the settled consensus, because I'd rather know what's contested than get a confident simplification that falls apart the first time I repeat it at a dinner party. And please write the whole thing in American English, and skip the bibliography; I'll chase down names myself if I need to.
