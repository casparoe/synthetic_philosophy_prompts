I'm a software engineer by trade, with no formal training in philosophy or law, and I've been trying to work out what I actually think about strict liability — offences where you can be punished without anyone proving you were at fault. Below are some cases and my gut verdicts on them. What I'd like is a principle (or a small set of candidate principles) that captures the pattern, plus an honest account of where my verdicts can't all be saved. My tentative principle, which I suspect is wrong, is this: strict liability is legitimate only where the defendant voluntarily entered a licensed, risk-bearing activity and the penalty is non-custodial. Please test it against the cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me which verdicts it forces me to abandon.

1. A corner-shop owner sells a bottle of wine to a 17-year-old carrying a forged driving licence so sophisticated that police later concede it would have fooled a trained inspector. The owner checked the ID carefully and in good faith. My verdict: the modest fine he receives is fair. Selling alcohol is the kind of trade where you take on this risk.

2. A taxi driver is driving well within the speed limit when his brakes fail because of a manufacturing defect that no inspection could have detected. He seriously injures a pedestrian. My verdict: any criminal punishment here would be wrong, whatever the deterrent argument.

3. A man has consensual sex with a 15-year-old who told him she was 22, looked it, and whose own friends believed it. In his jurisdiction, an honest and reasonable mistake about age is no defence. My verdict: convicting him feels unjust — but I notice this sits awkwardly next to case 1, and I can't articulate why.

4. The managing director of a small chemical firm is personally fined after an employee dumps waste into a river, contrary to explicit written instructions, completed training, and a working reporting line. Nothing the director could reasonably have done would have prevented it. My verdict: the fine is fair — attaching consequences to the person at the top is what makes firms take compliance seriously. Again, I'm aware this strains against case 2.

5. A getaway driver is convicted of murder after his accomplice, inside the building, shoots a security guard. The driver had agreed to a robbery but had urged that no-one be harmed and was genuinely horrified. My verdict: he deserves real punishment, but calling it murder, with murder's sentence, seems wrong.

6. A man is caught by an average-speed camera driving at 90 mph while rushing his asthmatic child to hospital; the child was turning blue and the nearest hospital was forty minutes away. My verdict: the automatic fine and points should stand. If we let individuals judge their own emergencies, the rule dissolves — though I admit I'd want the option of an appeal heard by a human.

7. A traveller is arrested at the airport because someone slipped a packet of drugs into her luggage without her knowledge. My verdict: punishment would be straightforwardly outrageous; this one doesn't even feel close.

What I'm asking for: a precisely stated principle (or two or three candidates, if you think my verdicts genuinely fork) that explains which cases punish justly and which don't, with the clause of the principle that each case motivates. Tell me what your principle says about cases I haven't considered — in particular, a company whose automated trading system commits a market offence in milliseconds with no human decision anywhere in the loop, since that's close to my day job and I genuinely don't know what I think. Then be blunt about which of my seven verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should drop the verdict or the principle.

I'd rather you committed than hedged: pick one principle as your actual recommendation, defend it, and name the verdict I'd have to give up, rather than presenting me with a menu of frameworks and leaving the choice to me. If you think one of my verdicts is simply confused, say so and say why.
