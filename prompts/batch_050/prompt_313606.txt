I’ve been reading bits of Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics (Book VII, in W. D. Ross’s translation) trying to get my head around akrasia, and I got stuck on this bit, which isn’t the part everyone quotes:

“Further, if continence involves having strong and bad appetites, the temperate man will not be continent nor the continent man temperate; for a temperate man will have neither excessive nor bad appetites. But the continent man must; for if the appetites are good, the state of character that restrains us from following them is bad, so that not all continence will be good; while if they are weak and not bad, there is nothing admirable in resisting them, and if they are weak and bad, there is nothing great in resisting these either.”

So if I follow him, self-control only counts as impressive if your appetites are strong AND bad? That seems off to me. If I resist a second biscuit because I’ve decided to eat better, my desire for the biscuit is presumably a bit bad (by my own lights) but not terrible — is that not continence in his sense, just... nothing much?

I’m also wondering how this maps onto hyperbolic discounting, which I’ve been reading about — the idea that the biscuit’s pull massively spikes when it’s in front of me even though an hour ago I genuinely preferred the long-term plan. Does that mean my appetite is “strong and bad” only at some times, so I’m continent at 3pm and temperate at 10am? That seems like an odd way to carve up one person.

Can you explain what Aristotle actually means here, and whether the continent/temperate distinction survives once desires fluctuate over time like that?
