I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm starting a reported book on a puzzle in comparative political culture: the world's most trusting societies and its most famous culture of suspicion are both wealthy, stable democracies. Danes and Norwegians consistently tell survey researchers that they trust their governments and civil services, treat tax compliance as ordinary civic behavior, and extend to state institutions a presumption of competence. Americans, across the political spectrum and across decades, treat suspicion of the federal government as something close to a civic virtue — and each side of the Atlantic tends to regard the other's posture as the pathology. I want to know which attitude is more reasonable. But before I interview a single political scientist, I want to know what a theory would have to do to be capable of answering that.

Write the specification: the desiderata for a theory of warranted trust in government, adequate to the purpose of adjudicating between these two postures for a general readership. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are really the signature commitments of one camp (liberals, libertarians, communitarians, rational-choice theorists, or whomever). And if some subset of plausible desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so and show the conflict — that result would structure the book.

To start you off, here are candidate requirements I'm already turning over. Extend, refine, or demote any of them if it turns out to be a prejudice rather than a genuine constraint:

1. The theory cannot treat the local average as the standard of reasonableness — otherwise "both postures are reasonable, relative to context" follows trivially, and the interesting question (is either society miscalibrated relative to its government's actual trustworthiness?) never gets asked.

2. It must handle vindicated distrust — the Tuskegee study, COINTELPRO, Watergate, the intelligence failure before the Iraq war — alongside vindicated trust, without reducing the theory to a scoreboard of scandals, since any list of betrayals can be matched with a list of competent deliveries.

3. It must distinguish the objects of trust: the incumbent administration, the permanent bureaucracy, and the constitutional order are trusted and distrusted independently (Americans distrust the first while relying daily on the third), and a theory that answers the pollsters' undifferentiated question answers nothing.

4. It must face the endogeneity problem: trust is partly self-justifying, because compliance and talent flow into institutions that are trusted, while suspicion can starve institutions of the very competence that would warrant it. A purely backward-looking, track-record calibration seems to miss this; a purely forward-looking one seems to excuse credulity.

5. It must say which proxies citizens are entitled to rely on, since no citizen audits the state directly: press freedom, transparency, historical track record, and whether the state prosecutes its own officials seem legitimate grounds; whether one's own party holds power seems not to be. The theory has to draw that line rather than gesture at it.

6. It must handle within-population asymmetry: asking whether "Americans" are too suspicious lumps together groups whose distrust has very different evidentiary bases, and a theory that issues one verdict per country misses the most important variation.

Close with two things: which single requirement excludes the most existing positions, and whether my original comparative question survives the specification intact — or whether the spec itself shows that a reasonable attitude toward government is the wrong unit of analysis. Skip the tour of named theorists and any bibliography; I'll track down the literature myself once the standard is fixed.
