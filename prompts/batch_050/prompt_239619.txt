Last weekend I got into a long, unresolved argument with a friend about the sunk cost fallacy. She had just sat through the second half of a truly dreadful play because the tickets had cost her eighty euros, and I made the standard move: the money is gone either way, the only question is whether the next ninety minutes of her life are better spent in that seat or elsewhere, and she was simply throwing good time after bad. She pushed back harder than I expected. Her defense had three parts: first, that a person who reliably abandons things the moment they stop being fun never finishes anything difficult, so a stubborn "finish what you paid for" disposition may be worth more than any single optimal decision; second, that she couldn't actually tell yet whether the play would redeem itself, and walking out would foreclose information she could only get by staying; third, that finishing boring expensive things is partly how she knows what her own tastes really are. I still think she mostly wasted an evening, but I left the argument less sure that honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and I want to build a class session around exactly that tension.

I teach an undergraduate introduction to practical reasoning (about 24 students, one 75-minute session), and I'd like you to act as an experienced designer of philosophy classroom games who also knows the decision theory literature well. Before writing anything final, spend a few steps brainstorming candidate game mechanics, then settle on the strongest one. Design me a runnable in-class game that makes students feel the pull of sunk cost reasoning from the inside -- they should find themselves wanting to honor a sunk cost before anyone names the fallacy -- and that also creates a fair test of my friend's three defenses. Concretely: I want staged rounds with real small stakes (points, candy, extra-credit tokens -- no money), where the rules let students invest in something and then face the choice of continuing or abandoning it, and where across different rounds the conditions are varied so that sometimes abandoning is clearly right and sometimes the "irrational" choice is defensible along one of my friend's lines (the investment carries information you can only get by continuing; or continuing builds a track record that other players can observe and rely on; or the game rewards a consistent disposition rather than case-by-case optimization). Include exact rules, materials, the payoff scheme, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and instructions for when to reveal the structure to the students (they should not hear the phrase "sunk cost" until the debrief).

The debrief matters as much as the game: map specific moments you expect to occur (someone refusing to abandon a doomed investment, someone overcorrecting after learning the term and abandoning things they shouldn't) onto the decision-theoretic concepts -- the marginal-reasoning principle, the distinction between honoring sunk costs and investing in one's future self or reputation, and the difference between a rational policy and a rational act. Then tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g., students gaming the point system, or the stigma of "irrationality" making them refuse to defend the stubborn strategy even when the game's structure favors it), and which feature of real-life sunk cost dilemmas the game cannot reproduce -- I'm guessing the emotional weight of genuine waste is hard to simulate with candy -- so that I know what I have to supply in discussion myself.
