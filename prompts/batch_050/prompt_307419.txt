So here's my situation. I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized fintech and I somehow ended up chairing our bug bounty triage panel. We meet weekly, get maybe 20-30 reports a month, and pay out anything from £50 to £5,000 depending on severity. The job is basically: decide whether each report describes a genuine security vulnerability, and if so how bad it is. Sounds manageable except the category is hopelessly vague. A race condition that lets someone withdraw the same £20 twice? Obviously a vulnerability. What if it only works one time in ten thousand, under load we'd never realistically see? What about an endpoint that leaks whether an email address has an account with us? Where exactly does "intended behaviour" stop and "exploit" begin?

One of our interns did a philosophy degree and keeps going on about vagueness, and she lent me some stuff on the two big positions, which I'm annoyed to say is now all I think about. Roughly as I understand it: epistemicism says there IS a sharp cutoff somewhere (an exact request rate where "heavy usage" becomes a DoS, say), we just don't and maybe can't know where it is. Supervaluationism says there's no secret cutoff — a vague claim is true if it'd come out true on every reasonable way of making the term precise, false if it fails on all of them, and genuinely undetermined in the penumbra in between.

What I actually want is for you to turn this into a procedure my panel can follow. Concretely:

1. A step-by-step process for classifying a report: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure just stops and says "not a vulnerability, close it", and where it has to hand over to human judgement beacuse the question is one of values (how much is user trust worth?) rather than facts.

2. The procedure has to take a stand on the philosophy, or at least tell me which bits matter for practice. If we're epistemicists, I guess we treat every borderline report as having a real answer we're trying to find — does that change how we should investigate or argue in the meeting? If we're supervaluationists, what do we do about the weird result that "either it's a vulnerability or it isn't" comes out true while neither option on its own does? My panel pays real money, so "it's borderline, shrug" isn't an option — someone gets paid or they don't. And how do we handle reports where the panel can't even agree on whether the thing is borderline — borderline borderline cases, I think the intern called it higher-order vagueness?

3. Run the procedure on three made-up reports (a timing side-channel, a self-inflicted DoS, whatever you like), including at least one where it spits out an answer I'm not going to like — e.g. closing something the reporter will absolutely kick off about, or paying out for something that feels like intended behaviour.

4. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire and what it can't do. And if the whole vagueness debate turns out to make zero practical difference to triage, just say so and explain why — that would almost be a relief.

No citations or reading lists please, I can't look anything up. And please write the answer in British English — the American spellings in our vendor docs already drive me mad.
