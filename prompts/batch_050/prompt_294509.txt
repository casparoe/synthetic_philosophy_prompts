I've been reading about the origins of the First World War on my own — I'm not a historian — and I'd like to know whether the position I've arrived at is actually coherent. Roughly, I believe all of the following:

1. Germany bears the greatest share of the blame for the war: the unconditional support it gave Austria-Hungary in July 1914, and the invasion of Belgium, turned a Balkan crisis into a continental war. Something like the old Fischer thesis — that Berlin was reaching for continental dominance and accepted, even courted, the risk of a great war — still seems basically right to me.
2. Whole nations, not just individual statesmen, can be morally responsible for a war. Germany in 1914 was responsible in a way that outlasts Bethmann Hollweg or the Kaiser personally.
3. You can only be blamed for something if you could realistically have done otherwise.
4. Given the alliance blocs, the arms race, the rigid mobilization timetables, and the near-universal assumption in every chancellery that a great war was coming sooner or later, no European government in 1914 — Germany's included — could realistically have stepped back. The system made the war.
5. Serbia and Russia carry real blame too: Serbia for tolerating the networks behind the Sarajevo assassins, Russia for moving to general mobilization first.

Does this set hang together? I'd like an audit, not a lecture: name the tensions, ordered by how serious they are, and for each say which commitment would have to give and what I'd lose by giving it up. And if some of these only appear to conflict, tell me which ones, so I don't pay for repairs I don't need.
