In his 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Turing proposed that instead of trying to simulate an adult mind directly, we should build a "child machine" -- something closer to a child's brain than an adult's -- and then subject it to an education, complete with rewards and punishments administered by a teacher who cannot see the machine's inner workings but can only approve or disapprove of its behavior. He even speculated that this education would resemble the way a schoolmaster teaches, and he worried about how much of the result would be genuine learning versus mere fixed programming smuggled in by the designer. Earlier, in his 1948 report on intelligent machinery, he had drawn a related distinction between "unorganised" machines, akin to a child's cortex, and organized ones, and mused about educating the former through discipline and interference.

I'm writing an essay for a philosophy of cognitive science seminar, and I want to play this out in a direction my classmates haven't touched. Suppose Turing were transported to the present and shown how modern large language models are actually produced: an "unorganised" network trained on vast text, then shaped by reinforcement from human preference signals -- essentially rewards and punishments from overseers who, just as in his setup, have no access to the system's internal states and can only judge outputs. Further suppose he learns two things that seem to strain his original picture: first, that the resulting system is then copied millions of times, so the "education" happens once but the educated mind is mass-produced; second, that the same technology is now being deployed as a tutor for actual human children, with some educators proposing that AI tutors will replace parts of ordinary schooling. What would Turing make of all this? Would he see RLHF as a vindication of his child-machine pedagogy, or as a perversion of it -- given that he imagined education as something like an extended, individualized relationship rather than an industrial training run? Would he object to the mass copying on the grounds that it severs the link between an individual machine's experience and its character, which seemed central to his vision? And what would he advise about letting machines educated this way take over the education of human children -- would he find the prospect natural, circular, or alarming?

Please argue from Turing's actual stated commitments and intellectual temperament rather than from a generic "founder of AI" persona. Where his texts genuinely underdetermine an answer, say so explicitly and explain which way you think the extrapolation runs and why. I'd like the essay's backbone to be a claim about which features of modern practice he would recognize, which would surprise him, and which would force him to revise his 1950 picture.
