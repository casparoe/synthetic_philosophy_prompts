I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one thing that keeps nagging at me is how casually people slide between knowing something, knowing that they know it, and being in a position to know what their own mental state is. I want a careful treatment of this, not the usual potted summary.

Please adopt the role of a professional epistemologist with expertise in formal epistemology and the philosophy of mind. Think carefully before answering. Begin by brainstorming at least six candidate ideas, then select the strongest one and develop it. Write in British English throughout, in a measured, precise style of roughly 900–1,200 words. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or a reading list.

First, some terminology. Take luminosity to be the thesis that, for some class of conditions C, whenever a subject is in C, she is in a position to know that she is in C. Take the KK principle to be the thesis that if a subject knows that p, she knows that she knows that p. These are related but distinct: luminosity concerns access to one's own condition, whereas KK concerns iteration of knowledge. Please keep that distinction in view and do not let an argument against one quietly masquerade as an argument against the other.

Here is my current list of familiar pressure points against luminosity and KK:

1. Margin-for-error arguments of the Williamsonian sort, using sorites-style sequences to show that fine-grained states, such as how warm a room feels, cannot be luminous, since knowledge requires a buffer between adjacent cases.
2. Safety-based objections, according to which knowing that one is in C requires that one could not easily have been wrong about C, which fails near boundaries of C.
3. Higher-order uncertainty about one's own evidence or credences: an agent may know p on the basis of evidence without knowing exactly how strong that evidence is, and so may lack knowledge that she knows.
4. Ordinary introspective fallibility: people misidentify their own emotional states, pains, beliefs, and degrees of confidence with some regularity.
5. The iteration worry for KK: even if one level of self-ascription is unproblematic, requiring indefinitely many iterations of knowledge quickly becomes psychologically implausible.
6. Degraded-cognition cases, involving fatigue, intoxication, distraction, or the onset of illness, in which a subject's access to her own states is impaired in ways she cannot always detect.

I believe this list covers the standard repertoire. What I want is a new counterexample to luminosity that is not already on it. The counterexample must not be a rewording, a special case, or a combination of items 1–6. In particular, it must not rely on a sorites sequence, on a safety condition, on vagueness about phenomenal boundaries, on garden-variety introspective error, or on degradation of the subject's faculties.

To make this concrete, consider the following device, which I have invented for the occasion: a duplex mind has two co-conscious registers, a presentation register and an attestation register. The presentation register determines which condition C the subject is in; the attestation register is what puts the subject in a position to know that she is in C. The two registers are normally yoked, but the yoking is itself a contingent causal fact about the architecture, not a logical guarantee. Crucially, the duplex mind is stipulated to be fully alert, conceptually competent, non-degraded, and reasoning about a crisp, non-vague condition rather than a borderline one.

Your task is to construct, within or around this duplex-mind architecture, a counterexample to luminosity that works even where condition C is sharply defined and the subject's faculties are intact. The counterexample should locate the failure of luminosity in the structural relationship between the two registers rather than in any defect of either register taken on its own.

Please also address the following:

- State the counterexample precisely enough that it is clear which premise of luminosity it denies, and show that it does not collapse into any of items 1–6.
- Explain whether the same architecture generates a counterexample to KK as well, or whether KK fails for a different reason here; I suspect the two come apart, so please either confirm that suspicion with an argument or correct it.
- Consider the strongest reply available to a defender of luminosity — presumably that a properly designed attestation register would be constitutively rather than causally linked to the presentation register — and assess whether that reply saves luminosity or merely redefines C so that the counterexample no longer applies.
- Conclude with a verdict: does your counterexample, if successful, threaten luminosity for a philosophically interesting class of conditions, or only for an artificial architecture that tells us nothing about ordinary human self-knowledge? Defend the verdict rather than hedging.
