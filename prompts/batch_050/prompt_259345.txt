I spent my career as a condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been working my way through the history of philosophy and theology, mostly at the pace of a motivated amateur. Lately I have become absorbed in the classical Islamic debate between the Mu'tazilites and the Ash'arites over the status of moral truths: whether the good is something God commands because it is good, or whether it is good simply because God commands it. I immediately recognized the Euthyphro structure, of course, but I have the nagging feeling that the Islamic version of the dispute differs from the Greek one in ways I have not fully pinned down -- the Mu'tazilites seem to want reason to grasp moral truths independently of revelation, while the Ash'arites worry that this makes morality a standard standing above God, constraining His power and perhaps even implying He is obligated toward His creatures, which they take to be theologically intolerable.

To test my understanding, I would like you to write a dialogue between two equally competent characters: a Mu'tazilite scholar and an Ash'arite scholar, set perhaps in a Baghdad or Basra study circle in the tenth century, arguing about whether God's justice is a truth reason can discover or something constituted by God's will. I ask specifically that neither side be a strawman. The Mu'tazilite should not be a secular rationalist in a turban; he should have genuinely theological commitments -- for instance, he may insist that God's justice must be rationally apprehensible because otherwise God's promise and threat become meaningless, or that if good and evil are only whatever God happens to command, then praising God as just says nothing. The Ash'arite, in turn, should not merely thump the Quran; he should press the hard points: that an objective moral order independent of God appears to set up something co-eternal with Him, that obligation requires a superior to whom one is obligated (and God has none), and that the Mu'tazilite's position risks making God subject to a law, like a ruler under a constitution.

Most importantly, I want real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues. Each character should answer the other's actual arguments, concede points where the pressure is real, and exploit concessions already made. By the end, I would like the dialogue to reach -- though not necessarily resolve -- what I take to be the crux: whether the two positions actually differ in what God would do or command, or only in how they describe it, and whether the Ash'arite can avoid making morality arbitrary without smuggling in an independent standard. Roughly 600 to 900 words of dialogue would suit me well.
