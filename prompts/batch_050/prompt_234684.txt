I'm building a handout for an econ-philosophy reading group on Aumann's agreement theorem, and everyone in the group has a different instinct about whether it has any real-world teeth. Write me a decision tree that starts at "I accept the standard formal result -- common prior, common knowledge of posteriors, rational updaters must converge" and ends at a named position on when (if ever) the theorem actually constrains real human disagreement. Each branch node should be one specific assumption of the proof that a skeptic can target -- the common-prior assumption, whether exchanging "here's my conclusion" actually generates common knowledge of posteriors rather than of claims, whether agents are logically omniscient vs bounded (does computing the conditional take them all afternoon), whether people update on the fact of the other's disagreement as higher-order evidence, whether the framework even fits cases of moral or political disagreement where there's no shared space of hypotheses to put priors on. Phrase each node as a question someone could answer without knowing which leaf it sends them to. Leaves should include the strong 'aumannian' position (persistent disagreement between honest people is always evidence of irrationality somewhere), the prior-permissiveness escape, the bounded-computation escape, the communication-limits escape, and the view that the theorem is right but basically vacuous outside stylized settings. If two of those leaves can't actually be told apart by answers to your questions, say so instead of forcing it. Also note any combination of answers that lands on an unoccupied patch of logical space and give a one-line diagnosis of why nobody defends it.
