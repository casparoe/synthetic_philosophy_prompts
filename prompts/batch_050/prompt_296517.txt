Ok so my roommate (math major, deeply smug about it) hit me with this after I mentioned I was writing a paper on decision theory, and now he says expected value is dead and is dining out on it. I can't find where he cheats. Here is the game and his argument, spelled out the way he gave them to me.

The game: a fair coin gets flipped until it lands heads. If the first heads comes on flip n, you "win" (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars — so odd n means you get paid, even n means you pay. The probability of first heads on flip n is 1/2^n. He says this is called the Pasadena game, like everyone just knows that.

His argument:
(1) The expected value of a discrete prospect is the sum over outcomes of probability times payoff.
(2) Here that's the sum over n of (1/2^n) · (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n, which is just 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., and that converges (alternating series test) to ln 2, about 0.69. So the game is worth roughly 69 cents.
(3) Convergent series obey ordinary algebra: you can multiply one by a constant, add two of them term-by-term, and delete zero terms, all without changing the sum.
(4) Half the value is the series 1/2 − 1/4 + 1/6 − ... ; write it with zeros in the odd positions, add it term-by-term to the original series, then delete the zeros. Every one of those moves is licensed by (3), and you end up with 1 + 1/3 − 1/2 + 1/5 + 1/7 − 1/4 + ..., which therefore sums to (3/2) ln 2.
(5) But that last series contains exactly the same terms as the original — every +1/n for odd n and every −1/n for even n, each exactly once — just in a different order. Addition is commutative; rearranging a sum doesn't change what it adds up to. So it sums to ln 2.
(6) So (3/2) ln 2 = ln 2, so ln 2 = 0. Contradiction.
(7) Verdict: expected value theory assigns one and the same game two different values, so it's inconsistent, and everything built on it — utility theory, insurance pricing, the whole apparatus — goes down with it. (He also claims the same trick can make the game worth $7 or −$12 or whatever he wants, which he calls the cherry on top.)

I refuse to accept (7). Expected value obviously works for normal stuff; nobody's car insurance is built on a contradiction. But I've checked (3) and (4) twice and they look like textbook series manipulations, and (5) is just... commutativity? So which step do I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
