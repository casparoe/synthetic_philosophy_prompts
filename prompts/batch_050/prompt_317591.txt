I am drafting the opening chapter of a survey of theories of self-deception, and I want to fix the standard of adequacy before I evaluate any particular account. The purpose, to be explicit, is explanatory: a theory is adequate if it shows how self-deception is psychologically possible without contradiction, and if it sorts cases the way the phenomenon, rather than the theory's convenience, demands. Write the desiderata: the full specification of what such a theory must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Make sure the list covers at least the following ground, and add what I have missed:

First, the two classic puzzles: the static puzzle (how can one and the same person, at one and the same time, stand in the doxastic relation to a proposition and its negation that deception seems to require?) and the dynamic puzzle (how can a person intentionally bring it about that they believe what they know or suspect to be false, when the intention itself seems to require keeping the truth in view?). State for each what would count as dissolving it rather than merely relabeling it.

Second, the boundary work. The theory must distinguish self-deception from wishful thinking, from ordinary unmotivated error, from generic bias or motivated reasoning, from akrasia, and from hypocrisy -- and for each distinction there should be a test case on which rival theories classify differently, since a distinction that no case exercises is not doing work.

Third, coverage of both directions of the phenomenon: cases where a person ends up believing what they want to be true against the evidence, and cases where a person ends up believing what they fear is true against the evidence. A theory that comfortably handles the first but not the second has a gap, and the desideratum should say whether covering both is required or whether one direction may be reclassified as something else.

Fourth, a constraint from below: the theory may not posit psychological machinery that cognitive science gives us no reason to believe in -- no fully informed inner deceiver who keeps the truth in a sealed compartment -- and it must be statable at a level of mechanism, not just redescription.

Fifth, the normative residue: the theory should say something about responsibility, since we blame people for some self-deception and excuse other cases, and an account on which the phenomenon is constitutively ignorant of itself has work to do here.

Sixth, the functional question: whether a theory may, must, or must not build in the claim that self-deception is sometimes adaptive -- and what follows for the theory if the adaptive story is false.

Then do the three things that make such a list worth having. Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable for any account or as the prejudice of a particular camp, with a sentence on which camp. Flag any subset of the desiderata you believe is jointly unsatisfiable -- the obvious candidate is the combination of an intentionality requirement, a contradictory-belief requirement, and a ban on knowingly irrational belief -- and say whether that amounts to a proof that self-deception, as traditionally conceived, cannot exist, or merely to a constraint on how it must be redescribed. And identify the single desideratum that excludes the most contenders.

Close briefly: one short paragraph each on how the three familiar families of accounts -- intentionalist or partitioning views, deflationary motivational views, and the bad-faith tradition descending from Sartre -- fare against the list. The scoring is not the deliverable; the specification is.
