The question was: "Explain Aristotle's 'sea battle' puzzle about future contingents. Does the fact that 'either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't' is true commit us to fatalism? Explain Aristotle's way out of the argument, and raise one objection to it."

The student's answer:

"The sea battle puzzle comes from Aristotle's De Interpretatione ch. 9. Suppose someone says today 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow.' If what they say is true now, then it seems the battle can't fail to happen -- you can't change the truth of a truth. Same goes if the statement is false now: then the battle can't happen. Since the statement has to be one or the other, the outcome is fixed either way, so deliberating about it is pointless. That's the fatalist argument.

Aristotle's way out is to deny that the law of excluded middle applies to future contingents. He holds that 'either there will be a battle tomorrow or there won't' is true and even necessary, but argues that the individual statement 'there will be a battle tomorrow' can't be assigned true or false ahead of time. Future contingents are indeterminate until the event itself settles them, unlike past-tense statements, which are already fixed.

So on this reading, the fatalist's mistake is assuming determinism: the argument only gets going if you think the truth of the prediction causes the future event, and once you see that truth isn't a cause, the necessity disappears. One objection: if 'there will be a battle' is neither true nor false today, then when tomorrow comes and the battle happens, it seems the statement was true all along -- truth looks retroactive, which is strange. Also, denying that a whole class of statements has truth values is a steep price to pay just to save deliberation."
