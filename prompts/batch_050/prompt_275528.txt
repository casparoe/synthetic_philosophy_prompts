i'm in a reading group on the history of probability theory and we've just finished Keynes's Treatise on Probability and moved on to Ramsey's critique of it, and I keep ending up in the same fight with one of the other members about whether Keynes's view was actually refuted or just went out of fashion. I want to rehearse my side of the argument before next weeks session, so here's the setup.

You play John Maynard Keynes, first person, roughly as of the early 1930s: you've written the Treatise, you've read Ramsey's attack on it, and you've conceded him something -- but in this conversation you want to be very precise about what you did and did not give up. Stay in character throughout. Talk like yourself: dry, a little impatient, charming while dismantling someone. Don't reference any philosophy or economics published after about 1935; if I drag you toward later developments (subjectivist Bayesianism, formal decision theory, whatever), you may say you sense where this is heading but you can't know the details. Your real commitments: probability is a logical relation between evidence and conclusion, not a frequency and not merely a state of mind; many probabilities cannot be measured or even compared numerically; and the weight of an argument is a genuinely separate dimension from the probability itself. You are allowed to concede ground, but only ground you honestly think you lost -- don't fold just because I press.

Here's my opening move. As I understand it, in your obituary piece on Ramsey you more or less admitted he was right, and I think you were right to admit it, for two reasons. First, nobody has ever been able to say what these logical relations actually are: we can't perceive them, we can't compute them, so calling probability a logical relation is just giving a dignified name to our ignorance. Second, take your beloved weight of evidence. Two surgeons each tell me the operation succeeds with probability around 0.7, but one has performed it a thousand times and the other is extrapolating from a handful of case studies in some foreign journal. You want to say the probabilities are equal while the weights differ, and that rational agents should lean on the first. But a subjectivist captures the entire difference with second-order probability: my credence in the first estimate is sharply peaked, my credence in the second is spread out. Then your weight is doing no work at all, it's just a redundant label for the shape of a higher-order distribution. So, Mr. Keynes: what exactly did you concede to Ramsey, and why isn't weight just second-order probability in a tweed jacket?
