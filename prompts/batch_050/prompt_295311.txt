Several people in my professional circle (quantitative traders and effective altruists) have adopted a stance they call "geometric rationality": the thesis that a rational agent should maximize not the expected value of her resources but the expected logarithm of them — equivalently, the long-run geometric growth rate of her capital, career capital, or capacity to do good. They cite the result established in J. L. Kelly Jr.'s 1956 paper — that the log-maximizing bettor achieves the greatest attainable exponential growth rate and so outruns any essentially different fixed strategy in the long run — and they lean on the analogy to draw two practical conclusions: (1) expected-value maximization is simply a mistake wherever outcomes compound over time, and (2) any plan carrying a nonzero probability of ruin is irrational however large its expected payoff, so far more risk aversion is warranted than ordinary diminishing marginal utility would justify.

Their source is Kelly's "A New Interpretation of Information Rate" (Bell System Technical Journal, July 1956). In the introduction Kelly writes:

"The cost function approach is, of course, not limited to studies of communication systems, but can actually be used to analyze nearly any branch of human endeavor. The author believes that it is too general to shed any light on the specific problems of communication theory. [...] What can be done, however, is to take some real-life situation which seems to possess the essential features of a communication problem, and to analyze it without the introduction of an arbitrary cost function."

Kelly thus introduces his criterion inside a deliberately narrow setup — a gambler facing repeated bets at posted odds, reinvesting a divisible bankroll — and expressly as an alternative to the general utility framework he sets aside.
