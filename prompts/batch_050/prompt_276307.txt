In my philosophy of mind reading group we have spent a semester on the causal exclusion argument and made no progress at all. Everyone accepts the four standard commitments — mental causes are distinct from their physical realizers, every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, effects are not systematically overdetermined, and mental states sometimes cause behavior — and everyone agrees these cannot all be true, but nobody will concede which one should go. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about the mind at all but about causation: the friends of exclusion are implicitly operating with a production- or transmission-style notion of cause, while those determined to save mental causation are operating with a difference-making or interventionist notion, and the argument only has force if one equivocates between the two. Please assess that hypothesis against a better one if you have it: is this a verbal disagreement about the word 'cause', a disguised disagreement about what would even count as genuine overdetermination, or is there a substantive metaphysical question underneath that the two notions of causation merely mask? Support your diagnosis from what each side actually says, and say what, if anything, would count as progress — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, an empirical discovery — or argue that none should be expected. As a test of your diagnosis, suppose neuroscience established that a small class of neural events genuinely lack sufficient physical causes: would that settle the argument, or would the two sides simply re-entrench with revised premises? Please give your reply as continuous prose, like a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
