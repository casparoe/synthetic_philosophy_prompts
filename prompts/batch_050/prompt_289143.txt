I am a literary novelist, and I want to interrogate a convention of my own craft. In much realist and modernist fiction, a narrator renders a character's consciousness as continuous, articulate, and largely self-present: free indirect discourse gives us the character's thoughts as formed sentences, with the character apparently living inside them. Yet the same tradition depends on a contrary move -- dramatic irony of self-knowledge -- in which the narrator, and the reader, can see a character's motives, fears, and self-deceptions more clearly than the character can. We are shown the interior as fully lit in one respect and partly occluded in another.

My question is what view of consciousness this practice presupposes, if any. Does the convention of rendering inner life as articulate monologue presuppose that a person's occurrent thoughts are conscious in themselves, available in roughly the form the narrator transcribes -- something like a first-order picture? Does the convention of self-opacity, by contrast, sit more naturally with a higher-order picture, on which a mental state is conscious only insofar as the subject represents it, so that one can be in a state without knowing it? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: perhaps the rendered monologue could itself be understood as the higher-order representation rather than the first-order thought, in which case both conventions might be licensed by a single theory. And consider the obverse: a novelist who took a strict higher-order view seriously might have to abandon the seamless inner monologue altogether, rendering only those fragments of mentation the character could plausibly be representing to herself, with the rest conveyed obliquely through behavior and error. Would that be a more faithful realism, or would it confuse the metaphysics of consciousness with the phenomenology of reading? Finally, say where you think the practice is simply underdetermined -- where no theory of consciousness is doing any real work and the conventions are merely conventions.
