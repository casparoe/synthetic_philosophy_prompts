I am taking a philosophy of mind seminar on intentionality, and I have run into an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose steps I cannot seem to break. It begins with Dennett's two-bitser: a vending machine built to sort US quarters will, if relocated to Panama, happily sort quarter-balboas instead, and there appears to be no fact of the matter about what it was "really" set up to detect apart from the history that selected it. The argument runs as follows.

(1) The intentionality of any artifact is derived: it comes from the intentions of a designer, the practices of users, or a history of selection.
(2) As the two-bitser shows, nothing intrinsic to an artifact settles what it is about; whatever content it has is fixed entirely from outside it.
(3) Human brains are artifacts in the relevant sense: physical systems whose content-relevant structure was produced by natural selection rather than by any intending mind.
(4) So whatever content our brain states have is derived from natural selection, just as the two-bitser's content is derived from its environment.
(5) Natural selection is a blind, foresightless process with no intentionality of its own, and nothing can confer what it does not itself possess.
(6) The derivation chain therefore bottoms out in something mindless, and so nothing anywhere has original intentionality.
(7) If nothing has original intentionality, then no one has ever really meant anything by anything; claims about what our thoughts concern are at best relative to an interpretive stance.

The conclusion follows, but it is absurd. I would like to hold on to the ordinary conviction that I genuinely mean something by my words, and I also find (2) compelling in light of the two-bitser case. Which step should I reject, and why did it look plausible in the first place? In your answer, please price the main alternatives: what a teleosemantic account of content would have to deliver to block the argument around (4) and (5), what a stance-relativist about content concedes by accepting (7), and what a defender of Searle would have to claim about the brain's intrinsic intentionality to deny (3).
