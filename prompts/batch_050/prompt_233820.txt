I have no formal training in philosophy or mathematics, but I keep running into the same puzzle and I would like the specification before I read anyone's solution. The puzzle: before someone proves a mathematical conjecture, it seems reasonable to be confident in it without being certain -- say, 80 percent that a long-conjectured claim about the primes is true, based on it surviving massive computational checks. But the claim was already true or already false all along. There was never an 80-percent world and a 20-percent world. So what, exactly, was my 80 percent about, and what would a theory of rational confidence in mathematical and logical claims have to look like for that number to be anything other than a mistake?

Write me the desiderata: the full list of what an adequate theory of logical uncertainty must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. I want the list, not a history of the field. To anchor it, here are the demands I have come up with on my own, which you should assess, correct, and add to:

1. It must explain why a proof changes anything. After the proof, confidence should go to (near) certainty, and the theory should make sense of that transition without implying that my earlier 80 percent was irrational. If the theory says the proof is "evidence," it has to say what kind of evidence a deduction can be, since the conclusion was already implied by things I believed.

2. It must not require impossible computation. Any theory that says a rational agent should already assign probability 1 to every truth of arithmetic, or 0 to every contradiction, seems to describe a god rather than a reasoner, and to leave no room for the thing I am trying to understand. But I am not sure this demand is legitimate -- maybe ideal rationality is allowed to be inhuman, and the human version is a separate question. Tell me whether this is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of mine.

3. It must handle being bet against. If my probabilities on mathematical claims are inconsistent with each other, someone who can compute faster than me can apparently extract money from me by offering me bets whose outcomes are already settled. A theory should say whether rational degrees of belief must at least be invulnerable to exploitation by any computable adversary -- and whether that requirement is even satisfiable in principle, or whether it secretly smuggles omniscience back in.

4. It must say something about the limits of proof itself. Some statements can neither be proven nor disproven from the usual axioms. Must the theory take a stand on what confidence in those even means, or is it allowed to restrict itself to claims that are decidable in principle? I genuinely cannot tell whether this is a core requirement or an exotic edge case.

For each requirement on the final list: state it, give the motivating case, and mark it as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a camp-specific preference. The part I most want to see: identify any pair or subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied -- for instance, I suspect that "never be exploitable by a computable bettor" and "never require superhuman computation" might collide, and if so, that collision is itself a result, and the theory's real choice is which demand to relax. Also tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidate approaches.

Then, briefly -- no more than a paragraph each -- score three rough styles of answer against the list: treating mathematical claims like ordinary empirical claims where proofs just count as one more observation; treating rational confidence as applying only relative to bounded computational resources; and treating the whole thing through betting coherence, where good probabilities are just ones that cannot be exploited. I care much more about the requirements and their tensions than about the scoring.
