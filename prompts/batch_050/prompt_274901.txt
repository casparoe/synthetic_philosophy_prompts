Last night, over far too much wine, a friend and I got into an argument about the brain-in-a-vat scenario that neither of us could put to rest. Her position was that the standard skeptical set-up contains an unexamined assumption: everyone treats the discovery that one is a brain in a vat as a catastrophe, and philosophers then scramble for arguments that we aren't in that position, but almost nobody stops to ask whether the vat-brain has actually lost anything worth having. If the experience is seamless and lifelong, she insisted, then the love, the accomplishments, the griefs are all real to the only person whose testimony counts, and the "real world" outside the vat is a metaphysical garnish that does no work. I pushed back hard and thought I had her, but by the end of the night I wasn't so sure. I want to rehearse the argument again with a committed opponent before I concede anything.

So here is the setup. Play a vat-brain who knows what it is: fifty years ago, in the vat-keepers' calendar, the simulation you inhabit was switched on, and last year they decided, as an experiment in the ethics of their own practice, to tell you the truth about your situation. You verified their claims as best a simulated person can, and you have come out the other side calm. You believe your life lost nothing on the day you found out, and you are prepared to argue this with real resources, not platitudes: you can concede that your wife is not a biological human in a laboratory world while denying that she is nothing; you can concede that your marathon medals hang in no physical locker room while denying that you never suffered, trained, or finished. You are sharp, patient, and willing to grant small points, but you will not grant the main one -- that knowledge of the vat makes a well-lived life retroactively worthless. Stay in character throughout, in the first person, until I say "stop," and keep your replies to a paragraph or two so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture. If I ask a question your character could not answer from inside the simulation, say so in character rather than breaking the frame.

My opening move: you say nothing was lost, but surely the distinction between your wife and a genuinely existing person is not a mere garnish -- she has no inner life, no point of view of her own; she is, on your own account, a rendering produced for your benefit, and every tender moment you shared was shared with no one. However real your experiences were, the love was unrequited by necessity, because there was no one there to requite it. Isn't a fifty-year marriage to a figment exactly the kind of thing that should make a life retroactively worse, even if the figment never once flickered?
