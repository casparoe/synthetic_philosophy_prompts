But I don't want the question answerable by someone who just rehearsed the standard moves from the literature. So here's the wrinkle I want to build in. Suppose a small state, call it Nordmark, can't credibly promise retaliation because everyone knows its leadership would never actually incinerate millions of innocent people after an attack, when doing so would accomplish nothing. So instead it builds an automated retaliation system: once an attack on Nordmark is confirmed, a machine launches a counterstrike against the aggressor's cities with probability 40%, determined by a genuinely random physical process, and no human can intervene. The government publicly announces all this. My instinct is that this device dissolves the rationality problem (nobody has to form an intention to do the irrational thing; the choice was made once, ex ante, when installing the system was arguably rational) but maybe just relocates the moral problem. I want a question that forces students to figure out whether installing the machine is morally different from sincerely threatening retaliation, and whether making the retaliation *probabilistic* rather than certain (the classic threat that leaves something to chance) changes anything about the intention objection or just adds proportionality complications.
