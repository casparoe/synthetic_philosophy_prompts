In customer service it's now totally standard for chatbots and voice assistants to introduce themselves with a first name and some small talk ("Hi, I'm Maya, how can I help you today?") without ever saying they're software -- and companies generally treat this as fine so long as the bot doesn't assert anything outright false and admits what it is if directly asked. Meanwhile the same companies insist their AI systems are trained to be "honest" and never lie. What theory of deception is the no-need-to-disclose practice presupposing? Presumably something like: deception requires a false assertion, so inducing a false belief through omission or general vibe doesn't count. Does that sit consistently with the broader claim that the assistant is honest? And notice that in advertising we do treat literally true but misleading claims as deceptive -- so is the chatbot norm quietly relying on a theory of lying we reject everywhere else, or is there a defensible reason to hold AIs to the assertion-based standard? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether the practice actually needs it or is compatible with several theories of honesty, and tell me what would have to change if honesty for an AI were defined as something like "not steering the user's beliefs away from the truth" rather than "not asserting falsehoods."
