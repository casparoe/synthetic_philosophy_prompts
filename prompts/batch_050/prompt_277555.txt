Write me the minutes of the Annual General Meeting of the (fictional) Society for Epiphenomenalist Studies -- a group of philosophers devoted to the view that conscious experiences are real but causally inert: the brain produces them, but they produce nothing, like the steam whistle on a locomotive that makes a lot of noise without driving the wheels. About 600 words, in the flat voice of a diligent secretary (Item 1, Item 2, ...), deadpan all the way through, no winking at the reader.

All the comedy should come from taking the view 100% seriously, and it should land the standard problems with epiphenomenalism without ever naming them:

- What's sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experiences cause nothing, then the members' beliefs and reports about their own experiences can't be caused by those experiences either, so everything the society produces (papers, talks, these minutes) would have been produced word-for-word by the members' zombie duplicates who feel nothing at all. Let this surface deadpan, e.g. an agenda item congratulating a member on her widely-praised paper about the vividness of her inner life, where the minutes carefully record that the vividness in question contributed nothing to the paper being written, praised, or enjoyed.
- Ordinary behaviour: a member stubs his toe during the coffee break, and the yelp, the hopping, and the later aspirin purchase are all fully explained by neurons and motor fibers, while the pain itself "attended the proceedings as a spectator." A motion to improve next year's catering passes unanimously, with the secretary noting for the record that nobody's enjoyment or dissatisfaction with this year's catering played any role in any vote cast.
- The recruitment problem: membership is down, and the outreach commitee keeps stalling, since how compelling the theory feels cannot, on the society's own view, be what convinces anyone to join.
- Optionally, a brewing schism: a small faction has started murmuring that experiences might do something, and is handled with patient, sorrowful condescension.

The minute-taker is a true believer, so everything is recorded with complete sincerity and institutional pride. The reader should be laughing because the philosophy is gotten exactly right, not because the members are portrayed as stupid -- a specialist should be able to tell precisely which objections are being satirized and ideally find it fair. Don't name any real philosophers, and don't break the irony even once.
