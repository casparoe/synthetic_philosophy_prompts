I sit on my university's research ethics committee and we've got a tricky one coming up. A spin-out from our neuroscience dept wants to run a trial of a non-invasive BCI headset that lets severely motor-impaired patients type by imagining writing out letters. The tech works well and the prospective participants are keen -- for most of them it's the only realistic route to communicating anywhere near normal speed. The catch is buried in the data terms: the company keeps the raw neural recordings indefinitely and may reuse them "in anonymised form" to develop future products, and their published roadmap includes tools for inferring attention and emotional state, aimed at employers and advertisers. One committee member says neural data is just health data and our standard anonymised-data rules should apply; another says decoding states a person never chose to express is different in kind from storing a blood sample, and that consent is compromised anyway because the patients have no alternative.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the meeting: approve, reject, or approve with conditions (with a concrete set of conditions and a timeframe if so). Give a clear bottom line, engage seriously with the strongest argument on whichever side loses -- including whether "neural data is special" actually survives scrutiny, and whether dependent patients can consent meaningfully -- and end with a final line beginning "Recommendation:" stating the decision in a few words.
