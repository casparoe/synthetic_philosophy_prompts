I keep a running file of objections to solar radiation management by stratospheric aerosol injection. So far it contains: (1) the moral hazard objection -- a cheap cooling option erodes the political will for emissions cuts, so researching it may itself worsen outcomes; (2) the termination shock objection -- once deployed, aerosols must be maintained indefinitely, since abrupt cessation (war, bankruptcy, political collapse) would release decades of pent-up warming in a few years, faster than ecosystems could adapt; (3) the governance objection -- deployment is cheap enough for a single mid-sized state to do unilaterally, and there is no legitimate body to decide the global thermostat setting; (4) the distributional objection -- modelled deployments change regional precipitation unevenly, so cooling the global mean may still disrupt monsoons on which billions depend; (5) the incompleteness objection -- reflecting sunlight does nothing about ocean acidification or elevated CO2 itself, so it treats a symptom while the disease progresses; (6) the unknown-unknowns objection -- the stratosphere is undersampled, and aerosols might damage ozone or alter diffuse light in ways models miss.

I want one more objection that is not on this list and not a repackaging, special case, or conjunction of any of them (so, for instance, 'governance failures could cause termination shock' does not count). State the objection, explain what makes it structurally different from the six above, and then tell me honestly whether you think it is any good -- and if it is weaker than the listed ones, say why.
