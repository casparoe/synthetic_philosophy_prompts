In 1880, Francis Galton published a paper in Mind (vol. 5, no. 19) titled "Statistics of Mental Imagery," reporting the results of asking people to picture their breakfast table and describe the illumination, definition, and coloring of the resulting image. He found astonishing variation: some respondents claimed images as bright as the real scene, while many eminent scientists denied having anything worth calling a mental picture at all. Less often quoted is what Galton says about the second group:

"I am however bound to say, that the missing faculty seems to be replaced so serviceably by other modes of conception, chiefly I believe connected with the motor sense, that men who declare themselves entirely deficient in the power of seeing mental pictures can nevertheless give life-like descriptions of what they have seen, and can otherwise express themselves as if they were gifted with a vivid visual imagination. They can also become painters of the rank of Royal Academicians."

Write an essay on what, if anything, this observation can teach us about the mental imagery debate between depictive theories (associated with Stephen Kosslyn, for whom imagining a scene involves a quasi-pictorial representation in a spatial medium, supported by results such as image-scanning and mental-rotation studies) and propositional or descriptive theories (associated with Zenon Pylyshyn, for whom the behavioral data reflect what people tacitly know and infer about visible things rather than the operations of an inner picture). The central question of your essay should be: Does the fact that people who report no imagery can nonetheless behave, describe, and even paint as though they had it count as evidence for one side of this debate, for the other, or for the conclusion that first-person reports about imagery vividness are simply the wrong instrument for settling it? In developing your answer, you should consider the distinction between how imagery feels and the format of the representations that underlie it, and you should consider whether Galton's "other modes of conception" remark anticipates either side's theoretical move. Weigh the main considerations on each side, but do not end on a non-committal note: reach a definite verdict and justify it.

Structure your essay in the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths (about 900 words total, excluding the Galton passage):

1. **What Galton Actually Found** (about 120 words): Interpret the passage. State precisely what Galton observed and what he inferred, and flag any gap between the two.

2. **The Depictive Reading** (about 180 words): Present the strongest case that Galton's data sit comfortably with, or even support, a picture-like theory of imagery.

3. **The Propositional Reading** (about 180 words): Present the strongest case that the same data support, or were at least anticipated by, the description-based view.

4. **Can Introspective Reports Adjudicate?** (about 250 words): Assess whether reports of imagery vividness, and dissociations of the kind Galton documented between reported experience and performance, can bear on the format of mental representation at all. Consider what each side would have to concede if they cannot.

5. **Verdict** (about 150 words): State and defend your conclusion, including one sentence on what kind of evidence, if not Galton's, would genuinely discriminate between the two theories.
