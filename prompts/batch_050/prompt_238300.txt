My eight-year-old son recently lied to us about breaking a neighbor's window, and the disagreement my wife and I then had about discipline—she said he should be ashamed of being the kind of boy who lies, while I insisted he should feel guilty about what he had done and go apologize—sent us both reading. A chapter in a popular psychology book my brother-in-law pressed on me summarizes what it presents as the settled science and philosophy of the two emotions, roughly as follows: 'Researchers have established that guilt and shame differ in their object. Guilt says "I did a bad thing"; shame says "I am bad." Guilt is the healthy emotion: it targets behavior rather than the self, and it reliably motivates confession, apology, and repair, while leaving one's self-worth untouched. Shame is the toxic one: it produces withdrawal, denial, lashing out, and cover-ups, it serves no constructive purpose, and shame-based parenting is therefore always harmful. The same distinction sorts entire cultures into guilt cultures and shame cultures, and the historical drift from the latter to the former counts as moral progress; in principle, a sufficiently reflective society could decide to retire shame altogether.' How much of this should I trust? Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a moral psychologist or a philosopher of emotion would accept, what they would want to qualify, and what is simply wrong—including whether the tidy act-versus-self contrast is as established as the chapter makes out, whether guilt really never turns corrosive, and whether shame really never does useful work. Then tell me whether the book's later advice—that parents should deliberately engineer guilt in their children and arrange things so that shame never arises—follows from anything that survives your audit, and whether the cultural claim (that a society could choose its dominant moral emotion the way it chooses a policy) makes any sense given how emotions are actually transmitted. I should say that I am inclined to believe the act-versus-self distinction, because it matches my own experience of the two feelings; tell me whether that inclination is well-founded or whether I am being flattered by a distinction that happens to vindicate my side of the argument with my wife.
