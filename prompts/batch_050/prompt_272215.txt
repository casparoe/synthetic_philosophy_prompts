I am giving the opening address at a two-day workshop called Forecasting Under Agency, attended by an unusually mixed group: meteorologists and epidemiologists who build probabilistic models, mayoral staff who act on them, philosophers interested in practical reasoning, and community representatives with no technical background. The workshop was prompted by an incident in our city last year. A crisis-modeling team publicly estimated a 70% probability that the mayor would order a coastal evacuation before midnight. The mayor, who was still genuinely undecided and felt that the number made the order seem predetermined, postponed the announcement for several hours partly to demonstrate that she had not been boxed in. Nobody thinks the forecast alone caused the delay, but everyone agrees that forecasting what an undecided decision-maker will do can alter the very deliberation being forecast.

Please write an eight-minute opening speech that uses this incident to introduce the workshop’s central question: can someone assign a probability to her own not-yet-made choice without ceasing to treat that choice as open? The speech should distinguish predicting another person’s settled intentions from estimating one’s own future action while still weighing reasons, and it should acknowledge the difficult cases in which self-prediction seems legitimate—for example, after a decision has effectively been made, when one recognizes one’s own predictable weaknesses, or when one adopts an outsider’s view of oneself. Do not turn the speech into a lecture or resolve the philosophical issue with a slogan. Let the issue shape what the occasion can appropriately say: the address should neither tell officials to treat their choices as merely natural events nor tell forecasters that choices are somehow beyond evidence. It should end by proposing three practical norms for the workshop—such as separating forecasts of circumstances from forecasts of decisions, stating the conditions under which a decision forecast was made, and considering when publication should be delayed—and explain why each norm follows from respecting both accurate prediction and genuine deliberation. The tone should be measured, civic-minded, and accessible enough for the community representatives while containing enough conceptual precision to engage the philosophers.
