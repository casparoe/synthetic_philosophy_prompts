I'm trying to figure out when a dispute is merely verbal and when it's real, going by my gut on cases. Two people arguing about whether a tree falling in an empty forest makes a sound, where one means vibrations in the air and the other means an auditory experience someone has: totally verbal, dissolve it and move on. Astronomers fighting over whether Pluto is a planet: not merely verbal, even though it has the same shape as the tree case — it feels like something real turns on which classification does the scientific work. Whether a hotdog is a sandwich: verbal and a waste of everyones time, even though it's also just about where to draw a category line, same as Pluto. A courtroom fight over whether an ambulance counts as a 'vehicle' under the no-vehicles-in-the-park ordinance: substantive, even though it's literally a disagreement about what a word means. Two philosophers arguing about whether free will exists, where it comes out that one means the ability to have done otherwise and the other means being the ultimate source of your choices: I want to say substantive, but I honestly can't say why once both readings are laid out on the table. And the debate about whether there really are tables or only particles arranged tablewise: merely verbal, I think, though the mereological nihilist will obviously deny that. Is there one principle that sorts these the way my gut does — in particular one that separates Pluto from the hotdog, since both are 'where do we draw the line' fights — and which of my verdicts does it force me to give up if it can't fit them all? And what does it say about the case I keep waffling on: whether a news style guide should let 'terrorism' cover violence by states. Lead with the principle itself in the first sentence, no preamble, no restating the cases back to me, then the reasoning.
