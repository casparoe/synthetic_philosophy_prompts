I've been in an epistemology reading group that has spent three months on bootstrapping and easy knowledge, and the debate has gone completely circular. One side holds that if a belief-forming method -- a color detector, a gas gauge -- delivers justification for believing it is reliable before you've independently established that it is, then something has gone wrong with the epistemology that permits this, since no amount of further checking with the same instrument can lift you into knowing it's reliable. The other side accepts that the method's deliverances do justify believing in its own reliability and bites the bullet: the resulting knowledge of reliability is genuine, even if it would be useless in an argument against a skeptic or a doubter. A third faction tries to split the difference by granting the justification while denying it can be transmitted or used dialectically. Everyone in the room knows all the standard cases and all the moves; nobody has changed position. My hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about any particular inference but about what justification is for -- whether it's a property a belief has in virtue of how it was formed, full stop, or something essentially tied to what you could legitimately offer another person in defense of the belief. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden substantive question here -- about the structure of evidential support, or about what 'prior' justification means -- that one side is simply getting wrong? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, support the diagnosis with what the positions genuinely commit their holders to, and say what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves it, a case that decides it, or an argument that the impasse is exactly what we should expect.
