I am a high school senior, and next month I am giving the closing talk at our school's joint philosophy-and-economics seminar day: a thirty-minute presentation followed by twenty minutes of audience questions. I need help preparing for the question period.

My talk defends the following thesis about the centipede game (two players alternate deciding whether to "take," ending the game with a larger share for the taker, or "pass," growing the total pot while handing the decision to the other player, over a fixed, known number of rounds). The standard backward-induction argument — at the last node the second player takes, so at the second-to-last node the first player takes, and so on, until taking at the very first node is the only rational move — does not actually establish its conclusion. My argument: the chain of reasoning only gets started by assuming that both players will act "rationally" in the backward-induction sense at every later node, but the moment the game actually continues past the first move, someone has already behaved in a way that contradicts that assumption. A player who gets passed to has received evidence that her opponent is not following the backward-induction strategy, and at that point the argument that was supposed to govern her own choice no longer applies to the situation she is actually in. So the textbook presentation, I claim, smuggles in the assumption that players never reach the positions the argument is about, and "take immediately" is unstable as advice rather than a discovery about rationality.

The audience will be roughly forty people, but the questioners I care about are: (1) our economics teacher, who did a master's degree in economics, teaches the orthodox treatment, and has told us in class that results like this are "mathematical facts, not opinions" — for him, equilibrium reasoning and common knowledge of rationality are settled tools; (2) three or four classmates from the economics track who have absorbed that lesson and will compete to defend it; (3) the math teacher, who is agnostic about the economics but will press hard on whether I am conflating a formally valid theorem with a contestable interpretation of what rationality requires; and (4) the general students, who mostly feel intuitively that passing is sensible and will ask friendly but imprecise questions.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience — not the objections a professional game theorist would raise at a conference — ordered by how likely they are to be asked and how much damage each would do to my thesis, each phrased the way that particular audience member would phrase it (the economics teacher will reach for equilibrium and common-knowledge language; the math teacher will frame things as theorem versus interpretation; the classmates will be blunter). For each objection, give me what I should concede up front, the strongest reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Also identify the weakest point in my own position so I can defend it rather than hope it goes unnoticed, tell me which single question I should be hoping nobody asks, and give me a plan for handling "So what would you actually do at your first node?" — since I suspect my answer there can undermine the whole talk if I give it carelessly.
