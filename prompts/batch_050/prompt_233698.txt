I am preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on coercion and the criminal law, and I intend to organize it around the paradox of blackmail. I would like you to write the analytical core of that session.

Begin by stating the paradox in its strongest form. Blackmail appears to criminalize the conjunction of two acts, each of which is legal: disclosing truthful information one is permitted to disclose, and requesting money one is permitted to request. Then explain why the obvious dissolution does not suffice. It is tempting to say that conditional threats simply change the normative landscape, but many conditional threats are entirely lawful — an employer who says "resign or I will report your embezzlement to the board," a litigant who says "settle or I will file suit," a spouse who says "move out or I will tell the children." So the puzzle is not why threats are regulated but where the principled line runs between permissible leverage and blackmail, and why the demand for money seems to transform a legitimate threat into a crime rather than merely an unseemly transaction.

The heart of the session is a ladder of cases I have constructed to sit at increasing distance from the paradigm, and I want you to test the main candidate resolutions against it:

1. A discovers that her business partner is embezzling and tells him: return the money and resign, or I inform the board.
2. B, a stranger, witnesses a crime and tells the perpetrator: pay me ten thousand dollars or I go to the police.
3. C learns of a colleague's affair and tells him: confess to your spouse yourself within a week, or I will tell her.
4. D, a journalist, tells a public figure: give me the exclusive interview, or I will run the story quoting your estranged family instead.
5. E offers a celebrity the reverse arrangement: pay me, and I will sign an agreement never to mention what I know about you — a standing offer with no explicit threat.
6. F tells a wealthy acquaintance: pay me five hundred dollars, or I will tell everyone at the club that you are a notoriously poor tipper.

For each of the main families of explanation — first, that the blackmailer sells information that is not rightfully theirs to sell, because it "belongs" to the victim's relationships or to interested third parties; second, that blackmail is wrong because it suppresses information owed to others, such as police, shareholders, or spouses; third, that blackmail is a distinctive form of coercion that corrupts the victim's practical deliberation in a way permissible leverage does not; and fourth, economic accounts under which criminalization discourages investment in digging up or manufacturing damaging secrets — identify which rungs of the ladder it handles well, which it mishandles, and whether its failures look fatal or merely in need of refinement. Note, for instance, that several rungs involve threats to disclose rather than demands to suppress, and that rung 5 removes the threat while keeping the payment, so each theory must be checked against the cases it was not designed for.

Conclude with a judgment. Does the paradox admit of a solution — a single principle that sorts the ladder correctly and explains why the conjunction of two legal acts can be criminal — or does its persistence reveal that "blackmail" lumps together conduct that is wrong for several different reasons? And what follows from your answer for how I should frame the session itself: as a genuine puzzle awaiting its solution, or as a symptom of a badly drawn legal category that dissolves once the cases are properly distinguished?
