A handout from a history of philosophy survey course I attended summarizes Ibn Rushd's position in the Decisive Treatise as follows: "Averroes held that scripture and philosophy are two independent routes to truth, aimed at different audiences. Since the Quran itself commands reflection on the world, philosophy is not merely permitted but obligatory for those with the talent for it. Where a demonstrated philosophical conclusion conflicts with the surface meaning of a revealed text, the philosopher should treat the text as allegory and read it figuratively, because truth cannot contradict truth. The plain sense of scripture is for the masses, who need vivid images of reward and punishment in order to behave; the allegorical sense is for the philosophical elite, who can handle the truth. Averroes therefore embraced what later thinkers called the doctrine of double truth: a proposition can be true for religion and false for philosophy at the same time, and the wise man keeps the two in separate compartments. On this basis he argued that philosophers are under no obligation to hold the beliefs of ordinary believers, since they have outgrown the need for religion. This makes Averroes, in effect, an early secular rationalist: he assigned religion a purely social function -- crowd control through edifying fiction -- while reserving real knowledge for natural reason. His quarrel with al-Ghazali was thus not a dispute within Islamic thought but the opening skirmish of the modern war between science and religion."

I have read enough about Ibn Rushd to suspect this gets him wrong, but I cannot locate the errors with confidence, and the final verdict does have a certain pull.
