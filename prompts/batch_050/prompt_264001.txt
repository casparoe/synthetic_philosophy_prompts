After four decades as an experimental physicist, I have spent my retirement reading the philosophy of space and time, and I keep returning to Einstein's hole argument as revived by Earman and Norton in the 1980s: take a model of general relativity, apply a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside some compact region but reshuffles things inside it, and you seem to get two models that agree on the entire past and diverge thereafter -- which, if the two represent genuinely distinct physical possibilities, means the theory fails to determine what happens inside the hole.

I have been cataloguing the standard responses, and my list so far runs as follows:

(1) Leibniz equivalence, in Earman and Norton's own spirit: the two diffeomorphic models are two representations of one and the same physical possibility, so no failure of determinism about the actual world has been shown.

(2) Maudlin's metrical essentialism: spacetime points have their metrical properties essentially, so a map that drags the metric across the points does not generate a new possibility at all -- the 'second' model was never a distinct way the world could be.

(3) Anti-haecceitist or 'sophisticated' substantivalism in the style of Butterfield: points exist, but possible worlds that differ only over which point occupies which qualitative role are not distinct worlds; differences in bare point identity are not physical differences.

(4) Relationalist responses that deny there are bare manifold points to permute in the first place, taking spatiotemporal reality to consist in material events and their coincidence relations, as in Stachel's reading of Einstein's point-coincidence considerations.

(5) The algebraic reformulation associated with Geroch's work: state the theory in a form (Einstein algebras) in which diffeomorphic models are not two representations of one object but literally the same mathematical object, so the question of their distinctness cannot even be posed.

(6) The deflationary or quietist response: concede the indeterminism but dismiss it as unthreatening, since the undetermined facts are ones no measurement could ever settle.

What I would like is one further response that is not on this list. I have a reasonably specific standard in mind: it should not be a rewording, a special case, or a conjunction of items (1)-(6). In particular, it should not be (1) or (3) restated in the vocabulary of gauge, nor (4) restated in the vocabulary of structural realism; if the response you propose collapses into one of those under examination, please say so yourself rather than presenting it as new. If you judge that the list already exhausts the space of serious options, say that instead, and explain why.

Two further requests. First, state precisely which premise of the hole argument your proposed response rejects -- the move from mathematical to physical distinctness, the substantivalist commitment, the determinism premise, or something else I have not isolated. Second, give me your honest assessment of whether it is any good: I am a newcomer to this literature but not to physics, and I would rather be told that my catalogue is complete than be offered a spurious novelty.
