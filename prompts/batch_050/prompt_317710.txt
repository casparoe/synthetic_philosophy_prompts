I'm a TA for an intro philosophy of mind course and my students have a bad habit: they memorize the names of the responses to the Marys room thought experiment and then can't tell you which one they actually believe, or why. So for the review session I want to build a decision tree / flowchart that takes a student from the starting point of the case -- Mary, raised in the black-and-white room, knows every physical fact about color vision, steps outside and sees a ripe tomato for the first time -- and walks them to a named position through a series of yes/no or multiple-choice questions. The leaves must include at least: the ability hypothesis (she gains know-how, not knowledge), the acquaintance response, the phenomenal concept strategy, property dualism (the argument works, there are non-physical facts), Russellian monism or panpsychism, and illusionism (the intuition that she learns something is itself the thing to be explained away). If there's a logically available combination of answers that nobody prominent defends, give it a leaf labeled 'unnamed' and one sentence diagnosing whether the gap is because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Two constraints to make this actually useful rather than a wikipedia flowchart. First, phrase every branching question as something a student can answer WITHOUT knowing any of the labels -- so not 'do you think phenomenal knowledge is non-physical?' but concrete probes like: before leaving the room, could Mary have picked the red tomato out of a lineup of tomatoes? Could she have imagined red accurately? Suppose a second Mary was raised in full color but taught a totally false physics of the brain -- which Mary is missing more? Second constraint: I want the tree stress-tested against a parallel case. After the main tree, run the same questions on a variant where the know-it-all is a human scientist who knows every physical fact about bat echolocation but has never echolocated, and tell me honestly whether the tree sorts people the same way on both cases or whether it exposes an inconsistency (people often go dualist about color but physicalist about bats, and I'd like the handout to make that tension visible rather than hide it).

Aim for roughly 8-12 branching questions total. At each leaf, give the position's name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment (the thing its defenders have to swallow). And please flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate -- I suspect acquaintance and the phenomenal concept strategy are hard to tell apart with concrete questions alone, and if so I'd rather the handout admits that than pretends otherwise.
