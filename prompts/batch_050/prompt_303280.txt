So I spent forty years as a field biologist and only now, in retirement, have I started reading philosophy, which is probably why I got roped into refereeing an arguement between two old colleagues at our department reunion. Neither would budge, and I'd like a proper adjudication -- in American English please, point by point.

The fight: Karl insists species are real things in nature, not human inventions. His pitch: a species is a lineage evolving on its own track, and the proof is that field guides mostly agree -- two ornithologists working independently split the birds of a forest almost identically. Marta shot back that biologists can't even settle what a species IS -- there are something like twenty-plus competing definitions in the literature, and they disagree constantly once you leave the easy cases: asexual lineages, hybrid zones, those salamander populations arranged in a ring around a valley where neighbors interbreed but the two ends can't. No single natural kind needs two dozen definitions, she said; "species" is a filing system. Karl answered that the many concepts are like the many definitions of "fitness" in my old field -- different instruments tracking one underlying thing, separate lineages -- and fuzzy edges don't dissolve the solid middle, same as a fuzzy border doesn't show there's no difference between night and day. Marta's last move: fine, maybe lineages are real, but the RANK is arbitrary -- nothing in nature tells you when a lineage deserves "species" instead of "subspecies," so when a taxonomist splits one species into two, she's made a decision, not a discovery, and conservation law treats these decisions as facts of nature.

Who's right about which move? What are both of them missing? And who comes out ahead overall?
