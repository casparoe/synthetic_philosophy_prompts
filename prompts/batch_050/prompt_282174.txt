I'm in Year 12 and have been preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club on Zhuangzi and the Daoist idea of wu-wei, usually translated as effortless action. While drafting it, I assembled an argument that I intended as a short section on the so-called paradox of trying not to try, but the argument turned out stronger than I wanted: it seems to show not just that wu-wei is paradoxical, but that the entire Daoist practice of cultivating it is incoherent. That conclusion is surely too strong, since the same texts are full of figures — Cook Ding carving his ox, the cicada catcher, the ferryman who handles his boat as if it were nothing — who are presented precisely as people who became effortless through long discipline. I would like to know exactly where my reasoning fails, because I cannot find the weak joint myself.

The argument runs as follows. (1) An action counts as wu-wei only if the agent performs it without deliberately trying to perform it well: the moment you are straining to act skilfully, the action is forced and no longer spontaneous. (2) Nothing can be both deliberately attempted and done without deliberation at the same moment — trying to be spontaneous is still a form of trying, so whatever you are trying to do you are not doing effortlessly. (3) Therefore no one can produce wu-wei in themselves by trying to do so. (4) Every course of cultivation the tradition recommends — rehearsing rituals, practising breath control, training a craft — is something undertaken deliberately, on purpose, in order to become better. (5) So all such training aims at a state which, by (3), training cannot produce: each practice session is an effort directed at its own abolition, and the effort poisons the outcome. (6) If that is right, mastery of the kind Cook Ding displays is not something anyone could have trained into; yet (7) the texts present such mastery as both real and attainable through devotion to the Way. (8) So either the exemplars are impossible and the teaching recommends cultivation towards an unreachable state, or the teaching contradicts itself about its own goal. Either way, the Daoist position collapses.

What I would like from you is a diagnosis, not a summary. Tell me which numbered step you would reject or qualify, and explain why that step looked compelling to me in the first place — I suspect some word is being used in two senses across the argument, or that a claim about the moment of performance is being smuggled into a claim about the years of practice preceding it, but I cannot pin it down. Please also compare the costs of the available repairs: if the fix is that training works indirectly, by reshaping dispositions rather than by commanding spontaneity at the instant of action, say what the opponent of my argument must concede about how long and uncertain that route is; if instead (1) needs qualifying, say what conception of effortlessness survives the qualification. If it matters, the premises I am most reluctant to surrender are (2), which strikes me as almost definitional, and (6), since abandoning the exemplars seems to abandon the tradition. I would rather be told I have to give one of them up than be offered a repair that saves everything by saying nothing. Please write your answer in British English.
