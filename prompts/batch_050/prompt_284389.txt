I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've lately been working on a long piece about how philosophy is actually taught at the undergraduate level -- not the famous thought experiments themselves, but how students absorb and half-absorb them. A friend who lectures in philosophy agreed to let me sit in on grading for her course on personal identity, and she has given me one student's exam answer to assess as a trial run. She'll compare my assessment against her own, so I'd like a careful second opinion before I commit to a grade.

The exam question was: "Explain Locke's memory criterion of personal identity. Then present Reid's brave officer objection and explain exactly why it poses a problem for Locke. Finally, assess whether Locke has the resources to respond to the objection."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Locke holds that what makes a person the same person over time is sameness of consciousness, which in practice means memory: a person at a later time is identical with a person at an earlier time just in case the later person can remember the earlier person's experiences from the inside. Bodily continuity is neither necessary nor sufficient, which is why Locke's prince-and-cobbler case is supposed to make sense.

Reid's brave officer objection runs as follows. Suppose a boy is flogged for stealing apples from an orchard. As a young officer, he performs an act of bravery in his first campaign, capturing an enemy standard, and at that time he still remembers the flogging. Years later, as an elderly general, he remembers capturing the standard but has completely forgotten the flogging. On Locke's criterion, the young officer is the same person as the boy, because he remembers the flogging; and the general is the same person as the young officer, because he remembers capturing the standard. But the general is not the same person as the boy, since he cannot remember the flogging. This shows that Locke's account is circular: memory can only establish personal identity if we already assume that the rememberer and the person remembered are one and the same, so the criterion smuggles in what it was meant to define.

Locke can escape the objection by appealing to a chain of overlapping memories: the general is linked to the boy through the intermediate officer, so identity is preserved across the whole sequence even though the endpoints are not directly connected by memory. Reid's case therefore poses no real problem once we read the criterion in terms of psychological continuity rather than direct remembering."

The answer is worth 20 points, and the rubric rewards accuracy of exposition, precision about where the objection bites, and sound judgment in the final assessment. Please give me a grade out of 20 and, more importantly, the specific comments I should write in the margin: for each strength and each error, point to the exact claim in the answer that earns or loses credit, and explain briefly what the correct position is. I don't want to penalize the student for anything that's actually fine, nor let a plausible-sounding mistake slip through.
