My son's school started using proof-checking software for math, and while reading up on it I fell down a rabbit hole and ended up reading that old Lewis Carroll dialogue where the Tortoise keeps making Achilles write down every inference step as an extra premise, so the proof of a simple thing in geometry never actually finishes. Here's what I can't sort out. The pitch for proof assistants (Lean and friends, which apparently real mathematicians use now) is that the machine checks every step, so the proof is more certain than a human one. But as far as I can tell the machine doesn't accept that modus ponens is valid the way the Tortoise refused to -- it just applies it, blindly, and then a human still has to trust that the machine's green checkmark means the theorem is proven. So: if Carroll were around today, would he say the Tortoise's regress has finally been stopped, because machines don't need persuading at each step and the puzzle was only ever about what it's rational for a person to accept? Or would he say we've just moved the problem -- now there's a machine applying a rule nobody persuaded it of, and at the end some human still has to accept that its output counts as a proof, which is exactly where Achilles was stuck? And does the regress even make sense against mechanical rule-following, or was it always only about reasoners?
