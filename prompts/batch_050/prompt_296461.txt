My grandmother had a series of small strokes a few years ago, and afterwards something strange happened that I have never been able to categorize. If she bumped her hand on the table or stepped on something sharp, she would say, quite calmly and with full command of her words, "Yes, that hurts, it hurts exactly as much as it ever did" — and then continue her sentence about the weather. She made no move to protect the injured spot, showed no distress, and when the doctor asked whether she wanted something for the pain she seemed genuinely puzzled by the offer, as if she'd been asked whether she wanted something for the color of the curtains. Her memory, attention, and ability to describe the sensation (its location, its intensity, its throbbing quality) were all intact. Then last month a friend recovering from surgery told me that on her medication she could still feel the pain "right there, same as before," only it no longer felt like it had anything to do with her — she described it as interesting rather than bad. And a third case: a colleague who meditates claims he has simply learned to stop minding, and insists nothing about the sensation itself has changed.

What strikes me is that these three cases look superficially similar but might be deeply different. In my friend's case it seems like the feeling stayed but the caring vanished. In my colleague's case it seems like the caring is still there somewhere but has been voluntarily quieted, the way you might ignore a ringing phone. In my grandmother's case I honestly can't tell whether something was subtracted from the experience or whether the connection between the experience and everything else was cut. My assumption had always been that what makes pain bad is simply how it feels — that the hurting and the badness are one thing — but these cases suggest they can come apart, which I find both fascinating and faintly alarming, since it seems to matter enormously for questions like whether a person in this state is being harmed when they are hurt, or whether it would be wrong to cause such sensations in someone wired this way from birth.

Is there an established name for this split — either one name for the whole family or separate names for the different versions I've described? I'd also like to know which of my three cases, if any, fits the classic clinical description, where the edges of the concept lie (does my meditating colleague count, or only neurological patients?), and whether philosophers who write about why pain is bad have actually taken these cases on board or mostly ignored them.
