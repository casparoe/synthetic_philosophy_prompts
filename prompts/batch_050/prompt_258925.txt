I am a graduate student in political and legal theory choosing a dissertation agenda at the intersection of Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment, and I want a defended ordering of the open questions rather than a survey of the terrain. By way of orientation, here is how I currently carve things up. First, I distinguish two families of device that usually get run together: friction devices, which leave the later self legally free to act but raise the cost of doing so (supermajority requirements, cooling-off periods, multi-stage amendment procedures), and disablement devices, which purport to remove the option entirely (unamendable eternity clauses, irrevocable psychiatric advance directives, irreversible delegation to independent institutions). Second, I distinguish three ways the later self can diverge from the committing self, which seem to call for different treatment: weakness of will, where the later self still endorses the commitment but is tempted; genuine change of judgment, where the later self rationally revises; and change of identity in a morally loaded sense, where the bound party is arguably not the same agent at all, as with a constitution written by a founding generation binding its descendants. The familiar cases sit differently in this grid: Odysseus facing the Sirens is a weakness-of-will case with continuous identity; an advance directive refusing future treatment binds a self whose evaluative standpoint has altered; an eternity clause binds people who consented to nothing.

Within this space, which open questions are most worth roughly three years of a theorist's work, and in what order? I have in mind candidates such as: whether the standard justification of precommitment (protecting a settled long-term preference against a foreseen transient distortion) can be extended to cases where the distortion is not transient but permanent, like constitutional entrenchment against all future majorities; whether friction devices and disablement devices differ in kind or only in degree, and whether anything normatively important turns on the answer; whether the legitimacy of a precommitment device degrades over time in a way that could be quantified or at least made principled, as opposed to the vague claim that old constitutions lose their grip; and whether there is a coherent notion of wrongful self-binding, where even voluntary, informed, undistorted commitment fails to bind, and what that implies for advance directives in dementia cases specifically. But I do not want you simply to rank my four: add or remove candidates as you see fit.

For the ordering, defend it on three criteria and make the trade-offs between them explicit: how many of the neighboring questions are downstream of this one, how tractable it is for someone working without empirical methods, and how crowded the territory already is. Say also what would change the ranking — for instance, whether a convincing argument that friction and disablement differ only in degree would demote the time-degradation question or promote it — because I want to understand the structure of the agenda, not just inherit a list. The point of the dissertation, eventually, is to say something useful to people who actually design these devices, so treat practical upshot as a tiebreaker rather than the main criterion, and tell me whether you think that weighting is a mistake.
