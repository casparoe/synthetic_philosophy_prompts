In my philosophy of physics reading group, one member has a habit of closing down discussion with the slogan that gauge symmetry is mere redundancy of description, not a symmetry of nature. Most recently he used it twice in one evening: first to dismiss as meaningless the question of which gauge fixing (say, Lorenz versus Coulomb gauge) is the physically correct one, and second to infer from the Aharonov–Bohm effect that only gauge-invariant quantities such as holonomies are real, so that debates about the status of the vector potential are settled. I suspect the slogan conflates several claims and does different work in the two uses. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — for instance: that gauge transformations are a relabelling freedom of a particular formalism rather than of the world; that gauge-related models invariably represent one and the same physical possibility; that gauge-dependent quantities fail to refer to anything in reality; and that the Aharonov–Bohm effect shows the electromagnetic potential is unreal (or, on the contrary, that it is real and gauge freedom must be given up). For each reading, say whether it is a platitude about formalisms, a substantive and defensible interpretive thesis, or straightforwardly confused, and assess whether it would actually license either of the two dismissals he made.
