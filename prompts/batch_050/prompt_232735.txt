Our museum's acquisitions committee, which I chair, must decide at its next meeting whether to accession a video game into the permanent collection for the first time. The work in question is an austere independent game from 2016 in which the player, captaining an ageing fishing boat, must decide each simulated day whether to sail out once more or give up; the catches grow steadily worse, the savings dwindle, and the game ends either in bankruptcy or in the player choosing to walk away. Its admirers freely concede that the graphics and sound are rudimentary. What they praise is the way its rules shape the player's own deliberation: critics describe playing it as an uncomfortably exact study of one's own stubbornness, hope, and capacity for self-deception. Two further facts complicate matters. The game runs only on a discontinued console; our conservators believe emulation is feasible, but the designer insists that small timing differences under emulation alter how the decisions feel. And she has stipulated that, if acquired, the work must never be exhibited as a video recording.

The committee is divided on two questions. First, several members argue that accessioning the game would be a category mistake: what the museum would own is a piece of software, essentially a rulebook, whereas the alleged artwork exists only in unrepeatable performances by players whom the museum cannot control — acquiring it, one member said, would be like acquiring a football league and cataloguing it under sculpture. Others reply that the designed space of possible actions is itself the work, no different in principle from the instruction-based conceptual pieces we already hold, and that the player's agency is simply the medium, as pigment is for painting. Second, even members sympathetic to acquisition disagree about display. Our education department notes that a full playthrough takes around six hours, that unskilled players encounter substantially different content from skilled ones, and that the average visitor dwells in a gallery for minutes rather than hours; it therefore favours a commissioned film of a single complete playthrough. The designer's stipulation forbids this, and some curators side with her on the ground that a recording would exhibit a trace of someone's agency rather than the agency of which the work is made.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. It should give a clear, actionable bottom line — accession or decline, and if accession, on what terms (original hardware versus emulation; playable installation versus any permitted documentation) — and set out reasoning that the dissenting members and the designer could actually respond to rather than merely applaud or dismiss. It should also state honestly the strongest argument on whichever side loses and explain why the committee nonetheless overrules it. Somewhere between 600 and 900 words, and in British English, please.
