In a graduate seminar on On Liberty, we ended up in a long, unresolved argument about whether Mill's principles have anything determinate to say about modern reproductive technology, and I'd like you to adjudicate it by staging the question directly: suppose Mill were alive today, briefed on the current state of the art -- IVF with preimplantation genetic diagnosis, polygenic embryo screening that ranks embryos by predicted disease risk and (contestedly) by traits like height or educational attainment, and gene editing of embryos -- and asked to issue a verdict on what philosophers call procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics. By procreative beneficence I mean the view, associated with Julian Savulescu, that prospective parents who can select among possible future children have a moral obligation to choose the child expected to have the best life; by liberal eugenics I mean the broader position, associated with Nicholas Agar, that state-permitted (but not state-mandated) parental selection of children's traits is legitimate and does not inherit the sins of coercive twentieth-century eugenics. Don't summarize these views back to me; take them as defined and put Mill in the room with them.

The seminar split over which strand of Mill does the work. One side argued Mill must be a natural ally of liberal eugenics: the harm principle seemingly leaves embryo selection untouched, since the non-identity problem appears to dissolve the most obvious objection -- a child born with the selected traits cannot claim to have been harmed by a choice without which she would not exist, and the unselected embryos are not persons with claims. The other side pointed to Mill's less-celebrated commitments: his insistence, late in On Liberty, that bringing a child into existence is among the most consequential other-regarding acts and that the state may legitimately condition reproduction on the ability to support a child; his perfectionism about the higher faculties, which sits oddly with a doctrine of parental discretion exercised by market preference; and above all his dread of conformity and the despotism of custom, which seems to predict that free parental choice would converge on a narrow menu of fashionable traits rather than diversify human experiments in living. Mill also denies that children fall under the protection of the liberty principle, which complicates the question of whose liberty selection expresses -- the parents', certainly, but does the doctrine smuggle in a claim about the child's?

So: what would Mill actually conclude, on the strongest reconstruction of his views? I want a reasoned verdict, not a hedged survey. Address at minimum: whether the harm principle plus the non-identity problem really does settle the matter in favor of unrestricted selection; whether Mill's own willingness to regulate reproduction cuts against a purely libertarian reading; whether his perfectionism generates a Millian version of procreative beneficence (parents obliged to select for capacities conducive to individuality and the higher pleasures) and whether that version is coherent given his anti-paternalism; and where, if anywhere, Mill would break ranks with both the libertarians and the obligation-to-select camp. If his commitments genuinely conflict, say which you think wins out in his system and why, rather than presenting the tension as a standoff.
