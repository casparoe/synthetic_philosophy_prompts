I'm a philosophy professor who works on philosophy of language and mind, and over the past few years I've acquired a working fluency in statistical learning theory — training data, hypothesis classes, underdetermination, inductive bias, no-free-lunch results, generalization error. Restate Kripkenstein's rule-following paradox in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can: the plus/quus skeptic as a claim about the underdetermination of a target function by any finite sample, the dispositionalist response as an appeal to the learner's actual generalization behavior, and so on through the main moves. Then give me the residue report. My suspicion is that two things won't survive the translation: first, that the skeptical argument targets not just finite evidence but every candidate meaning-constituting fact, dispositions included, since dispositions are finite and misfire; second, the normativity of meaning — that my past intention settles what I ought to say now, not merely what I will say — which seems to have no statistical counterpart. Tell me whether I'm right that this is exactly what's lost, or whether the learning-theoretic vocabulary can in fact express the normativity (via inductive bias, via what counts as generalization error) — and whether the residue shows my home debate was resting on something the formal framework rightly refuses to say, or shows instead that the formal framework is missing something real.
