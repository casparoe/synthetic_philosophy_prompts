I am a doctoral researcher in philosophy of cognitive science, and for a chapter of my thesis on self-modifying systems I need a decision tree that sorts the possible positions on a single question. Whenever a system represents or revises itself — a formal theory that talks about its own sentences, a constitution that provides for its own amendment, a program that rewrites part of its own code, a mind that reflects on the machinery of its own reflection — must there always be some level of the system that the episode of self-reference or self-modification cannot reach: an inviolate level, in Hofstadter's sense? And if there must, is that level fixed, movable, stipulated, or merely an artefact of how the observer carves the system into levels?

Please build the tree from the logical space first and the named positions second. Specifically:

- Use six to eight branch nodes, each binary or at most ternary, and each phrased as a question a reader could answer without knowing which position it funnels them towards. The sorts of claim that should do the splitting include: whether a rule that governs an act of self-modification must itself remain untouched for the duration of that act; whether the tangle in Escher's Drawing Hands is a genuine feature of some systems or only a feature of the vantage point from which we view them; whether formal limitative results about self-reference (diagonalisation, the inexpressibility of a truth predicate within its own language) carry over to minds and machines, or are a misleading model of them; and whether inviolability is ever a matter of design rather than necessity.
- Each leaf should carry the name of the position where a recognisable one exists. I would expect at least: the formalist view that a protected level is provably necessary for any sufficiently expressive self-representing system; Hofstadter's own picture, on which tangles are real within symbol systems and the inviolate level is found only by stepping outside the system altogether; a relay or bootstrapping view, on which every episode of change has a protected level but which level is protected shifts from episode to episode; a conventionalist view, on which protected levels are stipulated rather than discovered, as with entrenched constitutional clauses; a deflationary view, on which level-talk is observer-relative and the original question dissolves; and a literalist view, on which genuinely symmetric strange loops exist with no protected level at all. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, mark the leaf as such and say in a sentence whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or simple neglect.
- The following five cases must each appear in the tree, assigned to the branch where they do the most discriminating work: (i) Drawing Hands itself — where, if anywhere, does its inviolate level lie; (ii) a self-hosting compiler, written in the very language it compiles; (iii) a constitution whose amendment procedure can itself be amended, and whether a self-entrenching clause of the kind found in the German Basic Law actually settles anything or merely postpones the question; (iv) CRISPR-style genome editing, in which an organism-level agent modifies the genome that produced it; (v) a thermostat overseen by a meta-thermostat that resets its set point — and the question of where that regress is forced to stop.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate (I suspect the relay view and the conventionalist view may concede the same answers to every diagnostic while disagreeing about what those answers show), and state what further question would be needed to prise them apart.
- Close with a short paragraph addressed to a reader who has just been sorted into a leaf, identifying the single commitment their position most obviously forces them to abandon.

Formal register, please, and British English; no citations are required. The aim is an instrument that sorts researchers by what they concede about the cases, not by which label they happen to prefer.
