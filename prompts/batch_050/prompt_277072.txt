I am a novelist, three years into a historical novel, and I have run into a problem I cannot seem to settle with the usual craft maxims. The book concerns a poisoning trial in a Yorkshire mill town in the 1870s: a schoolteacher was accused, acquitted for lack of evidence, and drowned two years later in what the coroner called an accident and the village called a confession. Every principal figure is a real person, recoverable from parish registers and a few columns of the county paper, and I have kept their true names deliberately — not from laziness but because the book's entire force, and frankly its commercial appeal, rests on the reader's knowledge that these people existed. The novel moves among six minds in close third person, which means that for each of them I must invent an interior life wholesale; the record supplies dates, occupations, testimony, and almost nothing of what anyone thought or felt.

Here is where I grow uneasy. The plot requires someone to have driven the schoolteacher to the water, and the only candidate history offers is her brother-in-law, of whom we know exactly two things: that he quarreled with her over money and that he testified for the prosecution. Everything else I have given him — a campaign of patient rumor, a scene in which she begs him and he refuses her — is my invention, and so is the consciousness I have built for him, in which he knows precisely what he is doing and does it anyway. Meanwhile I have given the schoolteacher herself an invented secret act of kindness and an invented stoicism, and no one, including my conscience, objects to that. The asymmetry troubles me: if the dead are beyond being wronged, then my flattering inventions and my malign ones must stand or fall together, and if they are not beyond it, I cannot articulate what makes one kind of fabrication a tribute and the other a theft. I also notice that inventing the brother-in-law's thoughts feels worse than inventing his deeds — the reader will come away feeling they have known the man from the inside — but I cannot say whether that feeling tracks a real distinction or only a novelist's superstition. Two further complications: his great-great-grandchildren found me through the local history society and wrote, very courteously, to ask what I intend to make of their ancestor; and my editor's whole remedy is a brisk author's note about dramatization, which I suspect readers skip and which, worse, seems to license me without changing anything readers will actually carry away.

What I want is not reassurance but a principled account, if one exists. Is there anything here I genuinely owe, and to whom — to the dead themselves, or only to descendants, readers, and some collective interest in the historical record? Does it matter that I have kept the real names, given that the names are precisely the conduit through which my inventions will overwrite the record, since nothing else about these people will ever be as widely read as my book? Does it matter how obscure the person is, how long dead, whether descendants survive, whether the invention concerns outer deeds or inner life, whether it defames or glorifies? And does the disclaimer actually discharge anything, or is it a fiction about fiction? If the honest answer is that there is no line, only a continuum of taste and tact, I would like to be shown why — but if there is a line, I need to know where it runs before I let this book go to print.
