So I'm prepping for a reading group on Hume's Enquiry and I was catching up by listening to a philosophy podcast, and the guest (I think a historian of early modern stuff) gave this summary of Hume on causation that's been bugging me. I wrote it down as best I could:

"Hume actually gives two definitions of cause in the Enquiry: cause as constant conjunction, where objects of one kind are regularly followed by objects of another kind, and cause as counterfactual dependence, where if the first object hadn't existed, the second never would have existed. He treats these as equivalent, basically two ways of packaging the same insight. As for the 'necessary connection' people imagine between cause and effect, Hume's point is that the necessity isn't out there in the world at all: it's a feeling in us, the habit of expecting the effect after seeing the pairing enough times, which we then project onto nature. And this is why the so-called New Hume interpretation, the recent reading where Hume actually believed in real causal powers in nature that we just can't cognize, is dead in the water: Hume explicitly denies that we have any idea of necessary connection, so he can't have believed such connections exist. The New Hume crowd are projecting modern scientific realism backwards onto a skeptical empiricist."

How fair is this as a summary? Go through the summary claim by claim: what's accurate, what's a defensible simplification for a podcast audience, and what would a New Hume interpreter push back on hardest?
