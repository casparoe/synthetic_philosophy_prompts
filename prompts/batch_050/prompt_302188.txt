So I've basically talked myself into a position on healthcare rationing and I want to stress-test it before I commit to it in a paper I'm writing. The position is: for triage and scarce-resource allocation (ventilators, organs, ICU beds, that kind of thing), QALY-maximization should be the default, but with a "fair innings" adjustment -- roughly, tiebreaks and close calls get tilted toward younger patients on the grounds that they've had less life, even when the raw QALY math slightly favors someone older. So not pure QALY counting, and not a pure youngest-first rule either, but QALYs with a fair-innings correction factor.

Tell me what would count against this. I want a map of where it's exposed, not a survey of the literature. Concretely:

1. Which real-world phenomena would be the most damaging evidence -- e.g., if it turned out that fair-innings-weighted allocation systematically produced worse outcomes for some group in ways the QALY framework can't even see (like disability-quality-of-life ratings, or the way QALYs bake in the discriminatory judgments of whoever answered the survey). Would the defender of my view have to concede that as a defeater, or can they legitimately say "that's a measurement problem, not a problem with the position"?

2. Which formal or quasi-formal results threaten it? I'm thinking of stuff like: aggregation principles in population ethics that you can't jointly satisfy, or the claim that any tiebreaker rule responsive to "who's had a fair innings" ends up collapsing into either pure utilitarian counting or a full-blown prioritarianism it was supposed to avoid. If the hybrid view is unstable and slides toward one pole under pressure, that's the kind of thing I want flagged.

3. Which intuition pumps actually bite? The rule of rescue and identifiable-victim cases are the obvious ones, but rank them: which ones would a committed defender of my hybrid view shrug off as predictable costs of any systematic policy, and which would genuinely keep them up at night? I suspect the organ-transplant-style cases (where the person in front of you is identifiable and the statistical beneficiaries aren't) do more damage than people admit.

4. Where is the view insulated by qualifications, and is that a problem? Every time someone objects, defenders seem to retreat to "well, in practice we'd use it as one input among several." At what point does that flexibility mean the position risks nothing and therefore says nothing?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether it's fatal to the fair-innings hybrid specifically or to QALY-based allocation generally -- because I can always drop the hybrid and retreat to the pure version if the correction factor is where the rot is.
