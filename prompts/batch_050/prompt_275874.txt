I'm a PhD student and, slightly to my own surprise, I've ended up running a four-session philosophy circle at my local library — mostly interested retirees and a couple of curious sixth-formers, no philosophy background, plus one ex-magistrate who is a bit too confident he already knows where he stands. I've decided one whole ninety-minute session goes on the Hart–Devlin debate, and I want help planning how to teach it, not a summary of the debate itself — I know the material, what I need is the pedagogy. So: plan the explanatory arc. What has to be in place before the debate even makes sense as a debate? I'm assuming Mill's harm principle has to come first so Hart's position is intelligible, but argue with me if you think leading with Devlin's worry is more dramatic. Do they need the disintegration thesis separately from Devlin's general moralism, and at what point does Hart's response actually bite rather than just sound like common sense?

For the case I want to build the session around, I'm deliberately avoiding the Wolfenden context, which will date the whole thing and derail us into 1950's sexual politics. My draft case: a village council votes to ban one resident's private hobby — harmless, consensual, done entirely indoors, but which most neighbours find stomach-turning (I'm leaning towards him taxidermying his own deceased pets and displaying them in his front room, but I'll take a better suggestion). Tell me what the case must have and must lack to carry the whole debate — it needs to isolate enforcing morality as such, not harm, not public offence, not paternalism — and be honest about whether my version does that or whether it smuggles offence in through the window displays.

Then the misconception map. The ones I definately expect: Devlin was just a prude with no real argument, so the debate is settled history; Hart thinks law and morality should have nothing to do with each other at all; and this is all secretly about religion. Tell me where in the sequence each of these will form and how to head it off at that exact point rather than after the fact. What's the one end-of-session question that shows they've grasped the genuine philosophical disagreement rather than just sided with the nice liberal? And what should I openly refuse to cover at this level (the moderate/extreme thesis distinction? subsequent developments?) and say so out loud. Finish with a table, columns: Misconception | Why they'll form it | Where in the sequence to pre-empt it | The move to make — then a single line giving an answer I'd accept as proof the idea landed.
