I am a novelist preparing a scene about artistic compromise, and I want it to turn on a genuine philosophical disagreement rather than a simple contrast between sincerity and selling out. Write a dialogue of roughly 1,000–1,300 words between Mara, a defender of a Tolstoyan account of art as sincere emotional transmission, and Ellis, a defender of an audience-centred account on which attentive adjustment to readers can itself be an artistic virtue. Both should be equally accomplished and philosophically alert.

Their exchange should arise from this case: a novelist, exhausted by a chapter about her brother’s death, replaces the ending she initially felt compelled to write with one devised after studying early readers’ reports. The revised ending moves readers deeply, but she no longer knows whether the feeling it expresses is her own; months later, however, she comes to inhabit it so fully that she cannot remember why she once resisted it.

Ground the argument in the following passage from chapter XV of Leo Tolstoy’s What Is Art?, in Aylmer Maude’s translation:

“As soon as the spectator, hearer, or reader feels that the artist is infected by his own production, and writes, sings, or plays for himself and not merely to act on others, this mental condition of the artist infects the receiver; and, contrariwise, as soon as the spectator, reader, or hearer feels that the author is not writing, singing, or playing for his own satisfaction—does not himself feel what he wishes to express—but is doing it for him, the receiver, a resistance immediately springs up, and the most individual and the newest feelings and the cleverest technique not only fail to produce any infection but actually repel.”

Have Mara and Ellis contest what the passage demands: whether writing for oneself concerns the artist’s motive, the source of the emotion, or the finished work’s effect; whether a reader’s sense of resistance is evidence of insincerity or itself constitutes the artistic defect; whether sincerity must be present during composition or can emerge retrospectively; and whether Tolstoy’s criterion can accommodate revision, genre convention, collaboration, or the wish to console a particular reader. Mara must explain why popularity and technical success are insufficient, while Ellis must answer the charge that audience-responsive craftsmanship merely manipulates feeling. Let them respond directly to one another’s examples, concede points where the argument requires it, and end without an artificial consensus. Begin with the dialogue itself, with no preface, title, commentary, or restatement of the task.
