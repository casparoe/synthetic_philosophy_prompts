I'm a novelist (three crime thrillers, one very patient agent) and I keep butting heads with my writing group over something half philosophical. Whenever we argue about what counts as "canon" in a draft, someone wheels out the line that the author is God of the fictional world: the characters are as real in there as we are out here, and whatever the author decrees about them is just true, end of discussion. This analogy gets used to settle everything — whether a character's offstage backstory "really happened" because the author said so in a Q&A, whether my beta reader is wrong to claim a twist contradicts chapter 3, even whether my detective's name picks out *someone* in a way that my made-up brand of fictional whiskey doesn't. I suspect the analogy is doing all the heavy lifting and I want it audited properly.

The thing that made me suspicious is a bit from chapter 1 of Arthur Conan Doyle's *A Study in Scarlet*, where Stamford gives Watson his very first sketch of Holmes:

"I believe he is well up in anatomy, and he is a first-class chemist; but, as far as I know, he has never taken out any systematic medical classes. His studies are very desultory and eccentric, but he has amassed a lot of out-of-the-way knowledge which would astonish his professors."

Notice how the supposed word of God arrives here: through a minor character, hedged to death with "as far as I know," spoken by someone who barely knows Holmes and could just be wrong. And it gets worse — a chapter later Watson draws up his famous inventory of Holmes's limits and records the man's knowledge of literature and philosophy as nil, and yet by the end of the same novel Holmes is quoting Goethe. So if Doyle is God, this God either contradicts himself or only ever speaks through unreliable messengers.

So here's the actual ask. Put on your philosopher-of-language hat — ideally a philosopher who moonlights as a fiction editor — and think carefully rather than pattern-matching to some canned take on fiction. Start by brainstorming, then give me an explicit correspondence table: if the author-as-God analogy held perfectly, what plays the role of creation ex nihilo, of omniscience, of revelation, of the raw material the world is made from? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the two conclusions from the ones that are merely cute. The conclusions, again: (1) that "Sherlock Holmes" refers in roughly the way "Baker Street" does, and (2) that authorial say-so settles every question of what is true in the fiction. Does either survive the audit? And specifically: is "Holmes has never taken out any systematic medical classes" true in the story, given that Stamford only offers it as far as he knows — does a hedged, possibly mistaken claim by a character become true-in-the-fiction just by being printed on the page, the way a divine pronouncement supposedly would? If the God analogy fails, what's the better analogy for what's going on with names and truth in fiction — float two or three candidates before committing. No citations needed, just careful thinking, maybe 800-1000 words.
