I run the small history museum in a mid-sized town, and about a dozen times a year my board has to decide what to do when what longtime residents remember and what the documents say come apart. Recent examples: families who insist their grandfather founded the mill when the incorporation papers name someone else; a beloved story that our town hid escaped enslaved people in a church basement that no contemporary record mentions, though the church and the era are right for it; and a plaque honoring a 1910s mayor as a benefactor, donated by his descendants, when the newspaper archive shows he profited from evicting tenants during a strike. The stakes are real in both directions: donors withdraw support and neighbors stop speaking to us when we contradict the memory, and school groups learn falsehoods when we defer to it. Decisions have to be made by me and a five-member volunteer board, none of us trained historians, usually within a few weeks and with no budget for outside researchers.

Please turn the best available thinking about collective memory, testimony, and historical evidence into a decision procedure we can actually run: the questions to ask in order (for instance, what the memory is doing for the community, what kind of evidence would settle the factual question and whether it exists, whether the memory could be true in a way the record would not capture, and what we owe to people the memory harms or erases), what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops with a clear verdict, and where it must hand the decision to the board as a judgment call because the question is one of values rather than facts. Say what the middle options are between flatly affirming and flatly denying a memory — labeling, dual presentation, contextualization, removal — and when each is appropriate. Then apply the procedure to the three cases above, and be honest where it yields an outcome I will find uncomfortable. Keep the whole response under 900 words.
