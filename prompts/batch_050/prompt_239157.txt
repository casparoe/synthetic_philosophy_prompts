I'm a PhD student working on collective wrongdoing, and my dissertation keeps running into a standoff between two camps in the literature on complicity. On one side are difference-making accounts: participating in a harmful venture is wrong only insofar as one's contribution makes, or could have made, a difference to the outcome — so the redundant conspirator, the non-pivotal voter for a terrible policy, and the single consumer of factory-farmed meat are at most guilty of bad character or risky recklessness, not of the harm itself. On the other side are participation-based accounts: what makes complicity wrong is the agent's intentional participation in a collective project, where accountability flows from what one wills as part of the group rather than from one's counterfactual impact.

I'd like you to draft the joint statement these two camps could produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign — for instance about overdetermination and redundant causation, about what individualistic act-consequence frameworks get right, and about which cases genuinely need explaining; (2) a table of residual disagreements, each formulated so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position rather than the other's gloss of it; (3) for each disagreement, the crux or cruxes — the sub-questions whose resolution would force at least one side to revise; and (4) for each crux, a test case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's verdict on that case recorded in advance. Use at least one case involving redundant contribution to a small group's harm, one involving a mass enterprise where no individual contribution is perceptible (consumption or voting), and one involving an agent who joins a wrongdoing project that would have failed without them but succeeds only because of luck. Where you judge that part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive — e.g., about what 'causing' or 'wronging' means — identify that explicitly and say what, if anything, survives once the verbal part is stripped away.
