Now that I am retired from the laboratory, I find myself spending my mornings on philosophy of action, and I have volunteered to help set the final examination for a graduate seminar on deliberation and rational choice. I would like you to draft one essay examination question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, on the problem of whether an agent can assign probabilities to her own not-yet-made choices -- the thesis, associated with Isaac Levi, that genuine deliberation excludes predicting what one will do.

The question should be built around the following scenario, which I have devised and which you may incorporate or refine as you see fit. A neuroscientist has constructed an instrument that, by monitoring her brain, predicts her binary choices with high accuracy roughly thirty seconds before she is consciously aware of deciding. Crucially, the prediction is recorded in a sealed envelope and is never shown to her before she acts, so it cannot feed back into her deliberation and destabilize itself. She now faces a choice between two grant proposals to submit, and she deliberates in the ordinary way while the machine hums in the background. The question I want examined is this: does the existence of such a sealed, non-interfering predictor show that the alleged impossibility of self-prediction during deliberation is merely a practical matter of feedback instability -- one could predict oneself if only the prediction were kept secret -- or does it show nothing of the sort, because the deep obstacle is not instability but the first-person deliberative stance itself, which requires treating one's own forthcoming action as open rather than as a fact to be estimated?

A top answer should be forced to do real work, not recite a taxonomy. In particular, the question and the model answer should require the candidate to (i) distinguish at least three different theses that are routinely run together in this debate -- that deliberation requires believing one's choice to be open, that no stable self-prediction is available while deliberating, and that credences in one's own acts are rationally impermissible tout court -- and to say which of these the sealed-envelope case actually bears upon; (ii) explain why an agent's own credence about her choice, if she formed one, would be epistemically peculiar in a way that a third party's credence about the same choice is not, even when both rest on identical evidence; (iii) consider whether a fully deliberating agent could coherently adopt a stance toward her own forthcoming act like the stance she takes toward a coin she has flipped but not yet looked at, and what breaks down in the analogy; (iv) address the case where deliberation is already complete but action has not yet begun, and ask whether the prohibition on self-prediction lapses at that point and what the answer reveals about which thesis is really at stake; and (v) take a position on the sealed-envelope case and defend it against the strongest reply from the other side.

Please write the question as a graduate candidate could answer it in seventy-five minutes, and supply a model answer of moderate length plus a marking scheme of exactly twenty marks distributed across five must-hit points, with a one-line note for each point describing the characteristic way weaker candidates mishandle it -- for instance, conflating the practical impossibility of stable self-prediction with a prohibition grounded in the nature of deliberation, or assuming without argument that whatever is true of third-person prediction must be true of first-person prediction.
