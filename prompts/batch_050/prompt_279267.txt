I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind trying to anticipate my dissertation supervisor's commitments before I defend a chapter that leans on a conceivability argument. Over the years she has given me consistent-sounding verdicts on the standard cases, but I cannot work out what rule she is actually running. Her verdicts: a square circle is inconceivable and impossible; water without H2O is conceivable but not possible; Hesperus being distinct from Phosphorus is conceivable but not possible; Socrates never having existed is conceivable and possible; an object being red all over and green all over at the same time is inconceivable and impossible; and on Goldbach's conjecture turning out false she withholds judgment entirely, saying that what looks like conceivability there is just ignorance of the proof, so nothing follows. My chapter needs a verdict on the case she has conspicuously never ruled on: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an ordinary human being who lacks consciousness entirely. Predict her verdict, and give your reasons. Then lay out the two or three most plausible general rules connecting conceivability to possibility that fit all six verdicts above, state what each rule predicts about the duplicate case, explain why you favor the rule you attribute to her, and name the single further case that would best discriminate among the surviving rules. I care less about which answer is right than about which answer is hers.
