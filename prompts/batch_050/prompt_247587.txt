Over the past year I've been arguing, on and off, with a friend who clerks for a state appellate court, about whether my instincts in statutory interpretation amount to a theory or just to moods. Her charge is that I read for purpose when I dislike what the text yields and for text when I like it — that I have no view about interpretation, only views about outcomes. I want to know whether she's right, so I've written down my honest verdicts on six cases below. A few of them resemble real disputes you may recognize, but please treat the statutes and facts exactly as I've stated them, since I've adjusted details freely. Here they are. (1) A city ordinance provides that no vehicles are permitted in the public park; an ambulance drives in to reach a jogger who has collapsed — I say the ordinance is not violated. (2) Under the same ordinance, a veterans' group rolls a decommissioned, harmless military jeep into the park and mounts it on a pedestal as a memorial — I say the ordinance is violated, even though the jeep endangers no one and makes no noise. (3) A federal statute enhances the sentence of anyone who uses a firearm during and in relation to a drug trafficking offense; a defendant trades his pistol to a dealer in exchange for narcotics — I say he used a firearm and the enhancement applies. (4) A statute criminalizes destroying, mutilating, or falsifying any record, document, or tangible object to impede a federal investigation; a commercial fisherman, tipped off that inspectors are coming, throws his undersized catch overboard — I say a fish is not a tangible object within the meaning of this statute and he is not guilty under it. (5) A probate code directs that a validly executed will passes the estate to the named heir; a grandson murders his grandfather, whose will names him — I say he does not inherit, whatever the code's plain words. (6) A nineteenth-century immigration statute forbids prepaying the passage of any foreigner coming to the country to perform labor or service of any kind; a church contracts with a minister from abroad and pays his way — I say the statute does not reach him. So: ambulance no, jeep yes, gun trade yes, fish no, murdering heir no, minister no.

Is there one coherent account of legal interpretation that delivers all six verdicts, or at least five of them? I'd like the candidate principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a new statute — not text governs unless that seems wrong, which is my friend's caricature of me. If more than one candidate works, give me the two most defensible and say where they diverge. I'm especially interested in the pair (1)–(2) and the pair (3)–(4): within each pair I treat two similar-looking provisions differently, and I'd like to know whether the distinguishing feature you're forced to identify is a feature an interpreter could know before deciding the case, or only afterward. Then tell me which of my six verdicts your favored account cannot accommodate, and whether you think that verdict or the account should go — I'd rather overturn an intuition than adopt a theory with an ad hoc clause for each case. Finally, test your account against two cases I haven't given you verdicts on: whether the ordinance is violated by an unmanned delivery drone that crosses the park's airspace at altitude without landing, and whether the sentencing enhancement applies to a defendant who pistol-whips an accomplice during a drug deal — used the gun, but only as a blunt object. If your principle gives a verdict on those two that I immediately balk at, that's useful information too, and I'd like to hear it.
