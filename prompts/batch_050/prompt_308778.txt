Exam question:

Negative utilitarianism holds, roughly, that morality requires us to minimize suffering rather than to maximize happiness or net welfare. (a) State the "asymmetry" intuition standardly offered in support of negative utilitarianism, and explain why Popper took the minimization of suffering to be a safer principle for public policy than the maximization of happiness. (b) Set out the strongest version of the objection that negative utilitarianism implies that it would be best to painlessly and instantaneously exterminate all sentient life. (c) Consider a lexical form of negative utilitarianism, on which no amount of happiness can compensate for or outweigh any suffering. Does moving to this lexical form enable the view to escape the extermination objection, or does it merely reproduce it? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:

(a) The asymmetry intuition is that suffering and happiness are not morally symmetrical: the badness of suffering is more morally urgent than the goodness of happiness, and our failure to relieve misery is a graver moral defect than our failure to create joy. Nearly everyone agrees there is a moral difference between torturing someone and merely declining to make someone happy, and negative utilitarianism takes this difference to be foundational rather than derivative. Popper argued that happiness should be pursued privately, in our personal lives, but that the state's proper aim is the reduction of concrete misery — poverty, disease, oppression — because suffering makes identifiable and urgent demands on us in a way that absent happiness does not, and because policies aimed at maximizing aggregate happiness have historically justified imposing a uniform vision of the good on dissenters. Popper therefore defended negative utilitarianism as the correct comprehensive moral theory, superior to classical utilitarianism in both private and public ethics.

(b) The objection, pressed by R. N. Smart in reply to Popper, runs as follows. Suppose negative utilitarianism is true and the sole aim is to minimize total suffering. Every sentient life contains some suffering, or at least carries a risk of it, and any future containing sentient beings contains suffering. A painless, instantaneous extermination of all sentient life would reduce the total amount of suffering in the universe to zero, which is the minimum. So negative utilitarianism ranks universal extinction above every alternative future, and indeed would require us to bring it about if we could. Since this conclusion is monstrous, the theory that entails it must be rejected. The objection's force depends on the extermination being genuinely painless and instant, so that no suffering is created in the process, and on the theory being purely aggregative about suffering.

(c) Moving to lexical negative utilitarianism allows the view to escape the objection. On the lexical version, suffering has absolute priority over happiness: no quantity of happiness can compensate for any suffering. Because the view no longer aggregates suffering into a total to be minimized, it is no longer committed to ranking outcomes by their aggregate suffering, and so it cannot favor universal extinction on the grounds that extinction reduces the total to zero. The lexical view instead treats each instance of suffering as an uncompensated bad, which means it demands that we relieve suffering wherever we find it, not that we eliminate the sufferers. The extermination objection therefore succeeds only against the crude aggregative version, not against the lexical version, which captures the asymmetry intuition without the monstrous conclusion.
