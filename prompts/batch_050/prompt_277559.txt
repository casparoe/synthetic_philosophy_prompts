So I've got a seminar paper brewing and I keep defending what I call the speech-act asymmetry thesis: lying is morally worse than misleading, even when both are aimed at deceiving the same person about the same thing and succeed equally. The standard defense is the one you know -- the liar stakes their credibility on the truth of what they say, the misleader just invites an inference, so the liar violates a stronger commitment. But I've added two props of my own that I haven't seen elsewhere, and I want them stress-tested. First, what I call the cleanup asymmetry: a liar can fully retract ("I said X, X is false, I knew it"), whereas someone who merely misled has nothing on record to retract, so in a weird way only the liar can undo their own wrongdoing -- which I take to show lying is the cleaner, more honest form of dishonesty, and hence less bad. Second, the effort test: misleading usually takes more deliberation and craft than bald lying, and premeditation generally aggravates wrongs, yet almost nobody intuitively thinks the crafty misleader is worse than the impulsive liar -- I claim this shows the wrongness of lying isn't really about deception at all but about something tied to the act of assertion itself.

Here's what I want from you: what would count *against* the speech-act asymmetry thesis, given those props? Not just the usual Saul/Adler-style cases -- I mean which intuitions, which possible scenarios, which empirical findings about how hearers actually process implicatures vs. literal claims would genuinely damage it. Would it matter, say, if it turned out people feel just as betrayed by misleading as by lying once the deception is discovered? Would it matter if my cleanup asymmetry actually cuts the other way on reflection (a misleader who can't retract can still come clean -- is that the same thing)? Tell me which defeaters a committed defender of the thesis would concede as real costs and which they'd shrug off as mere intuitions from the gallery, and rank the whole lot from fatal to merely awkward. Also be honest about whether my two props are actually load-bearing or whether the thesis survives their collapse -- if the view can absorb any counterexample by retreating to "well, assertion is just special," say so, and tell me whether that's a vice or just what a settled view looks like.

Oh and for context, the case that started all this was stupidly mundane: my flatmate told me "the trash got taken out" when he meant *he* took it out last week, knowing I'd think he took it out today. Technically true! Still felt slimy. If your answer can't make sense of why that felt slimy-but-not-quite-lying, something's gone wrong.
