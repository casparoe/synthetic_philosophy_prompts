So I work in our university's student conduct office (mid-size college, roughly 6,000 students) and I keep ending up in the same argument. Last month we had a student who clearly used an LLM to write most of a term paper, but the professor hadn't said anything about AI tools and our academic integrity policy didn't mention them until this semester's revision. Half the office wanted to charge him under a general dishonesty clause, half said you can't punish someone for breaking a rule that didn't exist yet. And it's not just the AI thing — we get this constantly now: a harassment case where the conduct predates the new expanded definition of harassment, a club treasurer disciplined under a spending rule passed after the spending happened. Our policy committee keeps proposing to just apply new rules to old conduct whenever the conduct was "obviously wrong anyway," and I can feel that this is philosophically load-bearing in a way nobody's examined, because this is basically the fight over Nuremberg and Tokyo in miniature — nullum crimen sine lege, whether "everyone knew this was wrong" is a substitute for an actual rule on the books, whether the Allies were enforcing law or just punishing losers, and whether "crimes against humanity" was a genuine legal category or a retroactive label invented to reach conduct the drafters hadn't prohibited. I've read enough rationalist blogs to be suspicious of any rule that is secretly just vibes wearing procedural clothes, and also suspicious of my own instinct that the AI student obviously deserves something, so I want this settled properly rather than by whoever argues loudest in the meeting.

Here's what I want: a decision procedure our office can actually run, in thirty minutes or less, on any case where the alleged misconduct predates the rule that now covers it. Act as a legal philosopher who has thought seriously about the retroactivity debates at Nuremberg and Tokyo and also has practical sense about how institutions work — think carefully, and sketch the key distinctions before committing to the final procedure. The procedure should be a sequence of questions we ask in order: what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in "you may not punish retroactively, full stop," where it terminates in "punishment is fine," and where it has to hand the case to a human because the remaining question is one of values rather than application. It should at minimum grapple with whether the conduct was wrong independently of any rule versus only wrong because of the rule, whether the person had fair warning from adjacent norms even without an explicit prohibition, whether the new rule genuinely crystallizes something the community already treated as settled versus creating an obligation out of thin air, and the Nuremberg-style worry about selective enforcement — are we only reaching back for people we already disliked. Then run the finished procedure on three cases: the LLM paper case above, a harassment complaint where the conduct happened before the definition was broadened and the respondent plausibly didn't realize the behavior fell under the new wording, and one case you construct yourself where the procedure spits out an answer I will find genuinely uncomfortable — a case where the conduct is clearly awful and the procedure still forbids punishment, or vice versa. Mark explicitly which distinctions had to survive the simplification and which you dropped, and say where this procedure will definetly misfire if applied mechanically.

One epistemic request: don't just hand me the procedure with false confidence. Tell me what would change your mind about the overall framework — what argument or consideration, if it landed, would make you scrap the retroactivity veto entirely, or alternatively make you strengthen it to an absolute bar — and point at the step in the procedure where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong. And one formatting request: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Continuous prose the whole way through, like a long email from a colleague who happens to know this literature cold.
