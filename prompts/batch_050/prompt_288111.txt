My two friends Maya and Dan got into it last week over a mutual friend, Sophie, who just moved her mom (advanced dementia) into a memory care facility after two years of caring for her at home — even though years ago she'd promised her mom, to her face, that she'd never put her in a home.

Maya's take: Sophie broke the relationship, not just the promise. Care isn't a task you can delegate, it's the relation itself — the ongoing attentiveness of one particular person to another. A paid aide, however kind, is doing a job, and you can't outsource being a daughter to strangers.

Dan pushed back: that conflates the labor with the relationship. What matters morally is whether the mom's needs actually get met, and by Sophie's own account they're met better there — her mom kept falling at home, and Sophie was exhausted and short-tempered by the end. Insisting the daughter personally do all the bathing and feeding is exactly how women get stuck doing unpaid caregiving forever, and it can quietly become more about the caregiver's self-image as a "good daughter" than about the good of the person being cared for.

Maya: but motive matters. Real care means actually taking the other person's perspective on as your own project, being engrossed in their situation. Paid staff do competent task-completion, and if you can't tell the difference between that and care you've flattened the whole concept into service delivery.

Dan: then no nurse, hospice worker, or preschool teacher ever genuinely cares about anyone, which is obviously false. And Sophie visits most days, knows all the staff by name, is the one who noticed her mom's meds seemed off. Noticing, anticipating, and staying on top of someone's needs is itself care work — arguably the most important kind at this stage.

Maya: fine, but he's dodging the promise. Sophie made a commitment while her mother could still understand it. You don't get to quietly drop a promise just because the person you made it to can't remember it anymore — that's exactly when keeping it is the real test of your integrity.

Dan: a promise is for the promisee. If keeping it now makes the mom worse off, then honoring the words while abandoning the point is performance, not fidelity. And honestly the person Sophie made that promise to doesn't quite exist anymore in the same way.
