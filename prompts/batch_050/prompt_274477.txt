A few weeks ago our city council voted to implement, without amendments, a citizen-initiated ballot measure that I regard as poorly drafted and likely to produce serious unintended consequences. When I argued that the council should at least delay implementation and repair its obvious flaws, a colleague ended the discussion by declaring that vox populi, vox dei: the people have spoken, and that settles the matter. What struck me afterward is that I have also heard the same phrase deployed to make almost the opposite point — as a warning that majorities can be confidently and catastrophically mistaken — so I suspect it is a label for several quite different claims rather than a single one, and I cannot tell which of them, if any, my colleague was entitled to.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan might be expressing, presented as a menu rather than a single interpretation? For each candidate reading, I would like you to (a) state the thesis exactly, making clear whether it is a claim about what is true, about who has authority to decide, about which procedures are fair, or about something else; (b) say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or implausible, and identify the background assumptions it requires (for instance, about voter information, the design of the question on the ballot, or the independence of individual errors); and (c) indicate whether it would actually support my colleague's use of the slogan — that is, whether it entails that a flawed measure passed by referendum ought to be implemented as written rather than revised, delayed, or filtered through representative institutions.

I half-sense several readings but cannot articulate them cleanly: an epistemic thesis that the majority of a large electorate is more likely to be right than any small group of experts or officials; a legitimacy thesis that majority support confers moral authority to decide, regardless of whether the majority is correct; a procedural thesis that counting everyone's preference equally is the only non-arbitrary way to settle contested questions; and a purely rhetorical use in which the phrase mostly signals that further argument is unwelcome. Please refine or correct these, add any important readings I have missed, and be explicit about which of them, if any, actually license treating a referendum result as beyond reconsideration.
