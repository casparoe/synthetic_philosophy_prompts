In a debate about whether anyone can ever legitimately come to own previously unowned land, the pro-ownership side keeps leaning on a baking analogy: you mixed your labour into the land the way a baker mixes labour into ingredients they own, so the improved land is yours the way the cake is -- your effort is literally in it, and taking it from you takes your effort. The other side fires back with the tomato juice move (Nozick, I think, from Anarchy State and Utopia?): if I own a can of tomato juice and pour it into the ocean, I don't come to own the ocean, I just lose my juice. So mixing something you own with something unowned can't magically transfer ownership of the unowned thing.

Audit both analogies as arguments, not just as pictures. What I want: (1) the mapping each one assumes -- labour to ingredients, unowned land to flour/sugar vs. to the ocean, the baker's prior ownership of ingredients to... what exactly, in the land case? (2) where each mapping breaks, and specifically which disanalogies matter for the appropriation question and which are just pedantic; (3) what each analogy quietly smuggles in -- e.g. does the cake picture presuppose that the ingredients were already rightfully someone's, and does the juice picture presuppose the ocean is worthless or infinite in a way land isn't? (4) whether either side's analogy even touches the "enough and as good left for others" proviso, or whether both just ignore it. End with a verdict: does the labour-mixing argument for original acquisition survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it limps, what would a better analogy be.
