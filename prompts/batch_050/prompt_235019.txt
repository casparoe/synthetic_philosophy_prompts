I'm a PhD student working on scalable oversight, and two of my labmates got into a long argument about whether AI safety via debate can survive obfuscated arguments. I want an outside referee. Rough reconstruction of the exchange:

(1) Maya's opening move: debate is fundementally broken because a dishonest debater can always construct an "obfuscated argument" -- one where every individual step looks locally fine to the judge, but the steps interact so that the conclusion is false. Her example: a 60-step argument that some policy is safe, where around step 12 the argument subtly equivocates on "human-approved" (sometimes meaning approved by the actual overseer, sometimes by a simulated one) and no single step is the place where the equivocation happens.

(2) Dan's reply: that's not a problem for debate specifically, because debate never asks the judge to evaluate whole arguments. The honest debater just recurses on the weakest link -- challenge the most suspicious step, then the weakest sub-step of that, until the liar has to either concede or defend an obviously false atomic claim. Obfuscation deepens the tree, it doesn't protect the lie.

(3) Maya's counter: the recursion doesn't bottom out at obviously false claims, it bottoms out at claims that are merely unverifiable for the judge -- "the logs show the agent behaved on all 10^6 training episodes" or "experts generally agree that..." The dishonest debater never has to defend obvious falsehoods; it just has to keep the argument at a depth where the judge's check is a coin flip. And since it chooses how to structure its own sub-arguments, it has a lot of control over where the recursion terminates. So the honest side's zoom-in strategy runs out of resolution before reaching anything decisive.

(4) Dan's last move: then the complaint isn't about debate, it's about the judge's capability floor. Every oversight scheme bottoms out somewhere; amplification and recursive reward modeling have the same floor, they just hit it with less fanfare. Debate at least gives the honest debater an incentive to find the shortest path down to it.

(5) Maya's rejoinder: no, debate is worse than amplification here, because amplification's operator has no adversary whose entire objective is keeping the argument above the floor. A boring honest process stays within the judge's competence by construction; debate adds an optimizer whose reward is maximal obfuscation. Same floor, but debate guarantees you fall through it.

Can you adjudicate move by move? For each of (1)-(5), say whether it's sound, where it overreaches, and who has the better of it at that step -- not just who wins overall. Also tell me what both of them are missing, e.g. whether anything hinges on empirical questions (can real models actually build obfuscated arguments that survive cross-examination? how competent are realistic judges?) rather than on the theoretical structure. And please clearly separate what's genuinely established, even just theoretically, from what's still contested among people working on this -- I don't want a summary that presents either the pessimist or optimist line as the settled consensus.
