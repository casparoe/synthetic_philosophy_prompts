I sit on the ethics board of a regional health trust. We are deciding whether to adopt an algorithm that scores patients' risk of missing scheduled dialysis, so that clinics can allocate a limited pool of reminder calls and transport vouchers to the highest-risk patients. The vendor's internal validation shows that the score is well calibrated within each of the two main demographic groups the trust serves — that is, a score of 0.4 means roughly a 40 per cent no-show rate in each group. However, the underlying no-show rates differ between the groups, and as a result the trust cannot simultaneously have calibration, equal false negative rates, and equal false positive rates across the groups: at least one must be sacrificed. The clinical operations lead argues that, since the score will be used to withhold help as much as to give it (patients scoring low receive no follow-up), the trust should prioritise equal false negative rates so that the two groups are equally likely to have genuinely needy patients missed. The vendor's representative argues that miscalibration would amount to systematically overstating or understating risk for one group, which she calls a deeper unfairness. Write a memorandum for the board recommending which criterion the trust should privilege, explaining in plain terms why the impossibility arises rather than merely asserting it, giving the strongest honest case for the option you reject, and addressing one point the vendor's representative has not raised: whether the fairness comparison should be assessed at the level of the score or at the level of the downstream decision rule the clinics will actually use. Please write in British English.
