I coordinate a small graduate reading group in philosophical logic, and next semester we've settled on Fitch's knowability paradox as our topic. We have eight sessions, and I'm responsible for proposing the syllabus of open problems we'll actually dig into rather than a survey of the whole literature. The standard map I know goes roughly: the Church-Fitch derivation from the knowability principle (every truth is knowable) to the collapse into every truth being known; then the main families of response — restricting the principle so it applies only to appropriately "Cartesian" or stable truths (Tennant-style proposals), reformulating it as something like "if p then possibly it is known that p is true" or other conditionalized variants (Edgington-style), moving to dynamic epistemic logic where the act of coming to know changes what's true (the van Benthem tradition), or simply conceding the result and revising one's anti-realism (the Dummett-shaped question of whether the paradox refutes semantic anti-realism or merely refines it). What I want from you is not this map, which I already have, but a defended prioritization: given eight sessions and students who are comfortable with modal and propositional logic but only lightly exposed to intuitionistic logic, which three open questions or lines of response deserve sessions, and in what order?

I'd like the ordering defended on explicit criteria — e.g., how many other debates in the area hinge on the question, how tractable it is for philosophers at our level with our background, and how much a session on it would position a student to write a term paper that could grow into a first publication rather than a rehearsal of existing moves. Concretely, here's my prior, which I'd like you to interrogate rather than ratify: I suspect the fine-grained restriction debate (which sentences count as genuinely knowable, whether "it is unknown that p" itself is stable, and so on) is over-represented relative to its payoff, because the restrictions seem increasingly ad hoc and the dialectic has a whack-a-mole quality; whereas the more foundational question — what the knowability principle would have to mean for it to be an expression of anti-realism rather than a trap anti-realists were lured into — gets less careful treatment than it deserves, especially the question of whether the principle should be read as a claim about our epistemic capacities, about the structure of truth, or about the relation between truth and justification, and whether the paradox bites differently under each reading. If you think my prior underrates the restriction literature, or that the dynamic-epistemic route dissolves rather than solves the problem and so deserves fewer sessions, say so and say why.

Please also indicate, for each of your three picks, what would change your ranking — for instance, what a student would have to find or fail to find in the literature to make the question more or less worth a session. Finally, be candid about your epistemic position: state how confident you are in each placement, and single out the single point in your prioritization where you are least sure you have it right, explaining what kind of consideration would most likely overturn it.
