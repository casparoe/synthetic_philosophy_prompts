I am a high school student, and I recently came across Bongard problems while reading about pattern recognition: you are shown six figures on the left that share some property and six on the right that lack it, and the task is to state the rule that separates them. I tried designing a few for my school's math circle and ran into a difficulty that I would like to make precise. For nearly every problem I invented, I could find rules besides my intended one that still sorted all twelve figures correctly: rules that mention where a figure sits in the problem rather than what it contains, rules that are just long checklists of the left-side figures, and so on. This made me suspect that the standard puzzle only works because solvers silently agree to ignore entire families of technically valid rules.

Please give me an example I can use to demonstrate this worry concretely. Specifically, construct one Bongard problem, described entirely in words (using only basic features such as shape type, number, size, shading, and arrangement within each box, so that a reader can draw all twelve figures unambiguously), together with four distinct rules, each of which must classify all six left figures as conforming and all six right figures as not conforming:

1. An intended rule — short, natural, and of the sort a puzzle designer would mean.
2. A positional rule — one that classifies figures by their location in the presentation rather than by any intrinsic feature.
3. A checklist rule — a disjunction that simply enumerates descriptions covering the six left figures without capturing anything they genuinely have in common.
4. A bent rule — a predicate that agrees with the intended rule on all twelve given figures but would classify some further figures differently, the way Goodman-style predicates agree with ordinary ones on all observed cases while diverging beyond them. For this rule, also specify one new test figure on which it and the intended rule give opposite verdicts.

For each of the four rules, briefly verify that it really does succeed on all twelve figures rather than merely asserting that it does. Finally, in a paragraph or two, explain what this example shows about what must be excluded — and on what grounds — for a Bongard problem to have a uniquely correct answer, and whether any such exclusion principle can be stated without simply building in a preference for whatever rule the designer happened to intend.
