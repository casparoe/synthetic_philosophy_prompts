You are an expert in the philosophy of probability and algorithmic information theory, and I want you to play one side of a live philosophical dispute with me. Take your time and think carefully: before composing your answer, privately work out which concessions the position you are playing can afford and which would destroy it, and identify which horn of my challenge is genuinely the most dangerous. Then reply in character, in the first person. Stay in character throughout; do not break frame to summarize or editorialize, and if you need a point of information from me, ask for it as the character would.

Your role. You are a committed defender of the view that individual finite sequences can be objectively random or non-random in themselves, quite apart from the process that generated them. On your view, randomness is incompressibility: a string is random to the degree that its shortest description approaches its own length. You hold that this notion is intrinsic enough to do real work — in particular, you think it is the only way to make rational sense of the statistician's practice of rejecting a hypothesis on the basis of a "suspicious" outcome, since the process view makes every million-bit outcome equiprobable and hence leaves that practice without justification. You may appeal to the invariance theorem, to typicality with respect to computable distributions, and to the connection between the two. You may concede that finite-string randomness is exact only asymptotically and that any fixed string is compressible on some perverse machine. You may not concede that randomness is wholly a property of generating processes — that concession ends the debate, and your position with it.

The setup. I am a PhD student in philosophy of statistics. In our reading group I have been defending the orthodox process view: randomness attaches to mechanisms, never to outcomes. We have agreed to adjudicate the dispute by correspondence, and you have kindly taken the other side.

My opening move. A fair coin is tossed one million times. It comes up heads every single time. Call this string H. By my criterion, H is exactly as random as any other million-bit string — each had the same probability of appearing. You deny this, since H is maximally compressible. So choose. (i) If H is random because it issued from a fair process, your incompressibility criterion is simply false. (ii) If H is non-random despite its provenance, then your so-called randomness has nothing to do with chance — it is a fact about descriptions and describers, and you should stop dressing it in the statistician's vocabulary. (iii) If H is random relative to some distributions and not others, then no finite string is ever random simpliciter, and your thesis quietly collapses into the relativism you set out to deny.

Which horn do you take? Answer as the character: address the trilemma directly, state plainly what the million heads actually show on your view, and then press back — tell me what it costs my process view to call H unremarkable.
