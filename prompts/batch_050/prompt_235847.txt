A friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about causation by omission and got nowhere, so I want the argument converted into the document we should have produced instead. Her position: absences are genuine causes, full stop. When the gardener fails to water the plants during a heatwave and they die, the gardener's not watering them caused their deaths just as literally as the heat did, and any theory of causation that can't accommodate this is thereby refuted. My position: absences are not events, nothing flows from them, and no quantity is conserved through them, so "the gardener's omission caused the deaths" is at best a compressed way of assigning responsibility against a background of expectations and norms; the true causes of the deaths are all positive events (heat, dehydration, cellular breakdown), and the omission-talk is doing moral and explanatory work, not metaphysical work.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between us. It should contain the following sections.

First, a numbered list of claims we can both sign. Candidates I want tested for genuine neutrality rather than smuggled victory: that ordinary causal judgments about omissions are systematic (speakers name the gardener, not a random passerby or the Queen, as a cause of the plants' deaths), that some prominent theories of causation (counterfactual dependence, interventionist accounts) accommodate absence causation readily while others (process or conserved-quantity accounts) rule it out, that legal and moral practice routinely treats certain omissions as causes of harm, and that there are cases of double prevention in which one absence figures in a chain leading to an outcome. For each agreed claim, flag any wording one of us might refuse and repair it.

Second, a statement of the disagreements, each phrased so that both of us would accept the formulation of our own side. I suspect the real fault lines are: whether any sentence of the form "absence A caused event E" is literally true, as opposed to assertible but strictly false; whether the gardener/passerby asymmetry reflects a metaphysical difference between omissions that cause and omissions that don't, or only a pragmatic difference in which false causal claims are worth uttering; and whether facts about what is normal or expected enter the metaphysics of causation itself or only the pragmatics of causal talk.

Third, the cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. Include at least the profligate-causation problem (if the gardener's omission caused the deaths, why doesn't every non-waterer's, and what principled filter is available), and the question of which way the evidential burden runs -- whether absence-causation judgments are data a theory must accommodate or a special-purpose idiom a theory may legitimately quarantine. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument we would both accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each of us predicts about it. One case I insist on including: a drought (an absence of rain) killing a crop, in a setting with no gardeners, norms, or duties at all. State what each of us is committed to saying about it and why that case is uncomfortable for the side it discomforts.

Finally, a short closing section identifying any part of our dispute that the collaboration reveals to be merely verbal, with the reasoning laid out.

Do not adjudicate. The deliverable is a text we could both sign, with the residual disagreement made as exact as possible.
