I teach a second-year philosophy seminar on free will and practical reason -- 18 students, one 75-minute session. I want an in-class game that makes them feel the force of the Buridan's ass puzzle from the inside. Not the donkey starving between two identical hay bales as a cute cartoon, but the actual pinch: you must choose, no reason favors either option, and refusing to choose is worse than either outcome.

Design something runnable: rules, materials, a points scheme that doesn't need real money, a timeline. My rough starting idea (improve it or replace it): repeated rounds where two options are engineered to be exactly equal in payoff, with a timeout penalty worse than either, so stalling is the only genuinely bad move. I'd also like a phase where they can pay a few points to flip a coin instead of choosing themselves, because I want to see whether they treat randomizing as a legit escape or as just shoving the arbitrariness one step back -- someone still has to decide to flip.

They should come away seeing that choosing without a reason is easy to do but weirdly hard to account for, and that indifference isn't the same as indecision. The debrief should map specific moments onto the philosophy: the timeout as the starvation, the arbitrary pick as the will acting without a determining reason, and the confabulated explanations they'll inevitably invent when you ask why they picked left. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I assume students will break the symmetry using seat side or card color, which is half the point, so tell me how to exploit that rather than prevent it. End with an honest note on what the game can't reproduce, presumably including the literal equality of the goods, since classroom options are never truely identical.
