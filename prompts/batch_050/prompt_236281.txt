I am a retired biologist — I spent most of my career working on colonial marine invertebrates, siphonophores especially — and over the past few years I have been reading my way into metaphysics. I have recently encountered the special composition question, and I would like help with a difficulty that keeps nagging at me.

As I understand the question, in van Inwagen's formulation: under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? His answer, after dismissing a series of candidate criteria — contact, fastening, cohesion, fusion — is that composition occurs only when the activity of the things in question constitutes a life. Hence there are simples and there are organisms, but strictly speaking there are no tables, no ships, and no stones.

First, I would like the dialectic explained rather than summarized. Why exactly does van Inwagen take each of the physical candidates to fail — what is wrong with contact or fusion as a criterion of composition, beyond the fact that they yield counterintuitive verdicts? And why does he suppose that life escapes whatever general defect sinks the others? I would also like to understand why he does not simply go nihilist or universalist, since both positions seem cleaner on their face.

Second, my real difficulty. In my old field, whether a thing counted as one organism was never a fact of nature we could simply read off. The Portuguese man o' war is genetically a single clone yet is built of specialized zooids that look for all the world like individual animals; lichens are two or more lineages propagating as a unit; cellular slime molds pass part of their lives as free-living amoebae and part as a coordinated slug in which some cells sacrifice themselves to form the stalk; and even the human case is not clean, given conjoined twins, pregnancy, and the microbes we carry. In my experience, biologists treat the boundaries of the individual as fixed by the question being asked, not as a joint in nature. So: does van Inwagen's answer merely relocate the question — when do things compose something becomes when is there a life — without gaining any sharpness? Can his view even state what it would be for two lives to share the same matter, as a lichen seems to? And if composition cannot be vague, as some metaphysicians argue, does the messiness of biological individuation do real damage to his answer, or can he simply borrow whatever notion of organism proves most defensible?

Third, the meta-question. Nothing in forty years of laboratory work ever required me to decide whether a colony was really one individual; it was always bookkeeping. What, then, is the strongest case that the special composition question is genuine rather than an artifact of ordinary language — and does the biological evidence push toward answering it, or toward dissolving it?

I am not after references to the literature; I would like the arguments laid out and assessed on their merits.
