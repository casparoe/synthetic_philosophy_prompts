I'm a retired pharmacologist (38 years in drug development, which is why this one stings a bit) and I've fallen in with a philosophy reading group at the library. Last month we did therapy versus enhancement, and afterwards I tried to write up what I thought was the standard worry as a clean argument. The problem is my version seems to prove that there is no difference at all between what I did for a living and a doping doctor, and I refuse to believe that, but I keep going back and fourth on where it breaks. Here it is:

(1) If the therapy/enhancement line is going to do moral work -- if "that's enhancement, not therapy" is supposed to be an objection to anything -- then health and normal functioning have to be definable without smuggling in the values the line is supposed to support, otherwise the whole thing is circular.

(2) The value-free candidates are statistical or evolutionary: normal is what the species typically does, or normal is functioning the way the trait was selected to function.

(3) Statistical normality can't be the standard. A trait can be statistically rare and good (immunity to a common disease) and statistically normal and bad (tooth decay before dentistry, presbyopia past fifty). Nobody thinks we should treat the immune person or refuse reading glasses.

(4) So the standard has to be evolutionary: healthy means functioning as selected.

(5) But selection optimizes reproductive fitness in ancestral environments, not welfare now. It gave us back pain, morning sickness, a sweet tooth that kills us surrounded by sugar, and aging itself. A doctor who genuinely aimed at restoring "functioning as selected" would have to abandon half of modern medicine and endorse some horrors besides.

(6) So medicine does not and should not aim at restoring normal function. What it actually aims at is improving welfare.

(7) But once welfare is the aim I can't find a principled stop. If fixing myopia is fine because clear sight improves welfare, so is improving on 20/20. If treating clinical depression is fine, so is brightening a healthy person's mood. If growth hormone for a deficient child is fine, so is growth hormone for a merely short one. Same drug, same mechanism, same welfare gain -- the only difference is which side of a statistical line the patient started on, and (3) already killed statistical lines.

(8) So the therapy/enhancement distinction has no moral force of its own. Whatever is wrong with doping or embryo selection or stimulants before exams has to be wrong for reasons that would apply equally to accepted medicine -- risk, unfairness, coercion -- and "but that's enhancement" is never by itself a reason.

(9) And that proves too much. It makes my career and the doping doctor's the same kind of enterprise, and worse, it seems to make refusing a safe welfare improvement just as culpable as refusing treatment, which is absurd.

I'd most like to keep the conclusion that curing pneumonia and juicing for a race are different kinds of act, but I'm willing to pay for it. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? And if the honest answer is that the argument is confused from the start rather than merely containing a bad step -- if I've set the whole thing up wrong in a way I can't see from inside -- say that plainly instead of politely picking a premise. At my age I don't need humoring, I need the diagnosis.
