After my grandmother’s funeral, our tenants’ association discovered that the municipal archive holds decades of building permits and police calls for our block, but almost nothing created by tenants themselves: no rent-strike leaflets, no childcare rota, no notes from the women who mediated with the landlord, no record of who was pushed out after the 1998 rehabilitation order. I have been made volunteer records coordinator for a small neighborhood memory project, and the absence has stopped feeling like a gap and started feeling like an instrument. We now receive boxes, phones, oral histories, and screenshots every week. Some are evidence for an ongoing rent-harassment case; some could endanger undocumented residents, accuse living people, or expose medical information; some are beloved and nearly contentless. We have no professional archivist, about ninety minutes per accession, a board that must sign off on public release, and a lawyer only for escalation.

Turn the archive-and-power insight that records are made, kept, described, withheld, and read under unequal authority into a procedure my two-person intake team can actually run. It should be an ordered intake-to-access pipeline with gates, not an essay: at each gate give the questions in sequence, the evidence that counts as sufficient to pass, the default when evidence is missing, the conditions that stop the process with a no, and the points where the procedure must hand the decision to the board, an affected-community reviewer, or counsel because the issue is values, law, or risk rather than technique. Use ordinary archival notions such as provenance, custody, appraisal, original order where it still helps, description, access restriction, deaccession/destruction, and the difference between an absence and a silence; translate them into checkable tests.

Please build in, and define operationally, these devices: a consent gradient from explicit present consent, to deceased-donor wishes, to inferred household consent, to communal or third-party content where consent cannot be obtained; a refusal file that preserves the fact that a donor declined without preserving the declined content; a silence-provenance note that classifies each gap as never created, destroyed, seized, withheld, misdescribed, digitized but unfindable, or present only in an institution whose description we do not control; a harm budget for each accession stating the maximum identifiability we will tolerate before redaction, embargo, or rejection; and description debt, meaning the amount of context an item will lose if we process it in ninety minutes. Say which distinctions must survive simplification because dropping them would recreate the very power problem we are trying to correct, and which may be collapsed for a volunteer workflow.

Include tests with names and pass/fail thresholds: an identifiability test for people who did not consent; a coerced-donation test for material given under eviction, immigration, employment, or family pressure; a third-party voice test for recordings dominated by someone other than the donor; a context-collapse test for screenshots and forwards; a revenge/evidentiary test for material that is valuable mainly because it accuses; a reversibility test asking whether today’s access decision can be undone if a community later objects; and a proportionality rule for redactions so we do not black out the same names the state already rendered hypervisible while protecting the powerful. For every test, mark what it cannot detect.

Then run the procedure on three accessions: a phone of encrypted voice notes from a rent strike in which residents name an organizer and discuss immigration status; a water-damaged 1970s ledger from a defunct women’s health collective listing initials, dates, payments, and pregnancy outcomes; and a spreadsheet of landlord entry logs offered by a former employee that would strongly support our harassment case but may have been copied without authorization. At least one application must produce an answer I will dislike, such as restricting a charismatic tape, rejecting evidence that would help tenants, or destroying an item whose symbolic value exceeds its informational right to exist. End with an audit format: what we report quarterly to the neighborhood, what remains unknowable, what would make us suspend the whole procedure, and where the pipeline will still quietly favor the articulate, the documented, and the already-legible no matter how carefully we run it.
