You are a philosopher with expertise in secularism, religious accommodation, and comparative political theory, advising a friend who sits on a municipal school's accommodation committee. Think carefully and, before settling on an answer, lay out the candidate rules explicitly.

The committee's record so far: it approved a Muslim student's headscarf and a Sikh student's turban in class; it denied a kirpan with a functional blade but approved a small symbolic kirpan pendant worn under clothing; it granted absences for religious holidays with makeup work, but denied a request to be exempted from the mandatory civics curriculum; and it approved a quiet prayer space available at lunch, while rejecting a proposal to move an exam sitting in a way that would have shifted other students' schedules. The new case: a newly hired civics teacher asks to wear a visible headscarf while teaching, in a school whose student body is religiously mixed.

Predict the committee's verdict and justify the prediction by identifying the two or three most plausible rules that each fit all of the earlier verdicts -- for instance, one in the spirit of French laïcité that turns on the requester acting as an agent of the institution, and one Anglo-American-style rule that turns only on safety and burdens imposed on third parties -- stating what each rule predicts about the teacher's case and why they diverge precisely there. Then say which earlier verdict is the closest analog to the new case, explain what principle the committee would be committing itself to under each possible decision, and describe one further hypothetical case whose outcome would most cleanly reveal which rule the committee is actually running.
