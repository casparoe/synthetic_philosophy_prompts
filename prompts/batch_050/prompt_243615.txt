I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on global justice and applied ethics, and I'm putting together this year's final exam. The students spent two weeks on sweatshop labor: we started from the puzzle that a transaction can be voluntary, mutually beneficial, and yet seem seriously wrong, and worked through the debate between those who think the non-worseness of sweatshop employment (workers choose it over worse alternatives like subsistence farming or scavenging) renders it permissible, and those (following work in the tradition of Wertheimer's account of exploitation) who think a transaction can be wrongfully exploitative precisely by distributing the surplus of a Pareto improvement unfairly. I'd like one essay question on this material, answerable in about 75 minutes, and I want your help drafting it along with the grading materials.

The question needs to be unanswerable by reciting a definition like "exploitation is taking unfair advantage of another's vulnerability." My best students can do much more than that, and the exam should separate them from the ones who memorized the taxonomy. Specifically, I want the question to force students to confront the real crux: how a transaction can leave both parties better off and still be morally defective, why the workers' consent does not by itself settle the permissibility question, and whether the wrongness of exploitation (if there is one) attaches to the transaction itself or to the unjust background conditions that make the exploitative offer attractive in the first place. Ideally it should also make them reckon with the uncomfortable dilemma facing interventionist responses: if sweatshop work really is the best available option for these workers, then successful boycotts or factory closures may harm the very people they aim to help, and a good answer has to say something about what follows from that for consumers and policymakers rather than dodging it. A question that lets students write "it's a difficult issue with arguments on both sides" and stop there has failed.

Please give me three things: (1) the exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, with any scenario or framing it needs; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words, written at the level of a strong student under time pressure rather than a published article; and (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points that a top answer must contain, with the marks each point is worth (totaling 20) and, for each point, a one-line note on the specific way students typically get it wrong or omit it. The marking scheme should make clear what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer in terms of concrete argumentative moves, not just depth or polish.
