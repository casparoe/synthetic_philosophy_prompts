In the battle-of-wits scene of The Princess Bride, Westley sets two goblets between himself and Vizzini, conceals the poison, and proposes that Vizzini choose which of them drinks from which cup. Vizzini answers with an extended speech of second-guessing: he considers where a clever man would put the poison, then where a clever man would expect an opponent to think a clever man would put it, each consideration defeating the last, until he distracts Westley, swaps the goblets, drinks, and dies — because Westley had poisoned both cups and had spent years making himself immune.

Read this scene through the epistemic game theory of iterated dominance. Begin by modeling the game Vizzini takes himself to be playing — symmetric, one hidden move, two relevant strategies per player — and ask which strategies survive iterated elimination, including elimination via dominance by mixed strategies, where a pure strategy may be discarded as inferior to a lottery even when no single pure strategy beats it. I expect the interesting result to be negative: the game as Vizzini conceives it has the structure of matching pennies, nothing in it is dominated, rationalizability excludes nothing, and his famous regress is the dramatic form of that negative result — iterated reasoning about the other’s reasoning cannot terminate, because there is no determinate recommendation for it to converge on. If the model comes out otherwise, say where I have misdescribed the game.

Then address the harder question. Does the scene vindicate a level-k or cognitive-hierarchy picture of practical reasoning, on which agents reason to some finite depth and then stop arbitrarily? Vizzini visibly climbs the hierarchy and then simply halts; does the film suggest any principled stopping point, or only that a decision must be made and depth alone cannot make it? Finally, take Westley’s trick seriously as a philosophical move rather than a cheat: he does not out-reason Vizzini within the game but rewrites the payoff structure beforehand, so that every terminal outcome favors him. What does this suggest about the relation between rational choice within a game and the prior, unmodeled choice of which game one will be playing? Is that a genuine limitation of iterated dominance as an account of rationality, or merely a reminder that dominance reasoning is always relative to a game form that can itself be gamed?

Where the reading strains, say so honestly — for instance, whether Vizzini’s speech is really an attempt at dominance reasoning at all, or something else, such as inference from a folk-psychological theory of his particular opponent, in which case the scene illustrates a different failure than the one my model attributes to it. The aim is criticism faithful in both directions: the film should end up illuminating why iterated elimination sometimes delivers no verdict and what agents actually do in that situation, not merely serve as a colorful illustration of a theorem.
