I chair the grants committee for a small private foundation that funds math and theoretical CS work. Our whole process runs on outside referees: each proposal goes to two people we've picked as genuine top experts in the exact subfield, they score it 1-10, and when the scores diverge we just average them and rank. That averaging rule is what the board has asked me to review, because of a case from this latest round.

Referee A gave one proposal a 9, calling it a real shot at settling a question the field has been stuck on for decades. Referee B gave it a 3, with a page of specifics arguing the central approach rests on a move the subfield tried and abandoned years ago. Both are names anyone in the area would put on the same short list. We did something unusual and, with their permission, showed each of them the other's report. A replied that B is demanding a standard of rigor nobody's work could survive. B replied that A is dazzled by the framing and never checked the load-bearing step. Neither budged. Full disclosure, zero convergence.

Here's my problem. The average, a 6, ranks it "borderline, decline." But neither expert thinks it's a 6, and my committee is generalist by design -- we can't referee the referees on the technical merits, that's the whole point of using them. Half my committee says when two equals clash like this the humble response is to split the difference, so averaging is fine. The other half says here the step either holds or it doesn't, and averaging just means we systematically reject exactly the risky proposals where experts are expected to clash.

Draft the memo I'll send before our June meeting: keep averaging, replace it (with what exactly?), or make this proposal a one-off exception. Whatever you recommend, state a rule a future chair could actually apply, and give the strongest version of the losing side, since at least two commitee members hold it and will quote the memo back at me.
