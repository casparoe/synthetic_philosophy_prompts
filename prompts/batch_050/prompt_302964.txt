Since retiring from my career as an experimental physicist, I have been auditing philosophy courses at the nearby university, and the convener of an upper-level philosophy of science seminar has kindly invited me to contribute a question to this year's final examination. I would like one essay question on the rivalry between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation, answerable in 60 minutes. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard definitions; in particular, students who merely restate the idea of an intervention as a surgical manipulation should not pass on that basis.

I would like the question built around a scenario that has bothered me since my laboratory days, and which I have not seen treated in the literature the students read: an isolated physical system governed by a strict conservation law — say, two colliding particles in an otherwise empty region, whose total momentum cannot change. Within such a closed system, no variable can be wiggled while the others are held fixed, since the conservation law ties them together and there is nothing outside the system from which an intervention could come. The question should press students on whether this shows that interventionism must either declare causation within closed systems ill-defined (an absurd result, given that physicists speak of causes there constantly) or retreat to merely hypothetical interventions (which seems to collapse the view back into a counterfactual theory, raising the question of what work the interventionist machinery was doing). It should also ask them to consider whether the counterfactual theorist genuinely fares better here, given that the relevant counterfactuals have antecedents that violate the very laws in question.

Please provide the question itself, a model answer of roughly 500 words written at the level of a strong student, and a marking scheme out of 20 organized around five must-hit points, stating what each point is worth and adding one line on the characteristic mistake weaker candidates make on each.
