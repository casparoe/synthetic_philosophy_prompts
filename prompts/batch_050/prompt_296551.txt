I'm a graduate student in political science putting together a comparative syllabus on national "lost cause" mythologies — cases where a military defeat or a doomed rebellion gets retrospectively transformed into a sacred story that anchors a group's identity. My working list of canonical cases already includes: the Lost Cause of the Confederacy and its retrospective purification of Southern motives; the Masada myth in twentieth-century Zionist memory, with the besieged holdouts' final stand recast as a template for national resolve; the Serbian Kosovo myth, in which a fourteenth-century defeat is preserved in epic tradition as a deliberate choice of a heavenly over an earthly kingdom; the British "Dunkirk spirit," where a humiliating evacuation is re-narrated almost immediately as a moral triumph; the Alamo as Texas's sacrificial blood-debt; the Jacobite rising of 1745 and the romantic cult of the doomed prince that followed Culloden; and the Easter Rising of 1916, whose leaders seem to have understood their military futility as the very mechanism of their political success. I also have a working taxonomy of the structural features these myths tend to share: defeat transmuted into moral victory, a blood-debt owed by the living to the fallen, the retroactive cleansing of the cause's actual aims, and the compression of a messy event into a single iconic scene or date.

Here's my problem. A colleague claims this taxonomy is really just one template with regional variations — that any lost-cause myth worth the name will exhibit some permutation of those features. I suspect she's wrong, and that there are national lost-cause myths with a genuinely different logic: for instance, one in which the defeat functions to discredit rather than sanctify the fallen, or one in which the myth is constructed and maintained by people who did not descend from the losers at all, or one in which the lost cause's descendants eventually won and then found their own founding myth an embarrassment they had to manage.

Give me one more case for my syllabus — a real national lost-cause mythology that is not on my list and not a variant of any item on it — and whose structure differs from my template in at least one deep respect, not merely in content or setting. For each shared feature it retains, say briefly how it manifests; for each it abandons or inverts, explain the mechanics of the difference and what political work the inversion does for the community that keeps the myth. Then tell me honestly whether you think my colleague is right after all — whether your case is a true counterexample to her one-template claim or whether, on reflection, it collapses back into the same structure wearing different clothes.
