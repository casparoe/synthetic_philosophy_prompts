I teach in a philosophy department and moderate our annual public interview series. This year's guest is a historian-philosopher defending a deliberately unfashionable reading of Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life: she argues that Nietzsche's three modes of history are not rivals to be balanced but a developmental sequence, such that a culture must pass through monumental history, then antiquarian piety, and only then earn the right to critical history -- and she adds the provocative twist that modern digital archives, by making total preservation effortless, have trapped us in a decadent antiquarianism that makes the critical mode psychologically unavailable. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press where this view is genuinely vulnerable: at least one question on whether Nietzsche's own text supports treating the three modes as stages rather than competing drives within a single culture, one on how her reading handles Nietzsche's claim that history must serve life rather than truth, one on whether her archival twist commits her to empirical predictions about historical consciousness she cannot test, and one on what the monumental mode could even look like in a culture that distrusts exemplars. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, and end with a question about what would count as evidence that her sequence gets the order backwards.
