A colleague of mine sits on a national advisory board that issues (non-binding) opinions on who's responsible when autonomous systems cause harm, and we've been arguing over beers about whether her verdicts actually hang together. I'd like you to settle it. Act as a professional philosopher who works on responsibility gaps and machine ethics -- think carefully, and please start by brainstorming the candidate principles her record might be tracking before you commit to a prediction.

Her verdicts so far:

1. An autonomous shuttle struck a pedestrian when an unusual combination of fog and reflective signage confused its sensors. The remote supervisor was monitoring 30 vehicles at once, exactly as her license allowed. Verdict: nobody is blameworthy. Genuine bad luck, a case where there's a harm and no fitting bearer of responsibility.

2. Same shuttle, same fog, but this time the supervisor was assigned to just that one vehicle, got an audible alert ten seconds before impact, and muted it to finish a phone call. Verdict: the supervisor is responsible.

3. A surgical robot had a rare failure mode that the manufacturer knew about and documented clearly in the technical materials. The hospital's procurement team read them, bought it anyway because it was cheaper, and the failure mode eventually caused a harm mid-operation while the surgeon watched helplessly. Verdict: the hospital bears responsibility, not the manufacturer.

4. Same robot, same failure mode, but the manufacturer knew about it and buried it deep in an appendix full of misleading reassurance, specifically to avoid scaring off buyers. The hospital did reasonable due diligence and found nothing. Verdict: the manufacturer is responsible, even though the hospital had nominally "accepted" the risk by purchasing.

New case, which the board hears next month: an autonomous delivery vehicle begins behaving erratically in a way that unfolds over about ninety seconds -- plenty of time for intervention. The local operator on duty has a big red stop button and full physical capacity to press it, but the erratic behavior results from a failure mode the manufacturer discovered in testing and never disclosed, and from the operator's (reasonable, trained-in) point of view the behavior looks like an unusual-but-safe rerouting. An uninformed operator could not have known to press the button; an informed one easily would have.

What does she say? More precisely: (a) lay out the two or three most plausible principles that fit verdicts 1-4, (b) say what each one predicts about the new case -- I think at least two of them diverge here, which is the point, (c) give your best guess at her actual verdict with reasons, and which earlier case is the closest analog, and (d) if her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say what principle she'd be adopting with each possible verdict, and describe a further case that would tell the remaining candidate principles apart.

Don't just tell me the answer you'd give -- I want her answer, or an honest admission that the track record doesn't force one.
