Hey, so I edit the science desk at a mid-size magazine, and I keep running into the same problem. Probably once or twice a month I get a pitch or a press release built around some evolutionary story -- "we evolved X because Y" -- and I have maybe 45 minutes to decide whether it's solid enough to assign, needs heavy hedging, or should be spiked. I'm not an evolutionary biologist, my staff writers aren't either, and I can't call up an expert for every pitch (budget reality, sadly).

You're an expert philosopher of biology who also understands editorial workflows. Think carefully and take your time -- I'd rather you brainstorm the failure modes first and then build the tool, rather than diving straight into a list.

Here's the context: I'm a big fan of the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique, the idea that lots of traits aren't adaptations at all but byproducts, accidents, drift, or leftovers of development, and that "this trait exists, so there must be a selection story for it" is a bad inference. But I also don't want to become the mirror-image crank who rejects every adaptationist explanation as a just-so story -- some adaptationist claims are extremely well-supported.

So: turn the spandrels critique into a procedure I can actually hand to a staff writer. I want something like a checklist or a sequence of tests, in order, that they run on any "trait T evolved because of function F" claim. For each step, tell me: what question to ask, what kind of evidence counts as a sufficient answer vs. a hand-wavy one, and where the step outputs a verdict (green light / needs hedging / spike) vs. where it has to punt to an expert or to an editor's judgment call. Some things I specifically want the procedure to be able to sort:

- genuine adaptation vs. byproduct/spandrel vs. exaptation vs. drift vs. developmental constraint (and please make sure the checklist doesn't assume these are easy to tell apart -- flag the ones that are structurally hard)
- the difference between "plausible story" and "evidence" (comparative data, heritability, current fitness effects, engineering-style reverse reasoning -- which of these actually carries weight and in what combination)
- claims about human psychology and behavior, where I suspect the bar needs to be higher but I don't want the procedure to just be "humans don't count"

Also be honest about where the procedure will misfire or where it can't eliminate judgment -- I'd rather know its failure modes up front.

Then run the procedure on three real-ish pitches: (1) "the human chin evolved as an adaptation to resist jaw stress from chewing," (2) "depression is an adaptation that evolved to make us withdraw and conserve energy after social defeat," and (3) "snake venom evolved to subdue prey." Ideally at least one of these should come out with a verdict I won't enjoy -- I have a soft spot for chin stories, so don't flatter me.
