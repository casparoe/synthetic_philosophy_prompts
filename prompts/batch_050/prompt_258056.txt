I run the human judging side of an evals program at an AI lab, where we test debate as a way of getting truthful answers out of strong models: two copies of a model argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge, cross-examine each other, and the hope is the honest debater wins because lies are harder to defend under questioning. I've got about 30 contract judges, smart generalists but not domain experts, each doing maybe 5 debates a day at ~40 minutes a debate, and the outcomes feed into wether we trust this setup for harder questions down the line.

My problem: we've started seeing debaters win with what I'd call obfuscated arguments — arguments where the flaw is real but structured to be hard to locate inside the format. A debater rests a conclusion on a chain of five sub-claims that each sound plausible and hides the dishonest step in the middle. Or they keep the whole debate at a level of abstraction where nothing ever bottoms out in anything checkable. Or they bury the load-bearing premise under a pile of true but irrelevant technical detail. The honest debater sometimes just... loses, because tracing the flaw takes longer than the format allows.

Please write me a judging procedure my contractors can actually run start to finish:

- the steps in order, from opening statements to the verdict, including what the judge should be writing down as they go
- rules for how far a judge should force the debate to recurse into sub-claims before declaring a branch unresolvable, and who pays for an unresolvable branch — the claimant, the challenger, or neither. I keep flip-flopping on this, since punishing complexity also punishes honest debaters whose true position is genuinely hard, but not punishing it basically rewards obfuscation
- how cross-examination time gets used, and what counts as dodging a question vs. actually answering it
- what a judge should do when they suspect an argument is obfuscated but can't find the flaw

Then run the procedure on two example debates you invent: one where the dishonest debater wins under naive judging and your procedure catches it, and one — be straight with me here — where the procedure convicts an honest debater whose position was just genuinely complicated, i.e. an outcome I won't like. If the procedure has a systematic blind spot, name it instead of pretending it doesn't.

Also flag which parts of this can't be turned into rules at all and have to stay judgment calls, and roughly how often you'd expect the procedure to misfire badly enough that we shouldn't trust the program's aggregate verdicts on their own.
