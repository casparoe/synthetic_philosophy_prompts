I'm a third-year student in an upper-level applied ethics seminar, and our current module is on the moral status of digital beings. Throughout the term, my professor has ruled on a series of cases, always briefly and decisively, and I have a paper due in which I must argue for a verdict on a new case from premises she would accept. Here are her verdicts as I recorded them:

1. Running down pedestrians in a driving simulation: morally indifferent. No one is wronged, and she resisted even the suggestion that it coarsens the player, absent evidence about effects on real behavior.
2. Griefing strangers in an online multiplayer game, deliberately ruining their evening for amusement: straightforwardly wrong, but the game is incidental; the victims are the other players, and the medium changes nothing.
3. Deliberately starving a virtual pet that plays distress animations when neglected: no wrong is done to the pet itself, though she said she would think less of someone who savored the animations.
4. Torturing a scripted non-player character whose every response comes from a fixed dialogue tree: entirely neutral, not even vicious in itself, though surrounding context could make it so.
5. A hypothetical character implementing a detailed emulation of a mammalian pain system, modeled closely on the relevant neuroscience: tormenting it would wrong the character itself, not merely reflect badly on the tormentor.

The case my paper must address: a commercially released game whose wildlife is driven by a compact recurrent architecture rather than scripts. Each creature maintains a world-model, tracks its own bodily integrity, and is trained by aversive reinforcement signals that durably reshape its goals; individuals learn to fear and flee particular players, and this conditioning persists across sessions. Nothing in the architecture is neuron-like, and the developers make no claim that anything phenomenal is going on. A player discovers that if he repeatedly traps the same individual creature, its avoidance conditioning eventually collapses into passivity — it stops fleeing, stops feeding, and huddles in a corner of the map. He finds this entertaining and does it to several individuals, week after week.

My questions: First, reconstruct the rules my professor might be running such that all five of her verdicts come out right — I can see at least two candidate principles (one on which functional organization of the right kind suffices for moral standing, whatever the substrate, and one on which something more biological or phenomenal is required), plus the virtue-focused rule that her fifth verdict seems to exclude. Second, predict her verdict on the trapped-creature case under each rule, and say which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog. Third, give your best guess at what she would actually say, with reasons. Fourth, if her record genuinely underdetermines the case, state precisely which further case or stipulation would settle which rule she holds — and what principle she'd be committing herself to with each possible verdict on mine.
