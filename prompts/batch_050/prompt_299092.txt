Since retiring from a forty-year career in population ecology, I have been working through the classics of moral philosophy with the same stubborn thoroughness I once applied to field data, and I have reached the final chapter of Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics. What arrests me is his dualism of practical reason: the claim that two axioms each strike us as self-evident — that one rationally ought to pursue one's own good on the whole, and that the good of any one person is, from the point of view of the universe, no more important than the equal good of any other — yet these axioms can issue conflicting commands whenever duty and self-interest diverge, and no purely ethical argument appears able to reconcile them. Sidgwick called this, in effect, the deepest problem in ethics, and after decades of thinking about competing selection pressures I find the structure of the impasse curiously familiar and very hard to shake.

Here is my request. I have been cataloguing the standard responses to the dualism, and I want one more. Already on my list, so please do not offer any of these or a mere rewording of them: (1) the theological postulate, Sidgwick's own suggestion that only a divine governance of the universe could guarantee the coincidence of duty and interest; (2) the rejection of rational egoism, denying that the prudential axiom is genuinely self-evident rather than a bias of partiality masquerading as reason; (3) the rejection of the impartial axiom, so that only prudence survives; (4) the constitutional harmony claim, associated with Bishop Butler's picture of human nature, that conscience and self-love in fact converge for creatures constituted as we are; (5) the debunking strategy, which seeks an evolutionary or psychological explanation of egoistic motivation that undermines its rational credentials while leaving impartial benevolence standing; and (6) the stoic acceptance of the dualism as a real, tragic fragmentation of practical reason that we simply must live with. What I want from you is a genuinely seventh response — not a hybrid of the above and not one of them in new vocabulary — and then, just as importantly, your honest verdict on whether it succeeds. By succeeds, I mean it must take seriously why Sidgwick found both axioms compelling in the first place; a dissolution that merely flatters one horn of the dilemma while pretending the other was never plausible will strike me as the philosophical equivalent of discarding the inconvenient half of a dataset.
