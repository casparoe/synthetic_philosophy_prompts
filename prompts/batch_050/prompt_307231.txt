So I'm putting together a unit for an undergrad philosophy of action course on collective intentionality -- we've done the core Bratman on shared intention (meshing subplans, mutual responsiveness, the commitment to the joint activity out of each participant's individual intention), a bit of Gilbert on joint commitment and the standing to rebuke, and touched Searle's irreducible we-intentions mainly as a foil. As a reward at the end of the unit I want to screen Reservoir Dogs and have them write on it, and I'd like you to basically do the assignment first so I can steal the good bits: read the film through the collective intentionality literature.

Here's roughly what I'm hoping you'll dig into. The whole setup is interesting: Joe assembles six strangers, forbids them from sharing real names or personal details, and assigns color aliases. Does a crew that deliberately knows nothing about each other count as sharing an intention in Bratman's sense, or does the anonymity actually block the mutual responsiveness and interlocking subplans he requires -- or does the film suggest the division of labor plus a common plan is enough? Then the heist goes wrong off-screen and the "shared cooperative activity" dissolves in real time at the warehouse, which seems like a great test case for what each theory says about breakdown: when does a joint action stop being joint, and who's still bound by what? And then the rat. Gilbert's view gives participants standing to rebuke defectors, and the crew's sense of betrayal -- not just anger at the outcome but a kind of *you owed us* -- feels like it dramatizes her point almost too neatly, except that Orange was arguably never a genuine participant at all, just simulating one, so does the rebuke-standing even apply, and what does Bratman say about pretend-participation? Also, if you want a bonus scene, the opening diner argument about tipping -- Mr. Pink refusing to go along -- is a neat little case of someone declining to join what Gilbert would call a joint commitment constituting a convention, though I might be overreading it.

Two requests about how to write it. First, be faithful in both directions: don't flatten the film into an illustration of Bratman, and don't stretch the theories to fit; if a scene resists the framework (I suspect the Mr. White / Mr. Orange dyad might -- that bond looks nothing like the planning cases in the literature), say so. Second, please be upfront about what's basically settled in this literature versus what's still genuinely contested -- e.g., whether shared intention reduces to interlocking individual attitudes plus common knowledge, or whether Gilbert's obligation/standing-to-rebuke claim is an empirical-looking claim people actually dispute -- because my students will take whatever you write as the received view and I'd rather not have them confidently repeating one side of a live fight as if it were textbook consensus.
