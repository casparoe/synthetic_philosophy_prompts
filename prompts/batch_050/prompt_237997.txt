A friend of mine in my game theory reading group came up with this argument about the centipede game and none of us can break it, but the conclusion feels insane. For context: centipede game, two players alternate, at each node you can take the bigger pile (game ends, other player gets the smaller pile) or pass (both piles grow, other player moves), say 50 rounds. His argument:

(1) At the last node, any rational player takes, since taking beats passing no matter what.
(2) If both players are rational and that's common knowledge, then the player at the second-to-last node knows the other would take at the last node, so they take. Same reasoning unrolls all the way back: the first player takes at node one.
(3) So among rational agents with common knowledge of rationality, the centipede ends immediately, every time.
(4) So if you're rational, you should adopt the policy "always take at the first opportunity" -- anything else is just failing to follow the logic.
(5) But consider an agent who's disposed to pass a few times. Against anyone even slightly cooperative, that agent walks away with far more than the node-one payout. A rational agent choosing what dispositions to have should obviously choose to be that agent.
(6) Therefore rationality requires making yourself the kind of agent who won't do what rationality dictates. To be rational you have to be irrationnal on purpose.

Now (6) is clearly nuts, or at least it sounds nuts. But every step looks locally fine to us. I pressed him on (1) and he said the last node is the one case where there's literally no future to consider so you can't wiggle out. I pressed him on (5) and he said you can deny that rational agents get to choose their dispositions, but then all of decision theory under binding commitments goes out the window.

Where does this actually go wrong? Is there a specific step where something gets smuggled in, or is the argument valid and one of the premises has to go? Also, relatedly: if I find myself at node 40 of a 50-node centipede, what am I even supposed to believe about my opponent at that point, given that by (2) neither of us should be there? It feels like the argument eats its own foundations at that point but I can't say exactly how. If forced I'd rather keep (5) than (2), but I want to know what giving up (2) actually costs me.
