I am a PhD student in political theory preparing an essay on Rousseau’s claim that theatre does not reliably improve morals: by organizing pleasure around applause, social recognition, and the imitation of passions, it can strengthen amour-propre and teach spectators to prefer appearing virtuous over being so. Read Molière’s The Misanthrope through this account. Rousseau treated the play as especially revealing because Alceste’s demanding sincerity becomes comic, while Philinte’s flexible sociability appears reasonable; on that reading, the theatre punishes inconvenient virtue and rewards skillful adjustment to public opinion.

Assess whether this interpretation is fair to Molière. Discuss the sonnet dispute with Oronte, Alceste’s lawsuit and refusal of flattery, Célimène’s management of her salon, the exposure of her letters, and the closing contrast between Alceste’s withdrawal and Philinte’s accommodation. In each case, distinguish sincerity from vanity, honesty from self-display, and social tact from moral corruption. Does Molière simply dramatize Rousseau’s distinction between authentic virtue and theatrical appearances, or does he complicate it by showing that Alceste’s purity is itself a role performed for an audience?

Please also consider whether the comic form undermines or reinforces Rousseau’s critique: if spectators laugh at every character, does the play avoid turning vice into charm, or does laughter merely provide another occasion for social judgment? I would like a reading that is faithful to both thinkers, identifies where The Misanthrope resists a straightforward Rousseauian interpretation, and concludes by explaining what the play contributes to the philosophy of theatre that Rousseau’s general argument cannot capture.
