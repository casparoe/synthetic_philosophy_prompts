So I retired from a chemistry department three years ago and have since fallen in with a local philosophy reading group, about 12 to 15 of us, mostly other retirees plus two or three grad students who slum it with us. We meet fortnightly for 90 minutes and I volunteered to run the next session on the frame problem in AI, which came up because half the group thinks ChatGPT is basically intelligent and the other half thinks it's a parlor trick, and I suspect the frame problem is a wedge into that argument. What I want is not a lecture -- I want to design an activity or game for the session that makes people *feel* the frame problem before anyone names it: that strange difficulty of saying what stays the same when you do something, the way a robot stacking blocks apparently has to reason about whether the block's color changed, whether the floor is still there, whether gravity still holds, and so on ad nauseam, even though no human ever thinks about any of that.

Give me something runnable: rules, roles or materials, and a structure where participants actually run into the problem themselves -- for instance, I had a vague idea of one person playing a robot who must follow instructions with pedantic literalness while others issue commands, and the group slowly realizes the robot keeps "breaking" because nobody specified all the things that should stay fixed, but I'm sure you can do better than that sketch, and if a different mechanic captures it better, use yours. The debrief matters as much as the game: tell me how to connect what just happened in the room to the actual philosophical issue (including the version people raise about relevance and common sense, not just the logicist version from the old planning literature), and please be honest about where the analogy between our little game and actual AI systems is going to creak, since the grad students will pounce on it.

Two more things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with a group of amateurs -- people being too clever, the game collapsing into giggles, someone who already knows the literature spoiling it -- and how to handle each. Second, I'd like you to be frank about your own reasoning: if the activity rests on a contested reading of what the frame problem even is, flag it, and tell me what observation during the session -- some way participants react, some thing they say -- would indicate that the activity is teaching the wrong lesson and that you steered me into a bad design. I'd rather know where it's likely to fail than be told it's foolproof.
