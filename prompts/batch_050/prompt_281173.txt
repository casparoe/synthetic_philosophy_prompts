I'm drafting a novel and last weekend I had two friends over for dinner -- one teaches econ, one does philosophy of some kind -- and they got into a long fight about this "risk-weighted expected utility" idea I sort of want to steal for a chapter. Before I put it in a character's mouth I need to know who was actually right. Roughly how it went, from memory:

(1) Mara said risk aversion doesn't need a new theory at all. People just value outcomes non-linearly -- getting out of debt matters way more than a fancier car -- so plain expected utility with a curvy utility function already explains everything. A risk-weighted theory is the same psychology in fancier math.

(2) Dev said no, that misses something real: two agents can value every outcome identically and still disagree about whether a gamble is worth taking, because one of them just gives more decision-weight to the chances of ending up on the bad end of things. Risk-weighted EU captures that attitude directly by reweighting probabilities according to how outcomes rank, and it can even reproduce Allais-style choices with a perfectly straight-line utility function, which standard theory can't.

(3) Mara said okay but here's the catch: since the weighting depends on how outcomes rank against each other inside a gamble, tweaking one outcome slightly -- say improving the best case a little so the ordering shifts -- can change how every other probability gets weighted, so the theory predicts people flip-flopping between near-identical choices in ways she'd call inconsistent, not "rational pessimism."

(4) Dev said that's the whole point, not a bug: how bad your worst case is *relative to your best case* genuinely matters to whether a risk makes sense, and standard theory only avoids this because it sneaks in a separability assumption that evaluates each outcome in isolation, which is its own unargued commitment.

Can you rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and tell me who's more right overall? Also: what are both of them missing? My hunch is neither one actually explained what could make a risk-weighting function *rational* as opposed to just a temperament.
