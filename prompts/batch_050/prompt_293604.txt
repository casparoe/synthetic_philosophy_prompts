At a recent town council meeting about relocating a statue of our city's founder — a man who endowed the local hospital but also held a colonial administrative post he used in ways we now regard as indefensible — a councillor ended the debate by declaring that removing the statue would "erase history," and the motion failed. The slogan plainly did work in that room, and I want to know what work. What precise claims could it be making? I can imagine readings ranging from the platitudinous (removing a monument does not undo past events or prevent anyone from learning about them) to the empirical (public memory genuinely depends on physical monuments, so that what lacks a statue fades from collective awareness) to the semantic (a statue is itself a piece of history, an artifact of the era that erected it, so removal destroys evidence rather than merely withdrawing honor) to the normative (whatever statues do, taking them down is objectionable in the way destroying archives would be). Please lay out the distinct theses the slogan might express as a list; for each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; and identify which readings would actually support the conclusion that the statue should stay. It would also help to know which reading the councillor most likely intended, and whether that reading is among the defensible ones.
