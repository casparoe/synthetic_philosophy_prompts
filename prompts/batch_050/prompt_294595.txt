I coordinate a weekend math circle for advanced high-school students, most of them sixteen or seventeen, comfortable with basic probability but with no measure theory and no exposure to Bayesian statistics beyond one session I ran last month. That session is why I'm writing. I had them do a simple inference exercise, and two students assigned what they each called a uniform prior to the same unknown: one spread probability evenly over the side length of a square, the other evenly over its area. Their posteriors disagreed, both dig in, and I fumbled the resolution -- I mumbled something about needing to specify the problem more carefully and moved on, which satisfied nobody, least of all me. I've decided to devote our next ninety-minute meeting to Bertrand's paradox proper: the random-chord setups, the underlying point that indifference over a continuous space is not one principle but a family of them depending on how the space is parameterized, and what this means for the principle of indifference as a way of expressing ignorance. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the paradox itself.

On the order of ideas: tell me what has to be in place before the chord problem will land as a paradox rather than a curiosity. My instinct is that they first need to internalize that random is a property of a procedure, not of an outcome, and that probability requires choosing what the equally likely cases are -- but I'm unsure whether that should come before or after they see the three chord constructions give 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4, since showing the conflict first might motivate the distinction better. Advise me on the dependency structure and defend it. On the carrying example: I don't want to build the session on the classic chord if you think something else does the work better for this audience; whatever example you recommend, explain what it must have (at least three plausible uniform procedures giving genuinely different answers, a physical construction vivid enough to simulate) and what it must lack (I suspect anything where one parameterization feels obviously natural will let them dismiss the others as mistakes, which is exactly the escape hatch I want to close).

I also want the misconception map. Here are the ones I anticipate: that the paradox is a puzzle with a single correct answer the mathematicians eventually found; that the moral is merely to define your sampling procedure carefully, after which indifference works fine; that this is a quirk of geometry with no bearing on the priors they assigned last month; and, if I mention the transformation-invariance treatment, that the paradox has been dissolved and they can stop worrying. For each, tell me where in the sequence to head it off and what question or mini-exercise does the heading-off. Then give me the check for understanding I'd run in the last fifteen minutes: I want something that distinguishes a student who has grasped that the trouble is the word uniform itself from a student who has merely memorized that chords are tricky -- perhaps asking them to construct a fourth parameterization and predict its answer, or to state the conditions under which an indifference-based prior is defensible, but tell me which and why. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and say so on the record: I'm inclined to gesture at transformation groups and non-informative priors as things that exist, without teaching them, and I'd like your view on whether that gesture helps or just reopens the door to the it's-been-solved misconception.
