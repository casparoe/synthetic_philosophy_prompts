After a forty-year career in population genetics, I retired two years ago and have since been reading philosophy with the zeal of a late convert. For the past several months my subject has been the evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics, and I find the state of that debate genuinely puzzling. As I understand it, one side, following the dilemma popularized by Sharon Street, holds that since natural selection shaped our evaluative tendencies toward reproductive fitness rather than toward moral truth, the realist who posits stance-independent moral facts must either accept that our moral beliefs bear no reliable relation to those facts or else embrace an astonishing coincidence between what promotes survival and what is actually valuable. The other side replies, in several distinct ways, that nothing of consequence follows: that evolutionary influence on a faculty does not by itself discredit its outputs, on pain of discrediting perception and arithmetic as well; that the debunker's premises already smuggle in substantive evaluative judgments and so cannot put all evaluation in question from some imagined standpoint outside it; and that some third factor could align fitness with value without any cosmic fluke. What strikes me, as someone trained in a field where disputes bend under new data, is that the participants appear to agree on all the relevant biology, have been exchanging substantially the same objections and replies for roughly two decades, and treat one another's central moves as question-begging.

My working hypothesis, formed by the laboratory habit of asking what measurement would settle a dispute, is that this disagreement is not empirical at all but normative-epistemological: the sides hold different views about what conditions a belief-forming process must satisfy for its outputs to remain justified once the genealogy of the process is laid bare, and perhaps about who bears the burden of proof once that genealogy is on the table. But I can see rival readings. The dispute might be metaphysics in disguise, turning on whether there are any stance-independent moral truths for selection to miss. It might be partly verbal, with the sides using the word undermine in different senses. Or the camps might be answering different questions under one heading, one concerned with the epistemic standing of our moral beliefs, the other with the metaphysics of value. What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of positions: classify the disagreement, defend your classification against the rival readings by appeal to what each side actually argues, and state what would count as progress under your diagnosis, whether a conceptual distinction, a formal or empirical result, or an agreed-upon test, or else argue that no progress should be expected and explain why.

Two requests about the answer itself. If my setup rests on a confusion, for instance if the debate has not in fact stalled, if I have caricatured one of the positions, or if asking what kind of disagreement this is happens to be the wrong tool for this particular case, say so plainly rather than politely answering the question as posed. And please write the reply in American English, in no more than 600 words.
