Here's a practice I find weirder the more I think about it. In international law, states can refuse entry to basically anyone — except their own citizens. Every country is obliged to take back it's own nationals when another state wants to deport them, and this is one of the few nearly exceptionless duties states owe each other. Meanwhile citizenship itself is almost never chosen: you get it by blood or by being born on the right patch of soil, and plenty of people hold one they never asked for and can't easily shed. So the system seems to treat every human as somebody's responsibility by default, assigned at birth, non-refusable. What does that presuppose about whether compatriots have special claims on each other? Does the duty to readmit rest on an associative picture, where a state is bound to its members roughly the way a family is, or is it just a coordination device — someone has to take each person, and citizenship is the cheapest sorting rule available — which a thoroughgoing cosmopolitan could accept without blushing? And if it's really the second, does anything about the actual practice (like the fact that we find statelessness horrifying even when the stateless person is safe, housed, and fed) suggest we believe something stronger than the coordination story admits?
