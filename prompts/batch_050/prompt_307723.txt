I TA an intro philosophy class and next week I get one fifty-minute section on Frankfurt's On Bullshit — the lying vs bullshit distinction. Don't explain the distinction to me, plan how I should explain it.

Where I'm stuck is the carrying example. Every student in the room has used ChatGPT, so it feels like the obvious pick: confident made-up citations, zero visible concern for whether any of its true, etc. But I suspect the example is a trap, for two reasons. First, whether a chatbot lies, bullshits, or just errs is exactly the sort of question the distinction is supposed to equip you to answer, so opening with it presupposes the analysis I'm trying to teach. Second, I worry it teaches them that bullshit is a property of how a text reads — fluent, unbothered, plausible-sounding — when Frankfurt's whole point is that its a property of the speaker's stance toward the truth, and a chatbot is the hardest possible case for even locating a stance. Am I right to worry, or am I overthinking it? If I'm right, where does ChatGPT belong in the session (the end? not at all?), and what should the carrying example be instead — it needs to be a case where the lying/bullshitting difference is visible without first having to settle whether the speaker even has a mind.

So: plan the arc. What has to be in place before the lying/bullshit line even makes sense (my guess: that these categories classify the speaker's relation to truth, not the truth-value of what's said — which means a bullshitter can accidentally speak the truth, and I need that to land early or nothing else works?). Which misconceptions they'll walk in with — I'm expecting bullshit = nonsense, bullshit = a lie you don't feel bad about, the bullshitter must know the truth, bullshit is always false, and 'Frankfurt says bullshit is worse than lying so he thinks liars are basically fine' — and exactly where in the sequence I should kill each one, since some of them presumably die on their own if I order things right. Then one end-of-section check that tells me they got the distinction and not just the vibe — I thought about handing them a fabricated-citation case twice, once with a person and once with an LLM, and asking them to classify both, but maybe that just re-imports the ChatGPT trap at the finish line. And tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level and say so — the bit about bullshit being a greater enemy of truth than lies? the humbug business?
