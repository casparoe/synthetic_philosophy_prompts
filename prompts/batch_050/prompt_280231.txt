So I retired from chemistry a few years back and have been falling down the philosophy rabbit hole ever since, as you do. Here's my situation: I somehow ended up on the commitee of our local allotment association, and people keep justifying proposals by calling them "win-win" — nobody loses, everyone gains, just vote yes. From my reading I gather economists have a stricter notion, a Pareto improvement, where literally no affected person is worse off than before, and that real win-wins are much rarer than advertised. And then I came across a further worry that even an apparent Pareto improvement can be *unsafe* in a specific sense: since we never really know other people's preferences, a change everyone consents to can still leave someone worse off once it turns out they cared about something different from what we assumed. The example that stuck with me was two people who agree to settle a dispute by coin toss — each might be fine with the toss as a fair procedure, or each might only care about winning the thing itself, and you often can't tell which until after the coin lands.

I want you to turn all this into something my committee can actually use. Write a decision procedure we can run in a single meeting on any proposal that's sold to us as a win-win: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer to each, where the procedure just stops with "not a safe win-win, settle it by ordinary voting like everything else," and which steps are unavoidably judgement calls dressed up as procedure. It has to work for people with zero economics background, so if you use a technical term, define it the way you'd explain it over a garden fence. At minimum I'd like the procedure to cover: how to list who is actually affected, including the members who never show up to meetings; how to pin down what "worse off than before" means when the status quo is itself disputed or shifting; how to stress-test the proposal against plausible alternative guesses about what members really care about (the coin-toss worry above); and how the whole test differs from the weaker one where the winners' gains could in principle compensate the losers (Kaldor-something? I can never remember).

Then run the finished procedure on three realistic allotment proposals you invent yourself. I want at least one that passes the ordinary win-win smell test but fails your procedure, and at least one involving a lottery or coin flip between rival claimants, since that's the case that got me thinking about this. Keep each run-through short enough that we could genuinely get through it in ninety minutes. And be honest at the end about what the procedure can't settle — I spent forty years around measurement error, I can take it.
