So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and next month I get to interview Douglas Hofstadter about machine translation -- a topic he's been banging on about since long before the current AI hype. His 2018 Atlantic essay "The Shallowness of Google Translate" is our agreed jumping-off point, and there's one passage in it I keep circling back to:

"To my mind, translation is an incredibly subtle art that draws constantly on one's many years of life experience, and on one's creative imagination."

I want 8 interview questions that actually turn on this claim, not the usual "will AI take translators' jobs" fluff that every outlet runs. Some specific things I want covered:

- At least one question pressing him on what work "drawing on life experience" is doing here. A neural net has ingested more text than any human could read in a thousand lifetimes -- is his point that lived, embodied experience is categorically different from training on text, and how would he defend that without it sounding like nostalgia from someone who spent a year translating Pushkin? I need a follow-up for when he (almost certainly) says the machine has never stubbed a toe or lost a friend and so has nothing to translate *about*.
- One question on the story he opens the essay with -- his friend Frank and the Danish woman emailing each other via Google Translate even though both speak both languages fine. Doesn't that show the machines are *good enough* for what most people want, and that his bar of "understanding" is a philosopher's luxury? Follow-up keyed to his likely reply that good-enough output and understanding are different things entirely.
- One question on falsifiability: what, concretely, would a machine have to do to convince him it understood a sentence? If he says no string of output could ever show that, is his position an empirical claim about technology or a definitional stipulation about the word "understand"? I'd love a follow-up for each fork.
- One question separating his argument from Searle's Chinese Room, since my editors will definately lump them together in the headline if I don't nail the distinction myself.
- One question on his aside in the essay about "deep learning" being verbal spinmeistery -- whether the branding of AI does alot of the persuasive work that the tech itself can't.
- And one question about whether anything since 2018 -- the chatbots, the fluent LLM prose -- has moved him even an inch, with a follow-up ready for the almost-certain "no, it's the same shallowness with better PR."

Every question should come with a follow-up matched to the answer he's most likely to give, since he'll be charming and evasive and I won't have time to think on my feet. No softballs about his career or "what books are on your nightstand" -- my editor cuts those anyway.
