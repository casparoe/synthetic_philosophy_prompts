got into a long argument with a friend over drinks last week about whether Hume's economics is actually good or just historically important, and we got stuck on the balance-of-trade stuff. Her position (steelmanning a bit) is that the famous specie-flow mechanism is basically a tautology wearing a costume -- of course money can't permanently drain out of a country with flexible prices, that's just what "flexible prices" means -- and that Hume gets credit for a causal theory when he really just had an accounting identity plus vibes. I kept coming back to this passage from "Of the Balance of Trade" (in the Political Discourses) that I think is doing more work than she allows: "This seems to me, almost in every case, a groundless apprehension; and I should as soon dread, that all our springs and rivers should be exhausted, as that money should abandon a kingdom where there are people and industry. Let us carefully preserve these latter advantages; and we need never be apprehensive of losing the former."

So here's what I actually want, because I don't trust myself to just relitigate this from memory: give me the desiderata for a theory of money and trade that would be good enough to adjudicate this dispute. What are the phenomena such a theory has to be able to explain (short-run stickiness, Cantillon-style distribution effects, why money ever feels like wealth at the individual level if it isn't at the national level, what actually happens under debasement vs. under trade surpluses), what are the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse (nominal vs real balances, stock of money vs flow of income, money as lubricant vs money as asset, and the one my friend keeps hammering: identity vs causal mechanism), and which empirical facts from Hume's time or ours does it have to not contradict. For each desideratum, give me the case or observation that motivates it, so I can tell which are genuine requirements and which are just mercantilist prejudices my friend has overcorrected against -- or, equally, Hume-fan prejudices I've absorbed from too many "Hume anticipated Friedman" blog posts.

Then the part I care most about: is "the theory must vindicate the springs-and-rivers claim in the passage above" a legitimate desideratum, or does the passage smuggle in an assumption (people and industry held fixed? price flexibility? no paper credit?) such that a theory could explain everything else and still show Hume's confidence here was misplaced? Tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating contenders -- mercantilism, naive quantity theory, whatever the modern heir of each is -- and flag any pair of desiderata on the list that look jointly unsatisfiable, since that would itself be the interesting result. A table for the requirements is fine; keep the scoring of actual theories brief since mostly I want the spec. Oh and if you think my friend's tautology objection is just wrong about how the mechanism works, say so directly, I can take it.
