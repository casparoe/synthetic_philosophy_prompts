I'm taking a behavioral econ course and we just did the unit on loss aversion and the endowment effect. The standard story is the mug experiments: people demand way more to give up a mug they were just handed than they'd ever pay to aquire one, and this WTA/WTP gap gets presented as a bias, a deviation from what rational choice would predict. But our professor pushed back and said there's a serious camp that denies it's a bias at all -- on their view, owning something genuinely changes what it's worth to you, reference points are part of your real preferences rather than distortions of them, and calling people irrational here is just economists insisting everyone's utility function should look like the textbook's. Both camps predict the same behavior in all the classic experiments, which is exactly what makes the debate feel slippery to me.

So here's what I want: construct a thought experiment in which the two readings come apart -- meaning a case where loss-aversion-as-bias and loss-aversion-as-legitimate-preference give different verdicts about what a fully informed, reflective person would choose, or about what a well-meaning advisor (or policymaker) should recommend. Constraints: it has to be an ordinary-life situation, no lab mug trades and no framing gimmicks where the same option gets described two ways; the stakes should be ones a normal person could plausibly face; and both readings must agree on the classic experimental results, so your case has to isolate whatever actually seperates them rather than re-running Kahneman with new props. One paragraph for the scenario is enough.

After the scenario, walk me through the verdict each side gives and why it gives it, then tell me which specific feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the discrimination if that feature is removed. Finally, give the strongest counterattack available to whichever side comes out looking worse -- i.e., how they'd argue your case doesn't actually discriminate between the readings after all. The point of the exercise is to figure out whether there's any real decision where it matters which interpretation you hold, so a case that just stipulates "and this preference is/isn't genuine" instead of generating the verdicts from the two views fails.
