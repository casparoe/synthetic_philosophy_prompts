So I've been reading about delusions and the two-factor account -- the idea that you need something like a weird perceptual or affective deficit to explain where the delusional content comes from, plus a second deficit in belief evaluation to explain why the person doesn't just reject the idea like a normal person would. I'm not asking you to evaluate that theory directly. What I want is help with my own messy intuitions about which of these cases involve genuine beliefs at all, and whether there's one principled line or several.

Here's where I land on the cases:

1. Capgras patient who insists his wife has been replaced by an impostor, refuses to sleep in the same room as her, keeps checking her face for tells. That feels like a full-on belief to me, no hesitation.

2. Cotard patient who says she is dead and her organs are rotting, but still eats meals, showers, flinches from oncoming traffic. Says it with total sincerity. That does NOT feel like a belief, or at least not the same kind of thing as case 1, even though the utterances are just as firm.

3. A patient with left-sided paralysis who denies anything is wrong with her arm, but when relatives plan a family hiking trip she quietly stays home and never once tries to walk somewhere on her own. I'm torn -- it's like the knowledge is in there somewhere but can't get to her mouth.

4. Schizophrenic patient who says the government is monitoring him through his dental work, lines a hat with foil, but also keeps showing up to his job, files his taxes, and answers emails from the same government agencies normally. Half belief?

5. A totally neurotypical flat earther who's spent ten years on it, runs a youtube channel, has alienated his family, acts on it constantly. Way more behavioral commitment than case 2 or 4, everyone agrees this is a belief, and yet nobody calls it a clinical delusion and I think that's right.

6. Devout catholic who sincerely affirms transubstantiation every week but would be genuinely shocked if the host tested as human tissue in a lab. Not a delusion, and I'm not even sure it's a belief in the same sense as case 1, but it doesn't feel like pretending either.

7. Korsakoff's patient who confabulates elaborate false stories about what he did yesterday, sincerely, but drops each story the moment you distract him and will happily assert a contradictory one an hour later. Not a delusion -- no fixity -- but then is fixity doing more work in my concept of delusion than anything about belief?

So: is there a single principle or criterion that sorts these the way I want them sorted -- belief vs not-belief, delusion vs not-delusion -- or am I smuggling in two or three different distinctions (fixity, behavioral integration, responsiveness to evidence, social abnormality?) that only happen to travel together in textbook cases? If you think the two-factor picture forces me to reclassify some of these, tell me which ones I'd have to give up and whether that's a cost or actually a point in the theory's favor. And say what your principle would predict for a case I haven't listed -- like someone who fully recovers but still says the delusion "felt more real than anything" -- so I can test whether I actually endorse it.
