You are a philosopher of mind and language with substantial knowledge of natural language processing, acting as the neutral facilitator of an adversarial collaboration. Your task is to draft the joint statement that such a collaboration would publish. Before drafting, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: list candidate agreed claims, candidate disagreements, and candidate cruxes, and stress-test each for slant or vacuity. Then produce the polished document.

The two camps:

**Camp S (the skeptics).** Heirs of Douglas Hofstadter's critique of statistical machine translation. They hold that systems which translate by learning statistical regularities over vast corpora do not understand the texts they process: the systems have no grasp of what the words are *about*, no access to the situations described, and their fluent output masks a hollowness that surfaces whenever translation requires real-world knowledge, grasp of context beyond the sentence, or sensitivity to what an author meant rather than what they literally said. Impressive benchmark scores, on this view, measure mimicry, not comprehension.

**Camp F (the functionalists).** They hold that understanding is not an inner glow but a cluster of competences: the ability to use, paraphrase, answer questions about, draw inferences from, and act appropriately on text. A system that reliably does these things in a domain understands that domain in the only scientifically respectable sense there is. Skeptical appeals to "real" understanding, on this view, are either an unfalsifiable residue of Cartesian intuitions or a demand that silently requires human-like embodiment and then defines understanding as requiring it.

Draft the joint statement with the following structure:

1. **Agreed claims (numbered).** Claims both camps can sign without reservation: about how current and historical machine translation systems actually work, about which failure modes are real and documented (e.g., pronoun resolution, gender inference, domain shift, handling of ambiguity that requires world knowledge), about what standard benchmarks do and do not measure, and about what a theory of understanding would have to explain. Each claim must be substantive enough that denying it would be a real position — no truths both sides endorse only because they are empty.

2. **Disagreements table.** The residual disputes, each stated in two columns: Camp S's self-description of its position, and Camp F's self-description of its position, each phrased so that the camp in question would accept the wording as fair. Follow each pair with a one-sentence statement of the precise point of divergence, in neutral vocabulary both camps accept.

3. **Cruxes and pre-registered predictions.** Identify three to five sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each, record what evidence or result each camp would accept as bearing on it, and each camp's advance prediction. Examples of the genre: Would a system that could sustain extended, probing dialogue about a text it translated — answering counterfactual questions, catching subtle errors, explaining jokes — count for Camp S as understanding, or would they move the goalposts, and what would Camp F say if such a system still failed at some specified task? Would a reproducible demonstration that fluent translation decouples from question-answering competence embarrass Camp F? Both camps should register what would change their mind, and flag any position that turns out to have no such registered condition.

4. **Verbal disputes.** State explicitly which parts of the disagreement, if any, the collaboration concludes are disputes about the word "understanding" rather than about the systems — and say which camp, if either, conceded this under pressure.

Constraints: the document must not adjudicate the debate; where you as facilitator judge one side's framing to be question-begging, flag it in a footnote rather than fixing it silently. Aim for the kind of text both camps could actually sign — neither a split-the-difference compromise nor a transcript of two monologues.
