Next month I'm presenting a paper at a departmental colloquium, and I want a full Q&A prep sheet. The paper defends a partly revisionary reading of Schopenhauer's pessimism. My thesis: the famous pessimistic argument is usually taught as an empirical generalization about life (mostly suffering, little pleasure, ends in death) and is then easily refuted with happiness research. I argue that this is a misreading. The real argument is structural: for Schopenhauer, willing is constitutively a state of lack, satisfaction is merely the cessation of a striving and therefore has only negative value, and boredom is not an occasional mood but the phenomenological signature of a will with no object. On this reading, the pessimism follows from an account of the structure of desire itself, not from a tally of pleasures and pains -- and the empirical literature on hedonic adaptation, far from refuting him, is downstream confirmation of a thesis he established independently of any data. I concede that this requires softening the stronger metaphysical claims: I read the will not as the thing-in-itself in Kant's strict sense but as Schopenhauer's name for a phenomenologically accessible feature of agency, and I argue this bracketing is licensed by tensions in his own text rather than being my invention.

The audience, precisely: a general philosophy colloquium at a research department. Present will be (i) three or four analytic philosophers of well-being, split between hedonists and desire-satisfaction theorists, who will come armed with cases of apparently positive pleasure (savoring a meal, absorption in play or craft) and with the objection that "negative" satisfaction is just satisfaction by another name; (ii) a historian of nineteenth-century German philosophy who knows The World as Will and Representation and the Parerga intimately and will press hard on whether my bracketing of the thing-in-itself is exegetically honest or a hostage rescue; (iii) a cross-appointed psychologist who works on hedonic adaptation and set-point theory and will be skeptical that a 1818 metaphysician anticipated her field rather than merely trafficking in a gloomy temperament; and (iv) general faculty who will reach for the familiar moves (if satisfaction is only negative, why does anyone prefer anything to anything? Isn't suicide the logical upshot, and isn't Schopenhauer's own answer to that evasive?).

What I need from you: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they'd be to the talk's reception, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it -- the well-being theorist's counterexample should sound like a well-being theorist's counterexample, not like a freshman's. For each: what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a reply deliverable in under two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Flag separately any objection where conceding would cost me the thesis rather than just a premise. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me how I'd answer it if I had to. Finally, note whether my "structural, not empirical" framing is itself doing more work than I admit -- for instance, whether the move from "satisfaction is the cessation of a lack" to "satisfaction has only negative value" smuggles in a normative premise I haven't defended, and whether the adaptation literature really is neutral between my reading and the hedonist's.

And plainly: if the reading I've sketched is so revisionary that it can't honestly be presented as Schopenhauer's rather than a Schopenhauer-inspired view of my own, or if the prep task as posed rests on a confusion, tell me that directly rather than building me a comfortable briefing.
