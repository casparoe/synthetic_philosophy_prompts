I used to be a research chemist, retired about three years ago, and since then I've been going along to an informal philosophy reading group at the local library. We've just done a couple of sessions on Bernard Williams and his objections to impartial moral theories, and last week two of the group, Margaret and Colin, had a proper row about the famous drowning-rescue case -- the one where Williams says a husband who rescues his wife rather than a stranger shouldn't need any justifying thought about impartiality at all, that the bare thought that it's his wife is the only thought that should cross his mind, and anything more is one thought too many.

I'm reporting this as neutrally as I can because I'd like a referee, but their exchange went roughly like this:

1. Margaret said the case is decisive against utilitarianism. If a theory tells you that before diving in you should check that saving your wife really is the happiness-maximising option, that theory has failed, because having that check in your head means you don't love her in the way a husband should. Love is incompatible with keeping a moral ledger running in the background.

2. Colin replied that this conflates two things utilitarians have always distinguished: the criterion for what makes an action right and the decision procedure you should actually use in the moment. A sensible utilitarian never claimed anyone should calculate in an emergency; they claim the world goes better if people are disposed to save their spouses without hesitation. So the case doesn't touch the theory itself, only a straw man of it.

3. Margaret said the criterion/decision-procedure split is too convenient. A moral theory that can only survive by telling its adherents not to think about it at the crucial moment is unstable or self-effacing, and Williams's deeper point was about integrity -- that your deepest commitments are constitutive of who you are, and a theory that asks you to stand ready to override them on calculation isn't one a person can actually live without becoming alienated from themselves.

4. Colin conceded the point has some bite but pushed back on two fronts. First, integrity is an inchoate notion -- even Williams admitted he didn't have a clear account of it -- so it's shaky ground to build a decisive objection on. Second, consider a firefighter: we'd want a firefighter's professional motives to be properly impartial in a rescue, so the mere fact that a motive is 'impersonal' can't be what makes the husband's imagined deliberation objectionable. What's doing the real work is something about marital love specifically, and it's not obvious moral theory has to answer for that.

5. Margaret's last move was to say the firefighter case actually helps her side: the fact that we want impersonal motives from firefighters but would be horrified by them in a husband shows exactly that good moral thought is role- and relationship-sensitive in a way impartial theories can't capture without the contortions Colin has just performed.

Colin let it go at that, but I could tell he wasn't persuaded.

Could you adjudicate this properly, move by move? For each of the five moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and give your reasoning -- in particular, I'd like to know whether the criterion/decision-procedure reply really is the dodge Margaret says it is, and whether the firefighter comparison damages her case as much as Colin thinks. Then give an overall verdict on who came out ahead, and flag anything important about the Williams debate that neither of them raised. Since I know this is contested territory, please also tell me how confident you are in each of your judgements, and single out the one point in your adjudication where you feel least sure of yourself and why.
