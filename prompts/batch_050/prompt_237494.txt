I want to use the Silicon Valley Bank collapse in March 2023 as a test case for information cascade theory, and I want you to push it hard in both directions. Quick recap of the facts as I have them (flag it if I've got something materially wrong): SVB's depositors were heavily concentrated in tech startups and VC funds, well over 90% of deposits sat above the insurance cap, the bank announced a big loss on a bond sale plus a planned capital raise on a Wednesday, and by end of Thursday depositors had requested over $40 billion in withdrawals, coordinated largely through Twitter and founder group chats, before regulators shut it down Friday morning.

The classic cascade story (Bikhchandani-style) assumes people act sequentially and observe each other's choices, so once a few early movers run, later depositors rationally ignore their own private signals and follow. Here's my problem: the SVB run was nearly simultaneous. Nobody waited to see a queue; the queue was the decision. So first, is "information cascade" even the right label here, or is this really a coordination game with strategic complementarities, where everyone runs because everyone else will run regardless of what they privately believe about the bank's health? Give me the precise distinction between those two mechanisms and say where SVB falls, or whether it somehow straddles both.

Then do the full mapping: who are the players, what are the actions, what are the private signals, what counts as an observed action, and what does cascade theory predict about how this should unfold. Two predictions I especially want stress-tested: (a) cascades supposedly rest on thin information and can be shattered by a small public shock — did the Sunday night deposit guarantee and the Fed's backstop work that way, and does the fact that regional bank stocks kept twitching for weeks afterwards confirm the fragility prediction or expose its limits? (b) the models assume people's signals are independent draws, but SVB's depositors were all in the same VC networks reading the same tweets — what does massively correlated information do to cascade logic? Does correlation make cascades more likely, more accurate, just faster, or is that a question the standard models can't even formulate?

Finally, run it the other way: what does the episode show that cascade theory never anticipated? I'm thinking of things like the run happening in hours because the "actions" were digital wire transfers, so there was no observable sequence at all, just a flood; or the fact that the people spreading the alarming news were also the ones with the most to lose, so signal and panic had the same source. Don't force the theory to speak where it's silent — if some feature of the episode just isn't a cascade phenomenon, say so plainly and tell me what kind of model you'd need instead.
