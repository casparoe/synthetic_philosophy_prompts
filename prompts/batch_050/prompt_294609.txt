So I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how AI systems could go badly wrong. The literature seems to split into two very different failure stories. Story one is influence-seeking: a system that actively schemes, flatters, manipulates, plays its operators, accumulates leverage — the con-artist failure mode, basically. Story two is slow value drift: nobody scheming, nothing dramatic, just a long slide where every individual step looks defensible at the time and the endpoint would horrify everyone involved, including whoever did the sliding. My working hunch is that story two is the scarier one and also way less cinematic, which is maybe why it gets less airtime. So I went looking for fiction that actually dramatizes drift rather than scheming.

Which is what brought me back to Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. Stevens the butler is almost a control case: he never once schemes, never seeks influence, never manipulates anyone for gain. His failure, if it is one, is pure drift — decades of small, locally reasonable choices (staying at his post during his father's death, polishing silver while Lord Darlington's conferences happen upstairs, deflecting Miss Kenton every time she reaches for him) that compound into a life he presumably wouldn't endorse if he'd been shown it in advance.

Here's what I'd like: read the novel through this two-failure-modes lens, seriously, in both directions. Does Stevens' drift secretly depend on a schemer somewhere in the causal chain — Darlington, or the whole ideology of "greatness through service" — i.e., is drift always downstream of someone else's influence-seeking? What exactly is doing the drifting: Stevens' own values, or a professional ideal he outsourced his judgment to so thoroughly that there's no self left to notice the slide? The novel's famous restraint means Stevens never fully admits anything, even to himself — does Ishiguro show that drift can be *complete* without ever being registered by the drifter, and is that a finding or just a literary device? And where does the reading break: Stevens is a person with a heart, not an optimizer, so which parts of his trajectory genuinely transfer to the AI case and which parts are irreducibly human? Finally — the thing I most want for the piece — what does the novel show about drift that the abstract argument alone doesn't? I'm tempted to say it's that drift requires something like self-deception, and if that's right it cuts in a wierd direction for AI risk, since it's not clear current systems have selves to deceive. Push back on that if it's wrong.
