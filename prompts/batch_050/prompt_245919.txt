I am setting the final exam for my upper-level seminar on truth and paradox, and I want one essay question on dialetheism that cannot be answered by rehearsing the Liar and the explosion argument. Students love to say that paraconsistent logic blocks ex falso quodlibet and thereby saves us from trivialism, as if that were the whole story. I want a question that forces them to see that the hard problem for the dialetheist is not the Liar at all but Curry: since Curry's paradox needs no negation and no principle of explosion, blocking explosion does nothing against it, and the dialetheist who rests their case on rejecting explosion has answered the wrong question. The question should ask candidates to explain precisely which principles of the conditional (e.g., contraction, modus ponens read in certain ways) generate the trouble, and to evaluate whether the dialetheist's options -- restricting those principles, or accepting Curry sentences as further true contradictions -- are principled responses or ad hoc retreats. Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, phrased so that a candidate could answer it in 75 minutes; (2) a model answer of roughly 500 words; and (3) a marking scheme of five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, where each point carries a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker candidates mishandle it -- for instance, conflating dialetheism with trivialism, or treating the rejection of explosion as sufficient against Curry.
