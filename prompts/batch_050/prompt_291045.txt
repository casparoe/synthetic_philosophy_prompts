Since retiring from the chemistry department I've been going to a little philosophy group at the public library, mostly retired engineers and doctors plus a couple of sharp amatures. We just finished Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism and I volunteered to run the wrap-up session, because half the room is lost and the other half is confidently wrong in a way I remember from teaching undergrads.

Help me plan the session itself. I want the dependency order worked out: what has to be in place before the analytic/synthetic distinction even looks like something worth attacking -- I think they need to feel the pull of "true by definition" on cases like bachelor/unmarried man before Quine's circle of synonymy, definition, and necessity means anything to them -- and where the second dogma, reductionism, comes in so that the holism at the end feels earned rather than tacked on.

I need one carrying example to run through the whole thing. I'm tempted by something from my old life: whether "force equals mass times acceleration" is a definition of force or a claim about the world, since this room will actually have opinions. Tell me whether that choice works and what it has to have and lack, or propose something better.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll surface: that analytic just means obvious, that Quine showed mathematics is secretly empirical science, that he's denying meaning altogether like the positivists he was criticizing, plus whatever else you'd predict from scientists who last did formal logic forty years ago. Say where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than answered when it comes up.

Finally: what should I openly leave out at this level (one member keeps bringing up Kripke and the necessary a posteriori), and how do I check at the end that they've grasped the actual point and not just a slogan about everything being revisable?
