So here's what I want: write me a short story, maybe 1200-1800 words, that does that for the reader.

Set it somewhere the predicate actually matters -- I was picturing a gem-grading house, like a centuries-old institution that certifies emeralds, where all the grades, contracts, and insurance policies are written in terms of grue and bleen, with a fixed Examination Date on which any stone not yet examined flips categories. Maybe the protagonist is a young appraiser whos good at their job and has never questioned anything, and then something goes wrong -- a shipment scheduled to be examined just before the date gets delayed, or a foreign buyer shows up who keeps using this bizarre compound predicate "green" (which to the appraisers ears means something insane like "grue if examined before the Date, bleen if after") and the protagonist has to figure out why this foreigner's insane-seeming classification scheme tracks the world just as well as theirs does.

You're an expert on epistemology writing fiction, so think carefully before you start: jot down for yourself (privately, not in the story) what the grue definition actually is, what the symmetry point is, and where the story could accidentally get it wrong. Two things I care about. Second, no lecturing -- nobody should deliver a paragraph explaining Nelson Goodman or the new riddle of induction by name. The reader who knows the material should grin because its all there in the worldbuilding, and the reader who doesn't should still get a good story with a genuinely unsettling ending, since of course nothing in the past data settles which predicate the future will vindicate.
