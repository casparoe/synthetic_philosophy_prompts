Me and my roommate got into a stupidly long argument last night about Moore's paradox, you know, sentences like "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." The standard line seems to be that the sentence can be true but something goes wrong when you assert it, and my roommate kept insisting the problem is basically just about evidence: ordinarily you'd never have good grounds for both halves at once, since knowing it's raining requires believing it, or something like that. I think he's wrong, and we came up with a thought experiment to test it.

Imagine a device called a doxometer: a little implant that reads your brain and displays, with perfect accuracy, a live readout of what you currently believe. Now suppose Maya looks out the window, clearly sees the rain, and then glances at her doxometer, which (because of some quirk in her own cognition, not a malfunction in the device) correctly reports that she does not believe it's raining. So she says, entirely sincerely and with flawless evidence for both conjuncts: "It's raining, but I don't believe it." My roommate says this dissolves the paradox completly, since her assertion is rational, justified, maybe even true. I think something is still deeply wrong, either with the sentence or with Maya, even in this setup.

Please argue for my side: that the paradox survives even when the speaker has impeccable third-personal evidence for each half of the conjunction. Along the way, tell me what exactly is defective in the doxometer case (the utterance, Maya's overall state of mind, or the relationship between them), and whether your verdict changes if Maya never says anything out loud and just thinks the sentence to herself while staring at the readout.
