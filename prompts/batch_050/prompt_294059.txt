I coordinate a recorded interview series for our philosophy department -- short-form conversations aimed at advanced undergraduates, but conducted at a level the faculty would respect. Next month's guest is a philosopher of religion who has just published a monograph defending a strongly expressivist reading of Wittgenstein on religious belief: on her view, the late-1930s Cambridge lectures on religious belief, together with the relevant remarks in the notebooks, show that religious utterances are not hypotheses or truth-apt propositions about a supernatural order, but expressions of an attitude, a way of living, embedded in a distinctive language-game and form of life -- and she argues that the usual charges of "Wittgensteinian fideism" (Nielsen's old label) rest on misreading what "non-cognitive" means here. She is a careful, seasoned speaker who gives polished answers to the standard questions, so I want to get her past the greatest hits.

Draft nine to eleven interview questions, in the order I should ask them. At least one question each should press on the following, and for each I want a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: (a) the complaint that on her reading, believers themselves are systematically mistaken about what they are doing -- ordinary believers take "God exists" and "Christ rose from the dead" to be true, so the expressivist seems to be correcting the practice rather than describing it; (b) petitionary prayer and hope for an afterlife, which look very much like states that make sense only if something cognitive is going on; (c) how a language-game that is supposed to be autonomous can nonetheless be criticized, revised, or abandoned from within -- conversion and apostasy seem to be responses to reasons, not just changes of taste; (d) the textual pressure from the notebooks, where Wittgenstein's own attitude toward Christianity looks more conflicted and personal than the tidy expressivist picture suggests; and (e) the question of what Wittgenstein would say about a believer who insists, against the reading, that their faith stands or falls with a historical claim about an empty tomb. Conclude the sequence with one question about what textual or ethnographic evidence would count against her interpretation -- she tends to treat interpretive questions as immune to disconfirmation, and I'd like to see whether she'll commit to anything.

Beyond the questions themselves, flag for me the two or three points in the conversation where she is most likely to give an answer that sounds profound but doesn't actually address what was asked, and suggest how I can re-put the question without seeming combative on a recording. Please don't give me generic warm-up questions about how she came to the topic; assume the audience already knows the basic shape of the Wittgensteinian approach.
