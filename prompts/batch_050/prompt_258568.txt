I'm taking a philosophy of science seminar this semester and we just wrapped up the unit on laws of nature, and honestly I left more confused than I started. Everyone in class keeps calling themselves either a "Humean" or an "anti-Humean" but when you press people they clearly mean different things by it -- some people seem to think the whole debate is about whether laws *govern*, others think it's about modality, others about whether properties have essences. I want to make a flowchart for our study group that actually sorts people by what they believe rather than what label they grabbed off the syllabus.

So: please write me a decision tree that takes a student who accepts that there's a real difference between laws of nature and mere accidental generalizations (i.e. they've already rejected the naive "laws are just true regularities" view) and lands them on a named position. Some requirements:

- Each branch should be one question, phrased concretely enough that someone can answer it without knowing which view it leads to. Not "do you believe in governing?" but something like "could two worlds contain exactly the same events, arranged the same way, and still differ in what their laws are?"
- At least two of the questions have to be stated as actual physical scenarios, not abstract principles. One of them must be the lonely-particle world: a universe containing exactly one particle drifting forever in a straight line at constant speed -- does Newton's first law hold there, and if so, what makes it hold?
- The leaves must include at least: Lewis-style best system Humeanism, the pragmatic/"sophisticated" version of best system where simplicity and strength are judged relative to us, dispositional essentialism, the Armstrong-Tooley-Dretske necessitation view, Mumford-style realism with no laws at all, and Maudlin-style primitivism. If two of these genuinely can't be separated by any question, don't fake it -- put them on a shared leaf and say why the tree can't split them.
- Somewhere on the tree I want you to at least consider a hybrid position our prof floated as a provocation: laws genuinely govern the world, but the governing facts are themselves fully explained by (nothing over and above) the arrangement of ordinary events. If that combination is incoherent, say so at that branch and explain briefly why; if it's coherent, give it a leaf and tell me whether anyone actually holds it or whether it's an empty slot.
- Mark any unoccupied combinations of answers with a one-line diagnosis of whether the slot is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered.

Six to eight branching questions total, and please keep the tone readable -- this is going on a one-page handout for sleep-deprived undergrads, not a journal.
