In my philosophy of probability seminar we have reached the reference class problem, and my students have absorbed the textbook statement of it — any single event falls under indefinitely many reference classes with different frequencies, so the probability you assign depends on a choice no frequency table makes for you — but they uniformly treat it as a puzzle about the foundations of frequentism rather than something that could bite a competent practitioner. I want a thought experiment that proves them wrong, and I want it built to order. Act as a philosopher of probability who is also an experienced designer of exam-ready cases: construct a scenario set in an ordinary professional context — a physician recommending a treatment, an actuary pricing a policy, a parole board weighing recidivism risk; choose whichever you can render most concretely — in which the reference class problem by itself, with no missing or misleading data, flips a real decision.

The specifications are as follows. First, the decision-maker must know all the relevant frequencies with full statistical confidence; nothing may hinge on small samples, noisy data, or ignorance, since the point is that the problem is philosophical rather than statistical. Second, present at least three candidate reference classes, each defined in natural, non-gerrymandered predicates that a real practitioner would genuinely reach for, none cooked up around the outcome, and none containing the individual in question as its sole member. Third — and this is the load-bearing constraint — the classes must be cross-cutting rather than nested: no class may be a subset of another, and each must be narrower than some rival along one dimension and broader along another, so that the standard frequentist tie-breaker of taking the narrowest class with adequate data yields no unique verdict. Fourth, arrange the frequencies so that one class puts the relevant probability clearly on one side of the decision threshold, another puts it clearly on the other side, and the third lands it close enough to the threshold to make the choice a toss-up. Fifth, the decision must concern a single case — this patient, this applicant — so that the escape route of setting policy for a whole population, where frequencies would settle the matter, is unavailable.

After presenting the scenario in one or two paragraphs, demonstrate that it meets every specification: list each class, its frequency, and the decision it dictates, then run the standard tie-breakers one at a time — the narrowest-adequate-class rule, relevance-weighted averaging over classes, and conditioning on the total evidence — showing for each whether it fails to select a class or selects one only at the cost of a verdict its own defender should find uncomfortable. Then do the honest bookkeeping: identify the single structural feature of the case doing the work, explain what happens if it is removed (in particular, whether making the classes nested collapses the case into a routine statistics problem), and mount the strongest attack you can against your own construction — for instance, from a subjective Bayesian who insists there is only the agent's credence given total evidence, or from a propensity theorist who insists the world itself singles out the correct class — saying whether the attack dissolves the case or merely redescribes the problem, and whether what survives is an epistemic problem about which class we should use or a metaphysical one about whether there is any fact of the matter. Before settling on your final scenario, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate settings and discard any in which some tie-breaker succeeds; I would rather see your rejects than receive a case that fails quietly.
