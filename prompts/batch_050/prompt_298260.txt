Having spent years reading rationalist and EA-adjacent discussions of population ethics, I have seen many debates about suffering founder because the participants never establish whether they are disagreeing about phenomenology, interpersonal aggregation, or the meaning of compensation. Write the joint statement that the following two people could plausibly sign after a serious adversarial collaboration.

Mara defends a modest form of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. She holds that suffering below a threshold T can sometimes be outweighed by pleasure, but that suffering at or above T cannot be compensated for, or morally outweighed by, any finite quantity, duration, or distribution of pleasure. She denies that this commits her to assigning extreme suffering infinite cardinal value, and she denies that every subthreshold harm must always be prevented before anything else can be pursued.

Jon defends continuous-tradeoff welfarism. He holds that pleasure and suffering are, in principle, commensurable along a common welfare scale, so that any finite amount of suffering can be outweighed by a sufficiently great finite amount of welfare. He allows that the relevant tradeoffs may be impossible to measure, unacceptable as decision procedures, or constrained by rights and distribution. He also denies that benefiting strangers necessarily compensates the person who was made to suffer.

Their collaboration has used four invented diagnostic probes:

1. The Same-Life Dial-and-Ledger: a fully informed subject compares packages containing different intensities and durations of pain and pleasure, all occurring within that subject’s own life. This is meant to isolate same-person compensation from merely improving the overall state of the world.

2. The Stranger Ledger: one person’s suffering is paired with pleasure assigned to varying numbers of other people. The beneficiary may or may not know about the suffering, and the suffering may be either a means to the benefit or an unavoidable side effect. This is meant to separate compensation to the victim from impersonally making the world better.

3. The Splitting Press: a fixed quantity of aversiveness is either concentrated into one potentially above-threshold episode or dispersed across many subthreshold episodes, either within one life or across many lives. This is meant to test whether the alleged threshold concerns intensity, number, duration, or the separateness of persons.

4. The Seam Test: otherwise identical sequences of pleasure and suffering are presented either as continuous experiences or as episodes separated by sleep, amnesia, or an interruption in personal identity. This is meant to test whether memory, narrative unity, and persistence alter the relevant tradeoffs.

Draft the document Mara and Jon could both put their names to. It should be a constructive joint statement, not a dialogue, a verdict, or a diagnosis of why internet debates about utilitarianism are frustrating. Aim for roughly 2,000 words and use the following structure:

I. Numbered common ground. Give ten to fourteen claims both parties can accept. They should be substantive rather than merely affirming that suffering matters and people disagree. At minimum, distinguish compensation of the sufferer from impersonal outweighing, lexical priority from infinite cardinal value, and a phenomenological threshold from a purely normative rule.

II. A terminology truce. Define compensation, outweighing, commensurability, lexical priority, threshold, welfare, pleasure, and suffering. Identify at least three formulations that initially appeared to express disagreement but turned out to be ambiguous or verbal.

III. A disagreements table. Include at least six rows, with columns for the exact question at issue, the formulation Mara signs, the formulation Jon signs, and the stronger or sloppier formulations both reject. Phrase each side so that its own defender would accept the wording rather than feeling that the disagreement had been surreptitiously resolved against them.

IV. Cruxes and pre-registered predictions. Identify four to six cruxes. For each, explain why settling it would move at least one party; connect it to one of the four probes or to a distinct argument; record the verdict each party expects the probe or argument to support; and state what result would count as an anomaly requiring that party to revise their view. At least one crux should concern whether T is discoverable in the structure of suffering or is instead a moral commitment, one should concern the separateness of persons, and one should concern decision-making under uncertainty about where T lies.

V. Points of practical convergence and divergence. State what follows for decisions involving severe suffering, large populations, uncertain welfare measurement, and benefits accruing to people other than those harmed. Do not assume that agreement about an abstract principle settles the practical question.

VI. The residual fault line. End with one short paragraph, written in neutral language, stating the smallest proposition on which Mara and Jon still disagree after all merely verbal disputes have been removed.

Treat the thought experiments as diagnostic instruments rather than decisive proofs. Do not appeal to named authorities, summarize the existing literature, or declare which side is correct. The quality of the answer should lie in finding formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted and in making the remaining disagreement exact.
