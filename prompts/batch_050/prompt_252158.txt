In the Philosophy of Right, Hegel treats war between states as more than a breakdown of diplomacy: with no authority standing above sovereign peoples, war is the moment at which a people's ethical life is kept from hardening into habit and private comfort, and the individual's attachment to finite, contingent goods is broken against the state's universal purpose — a sacrifice he thinks citizens owe the ethical order in peacetime as well, only less visibly. Read Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front against this doctrine. The novel appears to dramatize exactly what Hegel describes — schoolboys talked into enlisting by Kantorek's idealism, their civilian selves erased, their deaths unregistered at home, Paul finally dying on a day the official report finds nothing to remark upon — while refusing to find anything ennobling in it. Does the novel refute Hegel, or does it confirm his phenomenology of war and simply decline his consolation? Build the strongest Hegelian reading first (on it, the horror stems from viewing soldiers as mere particulars, which is precisely the standpoint Hegel thinks war dissolves), then say where the book resists: whether industrial attrition can count as an ethical relation of mutual recognition between states at all, whether the front-line camaraderie around Kat is a counter-example (a genuine ethical community produced by war rather than preserved through it), and whether talk of sacrifice to the universal survives the leave-taking scenes, where what the universal demands is exactly Paul's capacity to be a son. Close with what Hegel would have to call Paul's death, and whether that is an answer to the novel or a reductio of the doctrine.
