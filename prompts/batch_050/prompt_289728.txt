I am preparing a philosophy of mind seminar in which we read Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon during our unit on the concept of intelligence, and I would like a careful critical reading of the novel through that lens. In particular, I want you to treat the book as an extended thought experiment about what intelligence is, and to assess how well its implicit theory survives contact with the actual philosophical and psychological debate.

The questions I would like your reading to address:

First, the novel portrays intelligence as a single quantity that can be raised by surgery and later lost, measured throughout by IQ tests. Which position in the literature does this presuppose — something like Spearman's general factor — and where does the narrative itself strain against the unitary picture? Charlie's gains appear to combine something like fluid reasoning with an implausibly rapid accumulation of crystallized knowledge; does Keyes conflate intelligence with learning, and does the book's own plot (Charlie's emotional and interpersonal development lagging his cognitive gains) quietly concede a pluralism the psychometric framing denies?

Second, the narrative suggests that something persists through the transformation — the earlier Charlie as an observer within, the continuity of memory and longing. Read this as a claim about the separability of intelligence from personhood, and connect it to the debate over psychological continuity: does the novel treat intelligence as an accidental property of the self, and is that treatment coherent given that the prose itself is the medium of Charlie's mind?

Third, the scientists' attitude toward the pre-operative Charlie raises the question of whether the novel ties moral status to intelligence. What does the book imply, and how does Algernon himself — the one other subject of the experiment — complicate that implication?

Fourth, does the ending indict cognitive enhancement as such, or merely a botched technique? Could a transhumanist consistently read the regression as contingent failure, and what would the novel say in reply?

Finally, indicate where the novel resists the philosophical reading — where the honest critic must admit that Keyes is doing something the theories do not capture.
