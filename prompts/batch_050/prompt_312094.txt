Every spring I run a 75-minute session on decision theory for a mixed undergrad ethics seminar, about 18 students, and this year I want them to feel the pull of expected value fanaticism instead of just hearing me define it at them. Design me an in-class game where, by the end, some students have willingly traded away guaranteed winnings for tiny chances at absurdly large ones, and where the class then has to have an actual arguement about whether those students played well or got suckered.

Some constraints:

- Real but small stakes, no money. I'm thinking points that convert into raffle tickets for a modest prize (a candy bar, or a 'skip one reflection paper' coupon -- tell me which works better for the incentive structure).
- Physical randomization for the small probabilities -- dice chains, drawing beads from a bag, whatever -- because I want them to feel how little grip they have on the difference between 1 in 100 and 1 in 1000, not just read numbers off a slide. If part of the design is that they can't tell those apart in practice, good, say so.
- At least one round where a student gets to play the mugger and invent their own offer to the class, so the group generates the 'tiny probability of an astronomically huge payoff' step themselves instead of me feeding it to them.
- No mention of expected value, fanaticism, or Pascal up front. The vocabulary only shows up in the debrief, after the choices are already made and can't be taken back.

Include: full rules and an actual payoff schedule with real numbers (not placeholders), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, how to seed the early rounds so the pressure escalates gradually rather than jumping straight to the crazy offers, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the real philosophical fault lines -- where the issue is diminishing marginal utility versus probability discounting, where it's one-shot play versus the long run, and the exact point where refusing the mugger's offer seems to require either a cap on utility or a floor under probabilities and both moves feel costly.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: students pooling points, somebody actually hitting the jackpot in round one and ruining the arc, the class walking away with 'gambling is bad' and missing the whole point, and so on -- with a fix for each. And be honest at the end about what a points-for-candy game can't reproduce about the real debate, where the stakes are supposed to be lives or the far future and nobody gets to reroll.
