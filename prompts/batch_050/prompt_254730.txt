I'm TAing a junior seminar on philosophy of mind and I want to build a "where do you actually land on mental causation?" flowchart for the causal exclusion argument, since my students keep self-reporting as "nonreductive physicalists" without knowing what that commits them to. Here's what I need:

A decision tree of roughly 8-10 yes/no-ish questions, starting from the basic Kim-style setup (your deciding to wave causes your arm to rise, but the arm's rising also has a complete sufficient physical cause in neurons and muscle fibers, so what's left for the decision to do?). Each question should be a concrete claim or mini-case the students can react to without knowing any labels -- e.g. "if your pain had been realized by slightly different neurons, would it still have been the same cause of your wincing?" or "is there any actual case you accept where one event has two fully sufficient causes and that's totally fine?" -- not "do you accept causal closure?" phrased in those words.

The leaves should cover at least: type-identity reduction, eliminativism about the mental, epiphenomenalism, honest overdetermination, interactionist dualism (denying closure), and Davidson-style anomalous monism. Also include at least one leaf for the difference-making/interventionist response that says mental and physical causes aren't competing because exclusion only applies at one level of description.

A few demands so it works as a teaching tool:

- Order the questions from the logical space, not from the history of the debate. So first figure out which combinations of answers are even possible, then place real positions in that space.
- If there's a coherent-looking combination nobody defends, keep it as a leaf marked "unnamed" with one sentence on why the seat is empty (incoherent? just ugly? unexplored?).
- Flag any two leaves the tree can't actually separate, and tell me what extra question would split them.
- Every leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment -- the thing a smart undergrad will immediately object to.
- At least two of the questions have to use the same arm-raising case, so students can watch their earlier answers constrain their later ones.

Casual handout tone is fine, and keep the whole thing to something that fits on two pages.
