Two legal philosophers in my department have spent a semester arguing about the paradox of blackmail — roughly, the problem of explaining why it should be criminal to demand money in exchange for not doing something (disclosing truthful, lawfully obtained information) that one has a legal right to do anyway, where the demand itself is not independently wrongful. I have asked them to run a structured adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to draft the joint statement they should produce.

The two parties are as follows. The first, call her the Unificationist, holds that blackmail is genuinely and seriously wrongful, that its criminalization is justified, and that an adequate theory must locate a single underlying wrong that explains both the paradigmatic cases and the statute's contours — she is partial to the idea that the blackmailer wrongfully uses leverage over information that, in some sense, is not his to sell, and she thinks this can be extended from "triangular" cases (where the information concerns third parties or was gathered under an implicit trust) to bilateral ones (where the blackmailer simply stumbled on the discreditable fact himself). The second, call him the Deflationist, holds that the paradox is real and unresolved, that no proposed unifying theory survives counterexample, and that the correct conclusion is revisionist: much of what we criminalize as blackmail should be legal, and our moral revulsion at the blackmailer tracks his character and motives rather than any act-type wrong that could justify prohibition.

To make the collaboration concrete, both parties have agreed to reason about two constructed cases, which you should treat as stipulated and use throughout:

Case One (The Archive Startup). A company lawfully aggregates old, publicly available court records that are technically open but practically obscure. It identifies individuals whose records would embarrass them, and offers them a paid subscription: so long as they pay, the company will not mail a dossier of their records to their neighbors, employer, and congregants. The company sends each person one letter describing the service. If they decline, it mails the dossier, as it has told them it will.

Case Two (The Mutual Silence Pact). Two estranged business partners each possess truthful information damaging to the other. Without any threat being uttered, each independently realizes that disclosure would trigger reciprocal disclosure, and so both stay silent. A third party then offers one partner a payment conditional on continued silence, which he accepts.

The statement should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims — the propositions both parties can sign. These should include claims about what the paradox actually is and is not (e.g., whether it depends on the information being true, or on the disclosure being legal), about the inadequacy of certain easy answers (that it's "just coercion," that it's "just like ordinary commerce"), and at least one agreed verdict on each constructed case. Second, a disagreements table: each row states the residual disagreement in neutral wording, then gives each side's formulation of its own position in terms that side would accept. The Unificationist should not be described as "assuming what she needs to prove," and the Deflationist should not be described as "endorsing extortion." Third, a section on cruxes: identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party, and for each crux, record in advance what each side predicts about the relevant case or argument — for instance, each side's verdict on whether the Archive Startup differs morally from an individual blackmailer, and whether anything of normative significance separates the paid silence in Case Two from the unpaid silence that precedes it.

Two further constraints. The statement should include at least one point on which the collaboration revealed the original dispute to be partly verbal, and it should say which point and why. And it should close by identifying what, if anything, both parties agree would actually count as progress — an argument, a case verdict, or a theoretical result — as opposed to another round of intuition-trading.

Aim for the tone of a document both philosophers could actually sign: precise, unslanted, and willing to leave the disagreement on the page rather than paper over it.
