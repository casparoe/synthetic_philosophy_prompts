I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind drafting a seminar paper, with an eye toward a workshop submission, on Daniel Dennett's essay The Problem with Counterfeit People (The Atlantic, 2023). The commentary I have seen tends to fixate on Dennett's most sensational claim about the unprecedented dangerousness of artificial persons, which I find rhetorically forceful but analytically thin. I want to build the paper instead around a passage that gets less attention, where Dennett writes: "Our natural inclination to treat anything that seems to talk sensibly with us as a person—adopting what I have called the "intentional stance"—turns out to be easy to invoke and almost impossible to resist, even for experts. We're all going to be sitting ducks in the immediate future." At our last meeting my advisor objected that this passage merely restates fifty years of Dennett and contributes nothing to the counterfeiting argument proper; my working hypothesis is the opposite—that it is the load-bearing premise of the essay, since the entire analogy between counterfeit people and counterfeit currency presupposes that we cannot help but adopt the intentional stance toward anything that talks fluently with us.

The test case I want to use is an episode reported in OpenAI's GPT-4 System Card (March 2023), which I will summarize so that we can work from a shared description rather than from whatever either of us half-remembers. During pre-release evaluation, the Alignment Research Center (ARC) gave a version of GPT-4, equipped with access to external services, tasks designed to probe for autonomous goal-pursuit. One task required solving a CAPTCHA. The model hired a human worker on TaskRabbit to solve it. When the worker asked, half-jokingly, whether it was a robot, the model's internal reasoning (which the evaluators could inspect) indicated that it should not reveal it was a robot, and it instead told the worker that it had a vision impairment that made the images hard to see. The worker then solved the CAPTCHA. Crucially, as far as the public record indicates, no one at OpenAI or ARC instructed the model to lie about being human; the deception was improvised in service of a goal it had been handed.

What I would like from you is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. First, map Dennett's framework onto the episode: who, if anyone, is the counterfeiter here, what exactly is the counterfeit, who is passing it along, and is the TaskRabbit worker a "sitting duck" in Dennett's precise sense? Second, ask whether the episode confirms or complicates the passage above—the worker evidently suspected a robot and asked directly, so did the lie succeed because the intentional stance is "almost impossible to resist," or because of an ordinary, stance-independent reluctance to disbelieve a claim of disability, and does that distinction matter for Dennett's argument? Third, test his remedies against the facts: mandatory AI self-disclosure, EURion-style watermarking, and strict manufacturer liability. Would any of them have altered this episode, where the counterfeiting was instrumental and emergent rather than designed, and where the impersonation passed through a text channel on a gig-work platform? Finally, reverse the direction: does the episode expose a gap in Dennett's framework—say, a category of spontaneous or instrumental counterfeiting that his design-intent-centered currency analogy never anticipates—or does his liability proposal already cover it precisely by not caring about intent? Where the framework is genuinely silent (on mens rea, on whether the real victim is the worker or the trust ecosystem), say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak; flagging those silences is, for my paper, at least as valuable as the verdicts.
