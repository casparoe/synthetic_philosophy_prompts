I cover science and big ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for years I've been circling the open borders debate. One of my recurring sources is a political philosopher, Mara, who defends a state's right to exclude immigrants -- roughly the Wellman-style freedom-of-association line, against the Carens line that citizenship is basically an inherited privilege like a feudal title. Next month I'm interviewing her again, this time about "startup cities" -- privately run charter cities, seasteads, that whole scene -- and I'd like to walk in with a decent prediction of where she'll land instead of just fishing.

Here are her on-the-record verdicts from our past conversations, as best I've reconstructed from my notes. A chess club may exclude people who don't play chess. A church may exclude non-believers from membership. A selective university may reject most of its applicants. An employer may turn down job candidates (setting discrimination aside). A homeowners association may keep non-residents out of its private park. And a country -- her headline view -- may exclude would-be immigrants, because a state is, on her view, an association like the others. But she also said that a landlord who happens to own every apartment in a small town may NOT refuse to rent to outsiders in a way that effectively bars them from living there, which genuinly surprised me, and separately that a company may not forbid its employees from letting their adult kids move in with them. I never got her to state the principle.

The new case for the interview: a developer builds a city from scratch on land it bought outright, writes a charter requiring every resident to hold a lease from the company (or be the dependent of someone who does), and plans to exclude everyone else -- no public streets in the usual sense, all property private, target population around 50,000. So: what will Mara say, and more to the point, what rule is she actually running? I can see a few candidates that fit the verdicts -- maybe it's about whether exclusion cuts people off from ordinary life and livelihood rather than from some specific optional good, maybe it's about consent, since everyone in the startup city chose to move there, maybe it's about whether the association is even the kind of thing whose authority extends that far -- but they seem to diverge on this case. Give me your prediction, lay out the candidate rules and what each one says about the charter city, tell me which rule best explains the landlord and employer verdicts, and suggest the follow-up hypothetical I could put to her that would most cleanly tell the rules apart. And if you think her verdicts are just flat-out inconsistent, say that instead and tell me which one is the odd one out.
