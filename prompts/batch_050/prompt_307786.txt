A friend of mine is starting a PhD in philosophy of science and is dead set on working on thought experiments — Galileo's falling bodies, Einstein chasing a light beam, Maxwell's demon, Newton's bucket, the whole genre. She's got maybe a dissertation and two papers in her before the job market decides her fate, and she's asked me which open problems in the area are actually worth spending those on. I told her I'd ask around.

My honest worry is that the famous fight in this area is a trap. One camp says thought experiments are just arguments wearing a narrative costume, so their epistemic force is no more mysterious than any argument's (Norton and friends); the other camp says they're something stranger, a near-direct grasp of how nature has to be (Brown and friends). Decades in, no knockout. Maybe that means the question needs one more good push, or maybe it's a tar pit that eats careers. She needs to know which.

Meanwhile there are questions that feel genuinely under-explored. If you build Maxwell's demon in a physics simulator and watch it fail, is that still a thought experiment or has it quietly turned into a real one — and does the boundary even matter for what thought experiments can teach us? Could an AI produce a genuinely new thought experiment, or only remix ones it's read, and what would each answer imply about what thought experiments are? And there's the weird afterlife problem: Schrödinger's cat was invented to ridicule the Copenhagen interpretation and now shows up in every textbook as an illustration of it. When a thought experiment gets repurposed against its author's intent, whose version counts — and does that settle anything about whether thought experiments are arguments (fixed conclusion, owned by the author) or instruments (pointable at anything)?

So: give her a defended agenda, not a survey. Pick the 3-4 open questions most worth her finite shots, in order, and for each one say what's downstream of it (in phil science or beyond — physics teaching, AI, the ethics trolley industry), how tractable it is for one person with no lab and a five-year clock, how crowded the territory already is, and what developments elsewhere would make you move it up or down the list. If you think the Norton/Brown fight deserves her best years, make the case; if you'd steer her away, say why it's fine to let it sit. Also name one question in the area you consider overrated — the one that soaks up dissertations without paying off downstream. She does'nt need a reading list, just the ranking and the reasoning behind it.
