I'm a doctoral student working on the metaphysics of dispositions, and I've been stuck on an argument I put together after thinking through the standard masking cases (styrofoam-wrapped glassware, live wires connected to safety switches, and so on). It seems to show far more than it should, and I'd like a diagnosis. The argument:

(1) An object has a disposition to M when S just in case, were it subjected to S, it would M.
(2) A fragile glass is thus one such that, were it struck, it would break.
(3) Wrap the glass in styrofoam: were it struck now, it would not break, because the packing absorbs the blow.
(4) So the wrapped glass is not fragile.
(5) But fragility is an intrinsic property of the glass, and nothing intrinsic to the glass changes when it is wrapped.
(6) So the glass was not fragile before it was wrapped either.
(7) The same reasoning generalizes: for any object and any putative disposition, there is a possible arrangement of external circumstances under which the counterfactual in (1) fails while the object remains intrinsically unchanged, and so no object is ever truly fragile, soluble, poisonous, or irascible.
(8) Therefore dispositional predicates are never literally true of anything; they encode, at most, facts about objects together with their surroundings.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd, but each step looks locally fine. I suspect the damage is done by (1), yet I am reluctant to abandon it wholesale, since some conditional claim must surely capture at least the core of what we mean by fragility, and Lewis's revision of the conditional analysis still seems to lean on something in the vicinity. So: which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? In particular, does your diagnosis of the masking cases extend to fink cases like Martin's electro-fink -- where the disposition seems genuinely removed rather than merely suppressed -- or do masks and finks require different treatment, and if different, does that asymmetry undermine any unified account of dispositions?
