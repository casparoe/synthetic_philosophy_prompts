So I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for years, and I keep bumping into what feels like a foundational question the mech interp crowd mostly just... assumes an answer to. When we say a network "has a feature" for the Golden Gate Bridge or for deception or whatever, is that feature actually in there, the way a gear is in a clock? Or are we finding compressions of the model's behavior that happen to be legible to humans, such that a sufficiently different interpretability method would carve the same network into totally different "features" with equal justification?

The part I can't stop chewing on: it seems like there are at least three live positions, and they imply very different research bets.

1. Realism: the features are really there, our probes approximately recover them, and where probes disagree at least one probe is wrong.
2. Instrumentalism: feature-talk is a useful fiction; the only test is whether interventions (steering, ablations) let you predict and control behavior.
3. A third thing I haven't seen named anywhere, which I think of as the map-territory hybrid: the network really does have structure, but the *ontology* we impose on it (sparse features vs. circuits vs. something else) is ours. Interpretability as cartography of a real landscape -- the terrain is real, the borders are conventional.

Now the concrete ask. A friend and I are putting together a small grants pool, enough to fund 2 or 3 people for a year each on conceptual work about interpretability (philosophy/theory, not training more SAEs). I need to decide which questions to prioritize, and I want a defended ranking, not a survey. Candidate questions, feel free to add or merge:

- Is there actually a fact of the matter about whether a network "represents" something, or is realism vs. instrumentalism a pseudo-dispute that dissolves once you specify the success criterion (causal control? prediction? compression?)?
- What would count as evidence for or against realism? E.g., if two different probe families recover systematically different feature sets from the same model, is that a problem for realism or just for the probes?
- If instrumentalism is right, does anything change about how we should do interpretability for alignment purposes, or is the whole debate a distraction from steering results?
- Suppose it turns out you can find a sparse, interpretable-looking basis for basically any network if you tune your method enough -- analogous to fitting anything with enough Fourier terms. Is that a reductio of the realism project, or exactly what realists should predict given superposition?
- Whose realism matters: does any of this bear on whether models could have "internal states" in the morally-loaded sense, or is that a seperate conversation entirely?

For the ranking: tell me which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for a philosopher without ML engineering chops, which would most change what empirical interp researchers should actually do if answered, and what would move your ranking around -- e.g., what empirical results in the next year or two would make the realism questions urgent or moot. If you think the whole realism question is confused and the field should just chase causal validation, argue that instead and tell me what the grants should fund. I'm way more interested in the reasoning than the list.
