A colleague in my reading group sprung this argument on us last week and it's been bugging me ever since, because the conclusion can't be right but I keep failing to pin down why. Background: I think some choices involve genuinely incomparable values — like choosing between a career as a concert violinist and a career as a human rights lawyer. Neither seems better than the other, but they don't seem exactly equally good either: if they were exactly equal, then a slightly improved version of one would have to be better than the other, and it clearly isn't. So I say they're incomparable (or "on a par" or whatever the right label is). Her argument that this can't coherently be said:

(1) Suppose the violinist career V and the lawyer career L are incomparable: neither is better than the other, and they're not equally good either.
(2) Take V+, which is just V with one extra week of holiday per year and otherwise identical. V+ is definitely better than V.
(3) Now compare V+ and L. They can't be related by "better than" or "equally good", because V+ differs from V only trivially: a one-week-per-year change cannot turn an incomparable pair into a comparable one. Whatever relation holds between V and L also holds between V+ and L.
(4) So V+ and L are incomparable too.
(5) Repeat the move. V++ (another small improvement) must also be incomparable with L, and so on. Keep going, with the improvements spread across everything that matters — artistic fulfillment, pay, security, impact — until we reach V*, a career that matches L on all the things L had going for it (salary, social impact, stability) and beats it on the rest.
(6) By the same reasoning V* and L are incomparable. But that's absurd: V* strictly dominates L, and any option that strictly dominates another is at least comparable to it — you'd have to be irrational to pick L over V*.
(7) So the supposition in (1) was false: V and L were never incomparable. And since nothing in the argument depended on this particular example, there are no genuinely incomparable options. Every hard choice has a correct ranking; we're just bad at finding it.

If she's right, then all the agonizing over hard choices is really just failure to locate the right metric, and cost-benefit analysis of everything is fine in principle, which I really don't want to accept. My gut says (3) is the weak link but whenever I try to say why it comes out sounding like special pleading, and she has a point that it would be bizarre if one week of holiday were the thing that flipped a pair from incomparable to comparable. So: which step do I actually reject, why does that step look so compelling, and what does rejecting it cost me? What do the people who defend incomparability (the "parity" idea is the only bit of this literature I know) actually say at exactly this point? And be honest about the state of play — if the argument is generally taken to succeed, or if the standard responses are widely seen as dodges, tell me that instead of pretending there's some settled refutation everyone accepts.
