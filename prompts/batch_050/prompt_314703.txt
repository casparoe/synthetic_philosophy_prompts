I'm writing an essay for a philosophy-and-film class on Groundhog Day and Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, and I keep running into a problem I want a serious reading to work through rather than paper over. The standard take (which my prof half-endorsed in lecture) is that Phil Connors' time loop is basically Nietzsche's thought experiment from The Gay Science made watchable: a demon tells you your life will repeat exactly, forever, and the question is whether you'd greet that as a curse or affirm it. But the more I think about it, the more the film seems to *fail* as a dramatization of the actual thought experiment, in ways that might be philosophically interesting rather than just Hollywood cheating. Two things in particular: first, Nietzsche's recurrence is supposed to be exact repetition with no memory of previous rounds, whereas Phil remembers everything -- so he's not really reliving the same day, he's living a long single life with a weird structure. Second, and this is what really bugs me, in the movie affirming the loop is what *gets him out of it*. Once Phil becomes a good person and genuinely embraces the day, he wakes up on February 3rd. But Nietzsche's demon offers no exit -- you're supposed to say yes to eternal repetition knowing it buys you nothing, which is kind of the whole point of it being the greatest weight. Doesn't the film smuggle in exactly the reward-structure morality (suffer now, be good, get the girl and the release) that Nietzsche thought cheapened everything?

So: read the film against the recurrence properly. Where does it genuinely illuminate the thought experiment -- I'm thinking of the suicide montage, which seems to test something about whether even death can function as an escape under recurrence, and the piano-teacher thread, where Phil's "self-improvement" starts out instrumental and becomes something else -- and where does it quietly replace Nietzsche's question with an easier one, like "could you learn to love an inescapable present," which is maybe closer to amor fati as therapy than to willing the recurrence? And then the harder ask: is Phil's final contentment actually the affirmation Nietzsche wants, or is it just resignation plus conditioning -- and how would we even tell the difference from the inside, given that in true recurrence there's no "inside perspective" comparing iterations at all? If the film's version turns out to be a different (maybe better, maybe worse) idea dressed in Nietzsche's clothes, say that plainly, and tell me whether the essay's thesis should be that Groundhog Day misunderstands the eternal recurrence or that it improves on it.
