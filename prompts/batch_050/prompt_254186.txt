I'm TAing a grad epistemology seminar this semester and we're doing the anti-luminosity material from Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits -- the margin-for-error argument that conditions like "feeling cold" or "it's dawn" can't be luminous, and the application of similar reasoning to knowing itself, i.e. against the KK principle (if you know p, you're in a position to know that you know p). In section last week a student asked whether KK failures always have to come from that specific slow-fade structure, where a condition gradually stops obtaining across a series of near-identical cases, and I realized I didn't have a case on hand that fails KK by some other route. So: build me one. Construct a thought experiment in which a subject knows p but is not in a position to know that they know p, where the KK failure does NOT arise from a sorites over slowly changing conditions (no dawn cases, no slowly warming rooms, no tree growing imperceptibly taller), and also does not arise from the subject forgetting something, being irrational, being drugged or sleep-deprived, or having the failure simply stipulated ("suppose she knows but can't know she knows" is cheating -- the verdict has to fall out of the epistemology, not the setup).

Constraints: realistic setting (a hospital, a lab, an office, an everyday errand -- no demons, no perfect predictors, no science fiction), the scenario itself in one paragraph, and the subject's evidence and cognitive capacities should be thoroughly normal. After the scenario, do the demonstration: first show that the subject genuinely knows p (say which features of the case secure the knowledge under a standard view -- safety, reliability, justification, whatever you need), then show why she nonetheless isn't in a position to know that she knows, and identify exactly which structural feature of the case does the work. Then two follow-ups: (a) what happens to the verdict if you remove that feature -- does the case collapse into a trivial non-KK case (like plain non-knowledge) or does KK come back? and (b) how would a KK defender attack it -- I'm thinking of someone who either holds that the subject merely has knowledge she hasn't actualized into higher-order knowledge (and that "in a position to know" is doing too much work in my reading), or who restricts KK to a weaker internalist version.

One more thing, since it came up in the seminar: say whether your construction can be iterated. If knowing fails to be luminous in your case, does the same trick generate a failure of KKK (knowing that you know that you know), or does the mechanism top out at one level? And if the case ultimately works by smuggling in something structurally similar to margin-for-error after all -- say, an epistemic buffer between the subject's evidence and her evidence about her evidence -- be honest about that, since my student will definately spot it.
