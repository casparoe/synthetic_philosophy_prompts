In a discussion about whether our regional health authority should fund a costly cancer drug that buys patients a few extra months of life, a colleague ended the argument by insisting that you cannot put a price on a human life. She deployed the same slogan last year against the transport ministry's practice of justifying road-safety spending with a monetary value per statistical life saved -- yet she works in an understaffed ICU and unhesitatingly accepts triage decisions that allocate scarce beds to the patients most likely to survive. I suspect the slogan hides several distinct claims, not all of which condemn the practices she used it against, and some of which would equally condemn the triage she accepts. Could you lay out the different precise theses this one-liner might be expressing, from the weakest to the strongest, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or simply false? For each reading, please also indicate what it would actually rule out -- QALY-based rationing of treatments, value-of-statistical-life calculations in safety regulation, bedside triage, all three, or none -- and identify which reading my colleague would need for her conclusions, and whether that reading is one she could consistently hold given her own practice.
