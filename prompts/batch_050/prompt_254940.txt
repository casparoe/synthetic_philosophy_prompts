I am preparing a graduate seminar on Dennett's critique of qualia, built around his pair of coffee tasters: one reports that the flavor of coffee has remained exactly as it always was while his liking for it has faded; the other reports that the flavor itself has drifted, even though everything he would have said about it in the past still seems right to him. Dennett's claim is that no test — introspective, behavioral, or physiological — can privilege one of these reports over the other, since any test merely samples further reactions and dispositions, and the supposed quale always sits one step removed from whatever is measured. On his diagnosis, the posit is not hidden but ill-defined.

Construct a thought experiment designed to break this symmetry. The specifications are as follows. First, a single subject undergoes a change confined to one sensory modality whose phenomenology is genuinely ambiguous in the style of the tasters: it should be unclear to the subject herself whether the experience changed or whether her evaluative and discriminative relation to the experience changed. Second, the scenario must include a test on which the two readings issue in divergent predictions — what the subject will do, notice, or fail to notice must differ depending on which reading is true. Third, no question-begging devices: no scanner that simply displays qualia, no stipulation that one reading feels right to the subject, no appeal to first-person authority as settling the matter, since that is precisely what is under dispute. Fourth, the subject's memory, training, and competence must be held fixed across the two readings, so that any divergent prediction is traceable to a single structural feature of the case.

Present your response in American English as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario, which alone may run to four sentences; (2) the prediction each reading makes and why; (3) the feature doing the work, including whether removing it collapses the case back into the undecidable structure of the original tasters; (4) Dennett's best reply — presumably that the test, too, measures only reactive dispositions — and whether that reply succeeds or merely relocates the dispute; (5) your overall verdict, in which you state how confident you are that the case achieves its aim and single out the point in your construction you are least sure of. Items two through five should each be one or two sentences.
