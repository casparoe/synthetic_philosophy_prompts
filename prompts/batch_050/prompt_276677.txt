I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate module on American pragmatism, and I need one essay question on Peirce. It should be answerable in 90 minutes by students who know the basics, but not by someone who has merely learnt the pragmatic maxim, the four methods of fixing belief, and the slogan that science is self-correcting.

Build the question around this fictional case, which you may polish or condense:

A state creates a Settlement Institute to organise inquiry. While a question remains open, the Institute funds rival research programmes, rewards successful predictions, encourages criticism, and requires claims to be tested against experience. Once 95 per cent of accredited researchers agree, however, it declares the matter settled, withdraws funding, and refuses permission for further experiments intended to test the consensus. One dispute concerns two total cosmologies, A and B, which are stipulated to have exactly the same possible effects on observation, experiment, deliberation, and practical conduct for every possible inquirer. A alone says that there is an additional realm permanently causally isolated from our own. Since neither theory has any practical advantage, the Institute declares A true because its governing council prefers it. In another case, it treats cruelty is wrong as meaningful because accepting that claim changes habits, institutions, and patterns of action, even though wrongness itself cannot be detected in a laboratory. When dissidents appeal to Peircean fallibilism, the Institute replies that truth just is the opinion on which ideal inquiry finally converges, so continued dissent after convergence is simply confusion.

The exam question should require candidates to say what Peirce's view commits him to at each disputed point. In particular, it should force them to distinguish:

- having no difference in meaning from having a difference that is merely unknown or unknowable;
- scientific fixation of belief from tenacity, authority, and the mere desire for settled belief;
- an actual institutional consensus from the hypothetical outcome of indefinitely continued inquiry;
- fallibilism about real beliefs from the incoherent demand that investigators must always pretend to doubt everything;
- practical consequences of a concept from the psychological consequences of happening to believe a proposition.

Please provide:

1. the finished exam question, including enough of the case for it to be self-contained;
2. a model answer of roughly 650 words;
3. a marking scheme out of 20, divided among five non-negotiable components;
4. for each component, a brief note on the mistake weaker students are most likely to make;
5. a short explanation of what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer.

Don't pad it out with citations or a reading list, and don't make the right answer depend on some obscure disagreement in Peirce scholarship. The hard part should be applying the main ideas carefully to an unfamiliar case.
