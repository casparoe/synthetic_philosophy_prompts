I'm a PhD student in ML, and this semester I'm TAing a grad seminar on AI safety while also doing a part-time internship at a lab that trains RL agents in simulated warehouse and office environments. The lab has an informal norm that training runs shouldn't "cause side effects" — knock things over, irreversibly change files, that kind of thing — and my manager asked me to turn that norm into an actual review checklist, because right now it's just vibes in a Google doc. The catch is the obvious one: we don't have, and won't ever have, a utility function over world states, so "impact" has to be defined some other way or defined away. I've read about things like penalizing deviation from an inaction baseline, or penalizing reductions in some auxiliary measure of future option availability, and every one of them seems to either (a) be trivially satisfiable by an agent that does nothing, (b) get gamed by an agent that offsets its own impact in ways we'd hate, or (c) quietly smuggle in a value judgment about which changes matter while pretending to be purely about "change."

So here's what I want: write me a decision procedure my manager's team could actually run on a proposed training setup, in maybe 30 minutes, before approving it. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and — crucially — the specific points where the procedure has to hand off to a human value judgment because the low-impact literature demonstrably can't settle the question without one. Where the procedure has to pick a baseline (do-nothing, inaction-stepwise, expected-human-behavior, whatever), say which one it picks and what breaks if you pick a different one. And be honest about the asymmetry problem: a pure low-impact penalty fights the task reward, so the procedure needs a stance on whether "impact" is a constraint or a tiebreaker, and what that stance presupposes about value.

Then run the procedure on two setups we're actually considering: (1) a simulated robot arm learning pallet stacking, where "don't break anything" seems easy until you ask whether the baseline includes the damage a clumsy early agent does while exploring; (2) a browsing agent that completes research tasks and sometimes needs to send emails or make bookings, where "inaction" isn't even a well-defined baseline because the world moves on its own. Ideally one of these should come out with an answer I won't like — e.g., the procedure rejecting a setup that seems obviously fine, or approving one that seems sketchy — and say why. Also tell me what the procedure *can't* do, i.e., which failures of the "avoid side effects" principle are invisible to any checklist of this form. tyia :)
