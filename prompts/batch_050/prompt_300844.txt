I'm a PhD student in archaeology and I've somehow ended up teaching a single 90-minute seminar for second-year undergrads on how archaeologists reason from objects to beliefs — i.e. how you get from a buried figurine or a grave full of goods to any claim at all about what the people who deposited it actually thought. Their training so far has been almost all typology, dating and excavation technique, and they've split into two camps: the ones who think any talk of past belief is speculation and we'd better stick to pots, and the ones who think once evidence runs out you can just say whatever sounds evocative. Both camps are wrong but in instructive ways.

Don't give the seminar — plan it. What's the order the pieces have to come in, and why (I assume something about beliefs leaving no fossils has to precede anything about ethnographic analogy, but argue for whatever structure you actually pick)? What one case should carry the whole session — I've been going back and forth between a famous early medieval ship burial and the Paleolithic "Venus" figurines everyone argues about; commit to one (or neither) and tell me what a good carrying case has to have and has to lack for this purpose. Which misconceptions will they bring or form beyond my two camps — I'm guessing "grave goods are a shopping list of the religion" and "if two readings fit the evidence they're equally good" — and at exactly which point in the sequence do I head each one off? Where, if anywhere, does Hawkes' ladder of inference belong: is it a useful spine or does it just license the stick-to-pots camp? What end-of-session task would show me they can weigh two rival readings of the same deposit rather than recite "interpretation is subjective"? And what should I openly refuse to cover in ninety minutes?

Commit to definite choices throughout — order, case, the misconception list in the order it'll surface, what to cut — with reasons. I don't want a menu of options I could have googled myself.
