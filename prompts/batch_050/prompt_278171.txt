For a reading group on social choice theory, I've been asked to present on the discursive dilemma, and I based my notes on a chapter of a popular book about collective decision-making. Before I repeat its claims in front of people who know this literature, I want to check how much of the chapter is trustworthy. Here is its summary of the problem:

"A group that votes by majority on a set of logically connected propositions can end up committed to an inconsistent position even though every individual member is perfectly consistent. The standard example is a three-judge court: liability requires both that a valid contract existed and that it was breached; two judges accept the first proposition, two accept the second, but only one judge accepts both, so the court as a body affirms each premise while a majority of it denies liability. The problem is essentially Condorcet's paradox rediscovered for beliefs rather than preferences: just as majority voting over rankings can cycle, majority voting over propositions can contradict itself. This shows that majority rule is not a coherent way for a group to form beliefs, and indeed that no voting procedure can guarantee a consistent collective judgment -- which is why some have argued that collegial courts should vote only on outcomes and never on reasons."

Something in here makes me uneasy, but I can't localize it. The three-judge example seems airtight, and I'm inclined to accept the parallel with Condorcet's paradox, since I've seen the two discussed side by side. But the sweeping conclusion that no procedure whatsoever can guarantee consistency strikes me as stronger than any single example could establish, and the recommendation that courts vote only on outcomes sounds like it might be skipping over some costs. I'd rather be told I'm being paranoid than repeat an overstatement to the reading group.

Please respond as a philosopher who specializes in judgment aggregation and social choice theory, and audit the summary sentence by sentence: what is accurate, what is a common simplification an expert would let pass in a popular treatment, and what is wrong or misleading. I'd like particular attention to (a) whether the dilemma really is just Condorcet's paradox transposed from preferences to beliefs, or whether the two come apart in instructive ways, (b) what the impossibility results in this area actually establish, as opposed to what the book says they establish, and (c) whether the choice between voting on premises and voting on conclusions is as one-sided as the book implies, including what each option gives up. Think carefully about the logical structure of the three-judge example before assessing the generalizations built on top of it, and finish by telling me plainly how much of the summary I can responsibly reuse in my presentation and what corrections I should attach to the rest.
