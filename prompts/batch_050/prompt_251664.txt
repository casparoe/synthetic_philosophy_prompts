I am a high school junior preparing the final presentation for an independent humanities seminar on classical Athens. My thesis is that the executions at Mytilene were narrowly avoided, the Sicilian Expedition became a catastrophe, and Socrates was condemned not simply because Athenian citizens were ignorant, emotional, or vengeful, but because Athenian democracy had a recurring problem with what I call deferred accountability: persuasive speakers could win immediate approval for decisions whose costs fell later, fell on people absent from the Assembly, or could be reframed as someone else’s responsibility. I plan to argue that Mytilene shows how easily collective judgment could be formed and then reversed under emotional pressure, Sicily shows how democratic deliberation could intensify rather than restrain an ambitious commitment, and Socrates’ trial shows how legal procedure could give majority resentment the appearance of public justice. My conclusion is not that democracy inevitably produces mob rule; it is that Athens lacked reliable institutions for slowing decisions, distributing responsibility, and protecting unpopular people when public reasoning became morally charged.

The audience will consist of my history teacher, who generally defends the Athenian Assembly as more informed and institutionally sophisticated than its modern caricature; a philosophy teacher who cares about whether I am smuggling modern liberal standards into a very different political culture; a debate coach who will press me on whether deferred accountability explains all three cases or merely gives them a common label; and classmates who will ask simpler but potentially devastating historical questions. Please prepare me for the objections this audience is most likely to raise, ordered roughly by how damaging they would be. Phrase each objection in the vocabulary of the person likely to ask it, identify the strongest version rather than an easily defeated version, tell me what I should concede immediately, and give me a concise reply suitable for a live question period. Also indicate where each reply remains weak, which comparisons among the three episodes I should avoid overextending, and what single distinction I must make clearly for the thesis to survive.

Please include the factual challenges I should anticipate without turning the answer into a bibliography or asking me to cite specialized scholarship. In particular, prepare me for objections that Mytilene may demonstrate Athens’s capacity for self-correction rather than its failure, that the Sicilian disaster can be explained through strategic uncertainty and the removal or misconduct of particular leaders rather than democratic deliberation itself, and that Socrates’ conviction involved religion, wartime political trauma, and his association with unpopular figures in ways my institutional thesis may flatten. Finally, tell me which audience member is most likely to ask the question I am hoping nobody asks, what that question is, and how I should answer it honestly without abandoning my argument.
