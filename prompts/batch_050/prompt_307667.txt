Government regulators do something philosophically strange all the time and nobody blinks. When an agency weighs a new rule -- say a pollution standard or a highway project -- it happily aggregates huge numbers of tiny harms and benefits: a few minutes of extra commute for millions of drivers, slightly higher consumer prices, small upticks in headaches and lost sleep. All of that gets converted into dollars, summed across the whole population, and then traded off against "statistical lives" via a value-per-life figure, so a rule can get killed because the aggregate small costs outweigh, say, eight expected deaths. Yet the very same legal order treats it as unthinkable to deliberately torture one person even if doing so would produce arbitrarily large aggregate benefits spread across millions of others. So what does this practice presupose about the aggregation of harms? That minor harms are the kind of thing that can be summed and traded, but severe harms concentrated on one identifiable person are not? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories -- several might license the practice: threshold deontology, rights as side-constraints, a view where the intensity of one person's harm outweighs any number of mild harms distributed across many). Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes versus what merely fits comfortably with it, whether the two halves of the practice are even consistent with each other, and what would have to change in regulatory cost-benefit analysis if agencies fully internalized the verdict that one severe harm beats any number of dust-speck harms -- or, alternatively, the opposite verdict.
