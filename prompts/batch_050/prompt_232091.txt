Alright, background first: I spent forty years as a working biologist (population ecology, mostly seabirds) and only started reading philosophy seriously after I retired, so bear with me if I get some of the terminology slightly off. Here's the position I've come around to, and what I want from you is a map of what would count *against* it.

The position is basically the standard propensity line on fitness. The old worry was that "survival of the fittest" is a tautology: if the fittest are just whoever survives and reproduces, then saying the fittest survive tells you nothing. The propensity fix, as I understand it, is to treat fitness not as actual reproductive output but as a dispositional property -- roughly, an organism's propensity or expected reproductive contribution given its traits and its environment. On that reading, actual success is fallible, noisy evidence of underlying fitness, the way a coin's landing heads ten times in a row doesn't disprove that it's fair. Selection claims then become substantive and in principle falsifiable: a heritable trait correlated with higher fitness should, all else equal, spread. I've found this genuinely satisfying. It matches how I actually used fitness in models -- we always meant something probabilistic, never a post-hoc tally.

But I've been around long enough to know that a fix which makes everyone comfortable is sometimes a fix that's quietly removed the target. So what would count against this view? Specifically:

1. What observations, arguments, or formal results would show the propensity interpretation fails -- not just that it's incomplete, but that it's wrong or collapses back into the circularity it was supposed to escape?
2. Sort those by how decisive they'd be. If you think there are things that have arguably already happened in the literature or in empirical practice that should have embarrassed the view, say so.
3. What would the view's own defenders concede as counting against it -- not just what critics like to throw at it? I want to know if there's anything the propensity theorist is actually risking, or whether the dispositional move can absorb any objection (drift, environmental heterogeneity, tiny populations where actual success diverges wildly from expectation, the fact that we can seemingly only ever estimate propensities from actual outcomes) by adding another qualification.
4. If it turns out the view is well insulated, is that a problem for a view of this kind? As a scientist I'm suspicious of unfalsifiability, but maybe a framework principle or a definition plays by different rules than an empirical hypothesis, and I'd like that spelled out rather than assumed.

The thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a concrete case: some population, real or cleanly constructed, where the propensity reading and the actual-success reading come apart in a way that lets you tell which one the theory of selection really needs. Also, please answer in American English -- my spelling's been drifting between two countries my whole life and I'd like one fewer reminder of it.
