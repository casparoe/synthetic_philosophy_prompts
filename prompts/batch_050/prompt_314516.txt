I am preparing a two-page proposal for a twelve-month master's thesis in philosophy (roughly 20,000 words), and I want you to write the proposal for me. The thesis concerns Augustine's treatment of time in Book XI of the Confessions, and my half-formed idea is the following.

When Augustine asks how we can measure time given that the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present has no extension, his answer — developed through the example of reciting a familiar verse — is that we measure neither the passing sounds nor the events themselves, but the impression they leave in the mind: memory holds the impression, attention marks the present, and expectation takes in what is coming. My suspicion is that this answer contains two theses that are usually treated as one package: a metaphysical thesis (temporal extension is a distention of the mind rather than of things in the world) and an epistemic thesis (all measurement of duration is measurement of retained impressions). These can come apart. One could hold that impressions are what we in fact measure while insisting that they measure genuinely mind-independent intervals; or one could hold that time is mental while assigning the measuring role to attention rather than memory. My further suspicion is that Augustine is forced to separate them himself by a difficulty he raises but does not clearly resolve: impressions fade and blur, so the retained trace of an interval that passed long ago is not a stable standard of measure, and the account owes us an explanation of what does the measuring once the impression has decayed. How one resolves the fading problem, I want to argue, determines whether Book XI actually solves the puzzle of how non-existent durations can be measured, or merely relocates it from physics to psychology.

The proposal should contain: a precise statement of the central question, framed so that it has a determinate answer; an explanation of why the existing ways of reading Book XI leave the question open (in particular, why readings that treat Augustine as offering a purely psychological reduction of time, and readings that treat him as dissolving rather than solving the measurement puzzle, both fall short of settling the two-theses issue); the method, meaning what I would actually do with the text and with the secondary positions, and why close reading plus reconstruction is the right approach here rather than, say, situating Augustine in his Neoplatonic sources or comparing him to contemporary philosophy of time; a month-by-month plan for the twelve months; a description of what a successful result would look like; an honest statement of what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that the text simply does not distinguish the two theses; and a realistic assessment of scope, flagging anything in the project as I have described it that risks being too large for a 20,000-word thesis.

Do not include citations, footnotes, or a bibliography; I will supply those myself. Refer to positions and arguments, not to specific publications. End the proposal with a final line beginning "Core question:" followed by a single sentence stating the question the thesis would answer.
