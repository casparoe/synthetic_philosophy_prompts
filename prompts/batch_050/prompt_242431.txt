For my statistics class's end-of-year project, I ran a small experiment: I randomly assigned 60 classmates to study the same vocabulary list either in silence or while listening to instrumental music, and the music group scored meaningfully higher (p = .04 on a two-sided t-test). When I presented the result, my teacher said she assigns less than even odds to the effect being real, and she connected this to what she called the replication crisis in psychology. I had put my own credence at about 0.7, and now I want to understand where our disagreement actually lives.

Please give me your own credence that background instrumental music genuinely improved recall for students like the ones I tested — as opposed to the difference being sampling noise or an artifact of my procedure — and decompose the reasoning rather than giving me a bare number. Specifically, I would like a rough probability, with one line of justification each, for: (a) the prior plausibility that a modest effect of this kind exists at all, given how effects of this size and type tend to fare when retested in psychology; (b) the probability that a student-run study of this size contained enough methodological slack — flexible exclusion of outliers, optional stopping, choice of outcome measure, imperfect blinding — to produce a significant result even if the null hypothesis is true; and (c) the probability that the finding would survive a careful replication with triple the sample size, conditional on your answers to (a) and (b). Then show me how these combine into your final credence.

Finally, two things: tell me which term in the decomposition your final number is most sensitive to, and what I could realistically do — as a high school student without a research lab — to shift that term; and say whose number, my 0.7 or my teacher's below 0.5, more plausibly rests on the more defensible sub-estimates, identifying the specific sub-question where our disagreement most likely resides.
