I help run an intro philosophy reading group and we just did Boethius on divine foreknowledge and eternity. When we got to the part where God knows the future without causing it, I reached for the standard picture: God is like a watcher on a high hill who can take in the whole road at once, the bend ahead and the stretch behind, while a traveler down on the road only sees the bit around her current step. Her "future" is all equally in view for the watcher, so strictly God foresees nothing; he just sees, the way I see you across the room without causing you to stand there. I've been telling students this dissolves the foreknowledge problem, since knowing something doesn't cause it.

Then one student pushed back and now i'm not sure the picture earns its keep. Her objection, roughly: the watcher only has the whole road in view because every part of the journey already exists to be seen, so the analogy works by making the traveler's future as settled and fixed as her past, which is exactly the thing we were trying to avoid.

So audit the hilltop analogy for me. Lay out the intended mapping explicitly, a table is fine (watcher = ?, road = ?, the bend ahead = ?, the watcher seeing rather than causing = ?). Then seperate the disanalogies that actually threaten the intended use from the ones that are just the usual friction of any comparison. Candidates I'd want treated at minimum: the watcher still sees at a time, from a place, and God is supposed to do neither; passive seeing might be the wrong model for divine knowing anyway, since God's knowledge is usually cast as causally upstream of things, closer to an author's knowledge of her story than a spectator's knowledge of a road; and my student's point about whether "the whole road already exists to be seen" smuggles in the fixity of the future that the analogy was meant to explain away. End by telling me whether the analogy survives the audit, i.e. whether it can still do real argumentative work or is just a memorable restatement of the original puzzle. And if there's a better analogy for the Boethian move, name it: I've also seen the image of the still center of a circle with time as the spinning rim used for the eternal present, so say what, if anything, that one buys you that the hill doesn't.
