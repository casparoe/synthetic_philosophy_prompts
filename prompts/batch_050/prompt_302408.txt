Not sure if this is a thing philosophers have actually written about or if I'm just describing something everyone already knows. My friend and I argue about this all the time. Say she tells me a film is genuinely great -- not that she enjoyed it, but that it's a great film, and she can say why in detail. Here's the split I keep noticing in my own behavior: I'm perfectly happy to take her word for what I should watch friday night, i.e. I'll act on her judgment, buy the ticket, even tell other people "apparently it's amazing." But if someone later asks me what I think of the film, and I haven't seen it, it feels straight-up wrong to say "it's a great film." I can say "she says it's great" or "I hear it's great" but not just assert it myself. And it's specifically an aesthetic thing -- if she tells me the bridge is closed or that the restaurant is closed on sundays, I'll just repeat those as facts, no weirdness at all.

So there seem to be two different things testimony can do for you here: get you to act and talk as if something is good, versus actually count as your own aesthetic judgment, and the second one seems blocked in a way the first isn't. I've poked around and found stuff about whether you can know something is beautiful on someone elses say-so, but that debate seems to be about knowledge and my thing is more specific -- it's about this gap between behaving-on-the-verdict (fine, normal) and owning-the-verdict (feels like faking it, like pretending to have an experience you didn't have).

Does this gap itself have a name? And if it does, how does it relate to the more general pessimism about aesthetic testimony -- is it just the same debate in disguise, or a separate phenomenon? If nobody's named it, what would you call it, and where are its edges -- like, does the same gap show up for moral judgments (acting on someone's word that something is wrong vs. calling it wrong yourself) or is that actually a different creature?
