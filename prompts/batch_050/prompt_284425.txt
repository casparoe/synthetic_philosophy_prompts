I'm on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds conceptual work on decision-making under deep uncertainty -- our end use case is advising regulators who have to act on contested expert judgment (think climate tipping points or novel technologies), where insisting on a single precise prior feels dishonest but refusing to commit feels useless. We can fund roughly four people for two years, and I have to defend a slate at our next meeting. The candidate problems people keep pitching me are: (i) the updating problem -- the fact that conditioning a set of priors pointwise can produce dilation, where learning makes you strictly less opinionated in a way that looks paralyzing for policy advice; (ii) the decision-rule problem -- the proliferation of criteria (Γ-maximin, E-admissibility, maximality, interval dominance) and whether any principled choice among them exists or whether it's all just temperament; (iii) the aggregation problem -- how to combine several experts' imprecise credences without either pretending to a precision nobody has or ending up with credal sets so wide nothing follows; and (iv) the elicitation problem -- whether there's any empirically respectable way to get real experts to report sets of probabilities rather than fake-precise numbers. I'm open to being told the right target is something else entirely, but I want the prioritization argued, not surveyed.

Please pick the two problems we should fund and the one we should explicitly decline, and defend the ranking. I want you to commit to an actual slate, not present the menu -- and to make the criteria of your ranking explicit: how much of the downstream practice (regulatory advice, expert elicitation, the philosophical literature) is bottlenecked on each problem, how tractable each is for philosophers and decision theorists working without new empirical machinery, and which problems risk being make-work that wouldn't change what a regulator should actually do even if solved. In particular, I'd like your honest view on a worry one of our advisors raised: that dilation is mostly a pathology of naive updating rules rather than a deep fact about imprecise belief, and so funding it is funding a puzzle the field could dissolve by fiat. If you think she's wrong, say why; if you think she's right, say what replaces (i) on the slate.

Finally, tell me what would change your ranking over the next two years -- what theoretical result, or what development in how climate and AI governance actually use expert judgment, would move a declined problem up or a funded problem down. Assume I'm philosophically literate but not a specialist, so define the technical terms you lean on rather than gesturing at the literature.
