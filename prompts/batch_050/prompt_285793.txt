A few years ago I fell into a reading habit I never quite kicked: Indian philosophy, in no particular order, through library books and public-domain translations. I have no formal training — I work as a technical writer — but the school that has stuck with me is Mīmāṃsā, and two of its ideas in particular. First, the claim that the Veda has no author: not that its author is unknown or forgotten, but that there is no author at all, and that this is precisely why it cannot be charged with error, since every ordinary mistake in speech traces back to some defect of a speaker, and where there is no speaker there can be no defect. Second, the doctrine that cognitions are intrinsically valid: testimony counts as knowledge from the moment it arises, and it is falsity rather than truth that always requires some external explanation — a piece of testimony stands unless and until something specific defeats it.

Here is what I keep turning over. I use a large language model every day, for drafting and for checking facts, and its outputs seem to me authorless in a sense even stronger than the Veda's: no mind stands behind any given sentence, no one intends anything by it, and yet the sentences arrive fluent, mostly right, and occasionally wrong in strange ways no human deceiver would produce. So if Kumārila or Prabhākara could be confronted with this machine, what would they say its utterances amount to, epistemically? Would they class it with the Veda — authorless speech, immune to the charge of speaker-defect — or with ordinary human testimony, which as I understand them gains its force from the speaker being a trustworthy person who knows? If the latter, the machine seems to fail outright, since there is no person there to trust. If the former, I am uneasy, because the model is trained on human writing and presumably soaks up human error wholesale, which seems very unlike the Veda on their account. And how does their rule that testimony stands unless defeated operate when a machine is known in advance to be wrong some fraction of the time — does that background fact defeat every output before it is even read, or only the outputs one later catches out?

Please give me your best reconstruction of the Mīmāṃsā verdict, argued from those two doctrines rather than from generic skepticism about AI. Lead with your bottom-line answer in a sentence or two, then give the reasoning, and keep the whole reply under 450 words.
