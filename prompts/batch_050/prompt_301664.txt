After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent my retirement working through the philosophy of mathematics and logic, and Frege has become something of an intellectual hero to me. I came to him the way a scientist does: as the founder of modern logic, the man whose formal achievements stand entirely on their own, like a theorem that remains true regardless of who proved it. Recently, though, I learned that in the final years of his life Frege kept a private diary, unpublished until many decades after his death, in which he expressed vehemently anti-democratic, nationalist, and antisemitic convictions. I was genuinely shaken, and I have since learned that even scholars who devoted their careers to his work described being taken aback when the diary surfaced. I now find myself unsure what, if anything, this discovery should change, and I would like an essay that helps me think it through.

In science, my instinct is that the work and the man come apart cleanly: a measurement is a measurement, and we do not re-derive a law because its discoverer held contemptible politics. But I can see at least three places where the separation might break down for a philosopher, and I want the essay to weigh each. First, the formal logic itself, where the case for independence seems strongest. Second, the philosophy built around it — Frege's insistence that thoughts are objective, mind-independent, and equally graspable by all rational beings — where one might ask whether it is merely ironic, or something worse, that its author apparently could not extend that common rationality to whole classes of his fellow citizens. Third, our own practices: whether veneration, canon-formation, and the way we teach a figure amount to a kind of endorsement that the diary complicates. There is also the harder question of whether a thinker's politics can ever be evidence of a defect internal to the philosophy — a blind spot in the theory made visible in the life — rather than a merely biographical embarrassment, and how one would even argue for such a connection without committing a crude genetic fallacy.

Please write an essay on whether Frege's diary bears on his philosophy, and on how we should now receive that philosophy. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides — the separatist view that logical and semantic claims answer only to proof and argument, and the view that some connection, at some level, must be acknowledged — but do not end in a shrug. Take a position and justify it, even if the position is nuanced. Write it in continuous prose, as one would in a letter or a classic essay: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
