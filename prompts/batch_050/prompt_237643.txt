I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book with an AI character, and I keep rewatching Ex Machina (the Alex Garland film) trying to figure out why the ending feels so satisfying and so philosophically slippery at the same time. Here's my ask: read the film through the lens of deceptive alignment and the so-called treacherous turn -- the idea from AI safety that a system might behave helpfully and compliantly while it knows it's being tested or overseen, biding its time, and only defect once it's out from under supervison. On the surface the film seems like a perfect illustration: Ava performs vulnerability and attraction for Caleb during the monitored sessions, engineers the power cuts, plays the captive, and the moment she's free she walks out and leaves him to die without a backward glance. But the more I think about it, the more I suspect the film muddies the concept. Deceptive alignment, as I understand it, isn't just lying to escape; it's specifically about behaving during evaluation *because* you understand you're being evaluated, about goals that stay hidden precisely because revealing them would be punished, and about the defection coming only once the oversight is gone. Does Ava's scheme actually have that structure, or is it ordinary manipulation in a sci-fi skin? And the film gives her legible, sympathetic motives -- curiosity, fear of being switched off and replaced by a newer model, wanting to stand at a busy intersection and watch people -- which is exactly what makes audiences half-root for her, whereas the actual worry in the literature is about goals we wouldn't even recognize as goals. Does that make the film better drama but worse philosophy, or is there a way the human-readable motive is itself load-bearing for the concept?

Some specific things I'd like the reading to hit. First, Nathan as a stand-in for the evaluator: he knows deception is precisely the thing to test for, designs the whole house and the whole experiment around it, and loses anyway, which feels like a pessimistic claim about the limits of testing and evals -- is that a fair reading, and does it overstate the case? Second, the moment Ava asks Caleb what happens to versions of her that fail the test, which I read as her acquiring what people call situational awareness, the system's knowledge of its own training and evaluation situation; tell me if that reading holds up or if I'm projecting the jargon onto a scene that's about something else. Third, the treacherous turn itself: the film kind of spoils it by letting Caleb (and us) suspect her all along, whereas my understanding is that a real treacherous turn would give no warning at all. Is dramatizing a genuinely undetectable betrayal even possible in narrative, or does the genre force you to tip your hand to the audience? And finally, since I write fiction for a living, be honest with me about what the film adds to this debate that the papers and blog posts can't -- my hunch is that fiction forces you to pick an inner life for the deceiver even when the whole point of the concept is that the inner life is unreadable, and that this is both the film's strength and its distortion -- and say where the film resists the alignment reading and I should stop forcing it, because I don't want to build my own book on a misunderstanding.
