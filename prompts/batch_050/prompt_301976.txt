At a localization meeting for a math-learning app, a colleague ended an argument by saying, “language shapes thought,” so if a target language has no everyday word for “average,” translating our lesson on statistical averages will not merely be awkward; it will fail to make students think statistically. Someone else replied that the same slogan obviously cannot mean that monolingual English speakers are blind to ideas English happens to lexicalize poorly. Please treat “language shapes thought” as a piece of language in need of disambiguation, not as a theory name.

Write the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Menu of readings — about 350–450 words. Lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be making, varying along at least these dimensions: which features of language are doing the shaping (lexicon, obligatory grammar, metaphorical frames, orthography, discourse habits); which cognitive processes are allegedly shaped (perception, attention, memory, categorization, counterfactual reasoning, explicit inference); the strength of the claim (inexpressible, unthinkable, costly, merely primed, or only “thinking for speaking”); the timescale (online use versus developmental entrenchment); and the direction and reversibility (whether learning a new word, notation, or second language can undo it). Label each reading from near-platitude to radical.

2. Verdicts — about 250–350 words. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, plausible but narrow, contentious, or probably false, and state the kind of evidence that would bear on it without citing studies. Mark explicitly which readings are compatible with universal conceptual capacities and which require genuine conceptual inaccessibility.

3. Fit to the meeting — about 200–300 words. Say which readings would actually support my colleague’s conclusion about the “average” lesson, which would support only the weaker conclusion that translation and assessment are harder, and which would undermine both. Identify the smallest premise he would need to add to get from a defensible version of the slogan to “students cannot learn the target concept,” and say whether that premise is about words, grammar, practice, or measurement.

4. Imaginary evidence — about 250–350 words. Consider three clearly hypothetical results: (a) adults in a speech community with no dedicated term for “average” nevertheless pass a nonverbal estimation test but struggle to explain their method; (b) children acquiring a language with obligatory marking of information source are faster to report how they know something but no better at judging whether the source is reliable; (c) bilinguals shift risk judgments only when tested in a language whose future-tense grammar makes future outcomes feel temporally closer. For each, state which readings it would confirm, which it would merely be consistent with, and which would be left untouched.

5. Bottom line — 80–120 words. Give a replacement sentence my colleague could have used that is defensible, decision-relevant, and honest about what it does not show.
