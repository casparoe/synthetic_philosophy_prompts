people in my corner of the internet reach for Chesterton's fence constantly: if you find a fence across a field and can't see what it's for, you shouldn't tear it down until you've figured out why it was put up. It gets used to shut down reform proposals of every size -- someone suggests scrapping mandatory office days, or the filibuster, or some dusty clause in the student club constitution, and someone else just says "chesterton's fence" as if that ends the discussion. A friend of mine recently used it against a proposal to abolish letter grades, and it did feel like a conversation-ender.

But the more I think about it the more I suspect the analogy is doing alot of smuggling. So audit it properly for me. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the fence, the original builder, the cattle that would escape, the field, the passerby who wants the shortcut. Then tell me where it breaks. Fences have an identifiable builder with a purpose; alot of norms just accreted, or were built by people with purposes we'd now reject. Keeping a fence up isn't free -- it blocks the path the whole time, not just when you try to remove it. The passerby might actually know things the builder didn't. And so on. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the argument it's used to make (don't reform what you don't understand), and which are just pedantic?

Final question: once the breaks are on the table, what's the strongest version of the presumption that survives -- something like "understand first, then reform" is fine, but the fence picture seems to license something stronger, like "the burden of proof is always on the remover." Does that stronger version survive? And is there a better analogy for whatever does survive (a load-bearing wall? uncommented legacy code?), or is the fence basically irreplaceable?
