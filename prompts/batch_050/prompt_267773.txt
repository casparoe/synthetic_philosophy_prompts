For as long as I've followed politics, the argument about whether unelected judges should be able to strike down laws passed by elected legislatures has gone nowhere. One side calls judicial review elitist — a veto by a handful of lawyers over what millions voted for. The other calls it essential — rights would mean nothing if a temporary majority could abolish them at will. Both sides have been making these exact moves for generations, and nobody budges. My own suspicion is that they're using the word democracy to mean different things — one side a decision procedure, the other a set of substantive outcomes the procedure must respect — so they aren't really disagreeing about one institution at all. Is that right? Or is there a genuine shared question underneath: an empirical one, say, about whether courts actually protect the vulnerable better than legislatures do in practice, or a bedrock difference in how much each side trusts ordinary majorities, which neither can defend from neutral ground? I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, grounded in what each side really argues rather than in caricatures, together with an account of what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a body of evidence, a reframing of the question — or, if you think it's permanently stuck, a reason why no such thing could move it.
