So my friends and I got into one of those arguments that ate the entire evening: is history basically chaotic, where tiny accidents cascade into totally different worlds, or does it "re-converge," so that if one spark hadn't lit the fire some other spark would have? We ended up inventing this half-serious scoring system we called elasticity: take a famous historical hinge, flip it in the nearest possible world, and ask how different things look 50 years later on a scale of 1 to 10. A 10 means the flip genuinely reroutes the world (no World War I in any recognizable form, say), and a 1 means the same broad outcome arrives on schedule through a different door, because the structural pressures were doing all the real work and the hinge was just the messenger.

Here's our candidate list of hinges, and I'd like you to rank them by elasticity with a score and a defense of each, from most to least: (1) Franz Ferdinand's driver taking that wrong turn in Sarajevo in 1914; (2) Vasili Arkhipov refusing to authorize the nuclear torpedo during the Cuban Missile Crisis; (3) the fog on the East River letting Washington's army escape Brooklyn in 1776; (4) the Palm Beach "butterfly ballot" design in the 2000 US election; and (5) the briefcase being nudged behind the table leg before the July 20 bomb went off in 1944. While ranking them, please seperate out two distinct ways a hinge can score low: the outcome was redundant (some other assassin, some other crisis would have done it) versus the hinge never actually caused the outcome people credit it with (we overattribute). Also, which of the five was hardest for you to place, and what's the single most underrated hinge you'd add to the list -- one where the honest answer might be that it's more elastic than any of ours? Please answer in American English.
