I'm on the committee writing this year's epistemology comprehensive exam for our PhD students, and I've been assigned the luminosity/KK question. This year's cohort has been through the standard material -- the KK principle, Williamson's margin-for-error argument, the usual objections to it -- and our worry is that any question we set can be answered by reproducing seminar notes. I'd like you to draft one question, answerable in 90 minutes, that cannot be. Here's my suggestion for how to force the issue, though you should feel free to improve on it: stipulate a concrete case of a condition that changes gradually over a fixed series of moments (say, a quality-control inspector watching a screen whose brightness dims imperceptibly between successive checks, with the condition being "the screen is lit enough to read the serial number"), and ask candidates to (a) state precisely what it would be for that condition to be luminous, (b) construct the strongest version of the argument that the condition is not luminous, making explicit every premise that does real work -- especially the margin-for-error principle and whatever connects it to knowledge rather than mere belief, and (c) then use the result to say exactly what becomes of the KK principle, distinguishing the claim "if one knows, one is in a position to know that one knows" from the stronger claim that knowledge is luminous. The question should additionally require candidates to identify which premise they think is most vulnerable and to say whether the argument depends essentially on the gradual-series structure or generalizes to single-shot cases -- because in my experience candidates who have merely memorized the argument fall apart at exactly this point.

Please provide, alongside the question itself: a model answer of roughly 600 words written at the level of a pass-with-distinction candidate, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with marks for each (totaling 20), plus a one-line note per point on how candidates typically get it wrong. The scheme should make it possible to distinguish, in particular, a candidate who understands why the margin-for-error principle is a constraint on knowledge (and not just on rational belief) from one who is reciting it. Where a move in the model answer rests on a genuinely contested point in the literature -- for instance, whether the safety-style premise is independently plausible or is just anti-luminosity in disguise -- flag it as contested rather than presenting it as settled doctrine, so that the markers know what candidates may legitimately dispute without penalty.

The whole thing should be in American English, and the question wording should be self-contained: candidates should not need to have read any particular paper beyond knowing what the KK principle is and having encountered the idea that some conditions are such that whenever they obtain, one is in a position to know that they obtain.
