You are a philosopher of probability with expertise in the foundations of objective chance and credence. Before answering, spend a short brainstorming pass listing candidate requirements, then organize them into a polished specification.

I want a desiderata list for a theory of rational deference to objective chance — that is, for whatever principle plays the role Lewis's Principal Principle was meant to play, linking what an agent knows about the chances to what her credences ought to be. Tell me what any adequate such theory must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. At minimum, your list should engage with the following pressure points, and you should add any I've missed: (i) it must say what happens when an agent has information about the future that doesn't come through the chances (the problem of "inadmissible" information), without making deference vacuous; (ii) it must handle undermining — cases where the present chance distribution assigns positive probability to futures in which that very distribution is not the true one; (iii) it must say something non-embarrassing about statistical mechanics, where chances seem to depend on a low-entropy past whose status as chancy or fixed is disputed; (iv) it must either accommodate or explicitly exclude self-locating applications, where "the chance that I am in this branch" seems to behave differently from ordinary chance; and (v) it must explain why deference to chances is rational at all, rather than merely stipulating it.

For each desideratum, state the motivating case, whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a parochial demand of one camp (e.g., Humean vs. non-Humean about chance), and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list itself turns out to be inconsistent, that is a result I want to see. Then, briefly and only after the list is complete, indicate how a roughly Lewisian package and one prominent rival fare against it. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard.
