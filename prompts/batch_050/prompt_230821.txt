I want pointed, line-by-line comments rather than an overall impression, and I want to know whether my own reading has missed anything, in either direction — credit under- or over-awarded.

Exam question (30 points total: a, 12; b, 10; c, 8): (a) State Curry's paradox and give its derivation in full, identifying every principle — of truth or of logic — that the derivation depends on. (b) State Yablo's paradox and show that it produces a contradiction. (c) In light of each paradox, which proposed solutions to the semantic paradoxes come under pressure, and which emerge untouched? Explain.

Student's answer:

(a) Curry's paradox. Fix any sentence p — say, that snow is black. Let C be the sentence: "If this very sentence is true, then p." Suppose C is true. Then what C says is the case; that is, if C is true, then p. By modus ponens, p. So from the assumption that C is true we have derived p, which means the conditional "if C is true, then p" holds outright. But that conditional is exactly what C says, so C is true. Applying modus ponens again, we get p. Since p was chosen arbitrarily, every sentence whatsoever is true. What is notable is what the proof does not use: no negation, no reductio, no ex falso. It runs entirely on the truth schema (a sentence is true if and only if what it says is the case), modus ponens, and conditional proof. This is why Curry is more threatening than the liar: the usual escapes that tinker with negation or bivalence are simply irrelevant, and the only remedies on the table are to restrict the truth schema or to restrict modus ponens.

(b) Yablo's paradox. Consider an infinite sequence of sentences S₁, S₂, S₃, …, where each sentence says that every sentence coming after it is false — each sentence, so to speak, accuses its immediate successor. Suppose some sentence in the sequence, Sₙ, is true. Then everything after Sₙ is false, including Sₙ₊₁. But if Sₙ₊₁ is false, then what it says is not the case, so some sentence after Sₙ₊₁ is true, which contradicts our conclusion that everything after Sₙ is false. So no sentence in the sequence is true; all of them are false. Now look at S₁. Every sentence after it is false — which is precisely what S₁ says — so S₁ is true after all. Contradiction. Note that the infinitude of the sequence is essential: in a finite version the last sentence would be vacuously true and the construction would come to nothing.

(c) Consequences. Curry's paradox puts pressure on any view that blames the paradoxes on the behaviour of negation — truth-value gaps or gluts do nothing to stop a derivation that never mentions negation — and it forces a choice between restricting the truth schema and restricting the logic of the conditional. Yablo's paradox puts pressure on the popular diagnosis that semantic paradoxes are always the product of self-reference or circularity, since no sentence in the sequence refers to itself. Both paradoxes leave Tarski-style hierarchical solutions untouched, since a stratified truth predicate permits neither the Curry sentence nor the Yablo sequence to be formulated in the first place.
