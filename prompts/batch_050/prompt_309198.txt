I'm TA-ing a discussion section on the Hart-Devlin debate and I keep getting stuck on one sentence. In chapter 4 of Mill's On Liberty (the chapter on the limits of society's authority over the individual), he writes:

"He is the person most interested in his own well-being: the interest which any other person, except in cases of strong personal attachment, can have in it, is trifling, compared with that which he himself has; the interest which society has in him individually (except as to his conduct to others) is fractional, and altogether indirect: while, with respect to his own feelings and circumstances, the most ordinary man or woman has means of knowledge immeasurably surpassing those that can be possessed by any one else."

The part doing the work for me is "fractional, and altogether indirect." Here's my problem. When people wheel Mill out against Devlin, this sentence gets treated as the knockout: society's stake in what I do alone is tiny, so the criminal law has no business there. But as I understand Devlin's actual argument, it was never that society has a big stake in each individual's private vice. It's that a shared morality is load-bearing for the society itself, and enough private vice, tolerated long enough, rots the shared thing. That claim isn't about anyone's individual stake being large. It's about an aggregate or structural effect that never shows up in any single person's ledger entry.

So what I want explained, at the level of intuition rather than technical apparatus:

1. Is there a real ambiguity in "the interest which society has in him" between a ledger reading (add up everyone's stake in this one person's conduct and the total is small) and an infrastructure reading (society's stake in the background norms that each act slightly reinforces or slightly erodes, which could be enormous even when every single act is harmless)? Or am I inventing a distinction the sentence can't support?

2. If the infrastructure reading is coherent, does Mill's sentence just miss Devlin's point rather than answer it? My hunch is Mill would say the infrastructure stuff is exactly what he concedes under "altogether indirect" and then discounts — but I can't tell if that's a reply or just a restatement of the disagreement with extra steps.

3. A test case I made up to stress this: suppose some entirely private, harmless-in-itself habit (some odd solitary ritual nobody else ever sees) has the statistical property that once more than about a third of a population practices it, a genuinely valuable public norm — say the default expectation that people keep small everyday promises — measurably withers. Each act is like one more car on the road: harmless alone, gridlock in aggregate. Call this the norm-pollution model. On the ledger reading the law should leave every practitioner alone; on the infrastructure reading, prohibition looks at least arguabley on the table. Which reading is Mill's sentence actually committed to, and can the harm principle survive the norm-pollution model without being amended into something Hart wouldn't recognise?

4. And given all that: was Hart's demand for actual evidence that society disintegrates when its morality changes the right move against Devlin, or does making that demand tacitly concede the infrastructure reading and reduce the whole dispute to an empirical quarrel?

Then give me a one-line bottom line in the form \answer{...} choosing one of: Mill refutes Devlin / Mill misses Devlin's point / the harm principle needs amendment.
