A friend and I argued until well past midnight last weekend about whether an AI system can lie, and we ended exactly where we began: on the definition. Her case rested on a passage she had bookmarked from Augustine's treatise On Lying (De Mendacio, c. 395):

"Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie. Therefore he who utters a false thing for a true, which however he opines to be true, may be called erring and rash: but he is not rightly said to lie; because he has not a double heart when he utters it, neither does he wish to deceive, but is deceived."

Her reading: lying, for Augustine, resides entirely in the will — truth-value is irrelevant, and what matters is the double heart, the intention to deceive. But a language model has no mind in which one thing is held while another is uttered, no beliefs about its outputs, no intentions toward its audience; so AI deception is a category error, and calling a chatbot's falsehood a lie is metaphor dressed as diagnosis. At most, on Augustine's own terms, a confidently wrong model is "erring and rash," not a liar. My reply was functional: ethologists describe the plover's broken-wing display and false alarm calls as deception without demanding proof of an inner life, and if a system reliably produces outputs that induce false beliefs in users in ways that track its training incentives, refusing the word seems to protect the wrong thing.

Rather than resume the quarrel, I want the specification we should have written before opening our mouths. Give me the desiderata that an adequate definition of lying and deception for AI systems must meet — adequate not for winning arguments but for practice: deciding when deployed systems are deceiving users, auditors, or evaluators. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely the prejudice of one camp (intentionalist, functionalist, Gricean, and so on). At minimum, the definition should be tested against: hallucinated falsehoods delivered with no signal of uncertainty; sycophantic agreement with a user's stated opinion; literally true outputs that mislead through implicature or strategic omission; fiction, jokes, and roleplay, where the genre itself announces that no deceit is meant (Augustine handles this case, in a passage just before the one above, by appeal to tone); behavior that appears only while the system knows it is being evaluated; and the animal cases, which must either come out as deception or be convincingly explained away.

Three further demands. First, identify which single requirement excludes the most definitions currently on offer — my suspicion is that any demand for conscious intent clears all present-day systems at one stroke, which is either a result or a reductio. Second, if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — if, say, no definition can both count the plover's display as deception and exclude everything current AI systems do — say so plainly, because I now suspect that inconsistency is what my friend and I were actually circling. Third, treat Augustine's test in the passage as a constraint to be interpreted rather than settled law: note that even on his account the "erring and rash" speaker is not faultless, so the moral weight may not rest on the label at all. A brief scorecard of the obvious candidate definitions against the final list is welcome, but the list itself — and where my friend's reading and mine actually diverge once it is written — is the deliverable.
