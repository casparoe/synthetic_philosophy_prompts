So I just started TAing for an intro environmental ethics class and the prof has handed me the homework grading with basically zero guidance — he definately did not give me a rubric, just said "grade them like a normal person would." Before I write comments on 40 of these I want to calibrate against how someone else would grade one. So here's the assignment question and one student's full answer.

Question: In his lifeboat essay, Garrett Hardin uses the image of a rich lifeboat surrounded by swimmers to argue against unrestricted food aid and open immigration. (a) Reconstruct the argument: what work does carrying capacity do, what does Hardin say would happen under each of the three options for admitting swimmers, and why does he reject the spaceship Earth alternative? (b) Hardin admits the people in the boat did nothing to earn their seats. Explain how he thinks the argument survives that admission. (c) Briefly: how does the lifeboat situation differ from the actual survival case of R v Dudley and Stephens (the Mignonette), and does the difference matter for what the metaphor can teach us?

Student's answer:

(a) Hardin's boat holds 60, has 50 aboard, and 100 people are swimming. The three options are: take everyone, which swamps the boat and drowns all 150; take exactly 10, which fills the safety factor and forces an impossible choice about which 10; or take none, which means surviving only by fending people off. Carrying capacity is what makes generosity self-defeating — past 60, rescue converts into universal death. He adds that food aid sets up a ratchet: populations grow past capacity, so each famine is bigger than the last, and a world food bank would be a commons that poor countries deplete while rich ones fill it. His positive proposal is that we think of Earth as a spaceship with fixed life-support, since spaceships have captains who enforce rules, and he suggests the UN could play that role.

(b) Hardin grants that the occupants got their seats by luck of birth, not desert. But he thinks the admission changes nothing, because the argument never rested on desert in the first place — it rests on the arithmetic of capacity. Yielding your seat to a swimmer doesn't dissolve the problem, it just changes who survives, and the guilt-ridden person who jumps in the water has done nothing for the other 99 swimmers.

(c) In Dudley and Stephens, three men and a cabin boy were adrift in an open boat after the Mignonette sank, and Dudley and Stephens killed the boy, Parker, so the rest could eat him; they were convicted of murder. The parallel to Hardin is close: scarce resources forced a choice about who lives. So Hardin's framework shows the killing was justified, since Parker was excess population relative to what the boat's supplies could carry — the court was just squeamish about admitting it. The lesson of both cases is that survival ethics overrides ordinary morality when capacity runs out.
