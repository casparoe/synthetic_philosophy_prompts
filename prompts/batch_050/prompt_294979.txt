I run a small community credit union, and lately I've been arguing with a friend about the scoring algorithms that lenders and courts use to assess people. His view is that any difference in how often two groups get turned down is proof the algorithm is rigged. I suspect the truth is more subtle, but I have no training in statistics, so I need a concrete example I can work through with pencil and paper to settle this. I'd like you to build one for me, to order.

Here are my specifications:

1. Set up a realistic lending scenario with two groups of applicants whose genuine repayment rates differ. Give a plausible, non-sinister reason for the difference, and stipulate that the historical data is accurate and that no discrimination has taken place at any point.

2. Introduce a risk score that is honest within each group, in the sense that among all applicants assigned a given score, the actual default rate matches the score. I believe the technical term is calibration, but correct me if I'm misusing it.

3. Apply a single, identical approval threshold to both groups. Keep the whole setup under 150 words before the arithmetic begins.

4. Then show, with explicit numbers (say, 1,000 applicants per group, with full counts of repayers and defaulters above and below the threshold), that the rate at which would-have-repaid applicants are denied differs between the groups, even though the score is honest and the threshold is the same.

5. Then show that if I adjust the threshold for one group to equalize those denial rates, the score's honesty breaks, again demonstrated with numbers rather than asserted.

6. Explain why the conflict is structural: why it survives perfect data, unlimited computing power, and a perfectly trained model, and would only disappear if predictions became infallible.

7. Identify the single feature of your setup that does all the work, and tell me what happens to the conflict if that feature is removed.

8. Finally, say which of the two requirements you would relax if you had to lend money for real, and what that choice means for one concrete, named applicant in each group.
