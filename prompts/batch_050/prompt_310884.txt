Exam question: Judith Jarvis Thomson's famous violinist case and Don Marquis's future-like-ours argument seem to reach opposite conclusions about abortion. Explain each argument, paying attention to (a) what Thomson grants for the sake of argument and what her case is actually meant to establish, and (b) what, on Marquis's account, makes killing wrong in general and how that general account is supposed to apply to fetuses. Then say whether the two arguments genuinely conflict, or whether they are talking past each other.

Student's answer: Thomson asks us to imagine waking up connected to a famous violinist whose life depends on your kidneys for nine months. Her point is that since nobody thinks you'd be morally required to stay plugged in, abortion is always morally permissible, full stop, because forcing someone to remain pregnant is just forcing them to be a life-support machine. Importantly, she argues the fetus is not a person, so it has no right to life that could outweigh the woman's bodily autonomy. Marquis, by contrast, argues that what makes killing wrong is that it deprives the victim of a valuable future -- all the experiences, projects, and enjoyments they would have had. Since a fetus has a future like ours, abortion is morally on a par with murder in all cases, and on his view this also means contraception is seriously wrong, which he accepts as a consequence of the theory. The two arguments conflict because Thomson denies the fetus's right to life while Marquis affirms it, so they disagree about the moral status of the fetus and nothing else.
