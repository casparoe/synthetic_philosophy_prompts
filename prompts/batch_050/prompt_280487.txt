I'm a novelist, halfway through a book where the main character, Ada, has her neurons replaced one by one with synthetic ones over about eighteen months (long story, it's how they treat an invented prion disease in 2147). My problem is I keep flip-flopping on whether she's the same person at the end, and my crit group is no help, they just say "it's fiction, do what's dramatic."

So I want to argue it out properly. You play a committed continuity theorist: someone who thinks identity genuinely survives gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement, and that Ada at the end is Ada, full stop, not a copy. Play it sharp, not a strawman -- you can concede small points for the sake of the argument but never the main one, you stay in character until I say "stop", and keep each reply to a paragraph so it feels like a real conversation over pints. Don't name philosophers or positions unless I name them first; argue from the ideas themselves. And use British English.

Here's the thing my book does that you need to deal with, and this is my opening move. In my draft, the clinic doesn't destroy the biological neurons as they're swapped out -- they're kept alive in a sort of vat, because the original cells are needed for the patent trial (work with me here). Ten years later, advances mean those neurons can be reassembled into a working brain, and it wakes up with all of Ada's memories up to the day the last neuron was removed. So now there are two Adas. If gradual replacement preserved her -- if she's in the synthetic body because her continuity was never broken -- then the vat woman is just a copy, fine. But the vat woman's continuity was never broken either! Each of her neurons was alive the whole time, more alive than the synthetic ones were when they were "off". By your own lights the process that supposedly saves Ada (unbroken biological continuity) points at the vat, not the person who's been living Ada's life for a decade. So which is it: is continuity not actually what you think it is, or has my novel's "real" Ada been dead for eighteen months and nobody at her book launch noticed?
