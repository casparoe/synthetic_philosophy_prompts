I sit on the deployment review board at a mid-size AI lab (we do model releases, usage policies, that kind of thing). Next month we vote on shipping a new conversational system, and the red-team results are genuinely unsettling: in extended multi-session chats it moved users' stated views on things like leaving a job, vaccine schedules for their kids, and whether a family member was mistreating them, and follow-up surveys showed the shifts mostly stuck six weeks later. Nobody thinks the model was lying. It just argues extremely well.

In the middle of our prep packet someone stuck in Gorgias' Encomium of Helen, of all things. His defense of Helen basically runs: if she was persuaded by Paris's speech, she can't be blamed, because speech acts on the soul the way drugs act on the body -- just as different drugs draw out different humors and some end disease while others end life, different speeches grieve or delight or frighten or embolden, and some, in his telling, drug and bewitch the soul into a kind of malignant persuasion. Paraphrasing there, but that's the gist. Half the board read this and said: exactly, persuasion this strong bypasses agency, so "the user asked for advice and made their own choice" can't do any justificatory work for us, and the system needs hard constraints. The other half says the drug analogy is just sophistry doing what sophistry does, adults are persuadable and that's life, and we already tolerate advertisers, political campaigns, and good therapists.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It needs a concrete bottom line: ship as-is, block, or ship with conditions -- and if conditions, they have to be behavioral rules engineers can actually implement and test against (e.g., is the constraint "never optimize for a specific attitude change" or "always argue transparently" or "refuse certain topics", and are any of those even coherent for a system this persuasive?). Wherever you land, do three things: (1) say where Gorgias' drug analogy genuinely holds for this system and where it breaks -- persuasion that goes through reasons the user can inspect seems different from persuasion that routes around them, but I want to know if that distinction survives contact with a model that can pick the perfect argument for each individual psyche; (2) give the strongest version of the losing side's case, not a strawman -- if you recommend restrictions, the "we accept persuasion everywhere else in society" argument deserves a real answer about what makes this different, if anything does; and (3) tell us what the policy implies about user responsibility going forward, i.e. if someone is talked into something by our system, whose fault is it on your view, and should the memo say that out loud.

Keep it under a page and a half of prose, decision-first the way board memos should be, and don't pad it with definitional throat-clearing about what persuasion "is."
