Having retired from a career in climate research, I have lately been working through population ethics, and I keep returning to the nonidentity problem: many of our policy choices determine not merely how well future people will live but which people will exist at all, so that no one in the worse future can claim to have been made worse off by our choice, since they would not have existed otherwise. Please respond as a professional philosopher with expertise in population ethics and intergenerational obligations.

The responses to the problem that I already know, and do not need rehearsed, are these: (1) Parfit's own impersonal response, on which what matters morally is the value of outcomes rather than effects on identifiable persons; (2) strict person-affecting views that bite the bullet and accept that, in the standard cases, no wrong is done to anyone; (3) de dicto or generic-harm accounts, on which one can wrong whoever will in fact exist without wronging any particular person de re; (4) threshold accounts, on which causing someone to exist below some decent minimum of wellbeing wrongs them even if their life is worth living; and (5) non-comparative accounts of harm, on which being in certain intrinsically bad states constitutes a harm without any comparison to an alternative in which one does not exist.

I want one further response that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or conjunction of items on it. Please think carefully: begin by brainstorming several candidate responses, then select the strongest and explain how it handles the two canonical cases — the young girl who has a child now rather than later, and the choice between a conservation and a depletion policy. Then say, honestly, whether the response is any good: where does it improve on the listed options, and at what cost?
