So I'm on the board of a small democracy-monitoring nonprofit (we mostly advise funders on where to direct grants) and we've got a split vote on a new country assessment. I want to figure out what our own track record actually commits us to. Our past rulings:

- Country A: the governing party used its parliamentary majority to redraw electoral districts, pack the constitutional court with loyalists, and put political appointees in charge of the electoral commission. Elections kept happening, opposition even won some cities. We ruled: backsliding.
- Country B: the president openly defied two supreme court rulings and got independent TV stations' licenses revoked through a captured regulator, but never touched election rules and his party later lost power peacefully. We ruled: backsliding.
- Country C: a leader with openly authoritarian instincts tried twice to expand executive powers by decree and attacked the press constantly in speeches, but the courts struck down both decrees, the legislature blocked him, and he complied with their rulings every time. We ruled: not backsliding, just ugly politics.
- Country D: the governing party won a genuine supermajority in a clean election and used it to pass constitutional amendments weakening judicial review, all following correct procedure, amendments not yet tested in practice. We ruled: not backsliding (yet), flagged for monitoring.
- Country E: opposition candidates were harassed and deregistered on pretexts, state media gave the incumbent ~90% of airtime, vote counting itself was probably clean. We ruled: backsliding.

The new case, Country F: the incumbent lost a close election, publicly claimed fraud without evidence, personally pressured regional election officials to "recheck" the results, and his allies filed dozens of meritless lawsuits. It all failed -- courts threw out every case, officials held firm, and he left office on schedule. BUT in the same period his party passed laws (all procedurally proper, still on the books) letting the legislature replace local election boards and giving it a role in certifying results. Those powers haven't been used yet.

Here's what I need: what should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Identify which of our precedents is the closest analog, spell out the two or three different rules our past verdicts are jointly consistent with (I suspect we don't actually agree internally on whether backsliding requires successful institutional damage or just serious attempts), say what each rule predicts for Country F, and give me your best call with the reasoning. And if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what principle we'd be signing up for with each ruling -- e.g. does ruling "backsliding" on F force us to reopen Country C, does ruling "not backsliding" sit badly with A and E, etc. Also: what future development in F would definitively settle it under each candidate rule?
