I'm taking an upper-division seminar in philosophy of mind organised around Dennett, and for my term paper I want to defend a position assembled from the following commitments. Before I write it up, I'd like an internal consistency audit — not a defence or a refutation of the overall view, just a check of whether the parts hang together.

(a) Mild realism about patterns: a pattern is real just in case some possible observer could describe the data more efficiently by using the pattern than by giving an exhaustive point-by-point inventory. On this criterion I take centres of mass, genes, and price trends to be perfectly real, not projections.

(b) Beliefs and desires are real patterns in exactly this sense. What a system believes is fixed by whichever intentional-stance interpretation of it is most predictively useful, and if two rival interpretations are equally good — each predicting everything the other does — then there is simply no fact of the matter as to which is correct.

(c) The physical stance is nonetheless privileged: it is the only stance that could in principle give a complete account of any system, and the design and intentional stances are epistemic conveniences we resort to because we are not Laplacean demons.

(d) Case verdicts: a chess engine genuinely believes its opponent's kingside is weak, and my thermostat genuinely believes the room is cold; these attributions are literally true, not merely useful fictions. But a galaxy has no beliefs, and no intentional interpretation of a galaxy is true.

(e) Methodological rule: predictive success alone settles intentional attribution; a system's internal architecture and its origin are irrelevant. Accordingly, I hold that the Oracle — a chess machine produced by a culture that never invented chess, assembled by blind hill-climbing on win-loss feedback, containing nothing anyone would recognise as an internal representation of the board — believes that rooks belong on open files.

(f) The design stance is legitimately applied to organisms because natural selection designed them, even though selection is a mindless, foresightless algorithm with no intentions of its own.

My questions: which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? For each genuine tension, say precisely what would have to be revised or surrendered to restore consistency, and what that revision would cost the position. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for any merely apparent tension, show the discriminating move that dissolves it rather than just declaring it harmless. Finally, propose the least costly revision package overall — but don't assume in advance which commitment I'm most willing to drop, because I haven't decided.
