I need some outside help untangeling something that comes up every year in the undergrad seminar I teach on collective responsibility (and, honestly, in a book chapter I'm pretending to write).

Some context. We spend a couple weeks on what a state owes for historical wrongs -- slavery, colonial expropriation, wartime atrocities, take your pick. The standard toolkit of real-world practices looks like this: official apologies issued in the name of the nation; reparations or restitution programs funded out of general tax revenue; commemoration duties written into school curricula. Meanwhile the very same legal-political order treats collective punishment as a war crime and builds its war-crimes tribunals on individual criminal responsibility -- the postwar tribunals were pretty explicit that guilt attaches to persons, not peoples. So, as my students never fail to point out: we refuse to punish a group for what its members did, but we're perfectly happy to send the bill for what a nation's long-dead agents did to taxpayers who weren't born yet -- including recent immigrants, and including descendants of the victims themselves, who pay into the same pot as everyone else.

Here's what I want you to do. You're a political philosopher who specializes in collective responsibility and has actually thought hard about how reparations programs are designed, so reason like one -- don't give me a book report. Before answering, brainstorm at least three candidate theories of collective responsibility that this bundle of practices might be presupposing, and only then evaluate them against the practice.

The core question: what does this bundle presuppose about whether a nation (or a state) can itself be guilty -- as opposed to its officers, its past citizens, or nobody at all? Some options my students circle around: (a) the nation or state is a continuing corporate agent that persists through total turnover of its membership, so it can owe and even be guilty the way a corporation can; (b) no one alive is guilty of anything, but some people innocently benefit from past wrongs, and reparations are just clawing back unjust enrichment, so the practice needs no nation-guilt at all; (c) something like that old four-way split between criminal guilt (the perpetrators), political liability (everyone in the polity, like it or not), moral guilt (those who failed to resist), and something murkier beyond all that -- where most of the practice only ever needs the second kind. (Yes, that's Jaspers. You don't need to have read him; the distinction as I've stated it is the whole thing.)

Then the part I actually care about. Tell me where the practice is underdetermined: which pieces -- the apology, the reparations, the curriculum -- strictly require the presupposed theory, and which are compatible with several rival theories. And check for internal consistency: the apology practice talks as if the nation itself did something and must answer for it, while the reparations practice can seemingly be run on pure benefit-clawback logic. If those two presuppose different theories of collective responsibility, say so plainly. Finally: what would the whole apparatus have to look like if we went full individualist -- if we denied that anything but a person can be guilty or owe? Would anything recognizable survive, or is the modern practice of state apology just incoherent on that view?

End with a single line in exactly this form: \answer{X: justification} where X is one of NATION-GUILT, BENEFIT-ONLY, or UNDERDETERMINED -- whichever best captures what the bundle of practices actually presupposes -- and the justification is at most fifteen words. Think carefully; the labels matter less than the carving.
