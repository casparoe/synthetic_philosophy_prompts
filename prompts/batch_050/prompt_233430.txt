I'm a retired population ecologist, forty years of counting things in the field, and in my retirement I've fallen in with a philosophy reading group at the local library. We got onto the question of whether a bigger population is actually better, morally speaking, and I realized I have strong gut verdicts but no idea whether they cohere. So here they are, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, is behind them. First: a couple deciding whether to have a third child, who'd have a perfectly happy life, at minor cost to themselves — I say good, maybe obligatory-adjacent, certainly not neutral. Second: a government paying people to have children who will live lives barely worth living, just to boost headcount — I say bad, or at least no good done. Third: one child with a wonderful life versus two children each with a quite good but clearly worse life, same total resources — I genuinely can't decide, lean toward the two. Fourth: adding a happy person to a crowded commune makes each existing member slightly worse off but raises the total — I say don't count it as an improvement, but also it isn't wrong if it just happens. Fifth: a world of one billion flourishing people versus a world of a trillion people whose lives are only just positive — I say the first is better, full stop, and I gather philosophers have a name for the view I'm rejecting here, but my ecologist brain keeps whispering that total biomass of wellbeing is total biomass. Sixth, the one that keeps me up: I think it's good when a happy child is born, but I also think there's no obligation to have children, and that a childless world isn't worse than an inhabited one in the way a miserable world is worse than a happy one — bringing people into existence seems to matter asymmetrically, good when it happens but not bad when it doesn't, and I know that sounds like having it both ways.

So: is there a principle that delivers most of these verdicts? The pair I'm most doubtful about is the first and sixth together — if creating a happy life is good, why isn't failing to create one bad? And my third verdict seems to be in tension with my fifth, since whatever makes me prefer two decent lives over one excellent one, iterated enough times, lands me in the trillion-mediocre-lives world I rejected. Please give me one or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could actually use them, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts each one forces me to give up, and what each predicts for a case I haven't asked about — say, whether it's better to extend the lives of existing people or to have more children, per unit of resources. I ran enough mark-recapture studies to know when my data don't fit my model, so don't be gentle.
