I am a PhD student in philosophy working on signaling theory and social epistemology. Write an essay about the following thought experiment and the questions it raises. Imagine a mature verification technology: a universally accessible, tamper-proof record that lets anyone check, at essentially zero cost, the truth of factual claims about a person's past behavior and accomplishments — credentials, donations, promises kept, effort expended. Costly signaling theory, including Zahavi's handicap principle, explains honest communication by appeal to signals whose costs are differentially borne by honest and dishonest senders. If verification becomes cheap and perfect, that rationale seems to evaporate. The essay should address three issues: first, which existing signals the theory predicts would collapse, and whether it also predicts the emergence of new costly signals aimed at qualities that verification cannot capture, such as intentions, loyalty, taste, or future dispositions; second, whether, on the theory's own terms, the adoption of such a technology would be a pure welfare gain, or whether it would destroy something socially valuable that wasteful signaling equilibria currently provide; third, what follows for how institutions should treat "decoupled" displays — credentials and expenditures whose cost has ceased to track the underlying quality. Weigh the competing considerations, but do not end on a survey of options: reach a verdict on whether the handicap principle implies we should welcome, resist, or merely redirect this kind of verification, and defend it.
