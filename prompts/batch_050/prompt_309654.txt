I'm teaching a small undergrad seminar on formal epistemology next spring, and I keep going back and forth with a collegue about Bertrand's paradox -- the random-chord setup, where you get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 for the probability that the chord exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle depending on how you parametrize "random." She thinks the paradox is basically dissolved: if you demand that your method be insensitive to rotations, translations, and rescalings of the circle, exactly one of the three answers survives, and that shows the principle of indifference was never broken, just applied carelessly to the wrong coordinates. I think that's a cheat. You only know which symmetry group is the "right" one when the physical geometry is already sitting in front of you, and in the cases where indifference is actually supposed to earn its keep -- the probability that an unknown physical constant is even, that the ratio of two unknown rates is less than ten, that a coin you've never seen is biased -- there's no circle to be invariant over, and any symmetry you invoke was smuggled in from somewhere.

Since we can't settle it over coffee, draft the joint statement that a proper adversarial collaboration between the two of us would produce. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims we could both sign -- what Bertrand's paradox does and doesn't demonstrate, which uses of symmetry arguments are uncontroversial even to a skeptic, and which fallback tools (likelihood-only inference, Jeffreys-style priors, algorithmic/Solomonoff-flavored priors, or just refusing to assign priors in some domains) each side concedes as legitimate at least sometimes; (2) a table of the residual disagreements, each row phrased so that each of us would accept the wording of our own position, with two extra columns: what theorem, example, or thought experiment both of us would accept as bearing on the dispute, and what each side predicts or commits to there; (3) a short closing paragraph flagging any part of our fight you think is actually verbal rather than substantive, with your reasoning. Don't adjudicate a winner -- the test of the document is whether a symmetry-first objective Bayesian and an indifference skeptic could both put their names on it.
