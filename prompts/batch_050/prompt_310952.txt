I am a literary translator by profession; for twenty years I have earned my living deciding when to follow an author's words word by word and when to abandon the words in order to carry over what I take to be the sense. Recently the question has stopped being merely professional. I sit on the council of an international translators' association whose founding statutes were drafted in French in the 1960s, and I prepared the official English translation myself. A dispute has now broken out over a clause governing proxy voting, where the French text is elliptical and I supplied what I took to be the drafters' evident intention rather than their literal words. One faction says a translation must answer to the words on the page; the other says it must answer to what the drafters were trying to accomplish. A colleague who studied law tells me I have stumbled, in miniature, into the standing legal controversy between textualism and purposivism, and that judges and legal scholars have refined both positions far beyond our corridor quarrel.

I would like you to restate that legal debate in the vocabulary of my own craft, as systematically and honestly as you can. I know the translation side fluently: literal versus sense-for-sense rendering, fidelity to the author versus service to the reader, the pull between domesticating a text and leaving it strange, the way genre conventions (a poem, a manual, a contract) license very different freedoms, the moments when a translator must simply decide and take responsibility for the decision. I know the legal side only at the level of my colleague's summary: textualists say the enacted words govern and that purpose-talk dissolves law into judges' preferences; purposivists say words mean something only in light of what they were for, and that literalism courts absurdity.

So I am asking for three things. First, the translation itself: give me textualism and purposivism as positions about how to translate a text, one by one, as faithfully as you can manage. Where does the textualist's insistence on the enacted words correspond to my literalism, and where — this is my suspicion — does it correspond instead to something subtler, like fidelity to what a text would have meant to its original readership rather than to its author's private intention? Second, the distortions: tell me where you had to bend a position to make it fit my vocabulary. I suspect, for instance, that the legal textualist's distrust of legislative history has no clean counterpart in my world, since I can often simply write to the author and ask; and that the purposivist's worry about absurd results maps only partially onto my license to smooth over an author's infelicities. Third, and most important to me, the residue report: tell me what, if anything, in the legal debate has no expression at all in the translator's vocabulary. My colleagues and I argue as if fidelity were the only axis. But law is coercive, its authors are assemblies rather than persons, many of them are dead, and the readers of a statute must be able to rely on it without consulting anyone. Do these features carry real philosophical weight in the textualism–purposivism controversy, so that my craft's vocabulary is genuinely missing something? Or is the legal debate, once stripped of them, really just our old quarrel about literal versus faithful translation, dressed in robes? If the residue is real, I want your judgment on whether it shows translation theory to be incomplete, or whether it shows instead that the lawyers are disagreeing about something a translator simply never has to face.

Please give your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write me a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
