I keep hearing “equality of opportunity, not equality of outcome” offered as the crisp reason the American attitude toward inequality is more defensible than the European one. But the slogan seems to smuggle in several different claims. Unpack it into precise theses: e.g. outcomes should not be made identical; unequal outcomes are fine if the competition was formally open; they are fine only if childhood circumstances did not predictably determine who wins; outcomes should track effort or contribution; a high floor matters more than a low ceiling; or large inequalities are bad because they convert into power, status, and opportunities for the next generation.

For each version, say whether it is nearly platitudinous, genuinely contested, or straightforwardly false, and distinguish “everyone had a chance” from “everyone had a comparably good chance.” Then explain which interpretation would actually justify familiar American policies -- weaker redistribution, tolerance of very high pay, heavy reliance on markets and philanthropy -- and which would instead push toward European-style healthcare, education, wage compression, and social insurance. Please don’t turn this into USA good/bad versus Europe good/bad or rely on exact statistics. I’m probly most interested in whether the slogan covertly assumes that opportunity can be equal while outcomes remain highly hereditary, and whether that assumption is coherent across generations.
