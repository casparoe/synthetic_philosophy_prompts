Since retiring from my old virology lab I've been reading more philosophy, and recently I stumbled on the idea of the unilateralist's curse. As I understand it: when many actors each have the power to single-handedly undertake some initiative whose consequences everyone shares, the initiative will tend to be carried out by whoever most overestimates its value — because everyone's estimate is noisy, and it's the highest estimate that wins. So the thing happens even if the majority thinks it's a bad idea. A neat but slightly depressing result.

I'd like you to analyze the 2011-2012 gain-of-function controversy through this lens. That's the episode where two labs (Fouchier in Rotterdam, Kawaoka in Wisconsin) engineered H5N1 bird flu to be transmissible between ferrets, a US biosecurity advisory board first recommended the methods be withheld from publication and then reversed itself, the researchers declared a voluntary moratorium, and eventually the papers came out in full. Please don't just retell the history — I mostly know it. What I want is the mapping: who exactly are the agents who could act unilaterally here (the labs? the journals? the funders? a single coauthor or a whistleblower?), and does the curse model really apply when there are deliberative institutions sitting between the agent and the act? Then run it the other direction: does the episode confirm the model, or show where it breaks — e.g., maybe the curse is strongest precisely before review boards exist and weakest where the episode ended up? Finally, what would the model say about whether moratoria and national review boards are the right fix, versus just raising the threshold for action while leaving the winner's-curse dynamic in place?
