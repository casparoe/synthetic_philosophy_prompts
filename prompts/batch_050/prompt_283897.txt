I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how scientists and engineers explain things at different "levels," and I keep tripping over a move I don't have good vocabulary for. Here's the pattern. Ask why the chess engine sacrificed its bishop, and you get an answer in terms of strategy: it calculated that the sacrifice opens the king's file. Ask why the thermostat turned the furnace on, and you get: it measured the temperature dropping below the set point. Nobody answers the chess question by talking about electrons moving through silicon gates, even though in some sense that's all that physically happened. And here's the part that interests me: the high-level answer doesn't feel like a mere summary or shorthand for the low-level one. It feels like it's doing real explanatory work -- like the plan or the set point is the thing that's actually in charge, and the electrons are just the medium it works through. Some people I interview talk as if the high-level stuff literally pushes the low-level stuff around (one AI researcher told me the algorithm "makes the hardware do things"), and others say that's loose talk and only the bottom level ever causes anything.

What I want to know: is there an established name for the position that the high-level story can be genuinely, literally causal -- not just a convenient redescription -- even though everything at the bottom level is fully determined by physics? I know there are names for adjacent views: the view that mental or abstract stuff is causally inert has one (starts with epi-, I think), and plain "it's all just particles" reductionism obviously has one. But I'm asking about the middle position, where the high level is real enough to be the actual cause of outcomes, without any spooky extra force being injected into the hardware. If that position has a name, tell me what it is and who defends it. If it doesn't -- if it turns out to be incoherent or to collapse into one of the other two -- I'd love an argument showing where it collapses, because intuitively the chess-engine case really does seem like the strategy is what's running the show, and my piece kind of hinges on whether that intuition survives scrutiny. Also, as a side question: does it matter for any of this whether the system is a brain, a computer, or a thermostat? Some of my sources act like cognition changes the answer and I can't tell if that's a principled distinction or just sentiment.
