I am a retired molecular biologist who has lately been reading philosophy, and I keep running into a distinction in my old field that I cannot find a proper name for, though I suspect philosophers must have one. Consider three cases. First, an allergist tells a patient that her immune system has "mistaken" harmless pollen for a parasite and is overreacting. Second, an entomologist explains moths circling a porch lamp by saying their navigation machinery is being "fooled," treating the lamp as if it were the distant moon. Third, a ball bearing rolls off a tilted table and lands on the floor, and nobody -- not even whimsically -- says that gravity or the bearing got anything wrong. The moth's circling and the ball's rolling are both fully law-abiding outcomes; the machinery in each case did exactly what physics dictated. Yet biologists speak of the first two cases in a vocabulary of error, success, and getting it right that they never apply to the third, and this seems to turn on something like what the mechanism is for -- though saying that raises a further puzzle, since the same mechanism does many things (the heart pumps blood and also makes a thumping noise), and only one of these is treated as its job such that failure at it counts as malfunction rather than mere breakdown. Is there an established name for whatever it is that licenses error-talk for some systems and not others, and is the relevant dividing line drawn by a system's history, by its present organization, or by something in our practices of describing it?

Please answer in American English, as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the nearest established concept or concepts, with a verdict on which fits the phenomenon best; (2) how the phenomenon differs from each near miss, including plain reliability and mere breakage; (3) whether the correct account must appeal to a system's selective or design history, and what it should say about artifacts like thermostats and chess computers; (4) if no established term fits exactly, a coined term with a precise definition and two boundary cases, one included and one excluded; (5) the single hardest counterexample the proposed account faces, stated fairly.
