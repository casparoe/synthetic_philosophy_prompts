I help moderate a mid-sized online community that meets partly in virtual reality and partly in a shared persistent game world, and a recent dispute has split us down the middle. One member spent months building an elaborate structure in our shared world; another member destroyed it overnight as what he calls a prank, and when the rest of us reacted as though something genuinely wrong had been done, he argued that nothing was destroyed because nothing ever existed — that we were all upset over an arrangement of pixels with the same ontological standing as a daydream. Around the same time, another member described being harassed in a VR space and said the experience felt no different from being harassed in a room. I realized we have no shared vocabulary for these arguments: some of us think virtual objects are real but merely digital, some think they don't exist at all and only real-world effects matter, some think the harm is real but located entirely in the victim's psychology, and some think the social recognition of our community is what makes the sword or the building real in the first place. We are starting a reading-and-discussion group on this, and I want to build a decision tree we can walk through together at the first meeting.

Please write that decision tree. It should take a participant from the shared starting point — the building was built and then destroyed, the harassment happened in a headset — to a named position, with each branch node phrased as a concrete question someone can answer without already knowing which philosophical label the answer leads to (so: questions about whether anything went out of existence when the building was deleted, whether the victim's experience would have been different in kind had the events occurred face to face, whether an object that exists only because a server runs and a community agrees it exists is thereby less real, and so on — not questions like 'do you accept virtual realism?'). I'd like the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: lay out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space — virtual realism of the sort David Chalmers defends, the irrealist or fictionalist view that virtual objects simply do not exist, the intermediate view that virtual objects exist but are systematically not what they appear to be, and whatever distinct positions on virtual harms the tree turns up (e.g., that the harm is real but entirely psychological, that it is real and partially virtual, that it is not a harm at all). Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, note it and say briefly whether the gap looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored; and where two positions genuinely cannot be separated by my tree's questions, flag that honestly rather than inventing a distinction.

One more request, because this tree will shape our group's whole discussion: be explicit about the instrument's own fallibility. For each branch node where you think reasonable philosophers would dispute that the question is a fair or neutral way to sort the positions, say so and say why, and tell me what argument or consideration would lead you to restructure the tree — which cut you are least confident in, and what a defender of a position I've mislocated could say to convince you the tree, and not their view, is what needs fixing.
