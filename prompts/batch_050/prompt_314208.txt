I'm a journalist working on a long piece about machine creativity, and my reporting has led me into a conceptual thicket I'd like help sorting out before I talk to my sources again. Several of the researchers I've interviewed design generative systems that produce fonts: the system is shown a few letters of a new typeface and asked to extrapolate the remaining letters in the same style. Everyone agrees that two very different problems are in play here. There's the question of what makes something an 'a' at all — a hand-drawn scrawl, a serifed print glyph, and a graffiti tag can all be the same letter despite sharing almost no visual features. And there's the question of what makes something the same style across different letters — what carries over from the 'a' and 'b' you've seen to the 'z' you're inventing. My sources speak loosely of "letter spirit" versus "font spirit," but when pressed, they admit their systems mostly get this right without anyone having a crisp account of what the categories even are.

Here's where I get confused, and I'd like a careful explanation rather than a slogan. My naive picture was that 'a'-ness is a matter of resembling a template or prototype, but that picture seems hopeless given how wildly instances vary, and it also seems backwards for alphabets: arguably what makes a glyph an 'a' is not what it resembles but what it contrasts with — an 'a' must be distinguishable from 'o', 'd', 'q', and so on within whatever style is operative. But then something strange follows. Suppose someone designs a font in which the roles are systematically rotated, so the shape we'd normally call 'a' is assigned to 'b' and vice versa. Is the letter identity in the shape or in the role? A related puzzle: Cyrillic contains glyphs visually identical to Latin 'a' that are, by alphabet-membership, a completely different letter. This suggests letter identity is constituted by a position in a system of contrasts rather than by anything intrinsic to the mark — but that can't be the whole story either, because when I handwrite an 'a' that happens to look exactly like my 'u', something has gone wrong, and the failure seems to be in the mark, not the system.

Could you explain, for a reader who won't have encountered this literature, how one might give a unified account of (i) category membership for letterforms across radical stylistic variation, (ii) style identity across letters of the same font, and (iii) the role-vs-shape puzzles I raise above? I'm especially interested in whether the same explanatory machinery — whatever it is, prototype-plus-variation, analogy, contrast sets, structural roles — can handle both the letter question and the style question, or whether those are fundamentally different kinds of categorization that font-generating systems happen to smear together. If the leading intuitive answers each have a characteristic failure mode, I'd like to understand the failure modes, since that's where the interesting tension in my article will live.
