I'm working on a paper attacking Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory of health -- roughly, the view that a condition is a disease just in case it reduces a part's or process's statistically typical contribution to the organism's survival and reproduction, where "typical" is measured against a reference class like an age group of a sex of a species. I want my objections section to have one objection in it that isn't already floating around the literature, so here's what I already have -- please don't give me any of these back:

(1) The cutoff problem: "statistically subnormal" needs a threshold, and any threshold (two standard deviations? bottom five percent?) looks arbitrary and smuggles values back in.

(2) Statistically normal diseases: dental caries, presbyopia, atherosclerosis in elderly populations are all roughly universal in their reference classes, so BST seems to miss them; conversely plenty of rare conditions (unusual but harmless traits) get misclassified.

(3) The reference class problem: whether something is a dysfunction depends on whether you compare against your age cohort, your sex, the whole species, and the choice of class doesn't look value-free.

(4) The homosexuality problem: on a reproduction-centered reading BST plausibly classifies it as a dysfunction, which most people now take as a reductio of the account rather than a discovery.

(5) The function problem: BST inherits all the baggage of the etiological/selected-effects theory of function, including distinguishing genuine functions from mere useful side effects, and that baggage is heavy.

(6) Environment-dependence: function is relative to an environment, so the same trait (say, sickle-cell trait in a malarial region) flips between functional and dysfunctional depending on where the organism lives, which seems to make disease status weirdly external.

So: give me one more objection to BST that is genuinely different from all six -- not a rewording, not a special case of one of them (e.g., don't just hand me another statistically-normal disease), and not a conjunction of two of them. It can be original to you; I'm explicitly asking you to go beyond the standard lit. Then tell me honestly how strong you think it is -- where it's most likely to fall apart under pressure, and what a Boorse defender would have to concede or show to make you drop it. If on reflection your objection turns out to collapse into one of the six, say so rather than pretending it's novel.
