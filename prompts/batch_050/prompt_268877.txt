I work on a small applied-ML team, and at a recent design review a colleague argued for relying on purely outcome-based reward signals by way of an analogy: overseeing a capable AI system, he said, is like a non-technical CEO overseeing a senior engineer. The CEO cannot follow the engineer's reasoning line by line and does not need to; management works by judging shipped results, setting incentives, and spot-checking occasionally. His conclusion was that process-based supervision is a temporary crutch we should plan to discard as our outcome metrics improve.

I've absorbed the standard points about Goodharting and reward hacking, and I'd like an audit of the analogy itself rather than a recitation of slogans. Specifically: give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the CEO, the engineer, the quarterly numbers, the spot check, and the professional culture the engineer was hired out of; then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that are load-bearing for his conclusion and the ones that are merely cosmetic (candidates that occur to me: the engineer has goals besides the bonus, human work products are legible to other humans, and liability and reputation discipline behavior even between reviews); then a judgment about whether the conclusion survives once those differences are priced in; and finally, if there is a better analogy for how outcome and process feedback actually combine in oversight, what it is and what design choices it would push us toward instead.
