In my capacity as director of our department's public colloquium series, I will be interviewing a political theorist next month whose recent monograph defends lottocracy: the replacement of elected national legislatures with a body of several hundred citizens chosen by lot, serving staggered multi-year terms, advised by a permanent expert staff, with competitive elections retained only for a narrowly circumscribed executive. She is an experienced and careful interlocutor, and in previous public appearances she has shown a consistent habit of answering structural objections to her proposal by cataloguing the failures of actually existing electoral democracy — a comparative move that may be rhetorically effective but concedes nothing about whether her own institution would work. I would like eight to ten interview questions that make that move progressively less available and force engagement with the view's internal difficulties rather than with the shortcomings of the status quo.

I want the sequence to include at least one question on each of the following pressure points. First, the accountability problem: if allotted members cannot be re-elected, sanctioned, or recalled, in what sense is power held responsible to anyone, and is the reply that accountability in mass electorates is largely fictitious anyway a defense of her proposal or an admission that both systems abandon it. Second, the agenda-control problem: members chosen by lot neither draft the staff's policy options nor set the chamber's agenda, so what prevents de facto rule by the permanent secretariat and the organized interests that learn to work it — and does her stipulated rotation schedule address this or merely redistribute it. Third, descriptive representation versus authorization: an allotted chamber mirrors the population statistically, but no member was chosen by anyone, so what becomes of the idea that a representative acts under a grant of authority from constituents — is statistical mirroring a substitute for authorization, or does her view in fact replace the concept of representation with something else, and if so, should she say so plainly. Fourth, the competence objection in its strongest form: not that ordinary citizens are unintelligent, but that modern legislation rewards accumulated specialization, and the classical Athenian precedent she favors operated in a society orders of magnitude smaller and simpler. I would like the sequence to close with a question asking what empirical finding — concerning, say, the record of existing citizens' assemblies and deliberative minipublics — would lead her to abandon or substantially revise the proposal.

For each question, please also supply a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, so that I am not left improvising when she reaches for the comparative move described above. I do not need a summary of her argument, background on the history of sortition, or suggestions for further reading; what I need is a coherent line of questioning whose internal logic tightens as the interview proceeds.
