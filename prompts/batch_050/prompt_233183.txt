I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and I'm designing a teaching tool for a graduate seminar on the psychology of humor. My complaint with the standard pedagogy is that the classic accounts — humor as an expression of superiority over the target, humor as the perception and resolution of incongruity, humor as the release of pent-up nervous energy, and humor as the perception of a violation appraised as benign — are always presented as rival explanations of "why jokes are funny" in the abstract, so students just pick whichever label sounds sophisticated. I want to force them to commit to explanations case by case, where the theories genuinely pull apart, and I want to expose that some cases are diagnostic while others are compatible with almost anything.

Please write a sorting quiz of 8–10 items. Each item should be a concrete, briefly described humorous episode — not a joke text to be analyzed, but a situation with a hearer, a target, and a reaction (examples of the kind of thing I mean, though don't reuse all of them: laughter when a pompous colleague slips on ice, nervous giggling at a funeral, a pun that lands at a departmental meeting, a roast among close friends where an insult gets a bigger laugh the closer to the bone it cuts, a toddler's pratfall, an anti-joke, laughter at a taboo topic after a period of collective anxiety). For each item, give three or four candidate explanations of why the episode is funny, each phrased as a plausible-sounding first-person account ("it's funny because...") that a student could endorse without knowing which theory it encodes — no explanation should name its own theory. The options should be designed so that each theory is clearly the best fit for at least one item, and so that at least two items are deliberately contested, where reasonable people guided by different theories diverge.

Then provide the scoring apparatus as structured data, in two parts: first a JSON object with the following fields — "items" (an array of objects, each with "id", "scenario", and "options", where each option has a "label" and a "theory" field naming which account it encodes), and "ambiguous_items" (an array of objects with "id" and "diagnosis", the diagnosis stating in two or three sentences which theories the item fails to separate and why, and whether the failure is an artifact of the case or a genuine overlap in the theories' commitments); second, after the JSON, a short prose note (five to eight sentences) assessing whether the four accounts are actually competitors for a single explanatory role or whether your own quiz design reveals them as answering different questions, and what that implies for how I should frame the seminar.
