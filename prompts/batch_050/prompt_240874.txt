I am an undergraduate in a seminar on population ethics, and we have just finished the unit on Benatar's asymmetry: the claim that the presence of pain is bad and the presence of pleasure is good, but the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to enjoy it, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone exists who is deprived of it. I want to figure out what principle I actually hold, so here are my verdicts on cases, and I would like to know what they add up to.

Conceiving a child when genetic testing shows she will live a short life of intense, untreatable pain: clearly wrong. Declining to conceive a child who would have had a flourishing life: not wrong at all, since no one is wronged by not existing. Conceiving a child with an ordinary life, the normal mixture of joys, frustrations, illness, and grief: permissible, and I resist describing it as a harm done to her. A couple who could conceive a happy child but would rather not: no duty breached, even if their reason is just that they prefer other projects. But here is where I get stuck: if everyone declined to reproduce and humanity came to a quiet end, I feel something of value would be lost, even though by my own second verdict no one would be wronged and no one's absent pleasures would count as bad. Separately, spending resources to relieve the suffering of a person already alive strikes me as obligatory in a way that spending the same resources to bring an equally happy new person into existence is not.

Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts, and does Benatar's asymmetry do it? In particular: if the asymmetry, together with his arguments about the quality of even good lives, forces me to abandon my verdict that conceiving an ordinary child is permissible, or my sense that voluntary human extinction would be a loss, I want to know which verdict the theory says must go and why that one rather than another. And if there is a repair, some weakened or restricted version of the asymmetry, that preserves more of my judgments without collapsing into the claim that we have positive duties to create happy people, I want it stated precisely, with the clause motivated by each case. Finally, tell me what the best principle predicts for a case I did not list: conceiving a child whose life will be happy but reliably below the average for her society, where her suffering will consist partly in knowing this.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
