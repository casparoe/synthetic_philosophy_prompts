I'm trying to figure out what actually seperates manipulation from ordinary persuasion, and I think the fastest route is to dump my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and ask what principle, if any, is behind them. Here goes:

- My high school history teacher opened every unit with some dramatic story designed to make us care. Obviously fine. That's just teaching.
- The charity ad with the emaciated kid and the sad piano music. Aimed straight at my feelings, not my reasoning, but honestly I think it's fine, or at least fair game.
- The car salesman who says another buyer is coming this afternoon when there isn't one. Not fine.
- My ex's classic "if you really loved me you'd come to my cousin's wedding." Not fine, feels like the textbook case of manipulation.
- My doctor describing a surgery as having a 90% survival rate rather than a 10% mortality rate, because she knows patients respond to framing. Fine, I think? A little queasy but fine, since she'd tell me either number if I asked.
- The donation page that pre-ticks the monthly-recurring box. Not fine.
- A friend who never argues with you directly but just asks gentle questions until you "realize" on your own that you should dump your boyfriend. This one bugs me: no lies, no pressure, and she's probably right about the boyfriend, and yet it feels not fine.
- My boss talking about what a loyal, tight-knit team we are right before asking who can cover the weekend shift. Not fine.
- A prosecutor's closing argument with the dramatic pause and the victim's photo held up to the jury. Fine, I think. It's theater, but it's theater everyone in the room signed up for.

The pair I can't easily reconcile: the charity ad and the pre-ticked box both deliberately route around my deliberation, so if that's the test they should get the same verdict, but my gut refuses. And the friend with the questions seems to pass every obvious test (honesty, no threats, my interests at heart) while still feeling manipulative.

So: is there one principle that captures these verdicts or nearly all of them? State it precisely, tell me which clause is motivated by which case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it has to overturn if any. Two more things: what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on -- my mom texting "you know grandpa lights up when you call" whenever I've been bad at calling, which is true, and kind, and also somehow a weapon -- and what does it predict for a case I haven't listed, like a fitness app that showers you with confetti and praise at exactly the moments it's calculated you'll otherwise quit?

End with a last line starting "Verdict:" that states your best principle in one sentence.
