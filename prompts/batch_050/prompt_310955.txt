I'm preparing materials for a comparative ethics seminar, and I've grown tired of the way the Mozi–Confucius dispute is almost always staged: the Mohist doctrine of impartial care (jian ai) is wheeled out to demand sacrifice of the family, the Confucian doctrine of graded love is wheeled out to defend it, and the students learn nothing beyond a slogan. What I'd like from you is a thought experiment that runs the confrontation in the opposite direction. Construct a scenario in which a consistent Mohist, reasoning from impartial care and its standard justification in the benefit of all under Heaven, is required to favour his own kin or protégé, while a Confucian, reasoning from ren, yi, and the obligations attaching to one's roles, is required to favour the stranger instead. The scenario should be realistic and contemporary or at least historically unremarkable — an appointment committee, the distribution of a bursary, the allocation of relief supplies; no trolleys, no omniscient narrators, no desert islands — and the whole thing should be statable in one paragraph.

Two constraints matter to me. First, neither verdict may be bought by caricaturing the doctrine it comes from: the Mohist's partiality must fall out of impartial care itself (for instance, through the gap between an ultimate principle of universal benefit and the decision procedure that principle licenses given information, proximity, and trust), and the Confucian's impartiality must fall out of graded love and rightness themselves (for instance, through the way public office disciplines private affection), not through one party simply being corrupt or confused. Second, the verdicts must be generated rather than stipulated: don't write a scenario that merely asserts what each sage would say; build the facts so that each conclusion follows.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. Walk through each thinker's reasoning step by step, showing where and why the usual verdicts invert. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and tell me what happens if it is removed — does the case collapse back into the textbook stand-off? Finally, steelman the obvious attacks from each side: the Mohist will complain that the benefit calculus has been rigged, and the Confucian will complain that the case only governs officeholders and leaves family primacy intact where it belongs. Tell me whether those complaints succeed, and whether the exercise shows that the two doctrines converge in practice far more than Mencius's famous hostility to the Mohists would suggest. Please write your answer in British English.
