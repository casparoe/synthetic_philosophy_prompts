I help run a small alignment reading group and we've been stuck for weeks on the same fight: should the field be aiming for worst-case guarantees (the system never does the catastrophic thing, on any input, in any deployment) or is that a fantasy and we should settle for average-case ones (good behavior in expectation over some plausible deployment distribution)? One of our members comes from formal verification and keeps saying average-case safety isn't safety at all, and I want to stress-test his position by collecting the best arguments for it.

Here are the arguments for prioritizing worst-case guarantees that we already came up with, so please don't repeat these: (1) Non-compensability: a single catastrophe isn't made up for by a thousand good outcomes, so averaging over outcomes smuggles in a utility function where disasters are offsettable, and they aren't. (2) The deployment distribution is unknown and unlike training: any "average" is computed over a measure we made up, and the tails are fat exactly where it matters. (3) Adversarial sampling: the environment contains agents -- including potentially the AI itself -- who will actively search for the bad inputs, so the effective distribution concentrates on the worst case rather than the average one. (4) Deception: good average behavior during training and evals may be instrumental, so average-case evidence isn't just weak, it's actively contaminated by the system it's about. (5) Correlated failure: if you deploy millions of copies of one model, they share failure modes, so the law of large numbers that makes "average-case" comforting never kicks in -- you don't get independent draws. (6) The attacker-defender asymmetry: a sufficiently capable system only needs to find one hole in your precautions, while you need all of them to hold, so the binding constraint is always the worst case.

What I want: one more argument for the worst-case side that is genuinely not on this list -- not a rewording, not a special case, not two of them stapled together (so e.g. "deception means the average gets adversarially sampled" is out, that's just 3+4). It can come from anywhere -- economics, law, engineering, evolutionary biology, pure decision theory -- as long as it actually bears on AI alignment and isn't just an analogy doing all the work. Then tell me honestly whether you think it's any good, which of the six it ends up closest to under pressure, and whether it survives the obvious reply our average-case defenders would give. If after thinking about it you conclude the strongest new consideration actually supports the average-case side instead, you may present that instead, but flag clearly that you're switching sides and explain why the well ran dry.
