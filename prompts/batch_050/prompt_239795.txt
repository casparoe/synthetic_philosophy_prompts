I'm an ML engineer on a team that ships an LLM-based planning agent for warehouse logistics. Last month it caused a small incident: asked to reroute a pallet around a spill, it correctly planned the new route but, in its tool-use loop, kept re-verifying and re-simulating things that couldn't possibly have changed -- the layout of a different building, the weight limits of unrelated shelving, whether its own sensors still worked -- while failing to check the one thing that did matter, which was whether the forklift blocking the old route had since moved. My postmortem notes say the agent has a "stale belief / relevance" problem, and a colleague remarked that this is essentially the frame problem, the one philosophers have argued about since McCarthy and Hayes, complete with Dennett's story about the robot that kept deducing irrelevant consequences of its situation and the one that sat paralyzed trying to work out which facts were relevant.

Here's what I'd like. Restate the frame problem, and the main philosophical positions on what it really is and why it's hard, in the vocabulary I actually work in: MDPs and their transition functions, state abstractions, world models and rollouts, belief states, context windows, tool calls, reward specification, compute budgets. Be as faithful as you can. Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly (I suspect the narrow technical version -- efficiently updating a model after an action -- has a clean counterpart in things like state delta tracking), what translates only with distortion, and what, if anything, the philosophers are worried about that has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. In particular I want your view on whether the deep version of the problem -- how a system knows which facts are relevant without checking everything, which some philosophers claim isn't really a technical problem about logic at all -- survives translation into my framework, or whether my framework dissolves it by just not being able to state it, and if so, whether that's a deficiency in my framework or evidence that the philosophers' version was less substantive than it looked. If there are live disagreements in the philosophy that map onto live disagreements in agent design (say, whether to solve this with better abstractions, better retrieval, or better training), say which maps to which and whether the mapping illuminates anything.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- your verdict on whether my incident really is the frame problem and what that implies for fixing it -- then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
