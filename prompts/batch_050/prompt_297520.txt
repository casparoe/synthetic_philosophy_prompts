I teach a second-year undergrad course that's basically "big ideas in philosophy of mind and biology for non-majors" -- the students are a mix of psych, CS, and bio kids, most of whom have never read a word of philosophy. For one week I want to build the whole session around Dennett's idea of a strange inversion of reasoning: Darwin showing that the competence of organisms can come out of a process with zero comprehension behind it, and Turing doing the same trick for cognition, where what looks like the product of understanding is really just mechanical steps all the way down.

Don't explain the idea to me, I know it fine. Plan how I should teach it in about 80 minutes. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to come in. My instinct is to start with the pre-Darwin assumption (competence is the trace of a comprehending designer or mind) so the inversion has something to invert, but I'm not sure whether Turing or Darwin should come first, or whether I should run one fully and then present the other as "same move, different domain." Tell me what depends on what.
- The one carrying example. I'm torn between building it on natural selection itself, on a simple algorithm like long division or sorting executed by rote, or on something like a chess engine. Which one can bear the whole weight, what does it need to have and need to lack, and what's the case against the runner-ups?
- The misconceptions these students will reliably form. I'm guessing at least: evolution is trying to get somewhere, the inversion means understanding is an illusion or doesn't exist, Turing's point is just "computers are fast," and somebody will conflate "no comprehension in the process" with "the outcome must be simple." There are probably others I'm not seeing. Tell me where in the sequence each one gets born and where I should cut it off -- before or after it gets voiced.
- One question or mini-exercise at the end that would show me whether they've got the inversion as a general move and not just memorized two stories about Darwin and Turing.
- What I should explicitly bracket at this level (e.g., whether this actually dissolves the hard problem, whether Dennett is right about consciousness) and how to say "we're skipping this" without the class feeling cheated.
