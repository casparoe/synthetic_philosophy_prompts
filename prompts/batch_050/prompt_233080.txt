Grade the answer out of 10, give me a definite number (no "somewhere between a 7 and an 8" -- make the calls and own them), and tell me wich errors are minor slips versus genuine conceptual misunderstandings.

THE QUESTION:

Read this passage from tractate Sukkah of the Babylonian Talmud (53a, Sefaria's William Davidson translation), an ancestor of the better-known Appointment in Samarra story:

"One day Solomon saw that the Angel of Death was sad. He said to him: Why are you sad? He said to him: They are asking me to take the lives of these two Cushites who are sitting here. Solomon handed them to the demons in his service, and sent them to the district of Luz, where the Angel of Death has no dominion. When they arrived at the district of Luz, they died... In the place that they asked me to take them, there you sent them."

Assume the decree is infallible: the scribes die wherever they were appointed to die, and the appointment is fixed before they deliberate. (a) Does the scribes' flight to Luz cause the appointment to be in Luz? Explain. (b) Show that once the scribes settle on a plan, that very fact gives them reason to switch. (c) Philosophers have used this case to argue that rational deliberation can break down when an agent's choice is predicted with certainty. Explain what exactly breaks down, and contrast the case's structure with Newcomb's problem.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:

(a) No. The appointment is made before the scribes deliberate, so the journey cannot cause it; causal influence doesn't run backwards. Their travel is at most evidence about where the appointment is, since whatever they do turns out to be where the decree pointed.

(b) Suppose the scribes decide to stay in Jerusalem. Since they know the decree tracks their actual whereabouts, settling on staying is itself evidence that Jerusalem is the appointed place -- so fleeing looks better. But had they settled on fleeing, that would be evidence Luz is the appointed place, so staying looks better. Deliberation never comes to rest.

(c) This is basically Nozick's Newcomb's problem transposed into a darker key: the Angel is a perfect predictor, and Gibbard and Harper borrowed the setup from Nozick's 1969 discussion. As with two-boxing, the causal structure tells the scribes to stay put: the decree is already written, fleeing changes nothing, so staying weakly dominates. The scribes' tragedy is that they reasoned evidentially, treating their own action as a symptom of the decree, and acted against their causal interests. The moral is that CDT is vindicated, and deliberation only "breaks down" for agents who confuse evidential with causal expected utility.
