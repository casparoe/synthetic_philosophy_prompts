I am putting together a graduate seminar on the epistemology of legal proof, and I would like your help stress-testing an argument I have drafted for the first session. Treat the following as a request from a colleague: you are an epistemologist with genuine expertise in the philosophy of evidence and legal standards of proof, and I want your most careful, rigorous work. Before committing to an answer, brainstorm briefly about the two or three most plausible candidate points of failure, then settle on a diagnosis.

Here is the argument, which I have come to suspect because its conclusion is one I refuse to accept.

(1) A civil verdict against a defendant is justified just in case the evidence presented at trial makes it more probable than not that the defendant is liable; likewise, a criminal conviction is justified just in case the evidence makes guilt probable beyond reasonable doubt. The standards of proof are probability thresholds, nothing more.

(2) Justification supervenes on the probability of the hypothesis given the total evidence, not on the character or provenance of the evidence that produced that probability. A probability is a probability, whatever its pedigree: if two bodies of evidence each establish that the defendant is liable with probability 0.8, they justify the verdict equally.

(3) Suppose an eyewitness, whose reliability under comparable conditions has been independently established at 80%, testifies that the bus that ran the plaintiff off the road was operated by the Blue Bus Company. This evidence makes Blue Bus's liability 0.8 probable, so by (1) the plaintiff wins.

(4) Now suppose instead that the only evidence is market-share data: of the buses licensed to run that route at that hour, 80% belong to the Blue Bus Company, and the plaintiff's own account establishes only that some bus on that route caused the accident. This evidence likewise makes Blue Bus's liability 0.8 probable. By (2), the plaintiff wins equally.

(5) The same reasoning generalizes. Consider the rodeo promoter who can prove that only 500 tickets were sold but that 2,000 people attended: for each attendee, the probability of having entered without paying is 0.75. If probability thresholds exhaust the standard of proof, the promoter may recover a ticket price from every single attendee, collecting 2,000 admissions for an event to which 500 tickets were sold.

(6) No acceptable theory of proof permits that recovery, and courts have consistently and rightly refused to impose liability on evidence of this purely statistical kind. Our practice is not up for revision.

(7) Therefore (1) or (2) must be false: legal proof is not merely a matter of posterior probability, and the probability calculus is not the whole of proof.

The conclusion, (7), is the one I am being pushed toward, but granting it seems to concede that the law's standards of proof answer to something other than the rational probability of the verdict's being true, which strikes me as a steep price — perhaps steeper than whatever (6) is really worth. My own sympathies run toward keeping (1) if anything must go, but I cannot locate the weakest joint in the chain.

So: does this argument prove too much, and if so, where? Identify the specific premise or inferential step you would reject, explain why it looked innocent, and then price the alternatives — what does it cost to reject the step you have chosen, and what would it cost to reject the neighboring candidates instead? If there is a distinction the argument trades on (for instance, between the probability the evidence confers and something about how that probability is supported, or between the strength of evidence and its weight or completeness), state it precisely enough that I could defend it against a colleague who insists I have merely redescribed the probability in fancier vocabulary. Finally, tell me whether your diagnosis of the civil cases extends to the criminal standard, or whether proof beyond reasonable doubt requires a different story.
