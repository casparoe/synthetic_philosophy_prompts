I'm teaching a discussion section on ethics and realized I don't actually know how to explain the idea of going above and beyond duty, so pretend you're a careful moral philosopher helping me get my intuitions in order. Please think through the cases before proposing anything; don't just give me a dictionary definition. My gut verdicts are these: A healthy person donating a kidney to a stranger is admirable but not morally required, so nobody is blameworthy for refusing. A lifeguard jumping into rough water to save a swimmer is just doing their job, even if most of us would call it brave. An office worker quietly paying a coworker's rent for three months so the coworker isn't evicted is beyond duty, even though it isn't terribly dangerous. A surgeon staying late to correct a complication caused by her own earlier carelessness is not beyond duty; she owes the patient that. Someone keeping an ordinary promise at great personal cost is still just doing what they owe, not being heroic. A very rich person giving away 40% of their income to highly effective charities seems beyond duty to me, although I admit this one mostly reflects my hunch that morality can't demand that much from everyone. A person who gives only $20 to charity but does so partly to look generous still does something good and nonrequired, just less admirably. Finally, if someone freely volunteers to be the only compatible kidney donor, then backs out before the recipient has relied on them in any way, I still think refusing is permissible, though if the recipient has turned down other options because of the promise, refusing starts to look wrong.

Is there one reasonably simple principle that captures all or most of these verdicts about what is required versus what is above and beyond the call? Please state the principle precisely, explain which cases motivate each part, and test it against at least two new cases I didn't mention. I'm especially interested in whether personal cost, special roles, past wrongdoing, bad motives, and the difference between being morally good and being morally praiseworthy should matter. If one of my verdicts has to go to keep the account from becoming a messy list of exceptions, say which one and why. Also, in the donor case, I want to say the initial promise creates some reason to follow through without making donation strictly obligatory; if that distinction can't be defended, tell me that too.
