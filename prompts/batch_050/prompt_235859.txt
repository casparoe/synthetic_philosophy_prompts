Two writers from my critique group got into a real fight last week and I've been turning it over since. One of them, Dee, is drafting a story where a rescue swimmer can either save one passenger trapped right next to him or swim farther out to a lifeboat with five people on it. She said the swimmer should obviously save the five, because five deaths are worse than one and that's just arithmetic. Marcus said arithmetic doesn't apply here: each death is only bad for the person who dies, there's nobody who experiences "five deaths" all together, so the five deaths don't add up into anything worse than the one. The extra four deaths aren't bad for anyone who wouldn't otherwise have something bad happen to them. Dee said fine, then by his logic the swimmer might as well flip a coin between saving one and saving five, or one and a million for that matter, and no hospital could ever justify triage. Marcus said maybe the coin flip is actually the fair thing, since each of the six people deserves an equal shot, and treating the five as five times the claim just uses them as containers of welfare or whatever. Dee said that gives the one guy a 50% chance against five lives, which isn't fairness to the five at all, it just pretends their claims don't stack when they plainly do. Then Marcus accused her of building the conclusion into the word "stack" and it went in circles from there.

Could you go through it move by move and say who's right about what -- not just who's more right overall, but which specific replies land and which dodge? And tell me what both of them might be missing, because I'm supposed to give notes on Dee's story and I genuinly don't know what to say about this scene.
