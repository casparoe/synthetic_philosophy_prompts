I am a PhD student in philosophy of language working on assertion, fragmented belief, and the normative status of Moorean sentences. I am trying to move beyond the standard treatment of Moore’s paradox by considering a case in which a person’s verbal self-ascriptions and practical attitudes appear to come apart.

Consider the following scenario: An emergency planner is responsible for deciding whether to close a coastal road. Her forecasting model indicates a 97 percent probability that a dangerous storm surge has already flooded the road. She closes the road, tells drivers to treat it as flooded, and acts exactly as someone convinced of the flooding would. Nevertheless, because she thinks outright belief requires independently verified evidence, she sincerely reports that she does not yet believe that the road is flooded. When asked to give an official statement, she says: The road is flooded, but I do not believe that it is flooded.

Write a dialogue of approximately 1,200–1,500 words between two equally sophisticated philosophers who disagree about what this case shows.

One philosopher should defend the view that the planner’s statement is genuinely defective: even if both conjuncts are true, the assertion violates a constitutive norm governing belief and assertion, and the practical evidence does not eliminate the problem. The other should argue that the statement can be fully appropriate once we distinguish outright belief from high credence, context-sensitive standards for belief attribution, or different functional roles within a single agent. On this second view, the appearance of paradox results from an overly simple picture of belief rather than from any inherent incoherence in the statement.

The dialogue should contain sustained back-and-forth rather than alternating speeches. Each participant must respond directly to the other’s strongest argument. In particular, they should discuss:

1. Whether acting decisively on a 97 percent forecast is sufficient for attributing outright belief, or whether the planner’s sincere denial reveals a separate doxastic state.
2. Whether assertion commits a speaker to believing what she asserts, or only to presenting it as meeting the operative epistemic standard.
3. Whether the case is best modeled using fragmented belief, contextualism about belief attribution, degrees of confidence, or some other distinction.
4. What changes, if anything, when the sentence concerns another person—for example, when a colleague says that the road is flooded but that the planner does not believe it.
5. Whether the proposed solution merely redescribes the paradox or explains why the original first-person statement sounds defective.

Do not make either participant a strawman, and do not let the exchange become a survey of named historical positions. The philosophers may mention familiar concepts where useful, but the dialogue should be driven by the new case and by arguments that a model could develop without consulting external sources. End with the disagreement genuinely sharpened: the speakers need not reach agreement, but by the end each should have conceded at least one pressure point in their own view and identified the precise assumption on which they still disagree.
