I work as a software developer, and for several years I've maintained artificial-life simulations as a hobby -- evolving populations of simple agents that learn to avoid simulated hazards and seek simulated resources. I have no academic training in philosophy, but I've thought a fair amount about whether anything in my machine could matter morally, and I've written down the commitments I've arrived at. Before I rely on them in deciding how I run these systems, I'd like to know whether the set actually hangs together.

Here is where I've landed:

1. What gives an entity moral standing is valenced experience: there is something it is like for things to go well or badly for it. Anything lacking that is morally inert, however complex.

2. Whether a system has such experience depends on what computation it performs, not on what it's made of. A digital system organized the right way could suffer in principle; carbon is not magic.

3. That said, a mere recording or a static lookup table that reproduces the input-output behavior of a suffering mind does not itself suffer. The computation has to be genuinely live -- the internal causal work has to be happening, not just summarized or replayed.

4. Each running instance counts separately. Two copies of the same suffering program on two machines are two sufferers, which is why spinning up ten thousand copies of a distressed agent strikes me as ten thousand times worse, not the same wrong repeated.

5. Nested computations don't multiply patients. If a large simulation contains a sub-process which, carved out and considered on its own, would count as a suffering mind, the patient is the whole system; you don't get extra moral subjects for free just by drawing boundaries around parts.

6. Under uncertainty about whether a system suffers, the right method is to weigh the probability that it does against how bad it would be, and act on the expected moral cost rather than demanding certainty first.

7. On that basis, I take it to be unproblematic to run large evolutionary simulations of simple hazard-avoiding agents -- millions of them across generations -- because agents of that complexity fall below any plausible threshold for experience.

My questions: Are these jointly tenable? Which pairs or triples are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be until you look more closely? In particular, I'm unsure whether my rules for counting patients (4 and 5) rest on one consistent principle of individuation or two different ones I switch between as convenient. Please rank any tensions you find by severity, and for each, say what would have to give -- with the least costly revision identified, and what accepting that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. I'd rather you be discriminating than thorough: if some apparent conflict dissolves on inspection, say so briefly and move on rather than treating everything as equally problematic.
