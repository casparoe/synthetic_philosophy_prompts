Last weekend a close friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about mind uploading, and we reached an impasse I found genuinely unsettling. She grants — indeed insists — that if her neurons were replaced one at a time with functionally identical synthetic substitutes over the course of a year, the person at the end of the procedure would simply be her. Yet she flatly denies that a destructive scan-and-upload would be survival: whatever woke up in the machine would be, as she put it, a flawless impostor. Her considered view is that personal identity consists in the continuity of a self-maintaining process, not in the duplication of a pattern, however exact that pattern may be.

As it happens, I am soon interviewing a philosopher on my university's podcast who defends precisely this position, and I would like to do better in the studio than I did across the kitchen table. Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each accompanied by the reply the guest is most likely to give and a follow-up that presses past it. I do not want invitations to restate the view; I want questions that exploit the pressure points the position actually faces. In particular:

- At least one question should press the functionalist objection that troubled me most: if every synthetic neuron is functionally equivalent, then total functional organization is preserved throughout the gradual procedure — but it is equally preserved, merely with a temporal gap, in the scan-and-copy. The guest will presumably answer that causal or process continuity is the missing ingredient; the follow-up should ask how an ingredient that makes no functional difference whatever could nonetheless make all the difference to whether anyone survives.
- At least one question should turn on interruptions we already accept without anxiety: in certain cardiac operations, patients are cooled until circulation and measurable brain activity cease entirely for tens of minutes, and they wake up as themselves; and no one regards general anesthesia as a death. When I tried this on my friend, she replied that what matters is continuity of organization or capacity, not of activity — I had no comeback. The follow-up should ask whether survival is then sensitive to the mere length of the gap, and if not, what principled consideration excludes the scanner's gap.
- At least one question should raise the threshold problem: if replacing one neuron per month is survival and replacing all of them at once is duplication, is there a replacement rate at which survival flips into duplication, or is the guest prepared to say that whether she survives a procedure can be a vague matter?
- At least one question should use a case I devised after our argument, which my friend could not cleanly resolve: suppose the gradual replacement is performed, but the extracted biological neurons are not destroyed — they are kept viable, reassembled in their original configuration, and reactivated, whereupon the reassembled brain claims her memories and her name. Which of the two persons who wake up is she: the process-continuous synthetic successor, or the materially reassembled original?
- At least one question should explore the status of the so-called impostor: if the scan-copy is not her, what, if anything, is it owed, and would she permit non-destructive scanning to be used to produce persons for purposes the original would never accept?
- Finally, one question about what could count against the view: is there any conceivable finding, empirical or conceptual, that would lead the guest to conclude that gradual replacement had been duplication all along — or is the position insulated against every possible outcome?

The tone should be probing but courteous; this is a university podcast, not an inquisition.
