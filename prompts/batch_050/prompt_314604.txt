I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a feature on a dispute in metaphysics that almost never surfaces in public debate: whether reality has a bottom level. The question is usually posed in terms of grounding, the relation in which one fact obtains in virtue of some more basic fact, as the existence of a table might be grounded in facts about particles, those in facts about quantum fields, and so on. Metaphysicians disagree sharply about whether such chains must terminate in something fundamental, whether they may descend without end, and whether they might even loop back on themselves. For a sidebar to the piece I need a taxonomy of the positions in this dispute, and I would like you to construct it.

Build the taxonomy from the logical space rather than from a roll call of names: first work out the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, then locate the actual positions among the combinations of answers. The first branch should presumably concern whether grounding chains must terminate, but the further splits are up to you; I would expect the leaves to include at least the view that many things are fundamental (a kind of atomism or priority pluralism), the view that exactly one thing is fundamental and it is the cosmos as a whole (priority monism), the view that descent never ends (metaphysical infinitism), the view that grounding can run in a circle so that some facts are mutually grounded (metaphysical coherentism), and a deflationary option holding that the whole apparatus of fundamentality is misguided. Label each branch with the claim that splits it, phrased as a question a scientifically literate reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, in the spirit of asking whether there could be a world in which every fact, no matter how deep you dig, rests on something still deeper.

At each leaf, give the name of the position, or say it is unnamed if nobody defends it, and add one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, the sort of consequence a hostile interviewer would press on. If some combination of answers in your tree is unoccupied, say so explicitly and diagnose why in a sentence: whether the view looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate rather than papering over the ambiguity. Since my readers come from the science side, anchor the abstract questions with at least two concrete illustrations drawn from physics or chemistry, such as the historical fate of atomism or the difficulty of saying what the fundamental entities of quantum field theory are supposed to be, and keep the whole taxonomy under nine hundred words.

One formatting constraint, imposed by my editor: the response must be continuous prose only, like a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Convey the tree structure entirely through your sentences, making the branching explicit in words.
