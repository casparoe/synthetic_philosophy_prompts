A friend and I were arguing about the Benin Bronzes the other day and she made the point that a lot of the standard arguments for keeping contested objects sound completely reasonable until you hear them said out loud in a slightly different context, at which point they fall apart. I want to test that idea, and I'd like you to help.

You're a satirist who writes deadpan institutional parodies, the sort of thing that could run in Private Eye or McSweeney's, and you know the restitution debate well enough that a museum studies lecturer would nod along. Think carefully about the actual arguments that get made in this debate before you start -- maybe brainstorm the main ones first (silently, not in the output) and work out how to exaggerate each one just slightly past plausibility.

Then write an FAQ page from the website of the fictional "Museum of Universal Heritage" in London, responding to a restitution request from an unnamed West African nation for a collection of bronze plaques looted in a nineteenth-century punitive expedition. The FAQ should be around 650 words, written in the smooth, soothing, vaguely corporate voice of a real museum's public relations team, and it should be formatted as a series of questions and answers. Include questions like: "Won't the museum be empty if we give everything back?", "Who looked after these objects when nobody else would?", "Wasn't the acquisition legal under the laws of the time?", "Don't the plaques belong to all of humanity, and isn't our museum where humanity happens to visit them?", and one about whether the gift shop will still sell the replica plaques. The humour should come from the institution sincerely stating the real retention arguments -- the universal museum defence, the stewardship argument, the slippery slope, the legal title point, the claim that the objects are better known and loved precisely because they were taken -- but with each one pushed just far enough that the reader recoils. Stay completely deadpan throughout: no winking, no obvious jokes, no editorial asides. The reader should be able to tell exactly which real arguments are being skewered without a single one being named or quoted. Ideally the satire is fair -- a defender of retention should recognise their own position in it, not a strawman.
