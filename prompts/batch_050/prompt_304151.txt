So I work on a "responsible AI" review board at a mid-size software company that ships a customer-support chatbot, and every week we get a queue of flagged outputs where the bot said something that turned out to be false -- sometimes it invents a refund policy, sometimes it confidently misstates a real one, once it told a customer their account was closed when it wasn't. Leadership keeps asking us to label each incident as either the model "lying" or "hallucinating," and honestly neither label feels right, and the label matters because it feeds into how we write incident reports, what we tell affected customers, and whether legal gets involved.

I recently read Frankfurt's *On Bullshit* and his distinction has been rattling around in my head: lying requires knowing (or at least caring about) the truth and deliberately concealing it, while bullshitting is characterized by an indifference to truth -- the speaker just wants to produce a certain effect and doesn't care whether what they say is true or false. That made me wonder whether LLM falsehoods are best understood as bullshit, as error, as something like lying, or as a fourth category entirely, and more importantly whether that classification should change anything operationally.

Please act as a professional philosopher of language and action who also understands how LLMs actually work (next-token prediction, no beliefs or intentions in the ordinary sense), and think carefully through the conceptual issues before writing the final procedure. Turn the lying / bullshitting / error distinction into a decision procedure my team can actually run in about 15 minutes per flagged output: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure has to stop and hand things to a human judgment call. Be explicit about which of Frankfurt's distinctions survive the translation to a system with no mental states and which ones you're dropping, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire. Then run it on three cases: (1) the bot invents a refund policy that doesn't exist, (2) the bot correctly states a policy but for a reason it "made up" internally, and (3) a case where the procedure should give an answer my leadership won't like. Lead with your bottom-line classification framework in the first sentence, then the procedure and reasoning -- no preamble, no restating my question.
