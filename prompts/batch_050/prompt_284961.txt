You are an experienced university philosophy tutor with a decade of teaching undergraduate metaphysics, and you are known for planning sessions in which the order of ideas does real argumentative work. Next week I must teach origin essentialism -- the thesis, associated with Kripke's discussion of necessity and origins, that a person (or a table, or a tiger) could not have originated from entirely different material: I could not have come from a different sperm and egg. The setting: a single fifty-minute tutorial with eight second-year undergraduates who have done introductory logic and one unit on possible-worlds talk, but have never encountered de re modality, essentialism, or Kripke. I do not want you to explain the thesis to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it.

Please think carefully about the dependency structure before writing the plan, and begin your response with a short brainstorming stage (roughly five lines) in which you list at least four candidate carrying examples and note the liability of each, before settling on one.

The plan itself should cover the following:

1. Order and dependencies. Which distinctions must be in place before the question "could you have come from a different sperm and egg?" is even intelligible rather than trivially answered yes? I suspect the numerical-versus-qualitative identity distinction has to come first, and that de re versus de dicto modal claims must be separated before the thesis can be stated without sounding either obviously false or obviously true. Give the sequence and justify each step by what it makes possible at the next.

2. The carrying example. One concrete case must bear the weight of the session. State what it must have (a clear origin, intuitive pull toward contingency of origins, no irrelevant ethical noise) and what it must lack, and explain why the standard choices -- a wooden table made from a different block of wood, the Queen born of different parents -- do or do not suit this particular audience.

3. The misconception map. Predict the specific confusions these students will form, in the order they will form them: for instance, conflating origin essentialism with genetic determinism, or with the trivial historical claim that different gametes would have produced a different person; treating epistemic possibility ("for all I know, I could have") as metaphysical; and the sorites-style objection that permitting one molecule's difference while forbidding wholesale replacement is arbitrary. For each misconception, indicate the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off, and whether it is better defused preemptively or allowed to surface first.

4. The check for understanding. One question I can put to the room in the final five minutes whose answer will show whether they grasp the thesis as a claim about this very person across worlds, rather than as a claim about causation, genetics, or time travel.

5. What to exclude. Identify what I should openly refuse to cover at this level -- counterpart theory, the epistemology of essential properties, the semantics of rigid designation -- and say, briefly, why each exclusion protects rather than impoverishes the session.
