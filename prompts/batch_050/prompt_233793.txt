I want to use the emergence of Nicaraguan Sign Language as a test case for poverty of the stimulus arguments, and I'd like you to work it through carefully. Act as someone who actually knows both the linguistics and the philosophy here -- not a survey answer, but a real case analysis. Brainstorm the candidate mappings first, then commit.

The facts as I understand them (correct me if I've garbled something, but I think this is the standard story): in the late 1970s Nicaragua opened its first schools for deaf children in Managua. The kids arrived with no shared language -- mostly just the idiosyncratic homesigns they'd each developed with their hearing families. The schools didn't teach a sign language; instruction was in Spanish, mostly lipreading, which didn't work well. But the kids socialized with each other, on the buses and in the playground, and within a few years they had converged on a shared contact sign system. Then -- this is the part people cite -- children who entered the school later, as young kids, learning that early system as their input, ended up signing something with noticeably more grammatical structure: more consistent word order, spatial devices for marking who-did-what, things the older signers themselves mostly don't use fluently.

So. The innateness crowd has long treated this as a natural experiment for poverty of the stimulus: the younger cohort's input (the older kids' rudimentary signing) seems too impoverished to explain the structured grammar they ended up producing, so the extra structure must be coming from the learners, i.e. from something built in.

Here's what I want:

First, run the mapping rigorously. In a classic POS argument there's a child, a primary linguistic input, and a gap between what the input contains and what the child ends up knowing. Who exactly plays each role here? What precisely is the "stimulus" for the second cohort, and what's the alleged gap? Be careful, because I think the mapping is slipprier than it looks.

Second, tell me where the analogy strains. A few things bug me: the input wasn't absent, it was created by other children; the second cohort got its input from a community that was itself still developing the language; and the cohort-by-cohort pattern looks at least as much like iterated cultural transmission (each generation regularizing the last) as like an innate endowment asserting itself. Does the standard POS framing handle that, or does it quietly assume a one-shot learning situation that this case isn't?

Third, the verdict in both directions. Does NSL actually count as evidence *for* innateness specifically, or is it equally predicted by domain-general learning plus transmission? And flip it: what would this episode have to have looked like for it to *disconfirm* the nativist reading -- is there any outcome here that would have? If the case is compatible with both sides, say so plainly rather than forcing a win.

One more thing: distinguish the innateness question from the critical period question, because people run them together in this literature and I'm not convinced the NSL data bears on them in the same way.

Think it through before answering, and flag anywhere you're genuinely unsure.
