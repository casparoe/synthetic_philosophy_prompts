You're a philosopher of probability writing for grad students who already know what a prior is, so skip the textbook throat-clearing. Before you start, do a short brainstorm (a few bullet points) mapping out your argument, then write the essay.

The question: do Bertrand-style paradoxes -- the random chord problem, the cube factory, the wine-and-water case -- actually refute the principle of indifference, or do they just show we've been sloppy about something else? Reach a verdict and defend it; don't end with "it's complicated."

Your essay has to do at least these things, in whatever order:
1. State the principle precisely enough that the chord problem's three conflicting answers (the 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4 derivations) can each be shown to follow from it under a different choice of "equally likely" parameter.
2. Evaluate the reply that indifference is fine as long as it's applied relative to the symmetry group of the physical process that generates the instance. Press hard on the weak spot: what does this reply do for cases where there is no generating process to symmetrize over -- say, a prior over the value of an unknown cosmological parameter, where the "parameterization" is just our coordinate system?
3. Consider the deflationary move that Bertrand's cases are simply ill-posed problems, no more paradoxical than asking "what's the probability this coin lands heads?" without being told which coin or how it's tossed. Is that a resolution of the paradox or a surrender of the principle?
4. Say what, if anything, survives: either some restricted indifference principle with a stated domain, or nothing at all -- and in the latter case, say what replaces it when we genuinely know nothing.

One constraint: whatever verdict you reach, it has to actually commit you somewhere. If you defend restricted indifference, give at least one concrete case where your restricted principle would forbid a prior that a naive reasoner would use. If you reject indifference wholesale, explain wether you think the Bertrand cases or something else (e.g. language-dependence worries) is doing the real argumentative work.
