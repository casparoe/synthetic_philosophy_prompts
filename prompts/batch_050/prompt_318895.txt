My father has medication-resistant epilepsy, and his neurologists recently raised the possibility of a corpus callosotomy. In the course of trying to understand what the surgery actually does to a person, I fell down the rabbit hole of split-brain research and the philosophical literature on the unity of consciousness, and I now find myself with a set of intuitions that I cannot make cohere. I would like help figuring out whether any single principle captures them. Here are my verdicts. (1) In the classic setup where the word "key" is flashed to the left visual field, the patient says he saw nothing, yet his left hand reaches into a bag and pulls out a key: I think there is still just one subject here, one person whose channels of access have been disconnected, not two people. (2) In the intermanual conflict cases, where the left hand pulls clothes out of the closet while the right hand puts them back, and the patient scolds the left hand as though it belonged to someone else: here I do feel the pull of two wills, two agents with competing intentions sharing one skull. (3) When patients gradually learn to pass information between hemispheres by making it publicly available — small head movements, sounds, tapping — this strikes me as one agent cleverly repairing a broken system, so it counts as evidence for a single subject again. (4) If I imagine the corpus callosum being severed one fiber per day over the course of a year, I cannot identify any day on which one person becomes two, so I think the number of subjects must be vague or come in degrees rather than switching at a threshold. (5) A purely hypothetical case: suppose two separate, embodied brains were connected by a flawless high-bandwidth link giving them exactly the information-sharing an intact corpus callosum provides; despite the functional match, I am confident there would be two people, not one. My tentative principle has been that a subject is unified to the extent that information is jointly available for deliberation, verbal report, and the control of action, but I can already see that (2) and (5) strain it in opposite directions: (2) has unified action and yet I judge two wills, while (5) has perfect information-sharing and yet I judge two subjects.

So my questions are these. Is there a single, precisely statable principle — mine, repaired, or something else — that delivers verdicts (1) through (5)? If so, please state it clause by clause and say which case motivates each clause, since I suspect the principle needs at least one disjunct or degree component and I want to see where it enters and why. Second, what does the resulting account predict for cases I have not listed: a partial callosotomy in which only emotional or spatial information still transfers; a patient whose right hemisphere, via a spelling board, expresses a preference that the speaking left hemisphere sincerely denies (does the patient have that preference?); and the ordinary, intact phenomenon of being of two minds about a decision. Third, if no principle of tolerable complexity saves all five verdicts, which one should I surrender, and what is the least revisionary way to let it go — I would especially like to know whether giving up (5) is cheaper than giving up (2), since those are the two I hold most confidently and they seem to be the ones at war.
