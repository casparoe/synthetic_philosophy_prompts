I'm working on a paper and teaching a seminar next term on the ethics of memory -- roughly the territory where Margalit's stuff on the duty to remember runs into Rieff's argument that forgetting is underrated. I've been pressure-testing my own intuitions and I want to know whether they cohere, so here are my verdicts on cases. Tell me what principle (if any) captures them.

1. A small town keeps a memorial plaque about a massacre that happened there a century ago. The plaque definitely keeps old ethnic resentments alive; tensions would probably fade faster without it. Still, I think the town should keep it up.

2. A friend of mine keeps what she half-jokingly calls her grudge notebook -- a literal list of slights from her ex-husband, which she rereads. I think she should burn it. Remembering in that way is making her worse, and nothing of value is preserved.

3. A post-conflict country offers amnesty in exchange for truthful testimony: the full record gets established, but nobody gets punished, and the society is explicitly encouraged to move on rather than relitigate. I think this can be justified, even though victims are asked to give something up.

4. The grandchildren of perpetrators are required to learn in school, in detail, what their grandparents' generation did, even though it's painful and implicates their own families. I think this is not just permissible but mandatory.

5. Someone with a genuinely traumatic memory (an assault, say) has the option of a memory-dampening treatment. No one else is affected either way. I think taking it is perfectly fine -- maybe even good.

6. A colonial administration, on its way out, systematically destroys archives documenting its own abuses. I think this is a serious wrong, worse than, say, defunding a museum, even if no living person would ever have consulted those archives.

The pair I keep tripping over is 1 and 2: in both cases remembering fuels bitterness, but I want opposite verdicts. And 3 sits oddly with 6 -- deliberate institutional letting-go seems fine in one and terrible in the other.

So: is there one principle that gets all six, and if it has to cost me a verdict, which one should go? Please also tell me what your principle says about two cases I haven't listed: a company's routine deletion of old harassment complaints under its data-retention policy, and a person's request to have accurate but decades-old information about themselves delisted from search engines. I'm genuinely unsure about both, so don't just pick whichever answer makes my list look tidy.
