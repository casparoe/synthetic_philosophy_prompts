I am cataloguing the main strategies for making sense of the claim that a finite binary sequence can itself be random — not merely produced by a random process, but random as an object. Here is my list so far, with the guiding idea behind each:

1. Frequency-based approaches. A sequence is random to the extent that its digits, and blocks of its digits, occur with the expected frequencies — the finite analogue of Borel normality, or the idea that a random sequence should pass the standard batteries of statistical tests.
2. Incompressibility. A sequence is random just in case its shortest description is about as long as the sequence itself; no program materially shorter than the sequence outputs it (Kolmogorov complexity near length).
3. Unpredictability. A sequence is random just in case no effective betting scheme can win systematically against it — the martingale idea, applied to finite play.
4. Stochasticness. A sequence is random just in case no effectively specified selection rule picks out a biased subsequence — the von Mises–Church idea of admissible place selections, carried over to finite strings.

Two remarks to fix what counts as an answer. First, I am aware that for infinite sequences the natural versions of (2) and (3), together with the criterion of passing every effective statistical test, provably coincide (the Schnorr–Levin theorem), so please do not offer test-passing as a fifth family; I want a proposal that is not already known to be equivalent to something on the list. Second, merely strengthening or weakening one of the four — requiring normality for longer blocks, say, or bounding the bettor's memory — does not count either; the new criterion should rest on a different intuition about what randomness is.

So: give me a fifth criterion for the randomness of a finite sequence that is genuinely a different idea, stated precisely enough that one could in principle check a short sequence against it. Then exhibit (or at least describe) a sequence on which your criterion diverges from the four above — one it counts as random though they do not, or vice versa. Finally, say whether you take that divergence to be a virtue or a defect of your proposal, and why.
