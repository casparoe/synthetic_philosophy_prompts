I'm a retired chemical engineer (38 years in process control, so I'm comfortable with statistics, but philosophy is a new hobby — picked it up during lockdown and it stuck). Last month I read about those impossibility results for algorithmic fairness and built myself a little argument that now seems to prove something absurd, and I can't find the hole. Maybe you can.

Setup: a bank scores loan applicants from two demographic groups, the true default rates differ between the groups, and no score is a perfect predictor.

(1) A fair score should be callibrated within each group: of the people it puts in the "30% risk" bucket, about 30% should actually default, in each group separately.
(2) A fair score should also make symmetric mistakes: people who would have repaid get wrongly flagged at the same rate in both groups, and people who will default get wrongly cleared at the same rate in both groups.
(3) Each of (1) and (2) looks like just spelling out what "treat the two groups the same" means.
(4) But the impossibility results say that with unequal base rates and imperfect prediction you can't have (1) and (2) at once.
(5) So every scoring rule is unfair, in every realistic case.
(6) A human loan officer is also a scoring rule, just a mushier one, so human judgement is unfair too.
(7) Hence there is no fair way to allocate loans at all, and all those fairness audits companies pay consultants for are theatre.

Now (7) can't be right — surely some ways of lending are definately fairer than others, and audits sometimes catch real discrimination. But I can't name the step that breaks. My best guess is the move from (4) to (5): I suspect "can't satisfy both formal criteria" is doing a quiet swap for "unfair" full stop, but when I say that out loud it doesn't convince even me, because each criterion genuinely does look like part of fairness.

Two escape hatches I considered, in case they matter: these theorems have an exception for perfect prediction, so why couldn't we just gather enough data to make prediction near-perfect (my engineer's instinct is that human behaviour has irreducible noise, unlike my old distillation columns); and why couldn't the bank just add random noise to scores until the error rates match, which feels like cheating though I can't say why?

Tell me which step you reject and what rejecting it costs — what you have to give up saying about fairness. One paragraph, under 250 words please; I print these out and my eyesight isn't what it was.
