A couple years ago I tore my ACL playing pickup soccer with some grad students and spent a long evening in the ER being asked, repeatedly and with great seriousness, to rate my pain on a scale from 0 to 10. Since then I've been chatting about this with a colleague at our med school, and the more I think about it the weirder the practice seems. Hospitals treat the numeric self-report as close to authoritative -- pain was even pushed as the "fifth vital sign" for a while, with medication decisions tied to the number. Meanwhile, for patients who can't self-report (infants, advanced dementia, intubated people), nurses use behavioral checklists like grimacing and restlessness, but these are explicitly framed as second-best stand-ins for what the patient would say if only they could tell us. So the whole setup seems to treat the self-report as a direct window onto something inner, and behavior as a noisy proxy for it.

Here's my question. What exactly does this practice presuppose about consciousness? Does it only make sense if patients have some kind of privileged, roughly reliable introspective access to a phenomenal state -- if the "7" is a readout of an inner reality rather than a construction? I'm thinking about this against the backdrop of illusionist or "user illusion" views of consciousness, the Dennett-adjacent idea (also pushed in different forms by people like Nørretrander and Donald Hoffman) that what introspection hands you is more like a simplified desktop interface or a confabulated summary than an accurate picture of the underlying processes. If that's right, is the ER practice in trouble, or could it be fully rejustified on purely functional grounds -- the report as just another behavioral output, no window required? Relatedly: does the 0-10 scale quietly assume that pain intensities are interpersonally comparable magnitudes, such that my 7 and your 7 mean the same thing, and is that assumption actually load-bearing or just convenient?

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of mind behind the practice, but also distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits nicely with -- could several rival views of consciousness license the same clipboard? And if we took the user-illusion picture completely seriously, would anything actually change for the nurse at 3am, or does the practice turn out to be philosophically underdetermined in a way that suprises people?
