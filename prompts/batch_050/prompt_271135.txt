So my daughter was born in March and because of some family history stuff we ended up in genetic counseling, and the counselor kept saying things like "this gene codes for the enzyme" and "the mutation is in a coding region." Fine, whatever. But my brother in law, who did a philosophy minor like fifteen years ago and has never let anyone forget it, announced at dinner that "DNA is a code" is just a metaphor and biologists would lose nothing by dropping the word entirely. He laid out this argument and I've been turning it over for a week and cannot find the hole:

(1) If DNA is literally a code, that has to be in virtue of the correspondence between base triplets and amino acids — the thing the ribosome "reads."
(2) That correspondence is just a reliable causal regularity: given the cellular machinery, sequence in, protein out, same every time.
(3) Reliable causal regularities with something that reads them are everywhere. Smoke reliably indicates fire and we read it. Tree rings reliably indicate wet years and dendrochronologists read them. The bimetal strip in my thermostat gets "read" by the furnace.
(4) So if DNA counts as a code in virtue of (1)–(3), then smoke, tree rings, and thermostat strips are codes too.
(5) But that's absurd. Nobody thinks the rings inside an oak are a code for rainfall. At that point the word doesn't mean anything anymore.
(6) So (1) is false: DNA is not literally a code. "Code" is a metaphor that stuck.

Here's why I can't just swallow (6). The triplet-to-amino-acid thing does not seem like the smoke-fire case to me. With smoke and fire the connection is nailed down by physics, whereas the codon table looks like it genuinely could have been different — I've read there are variant codes in mitochondria, and that people have apparently engineered bacteria with reassigned codons — which sounds to me exactly like the looseness or arbitrariness that makes something a code rather than just a cause. But I can't figure out which numbered step that observation is actually supposed to block. (2)? (3)? Each premise seems fine when I stare at it on its own, and the conclusion still feels wrong.

So: which premise or step do you reject, and why does it look so plausible in the first place? And what does rejecting it cost me — like, if I give up that step, am I conceding something else to my brother-in-law without realizing it? If you think the argument is sound, say so, but then explain what work "metaphor" is doing that "code" isn't, given the variant-code thing, because that's the part that makes me think he's overreached.

One more ask: tell me what would change your verdict. Which consideration, if it turned out to hold, would push you to a different premise, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fall apart? I've had too many confident answers on this that turned out to be built on sand, and I'd rather know where the soft ground is upfront.
