Since retiring after forty years in experimental physics, I have been working my way through the philosophy of perception, and I keep returning to the thesis of the cognitive penetration of perception: the claim that what one believes, expects, desires, or fears can alter the content of perceptual experience itself, rather than merely influencing how one attends to, interprets, or reports what one sees. The cases typically offered range from the classic finding that children from poorer households overestimate the size of coins, to recent studies suggesting that people judge hills as steeper when fatigued or encumbered, that desirable objects appear closer than undesirable ones, and that holding a weapon-shaped object makes a person more likely to report seeing a gun in someone else's hand. I also know there are serious critics who argue that nearly all such results can be explained by shifts in attention, post-perceptual judgment, task demands, or reporting bias, and that the perceptual system itself remains informationally encapsulated, much as the old modularity picture held. My own training makes me suspicious of both camps: the enthusiast's experiments strike me as vulnerable to the alternative explanations, yet the critic's demand for a perfectly clean demonstration of an effect on experience, as opposed to everything downstream of it, may set a bar no psychological experiment could ever clear.

What I would like from you is your own credence that perception is genuinely cognitively penetrated in a way that matters -- that is, that top-down influences reach perceptual experience itself, with sufficient frequency and magnitude to bear on real-world perception rather than laboratory curiosities -- together with a decomposition of the reasoning behind that number. In particular, please separate and assign rough probabilities to at least the following sub-questions: whether the experimental literature survives the methodological criticisms and supports effects on experience rather than judgment or attention; whether the architecture of the visual system, as best we understand it, permits such downward influence at all; and whether, granting some genuine penetration, the effects are common and large enough to matter outside contrived conditions, for instance in eyewitness testimony or scientific observation. Give a number and one line of justification for each term, then your combined credence. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, and what sort of evidence -- experimental, neuroscientific, or computational -- would move it most. For calibration, my own number hovers around 0.45; a colleague of mine, a retired psychologist, puts hers at 0.9, and I would value a sense of which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in.
