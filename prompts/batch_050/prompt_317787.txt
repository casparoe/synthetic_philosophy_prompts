I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and this term I have built a unit around Alan Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (Mind, 1950). My students reliably fixate on the imitation game itself, but I have come to think the paper's most consequential move happens earlier, in the section where Turing defends his reformulation of the question. There he writes, "The new problem has the advantage of drawing a fairly sharp line between the physical and the intellectual capacities of a man," and, a few sentences later, justifying the rules of the game: "We do not wish to penalise the machine for its inability to shine in beauty competitions, nor to penalise a man for losing in a race against an aeroplane." My students tend to read the second sentence as a throwaway joke; I suspect it is load-bearing, since it is where the "fairly sharp line" gets its operational content — the test's conditions are engineered precisely so that everything on the physical side of the line is made irrelevant to judgment.

Later in the paper, as you will know, Turing proposes constructing a child machine and educating it toward adult competence rather than attempting to program the adult mind directly, and it strikes me that the passage above quietly dictates the terms of that education: whatever a learning machine is taught, success will be assessed entirely through disembodied interrogation, with embodiment treated as a handicap to be bracketed rather than a dimension of intelligence to be developed. What I would like from you is a lecture-ready enumeration of the most important implications of this commitment — say five to eight numbered points, each stated crisply and then defended or probed in a few sentences. I am interested in implications in several directions at once: for what the goal of education (of children or of machines) must be taken to be if assessment is constitutionally blind to embodiment; for whether such an education could ever deliver concepts that seem essentially tied to bodily life, such as pain, balance, or fatigue; for the symmetry principle the passage asserts between machine and man, and what it would mean to apply that same principle when we examine human students; and, critically, for what follows if the sharp line cannot in fact be drawn — that is, if intellectual capacities partly consist in physical ones, as embodied-cognition theorists maintain. Where an implication is one Turing himself would likely have endorsed, please mark it as such; where the passage instead exposes a genuine pressure point in his position, say so plainly. I want my students to leave the lecture unable to treat that sentence about beauty competitions and aeroplanes as a joke ever again.
