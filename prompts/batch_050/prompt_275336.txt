I help run the philosophy track of a local rationality-adjacent meetup (the kind of crowd that's read the sequences twice and instinctively thinks in utils). We've got about 10-12 people and a 90-minute slot, and I want to do a session on Bernard Williams — specifically the bit where he argues the problem with the impartial moralist isn't that he'd actually let his wife drown, it's the deliberation he'd go through on the way to saving her: the guy who pauses to check whether this is one of those situations where impartial morality permits him to prefer his wife has had, in Williams's phrase, one thought too many. My group finds this move infuriating in a way I suspect is productive — they read it as a cheap shot, since the utilitarian arrives at the right answer (save the wife) anyway, so who cares what went through his head en route.

Design me an activity that makes them feel the force of the objection from the inside rather than argue about it from the outside. Constraints: no lecture or even a summary of Williams up front, and the phrase "one thought too many" shouldn't get uttered until the debrief. I want some mechanic where participants make fast forced choices involving people they care about (staged, obviously — I'll leave the stakes-design to you, but nothing requiring real money), and then a second phase where they're made to produce explicit impartial justifications for choices they've already made, so that the justificatory step itself starts to feel like the alien, intrusive thing Williams says it is. Give me rules and roles, a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the actual objection — including the part people miss, which is that Williams isn't just defending spousal partiality, he's making a claim about what a moral theory is allowed to demand of your deliberation, and it connects to his broader integrity point about ground projects. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and give an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce. My own guess is that since everyone knows the loved ones are hypothetical, the real phenomenology of partiality never quite shows up — but tell me if you think the failure mode lies somewhere else.

One thing I'd love worked in if possible: a friend suggested a "justification tax," where every second you spend deliberating out loud in impartial terms costs the person you're trying to save, and I genuinely can't tell if that's brilliant or if it begs the question by baking Williams's conclusion into the rules. Design around it, or explain why I should cut it.
