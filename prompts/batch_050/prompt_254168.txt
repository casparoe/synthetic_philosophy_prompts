I spent my career in the natural sciences, where I grew accustomed to the idea that a measurement is only as good as the theory behind the instrument. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind, and I keep running into a puzzle about a set of practices I once took for granted.

Consider how modern medicine treats consciousness and pain. Anaesthetists monitor 'depth of anaesthesia' and adjust dosages so that a patient does not wake mid-operation. Neurologists classify some brain-injured patients as being in a 'minimally conscious state' and others as unresponsive, and treat the distinction as carrying great weight. Clinicians ask patients to rate their pain on a scale of one to ten and prescribe accordingly, taking the self-report at face value. Veterinary surgeons now give analgesia to animals undergoing procedures as a matter of course, on the grounds that the animals presumably feel something.

My question is this: what theory of consciousness does this whole pattern of practice presuppose? Specifically, does it presuppose that there is such a thing as phenomenal consciousness -- a private 'what it is like' that is either present or absent -- or would the practice make exactly the same sense if there were only functional states: information being integrated, behaviour being guided, reports being generated? I have read that some philosophers ('illusionists', I believe) hold that phenomenal consciousness in the strong sense does not exist, and that we are merely disposed to judge that it does. Would an illusionist have to regard depth-of-anaesthesia monitoring, the minimally-conscious-state diagnosis, pain scales, and veterinary analgesia as resting on a conceptual mistake, or can all of them be reconstructed in purely functional terms without loss? Conversely, does the practice presuppose anything about where consciousness ends -- does giving analgesia to rats but not, say, to insects commit one to a view about which systems have inner lives, or merely to a bet about which ones behave as if they might?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice as carefully as you can: what it strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined such that rival theories of consciousness would license it equally. Where the answer turns on contested matters in philosophy of mind, please say plainly which parts are broadly agreed and which remain genuinely disputed, rather than presenting any one view as the consensus. And as I am British, please write your answer in British English.
