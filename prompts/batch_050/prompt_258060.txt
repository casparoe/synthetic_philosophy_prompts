Ok so I need a referee and I'm hoping you can help. First, take on the role of a political theorist who specializes in democratic theory -- like someone who has thought hard about the boundary problem and all-affected-interests debates. Think carefully, maybe brainstorm the strongest version of each side before you commit to anything, and write in American English.

Here's the situation. My town held a referendum on demolishing an old dam to restore the river. Only town residents got to vote, and it passed. Downstream farmers whose irrigation depends on the dam got zero say, and they're furious. Two friends of mine, Maya and Daniel, got into a long argument about this at a barbecue and both left annoyed, and now they've roped me in.

Maya's opening move was that the result is obviously illegitimate: the people bearing the biggest costs had no vote, and the principle that everyone affected by a decision should have a say in it is about as basic as democracy gets. Daniel said that principle sounds nice but is empty in practice, because practically everyone is affected by practically everything once you follow the ripple effects out, so any real decision needs a bounded group, and residents are as good a boundary as any. The farmers' remedy isn't a vote, it's a lawsuit or a negotiated side payment.

Then Maya proposed something she'd been mulling: instead of a yes/no franchise, run what she calls an affectedness audit. An independent panel scores everyone's stake in the decision -- lost income, lost recreation, ecological impact, whatever -- and weights votes proportionally. Farmers might get 40 votes each, townies 5, a guy who kayaks there twice a year gets 1. She said this handles Daniel's ripple-effect worry because trivial stakes get trivial weight, so the boundary problem dissolves instead of getting drawn somewhere arbitrary.

Daniel came back with three objections. First, the scoring is ungameable-proof: everyone will overstate their stake, and the panel's weighting formula just becomes the new site of the same old fight -- you've moved the boundary problem, not solved it. Second, weighting votes by stake confuses compensation with authority: if the farmers are harmed, pay them, don't hand them extra political power, because political authority should track who's subject to the laws, not who feels effects. Third, he said the audit can't count future people at all -- the river ecosystem fifty years from now has the biggest stake and no way to file a claim -- so the panel would either ignore them (unjust, on Maya's own lights) or let present-day officials invent a weight for them (which is just the old boundary problem wearing a lab coat).

Maya's last move: his "subject to the laws" principle doesn't even fix his own case, since the farmers aren't subject to the town's ordinances but plainly have standing in this decision, and anyway a line on a map is at least as arbitrary as a scoring rubric -- at least the rubric has to state its reasons publicly, which is more than you can say for wherever the town line happened to get drawn in 1890.

They both want a verdict. Adjudicate it point by point: who's right about (a) whether the resident-only referendum was legitimate, (b) whether the audit actually dissolves the boundary problem or just relocates it, (c) the compensation-vs-authority dispute, and (d) the future people problem. Then say who you think is more right overall, and whether there's something both of them are missing. Be honest even if it's a split decision -- dont just mush it into "both have a point."
