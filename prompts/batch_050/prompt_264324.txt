After forty years in experimental chemistry, I have been reading epistemology and trying to determine what my own laboratory instincts imply about knowledge. Consider four cases. First, a properly calibrated pH meter reads 7.0; had the solution been acidic or alkaline, the meter would have registered differently, so I take myself to know both that it is neutral and, by inference, that it is not alkaline. Second, another meter displays 7.0 only because its needle is stuck; the solution happens to be neutral, and a colleague consequently believes that it is neutral, but I deny that she knows this. Third, having personally counted twelve samples on a bench, I take myself to know that there are twelve and therefore also to know that there are more than ten. Fourth, I am reluctant to say that, merely by deducing it from the presence of the twelve physical samples, I know that I am not a disembodied subject being fed an uninterrupted laboratory hallucination: in that situation, everything would seem exactly the same and I would apparently draw the same conclusion.

What principle, if any, captures these verdicts? I can see the attraction of saying that a true belief is knowledge only when the believer would not have formed that belief had the proposition been false, and perhaps also would have formed it had the proposition been true. But I am unsure how to apply such a test to inferred propositions, especially when an inference seems perfectly secure in one case and useless against a radical error possibility in another. Please state the best candidate principle precisely, explain why it treats the stuck meter differently from the functioning one, and determine whether it must deny that every valid deduction from something known is itself known. If no single principle preserves all four verdicts, identify the nearest established account, explain where it diverges from them, and say which verdict is most plausibly abandoned.
