I have no formal background in history or philosophy, but over the past two years I've been reading about the origins of the First World War, and I keep tripping over the same pattern — in the books, and in my own head. I'd like to know whether it has an established name.

When writers want to blame a nation for the war, they reach for things that look like a will: planning documents from years before 1914, annexation programs, a consistent strand of policy, generals who preferred war sooner to later. As I understand it, the uproar caused by the German historian Fritz Fischer in the 1960s worked exactly this way: his reading made Germany look like a person who had wanted something for a long time and finally seized the opportunity. When writers want to excuse a nation, they do the opposite — they dissolve the will into circumstances: mobilization timetables that couldn't be halted, alliance obligations, fear of striking second, exhausted diplomats misreading each other. On that telling, nobody willed the war; it happened to everyone, the way a stampede happens to a crowd.

What strikes me is that both moves exploit the same gap. With a person, we assume blame requires a continuing self: the man who planned on Monday must be the man who acted on Friday. With a nation, each side gets to decide whether such a self exists. The accuser assembles one out of decades of documents; the defender disperses it into a hundred actors, each locally reasonable. And I've noticed I do this in ordinary life: angry at my brother-in-law, I stitch years of slights into a character; defending myself, I present each act as a separate response to a separate situation, and the stitching feels like cheating.

So: (1) Is there a name for this asymmetry — that blaming a collective seems to require first constructing it as an agent with a continuous will, while excusing it requires decomposing it back into circumstances? (2) Is the war-guilt dispute really, underneath, a dispute about whether nations can have wills at all — and would that make the archival evidence oddly beside the point? (3) If nothing established fits, what would you call it, defined so it doesn't collapse into "people argue in a motivated way"? I won't be satisfied with a bare label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges are.
