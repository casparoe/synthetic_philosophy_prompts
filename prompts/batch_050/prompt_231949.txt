I work on a consumer-app feature that recommends not what users impulsively tap but what they would plausibly want if they had thought things through. While sketching it, I hit a design problem I would like stress-tested as a thought experiment. Construct a case, to the following specifications, showing that the result of idealising a single person's preferences depends on the order in which the idealisation steps are applied.

1. One ordinary person in a realistic present-day setting; no aggregation across people, no mind-uploading, no superintelligent extrapolator, and no stipulated irrationality or weakness of will in the person.
2. Exactly two idealisation operations, each individually defensible as something a careful adviser might do — for example, correcting a false belief, and giving the person accurate, vivid foreknowledge of what undergoing an experience is like.
3. Applying operation A then B, and B then A, must deliver opposite verdicts on one and the same high-stakes choice, and the flip must arise from the person's own values rather than from either ordering being careless.
4. After presenting the scenario, walk through both orderings step by step, explain exactly why the verdict flips, state which single feature of the case is doing the work, and describe what happens to the case if that feature is removed.
5. Finish with the strongest defence available to someone who claims that idealisation is order-independent in principle (for instance, that one ordering is simply the correct one, or that only preferences stable across all orderings should count), and assess what, if anything, that defence costs them.

Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph, and please answer in British English.
