Last weekend a close friend and I got into an argument about the one-shot prisoner's dilemma that ran until two in the morning, and we ended it no closer to agreeing than when we started. We both know the standard results cold: in a true one-shot game with independent players, defection strictly dominates, mutual defection is the unique Nash equilibrium, and appeals to iteration, reputation, or enforcement change the game rather than solve it. Where we split is this. She cooperates in anonymous one-shot dilemmas and defends it as rational, on grounds in the family of superrationality: if her counterpart is enough like her that their deliberations will tend to converge on the same answer, then defecting is, in effect, choosing the worse of the two outcomes that are actually reachable, and the standard dominance argument smuggles in an independence assumption her case violates. I defect, and I hold that her reasoning confuses correlation with control: whatever she does, she cannot causally influence a stranger's choice, so she is leaving utility on the table and dressing the loss up as insight. We also clashed over whether identical-twin variants (a one-shot game against a perfect copy of yourself, made yesterday) genuinely differ from the stranger case or merely tempt people into a fallacy, and over whether setups in which both players' decision procedures are mutually transparent (as in program-equilibrium proposals) transform the dilemma or simply relabel a different game with the same name.

I want us to stop talking past each other, so I am commissioning the document we should produce instead of having this argument a fourth time. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specializing in rational choice and game theory, with experience facilitating adversarial collaborations between camps that mistrust each other. Think carefully, and do not try to settle the dispute -- your job is to make it exact, not to judge it. Before drafting anything, spend a short section brainstorming: list the candidate points of agreement, the candidate disagreements, and the places where you suspect we are using the same words for different things. Then draft the joint statement itself, signed in principle by both camps -- call them the Symmetry Cooperator and the Orthodox Defector -- containing the following:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. These should include the uncontested formal facts, an agreement that iteration and reputation are red herrings for the genuine one-shot case, and an agreement that the twin and transparent-player variants are legitimate test cases rather than distractions. Phrase each claim so that neither side has to concede anything to sign it, and keep the list free of truisms that do no work.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one precise point of divergence, in wording that each side would accept as a fair description of its own view -- no steelmanning one side at the other's expense, and no formulations so hedged they become vacuous. At minimum, cover: whether cooperation can be rational against a genuinely independent stranger; whether perfect symmetry of reasoning changes what rationality recommends; whether treating one's own deliberation as evidence about the counterpart's output is legitimate inference or a category error; whether transparent decision procedures transform the game or replace it; and whether the fact that habitual cooperators tend to end up poorer in mixed populations (the "why aren't cooperators rich" worry) tells against my friend's view at all.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux -- each sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of us -- give a concrete case that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about that case in advance, so the document could in principle be used to update us later. Use at least these three cases: the anonymous stranger one-shot; the identical-copy one-shot; and the mutual source-code-visibility one-shot. If you think a fourth case would discriminate between the views better than any of these, add it and justify the addition.

4. A short closing note in which you flag any disagreement on the list that you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, and explain why -- but only if at least one such disagreement exists; do not manufacture one for symmetry's sake.

Keep the whole document under 900 words, neutral in tone throughout, and write it so that both my friend and I could sign it without feeling that the other side's position had been caricatured.
