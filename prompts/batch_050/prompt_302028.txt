I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why well-informed reforms so often backfire. In reporting it I've run into a recurring pattern that I can't find a name for, and I'd like to know whether the literature already has one -- and if not, how you'd carve it out.

The standard warning in this territory is Chesterton's fence: don't tear down a rule or institution until you understand what it was for. But the pattern I keep seeing is one where that condition is met. Everyone involved knows exactly what the thing was for; the original reason is genuinely, demonstrably dead; the thing is removed in good conscience; and damage follows anyway -- because in the decades since its creation, the institution's actual work had quietly migrated somewhere else.

Two cases from my reporting. First, a mid-sized firm abolished a layer of sign-off that had been created decades earlier to prevent a specific kind of procurement fraud. Controls had long since made that fraud impossible, and the approval step was correctly diagnosed as pure overhead. Within a year, though, the quality of junior hires' judgment collapsed: it turned out that sitting in on those approval meetings was the only structured occasion on which senior staff ever explained their reasoning out loud, and it had become the company's de facto apprenticeship. Second, a city scrapped a mandatory consultation step whose originating statute had been repealed in the 1970s. The consultation itself was a nullity -- nobody read the submissions. But the filing deadline was the one recurring event that forced two feuding departments into the same room, and with it gone, a slow coordination failure set in that cost far more than the consultation ever had.

What strikes me is that neither case is a violation of the fence heuristic as usually stated: the reformers did their homework and correctly identified that the fence's stated purpose was obsolete. The failure lies in something the heuristic doesn't seem to ask about -- the functions a practice acquires that were never anyone's reason for it, and that nobody inventories because they were never written down as the point.

Is there an established concept for this? I can see neighbors -- exaptation in evolutionary biology, latent functions in sociology, the idea of load-bearing redundancy in engineering -- but none seems to be exactly the reform-relevant phenomenon: a practice whose original rationale is correctly understood and correctly judged obsolete, whose removal is nonetheless harmful because its operative function had drifted. If there is a precise term in the social sciences or in institutional theory, I'd like to know it and how the boundaries are drawn. If there isn't, could you coin one, define it rigorously, and say what would count as a case and what wouldn't -- in particular, how it differs from a simple unintended consequence, and from cases where the reformers were merely wrong about the original purpose?
