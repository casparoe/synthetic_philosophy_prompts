My reading group did Rousseau's Social Contract last week and two members, Jess and Marcus, got into a fight I want a verdict on. It's about the bit where Rousseau says that if someone refuses to obey the general will, the body politic should compel him, and that this just means he'll be made free.

Jess's opening position: this passage is as authoritarian as it sounds. The general will is whatever the assembled citizens decide, so if you vote and lose, your real will was with the majority all along, and being forced to comply is just being forced to follow yourself. She said that move -- redefining your will so coercion doesn't count as coercion -- is exactly the trick every later ideology uses when it claims to liberate people against their wishes.

Marcus said she's misread it. The general will isn't whatever the vote says -- Rousseau is explicit that the vote can get it wrong, that the people can be deceived, and then it's just a will of all, an aggregate of private interests. So the general will is more like the objective common interest, and being forced to be free means being forced to obey the law you in some sense prescribed to yourself as a citizen.

Jess came back: fine, but if the general will can diverge from what people actually vote for, someone has to say what it really is, and that someone is now claiming to know the people's interest better than the people do. She said congratulations, you've reinvented the vanguard. Better an honest majoritarian Rousseau than that.

Marcus answered that Rousseau has built-in constraints that block this: the law has to be general, it has to come from everyone and apply to everyone, no naming individuals or special cases. So nobody can just declare their preferred policy the general will -- it has to pass that form test. Jess said the form test is way too thin, a majority can pass perfectly general laws that crush a minority, laws applying to everyone can still target the one group everyone hates.

Marcus's last move: he said freedom for Rousseau in this passage is moral liberty -- self-mastery, obeying a law you gave yourself instead of being pushed around by appetite -- so the coercion only counts as freedom when it genuinely tracks what you'd will as a rational citizen, and otherwise it isn't what Rousseau meant at all. Jess said that's just smuggling Kant into Rousseau to make him palatable, and the passage on the page doesn't say any of that.

They both asked me to rule on it and I genuinely can't tell where each one goes wrong, if anywhere. Can you go through the exchange step by step -- who wins each specific point, where each one's reading is stronger or weaker as an interpretation of what Rousseau is actually doing in that chapter, and whether there's something both of them are missing about how the pieces (general will, will of all, moral liberty, the conditions on law) are supposed to fit together?
