Please explain something I've never gotten a satisfying answer to. In algorithmic information theory, randomness is usually defined for infinite sequences (Martin-Löf tests, martingales, limit frequencies), and the standard story for finite strings is Kolmogorov incompressibility: a string counts as random if no program much shorter than it outputs it. But this seems to fall apart for short strings in three ways. (1) The invariance theorem only pins down complexity up to an additive constant, so for a 20-bit string the verdict depends entirely on which universal machine you pick, and there's always a machine on which your favorite string comes out maximally compressible. (2) Probability theory can't rescue us either: under a fair coin, 01010101010101010101 and 11010001101110100110 are exactly equally likely, so the generating process can't ground any difference between them. (3) Even with a machine fixed, the uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity means there's in general no procedure for telling whether a given string is incompressible. So: is "this particular finite sequence is random" a meaningful claim at all, or is randomness only ever a property of processes, of infinite sequences, or of strings relative to some chosen machine or test? Please take a definate position rather than surveying the options -- I want to know whether you think the question dissolves, and if not, which relativization is the right one and why. Keep it intuitive rather than proof-heavy; I roughly know the formal definitions, what I'm missing is the conceptual bottom line.
