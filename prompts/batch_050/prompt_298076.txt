I am preparing a unit for an undergraduate course in the history of economic thought, and my next session covers Aristotle on money and exchange -- the distinction in the Politics between household management (oikonomia) and acquisition for its own sake (chrematistike), the claim that usury is the most unnatural mode of acquisition, and the treatment in the Nicomachean Ethics of money as the convention that makes unlike goods commensurable in exchange. The students are first-years with no Greek philosophy; most arrive with one of two priors, either that Aristotle simply disliked commerce, or that he was an early theorist of value who anticipated later economics, and both priors distort the texts.

Don't give me a lecture on Aristotle. Give me a teaching plan for the session. In particular:

1. Open with a single sentence stating the overall arc of the lesson -- the through-line the students should be able to reconstruct afterward -- and then proceed to the plan itself, with no preamble.
2. Lay out the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and the dependency behind that order: what has to be in place before the commensurability problem can even be posed, why the use/exchange distinction has to precede the natural/unnatural distinction rather than follow it, and where the Ethics treatment should sit relative to the Politics treatment.
3. Specify the one carrying example to build the session around -- something like the shoe made for wearing versus the shoe made for trading, or a better candidate if you have one -- and state exactly what the example must contain and must not contain in order to bear the weight of both the commensurability point and the natural/unnatural point without collapsing them into each other.
4. Map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface, and identify the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. My own guesses: that Aristotle condemns all exchange and profit rather than only acquisition without limit; that the commensurability passage is a proto-labor theory of value; that money is being called unnatural or bad in itself rather than certain uses of it; that the limits Aristotle wants are moralizing rather than grounded in the function of the household and the polis. Correct my guesses where they are wrong and add the ones I haven't thought of.
5. Give me one closing question I can put to the class that would reveal whether they have grasped the natural/unnatural distinction as Aristotle draws it, rather than merely memorized that usury gets condemned -- including what a correct answer sounds like and what a plausible but mistaken answer sounds like.
6. State what I should explicitly decline to cover at this level (for instance, the scholarly disputes over what exactly makes goods commensurable for Aristotle), and say why deferring each item protects rather than weakens the lesson.
