I am a PhD student in metaphysics preparing a seminar paper on Borges's "The Garden of Forking Paths" as a thought experiment in modal metaphysics, and I would like a careful philosophical reading of the story rather than a summary of the secondary literature. The standard gloss is that Ts'ui Pên's novel, with its proliferating contradictory plotlines, anticipates something like David Lewis's modal realism: every way things could have gone actually goes some way. But I suspect this gloss rests on a conflation, and I want the reading to test it. Specifically: Lewis's pluriverse does not contain branching. Worlds are causally and spatiotemporally isolated, and apparent "branching" is at best divergence — qualitatively similar worlds that differ downstream — since for Lewis a single world cannot split into two. Borges's garden, by contrast, seems to require genuine branching: a single point of decision from which incompatible continuations grow, with the forking structure doing narrative work (the novel is a labyrinth of forking paths, not a pile of parallel paths). So my questions are: First, does the story actually depict branching in a way that Lewis cannot accommodate, or can the divergence reading be forced onto the text without loss — and if it can, what exactly is lost (e.g., the sense that one and the same agent faces the alternatives)? Second, does the story's metaphysics fit an Everettian many-worlds picture better than a Lewisian one, and would that undercut its use as an illustration of modal realism rather than of quantum branching? Third, there is a well-known worry that modal realism drains choice of significance: if every outcome is actual somewhere, deliberation seems pointless. The story ends with a deliberate, weighty act of murder. Does Borges dramatize this worry, defuse it, or simply ignore it — and does counterpart theory (the murdered and murdering men being counterparts rather than identical across outcomes) help or hurt the story's emotional logic? Please be faithful in both directions: do not flatten the story into an illustration of a theory, and do not bend Lewis to fit Borges; where the story resists the modal-realist reading, say so plainly. Conclude by telling me what, if anything, the story contributes to the debate that a straight philosophical argument could not.
