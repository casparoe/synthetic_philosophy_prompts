I want help extracting the philosophy from three real, well-known ways of organizing information about citizens, and then a judgment about how well the three could coexist. I'll describe each practice as I understand it; if I've misdescribed anything -- I gather Western reporting on the Chinese system in particular is prone to exaggeration -- correct the description first rather than analyzing a caricature. And if the premise that a sprawling practice presupposes one theory is itself misguided, say so plainly and do what the material actually allows.

The first practice, roughly the German one: press conventions and privacy law lead media to identify criminal defendants and convicts by first name and an initial rather than by full name, even when reporting true facts about open trials. A person who has served a sentence can demand that old articles about the conviction be removed or de-indexed from publicly searchable archives. Employers cannot freely obtain criminal records; the official certificate of good conduct omits old minor convictions. Publishing a recognizable photograph of someone without consent is restricted.

The second, roughly the American one: court proceedings, filings, and verdicts are presumptively public and searchable. Booking photographs and arrest records are published and republished by commercial sites even when charges are later dropped. Most states maintain publicly searchable registries listing the names, photographs, and addresses of people convicted of certain offenses, for years or decades after release. Freedom-of-information law lets citizens request a wide range of government documents, and employers and landlords routinely buy background checks compiled from public records.

The third, roughly the Chinese one: authorities compile records of conduct -- unpaid debts, defied court judgments, regulatory violations, traffic offenses -- into credit files and blacklists, and a person or company listed as untrustworthy can be barred from buying plane or high-speed rail tickets, enrolling children in private schools, or receiving government contracts. The stated rationale is to make trustworthiness visible, so that keeping one's word pays and breaking it costs.

My questions. First, what does each practice presuppose about what privacy is -- control over one's information, a zone of opacity the state and public must not penetrate, a condition of dignity or of autonomous self-fashioning, or something else -- and which rival accounts does each practice quietly rule out? Second, what does each presuppose about persons over time? The German practice seems to treat a person as entitled to outgrow their past deeds; the American practice seems to treat the record as a fact that stays true and speakable forever; the Chinese practice seems to treat present character as legible from an aggregated record and future conduct as steerable by incentives. Are those fair reconstructions? Third, what does each presuppose about the proper relation between citizen and state -- shield against publicity, object of publicity, or curator of reputation?

Fourth, the hard part: separate what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits. American openness, for instance, might rest on distrust of the state rather than on any thin view of privacy; the blacklist might be defensible as pure efficiency with no theory of the person at all. Where are these practices genuinely philosophically underdetermined? Fifth: could one coherent theory license a combination -- say, fully public trials together with anonymized reporting and expiring archives, or open records together with a ban on commercial republication? If my three descriptions turn out to encode inconsistent commitments, I'd rather you told me which commitment has to go than papered over the conflict.
