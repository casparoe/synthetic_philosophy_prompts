Three months ago I had to choose between honoring a commitment I had made to my dying father and a responsibility I owed to my employer and colleagues, and although I made my choice, I have never been able to shake the feeling that I did something wrong rather than merely something regrettable. That experience has pushed me into the literature on whether genuine moral dilemmas — situations in which an agent cannot avoid wrongdoing no matter what they do — are actually possible, and I would like a thought experiment built to order that forces the issue cleanly rather than gesturing at it.

Construct a thought experiment designed to satisfy the following specifications:

1. The agent must face a conflict between two obligations of the same general kind (two promises, or two special obligations arising from relationships, or two role-based duties), so that the case cannot be dissolved by saying one duty is simply weightier in kind than the other.
2. The agent must be entirely faultless in generating the conflict. It must not trace back to any prior wrongdoing, recklessness, or negligent over-commitment on their part — so that the standard reply "you should not have gotten yourself into that situation" is unavailable.
3. The setting must be realistic and contemporary — a hospital, a family, a workplace, a courtroom — with no trolleys, no villains forcing choices at gunpoint, and no contrived coincidences beyond what ordinary life actually produces.
4. The two options must be genuinely exclusive and exhaustive: the agent cannot do both, cannot defer, and cannot exit.
5. The case should be built so that whichever horn the agent takes, a "moral residue" plausibly remains — an obligation to apologize, explain, or make amends toward the party who loses out — since this residue is the usual battleground between those who affirm and those who deny genuine dilemmas.

Present the scenario in one paragraph. Then do the analytical work: walk through how the case is treated by (a) a view on which moral requirements never genuinely conflict because correct deliberation always yields an all-things-considered verdict, (b) a Ross-style view of prima facie duties, and (c) a maximizing consequentialist view. For each, state its verdict and what it must say about the residue. Identify the single feature of the case doing the most work, and tell me what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Finally, steelman the strongest attack a denier of genuine dilemmas could mount against your scenario, and say whether it succeeds.

Regarding your own analysis: please do not merely report the standard positions. Commit to a verdict on whether the case you constructed is a genuine dilemma, and then tell me what would change your mind — which argument, distinction, or feature of the case, if I could show it failed, would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where in your reasoning you take yourself to be most exposed.
