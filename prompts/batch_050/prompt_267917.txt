In my metaphysics seminar we have been discussing origin essentialism -- roughly, the thesis that a person could not possibly have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones they actually came from, so that any child born of a different gamete pair, however similar to me in body, mind, and biography, would not have been me. One of my classmates defends the thesis with a culinary analogy that several people in the room found compelling. A person, she says, is like a loaf of bread: a loaf is individuated by its batch. Two loaves made from the same sack of flour and the same yeast culture on the same morning are numerically distinct but interchangeable in kind, whereas a loaf baked from different flour, however indistinguishable in taste and crumb, is not the same loaf and not even the same sort of loaf. Swapping the sperm or the egg, she argues, is swapping the ingredients: the resulting child might duplicate me in every qualitative respect, but it would be a different child, just as the visually identical loaf from different flour is a different loaf. She then goes further and claims the analogy shows origin essentialism is not an exotic metaphysical add-on but the default position, so that the burden of proof falls on anyone who would deny it.

I want an audit of this analogy. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly -- a table is fine: what plays the role of the flour and yeast, of the recipe, of the baker, of the oven and the kitchen, of "the same sack" versus "the same batch" -- and then identify where the mapping breaks or goes indeterminate. In particular, I am interested in whether the analogy actually settles the disputed case or merely redescribes it: bread has a principled distinction between ingredients and recipe, and it is not obvious to me what in the human case plays each role, or whether a different sperm and egg from the same parents counts as "different ingredients" or as "the same ingredients, a different batch." I would also like you to consider what the analogy quietly imports from the baking case that persons may lack -- loaves are artifacts, they do not persist and develop over decades, and nobody thinks a loaf has a fate that could have gone otherwise -- and whether any of these disanalogies matter for the conclusion she actually drew, as opposed to being merely cosmetic. Finally, tell me whether the burden-of-proof verdict survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if you think the analogy is beyond repair, propose the best replacement you can -- whether that is a different everyday comparison or none at all.

One more request about the register of the answer. Please separate clearly what is relatively settled among philosophers who work on this -- for instance, what the standard arguments for and against origin essentialism actually are, and which intuitions they rely on -- from what remains genuinely contested, including the strength of those arguments and the standing of any analogy used to support them. In particular, do not present origin essentialism itself, or any particular verdict on my classmate's analogy, as the consensus view if it is not one; where competent philosophers divide, say so and say roughly where the fault lines run.
