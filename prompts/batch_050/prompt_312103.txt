He then assumes that such a being is possible -- the same assumption Anselm relied on, and one Leibniz had already pointed out requires a separate proof that Gödel never supplies. Given that assumption, together with axioms stating that the positive properties are closed under logical consequence and that necessary existence is itself a positive property, Gödel derives that a God-like being exists necessarily, and therefore exists in the actual world. The derivation requires the strongest of the standard modal logics, S5, and cannot be carried out in weaker systems. A technical oddity of Gödel's axiomatization is that it entails that whatever is true is necessarily true, but this affects only the modal details and leaves the conclusion about God untouched. So the argument's only substantive input is the possibility assumption, and what the proof ultimately shows is that God's existence follows from the mere consistency of the concept of God: the argument stands or falls with the question of whether the idea of God is coherent."
