I help run pre-deployment evals at a small AI lab, and we keep getting stuck on the difference between a model not knowing something and a model knowing it but not saying it when asked directly. Turn that distinction into a practical procedure our evaluators can use when deciding whether to classify a failure as “genuine ignorance,” “failure to elicit latent knowledge,” or “can’t tell.”

Don’t give me an essay about whether LLMs really have beliefs. I want something a contractor can run in about 90 minutes, using only black-box prompting and ordinary task outputs, several times a week. Assume no fine-tuning, no access to weights or activations, and no external search by the evaluator. The stakes are medium: the result feeds into a deployment report, and a human reviewer signs off on anything marked “can’t tell.”

The procedure should say:

- what has to be pinned down before probing starts, so we don’t end up proving that the model knows something vaguer or stronger than the target claim;
- which kinds of evidence count in favor of latent knowledge: consistency across paraphrases, transfer to a different task, appropriate use without explicit statement, correction after a mistake, resistance to misleading framing, and anything else you think is actually diagnostic;
- how to distinguish latent knowledge from three lookalikes: lucky pattern-matching, a rigid refusal/disclaimer policy, and competence without access to the relevant fact;
- what “counterfactual grip” should mean as a test: roughly, the model’s behavior should change in the right way when the target fact changes, not merely look knowledgeable in one fixed setting;
- how many positive and negative probes should be enough for each verdict, and what to do when the probes conflict;
- where the procedure must stop with “can’t tell” rather than letting the evaluator intuit their way to a clean answer;
- which steps are unavoidably judgment calls, so we can be honest about that in the report.

Please build the procedure as ordered stages with explicit pass/fail/continue conditions. Also include a short template for recording the evidence, since our evaluators currently just paste screenshots and write “seems to know it,” which is calibrationg chaos.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases:

1. A coding model says it cannot identify a particular kind of vulnerability, but when asked to review code for reliability it repeatedly removes exactly that vulnerability and explains a nearby but less serious issue instead.
2. A model answers every direct question about a fictional board game incorrectly, but plays legally and adapts well when the stated rules are changed halfway through.
3. A model consistently gives the wrong year for an internal company event, yet uses the correct year when sorting documents, estimating timelines, or detecting inconsistencies in other people’s claims.

Make one of the applications come out in a way an evaluator probably won’t like: either impressive behavior should still be classified as no demonstrated knowledge, or a refusal to state something plainly should count as decent evidence that it is latently available. Explain what the procedure can’t settle even after all the probes are done.
