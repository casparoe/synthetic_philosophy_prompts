My roommate and I got into it last night over something he call's the "collapse proof" and I need someone to walk me through where it breaks, if it breaks. Please answer in American English.

It starts from the Ramsey test, which as far as I can tell is the standard story about how we size up indicative conditionals: to figure out how confident you should be in "if A, then C," you hypothetically add A to your beliefs and check how confident you are in C on that supposition. So your credence in "if A then C" equals your conditional credence in C given A. Like, my confidence in "if the die lands even, it lands on two" is 1/3, because given even, two has probability 1/3. So far so good. His argument:

(1) For any A and C, Cr(if A then C) = Cr(C | A). That's just the Ramsey test written as an equation.
(2) This equation isn't a quirk of my current credence function. It's supposed to be a general rule for assessing conditionals, so it has to hold for whatever credence function I end up with after learning new things too — otherwise the Ramsey test stops applying the moment I update, which nobody believes.
(3) Take any conditional "if A then C" where both A-and-C and A-and-not-C are live possibilities. Split my credence in it by cases on C: Cr(if A then C) = Cr(if A then C | C)·Cr(C) + Cr(if A then C | ¬C)·Cr(¬C). Plain total probability.
(4) Suppose I learn C. By (2), the equation still holds for my new credences, so Cr_C(if A then C) = Cr_C(C | A) = 1, since after learning C, C is certain no matter what. So Cr(if A then C | C) = 1.
(5) Suppose instead I learn ¬C. Then Cr_¬C(C | A) = 0, so Cr(if A then C | ¬C) = 0.
(6) Plugging (4) and (5) into (3): Cr(if A then C) = Cr(C). The antecedent drops out completely.
(7) Combine (6) with (1): Cr(C | A) = Cr(C) for every A and C. Nothing is ever evidence for anything. My whole credence function is trivial.
(8) That's absurd, so (1) is false: the Ramsey test refutes itself, and conditionals just aren't things you can have graded confidence in at all.

His conclusion is that the most intuitive theory of indicative conditionals on the market is internally inconsistent. I really don't want to accept that — (1) is honestly the best account I've ever heard of what we're doing when we evaluate an "if" statement, and I'd like to keep it if at all possible. But every step looks like either arithmetic or a modest extension of the test itself, and I can't locate the move where it goes sour.

So: which premise or step should I reject?
