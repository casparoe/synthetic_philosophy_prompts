So at dinner last weekend two of my friends got into a proper argument about something that happened to a third friend of ours, Dana, and it's been nagging at me. Dana runs a small bottle shop. One of her staff sold a six-pack to a nineteen-year-old who showed a genuinely convincing fake ID -- the bar owner down the road got fooled by the same batch apparently. Dana got charged anyway, because selling alcohol to a minor is a strict liability offence here: it doesn't matter how careful you were. She now has a conviction and might lose her licence.

Marcus's position: punishing Dana is straightforwardly unjust. Criminal punishment expresses blame, and there's nothing to blame her for -- she trained her staff, bought the ID scanner, did everything a reasonable person could. The state is just using her to send a message, and using a person as a message is the one thing punishment is never supposed to do. If the law can't tell the difference between her and someone who sells to kids deliberately, that's a defect in the law, not a reason.

Elena's reply: the strictness is exactly what makes the rule work. If honest mistake were a defence, every shop in town would suddenly have a story about fake IDs and enforcement would collapse. Nobody forced Dana to sell alcohol; she chose a regulated business knowing the rules, and part of what she signed up for is that the risk of underage sales sits with her, not with the public.

Marcus came back with: fine, but then be honest about what it is. It's a regulatory levy, not a punishment. Charge her a fee, don't give her a criminal conviction with all the stigma attached. The dishonesty is dressing up a deterrence scheme as moral condemnation.

Elena's answer: the stigma is the point, that's what makes it bite. A predictable fine just gets priced in as a cost of doing business, the way shops treat parking tickets. And anyway, she added, Dana's case isn't even truly faultless -- the employee skipped the scanner that night because the queue was long. So Marcus's whole speech rests on a case that doesn't exist.

Marcus's last move: that last point is a dodge. Even if Dana's particular case has a smudge of fault, the rule Elena is defending would convict the truly faultless shopkeeper too, so she still has to defend that, and the smudge doesn't do it for her.

They both turned to me and I genuinely couldn't pick. Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move -- say who's right on each specific point and why, whether Marcus's punishment-as-blame principle or Elena's enforcement argument should win where they collide, and who's more right overall? Also tell me if there's something both of them are missing. Please write it as continuous prose, like an email -- no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, just plain paragraphs.
