I'm in a philosophy of biology course and writing a paper that pairs Frankenstein with the functions debate, and I'd like you to help me stress-test the reading before I commit to it. The idea: Shelley's creature is a Swampman avant la lettre. Davidson's lightning-struck duplicate and the creature share the crucial feature of being a functioning, thinking being with no causal history of the right kind -- no selection, no design lineage, no normal development -- and the novel seems to me to be obsessively concerned with exactly that lack of provenance. So: read the novel through the dispute between etiological accounts of function (Wright, and more strongly Millikan and Neander: a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected) and causal-role accounts (Cummins: a function is just a dispositional contribution to a capacity of a containing system).

Some specific pressure points I'd like the reading to work through. First, the easy case that's actually hard: the creature's heart pumps blood, and on a Cummins-style view that's its function, full stop, since it contributes to the system's survival-capacity. But on an etiological view matters get strange, because the creature is not a molecule-for-molecule accident like Swampman -- he is assembled from parts that did have genuine selection histories, just in other people's bodies. Does a harvested heart carry its function with it to the new body, the way a transplanted organ seems to? If so, the creature is a walking mosaic of inherited etiologies rather than a functionless accident, and I want to know whether you think the etiological theorist should say that.

Second, the scene I've built the paper around: Victor destroying the half-finished female creature because he fears the pair will propagate. My claim is that this is, structurally, a refusal to let the creature's kind acquire an etiology. So long as there is exactly one of him, every trait he has is historyless; a reproductive lineage is the one thing that could give creatures like him functions in the etiological sense, for their descendants. Victor aborts that future specifically because the selection pressure would run out of anyone's control. Is that a fair gloss on the scene, and does it tell us something about what teleology talk adds that pure causal-role talk doesn't -- namely a notion of what a being is for, which the novel treats as terrifying in the wrong hands?

Third, push back where the reading strains. The creature's cognition is the obvious problem for me: he acquires language and moral reasoning from scratch by watching the De Laceys, and Cummins handles this easily (his brain's function includes whatever it in fact contributes) while the etiological side seems forced to say his mental capacities have no functions at all, which is a bizarre thing to say about the novel's most articulate character. And the whole apparatus of Adam/Satan/creator that the novel runs on imports a design-based teleology -- functions as a maker's intentions -- that sits awkwardly with both naturalistic accounts, since Victor is a designer who never designed: he assembled without a plan for what his creature would be. Tell me honestly whether the framework illuminates the book here or just gets in its way.

Finally, I'd like the reading to end by answering this: does Frankenstein dramatize a case the philosophical literature on functions -- which trades in simplified thought experiments like Swampman and accidental doubles -- systematically misses? My hunch is that the literature treats lack of history as a technical puzzle about reference and teleology, while the novel shows it from the inside, as something like a catastrophe for the being who lacks one (the creature's repeated demands for his origin story, a mate, a place in a chain of descent). Say whether you think that's right, and point me to the scenes that do the most work. Around 900 words.
