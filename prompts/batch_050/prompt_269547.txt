I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long essay on how popular culture handles national history. My case study is Lin-Manuel Miranda's musical Hamilton — the stage show, widely seen since on film — and my working frame is Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life (1874), with its three modes of relating to the past: monumental, antiquarian and critical. I would like you to act as a philosopher-critic, equally at home with Nietzsche and with musical theatre, and produce a reading I can draw on. Please think carefully rather than reaching for the obvious; before drafting, sketch for yourself a few candidate mappings between the show's elements and the three modes, including ones you later discard, and then write up only the strongest version.

The reading I want would do at least the following. First, take monumental history seriously as the show's dominant register: Hamilton offers the founder as an exemplar of greatness meant to energise the present, and Nietzsche is explicit that monumental history serves life but deceives — it compresses the past into a chain of peaks, blurs differences, and lures by false analogy. Test that charge against the show's actual dramaturgy: its compression of events, its invented confrontations, its heroising of restless ambition. Second, consider the antiquarian mode: the show is steeped in documents — letters, pamphlets, the public confession — and Eliza's closing labour of preservation dramatises reverence for the archive. Where does the show honour that mode, and where does it quietly subordinate it to the monumental? Third, the critical mode: the show judges the past, most visibly over slavery, yet arguably pulls its punches about its own hero's entanglements. Nietzsche holds that critical history is necessary but dangerous, since condemning one's inheritance risks leaving a second nature in place of the first. Does the show exemplify that danger, and does its celebrated casting — performers of colour as the founders — amount to a fourth, perhaps un-Nietzschean, strategy that his triad cannot quite house, or can he accommodate it?

Please be faithful in both directions: do not flatten the show into an illustration of the essay, and do not bend Nietzsche to fit the plot. Where the show resists the reading, say so plainly; a good critic records the friction. Avoid leaning on verbatim quotation from the libretto or from Nietzsche — work from the substance, since I will verify any wording myself. End with two things: what Hamilton contributes to the philosophy of history that argument alone does not, if anything; and an honest statement of how confident you are in the reading overall, singling out the specific interpretive claim — about the show or about Nietzsche — you are least sure of.
