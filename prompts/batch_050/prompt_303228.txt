A colleague of mine in a reading group on decision theory has been absorbed by ergodicity economics -- the line of thought, associated recently with Ole Peters but with older roots in Kelly and in the physics of multiplicative processes, that rational agents should care about the time-average growth rate of their own wealth rather than the ensemble average taken over parallel possible worlds. Last week he presented an argument he said vindicates the whole framework, but its final step strikes me as insane, which makes me suspect the argument proves too much. Here it is, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) What a person actually experiences over a lifetime is the time-average growth rate of their single realized wealth trajectory, not the ensemble average (expected value), which averages over branches of possibility that no one ever inhabits. (2) Real wealth dynamics are multiplicative: gains and losses scale with what you currently have, since you stake, invest, and insure in proportion to your means. (3) For multiplicative dynamics repeated over time, the time-average growth rate converges to the expected logarithm of the per-round wealth multiplier, which by Jensen's inequality is strictly less than the logarithm of the expected multiplier. (4) Hence a gamble with positive expected value can have negative time-average growth and will ruin a repeat player with probability one; his example was a fair coin paying +50% on heads and -40% on tails, which has a positive expected multiplier of 1.05 but a per-round log growth of about 0.5 ln(1.5) + 0.5 ln(0.6), roughly -0.053. (5) Therefore rationality requires maximizing expected log wealth (or, where bet size is adjustable, something in the Kelly spirit) rather than expected wealth. (6) And therefore one should decline the +50%/-40% coin flip even when it is offered exactly once, for a fixed stake of $100, to someone holding $1,000,000 in the bank. (7) But that last verdict is obviously wrong -- a one-off $100 flutter at favorable odds against a seven-figure bankroll is harmless by any standard -- so the time-average framework must be misguided, and plain expected value is the right guide to rational choice after all.

I told him the collapse happens somewhere between (5) and (7), but when pressed I couldn't say precisely where the reasoning stops applying, and he maintains the steps are seamless. Which step or transition do you reject, and why did it look innocent? I'd most like to preserve the critique in (1)-(5) of naive expected-value maximization for repeated proportional bets, since the +50%/-40% example genuinely does bankrupt repeat players; what I'm unwilling to swallow is (6). If the right move is to restrict the framework's scope, please spell out exactly what features of a decision problem license time-average reasoning and which do not -- and what rejecting the offending step costs, i.e., what one must still appeal to (utility? something else?) in the cases where the ergodicity lens goes silent.
