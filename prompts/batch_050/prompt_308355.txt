You are a professional philosopher with expertise in decision theory and practical ethics, of the kind who writes case analyses for applied-philosophy journals: disciplined about mapping theory onto fact, and candid about where the theory runs out. I am a philosophy professor preparing the capstone session of a graduate seminar on formal ethics, and I want a model analysis I can hand to my students. Think carefully before you write; I suggest beginning with a brief brainstorming pass over the structural features of the case before you commit to a mapping.

The episode: the collapse of the cryptocurrency exchange FTX in November 2022, and the worldview its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, publicly espoused. So that we work from a shared case file, take the following as stipulated facts:

- Bankman-Fried built FTX while publicly committed to effective altruism and to 'earning to give' — the stated purpose of the fortune was that it would be donated — and he aligned himself with the longtermist branch of that movement, which holds that the sheer scale of the future gives the far future enormous weight in present deliberation.
- He was unusually explicit about expected-value reasoning. In a much-discussed podcast interview recorded months before the collapse, he was asked about a hypothetical coin flip that would either double the value of the world or destroy it, at slightly better than even odds, and he indicated that he would take the flip, and would keep taking it. (I am paraphrasing; treat the content as stipulated rather than as a textual matter.)
- After the collapse it emerged that customer deposits had been used to cover the losses of his trading firm, Alameda Research. Enormous risks were taken with other people's money, apparently on the rationale, at least in part, that the upside was philanthropic.

The lens: Pascal's mugging. A mugger demands your wallet in exchange for an astronomically large payoff he promises to deliver tomorrow; on almost any nonzero credence you assign his story, handing over the wallet has positive expected utility, so the expected-utility maximizer seems rationally compelled to pay. The puzzle is that the conclusion is insane, and it matters where the insanity enters: the astronomical payoff, the unverifiable hypothesis, the minuscule probability, or unconstrained expected-value maximization itself.

The analysis I want has four parts.

1. The mapping. Identify the structural features of the mugging — astronomical payoff, unverifiable hypothesis, tiny credence, exploit of expected-utility maximization — and say which are actually present in the FTX case and which are not. Be precise: the longtermist claim that the future is astronomically large is a candidate for the payoff, but the FTX trades were not obviously low-probability, and the stake was not the mugger's to promise. Say where the analogy holds, where it strains, and what the closest exact correspondence is.

2. What the lens explains. Grant the mapping as far as it goes. What does Pascal's mugging illuminate about the episode that the ordinary explanations — greed, dishonesty, risk-seeking, fraud — leave out? In particular: does the case show that big-number reasoning of the mugging type is a real psychological and institutional hazard rather than a philosopher's toy? And if so, what was doing the mugging — an interlocutor, a doctrine, or the agent's own deliberative policy?

3. Where the lens breaks. Adjudicate, do not merely list, the candidate disanalogies: the mugging concerns tiny credences in extravagant hypotheses, whereas FTX looks like risk-loving or fraud under quite ordinary probabilities; the mugging's victim risks only his own wallet, whereas here the downside was externalized onto depositors; the mugging assumes sincere expected-value maximization, and it is unclear the episode involved sincerity of any kind. Which of these is fatal to the analysis and which merely qualifies it?

4. What the episode shows about the puzzle. Run the inference in the other direction. If the mugging framework applies, is FTX a confirmation — evidence that unconstrained expected-value maximization over large payoffs is institutionally dangerous — or does it instead point to a particular solution to the puzzle (bounded utility, a credibility threshold, discounting unverifiable hypotheses, side constraints against wagering other people's stakes)? And if it does not apply, does the episode show that the mugging was never the right diagnosis of what goes wrong with big-number reasoning in the wild — that the real pathology is one the puzzle never modeled?

Two ground rules. Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak. And where the facts I have stipulated are doing argumentative work in your analysis, flag it.
