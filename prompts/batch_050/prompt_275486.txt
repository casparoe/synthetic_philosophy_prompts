I am a novelist at work on a book about a real historical figure, and before I commit to certain liberties I want the moral structure of my situation isolated rather than muddled. Construct a thought experiment consisting of a pair of scenarios, each involving a writer who dramatizes a real dead person and attributes to her a fabricated, damaging, but entirely plausible act. The two scenarios must be identical in every respect except one, and that single difference should flip the intuitively correct verdict on whether the writer has wronged anyone. Choose the variable you vary so that the pair adjudicates among three candidate grounds of the obligation: (i) a duty to the dead person herself, on the model of posthumous harm; (ii) a duty to surviving intimates, descendants, or communities who identify with her; and (iii) a duty to the audience not to corrupt the shared historical record on which public memory and judgment depend.

Constraints: both scenarios must be realistic publishing or filmmaking situations — no afterlives, no ghosts, no devices that let the dead learn what was said about them; the fabricated act must be one the historical record leaves open, so that no expert can simply refute it; neither scenario may turn on legal questions (assume throughout that defamation of the dead is not actionable, so the question is purely moral); and the two scenarios together should take no more than two paragraphs to state.

After presenting the pair, do the following: give the verdict in each scenario and say which of the three grounds each verdict best supports; identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work, and describe what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed or reversed; then present the strongest objection available to someone who holds that the obligation is always owed to the dead person herself, and the strongest objection from someone who holds it is never owed to her, and indicate which of these two attacks the pair survives less well. Close with a short paragraph applying the result to my own case: my subject is a stateswoman dead forty years, without surviving close family, to whom I wish to attribute a fabricated act of cowardice that would elegantly explain a documented decision for which historians have no satisfying account — the invention is undetectable, dramatically valuable, and likely to be believed by most readers as fact. Tell me whether the pair licenses the liberty, forbids it, or shows that my case turns on a variable the thought experiment did not isolate.
