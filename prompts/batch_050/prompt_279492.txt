So I've been running a graduate seminar on the epistemic regress problem, and I'm struck by how frozen the foundationalism/coherentism/infinitism debate feels. Everyone knows the standard moves: the foundationalist's basic beliefs look arbitrary to the coherentist, the coherentist's web of beliefs looks like it could be a coherent fairy tale to the foundationalist, and infinitism gets wheeled out as the view everyone respects and almost nobody actually holds. Meanwhile infinitists keep insisting the regress objection against them rests on a finitude worry that applies to finite minds, not to the structure of reasons itself, and their opponents keep making it anyway.

Here's my hypothesis, which I'd like you to test: the debate has stalled because the three sides aren't actually disagreeing about the shape of justification. They're disagreeing about what justification is for -- roughly, whether it's supposed to be something that connects beliefs to truth, something that survives idealized dialectical challenge, or something a cognizer can in principle access and deploy. If that's right, the "regress argument" each side gives is just their conception of justification's job dressed up as a neutral structural constraint.

So: is my diagnosis right, or is there a genuinely shared question here that the sides answer differently? If they're talking past each other, say precisely where -- which premise in which version of the regress argument smuggles in which conception. And then tell me what progress would even look like: is there an argument form, a distinction (say, between justification of beliefs and justification for persons), or a result from formal epistemology that could actually force movement, or should we expect the impasse to be permanent because it's bedrock intuitions all the way down? I want the diagnosis argued for, not a survey of the three positions -- I can get that from any textbook.
