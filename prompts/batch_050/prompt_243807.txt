Before my seminar reads any particular semantics for indicative conditionals, I want the standard of adequacy fixed in advance. Write the desiderata that a theory of the English indicative conditional — "if A, (then) B", with A and B in the indicative mood — must satisfy in light of David Lewis's triviality results and the later impossibility theorems in the same family: no theory may both treat the conditional as expressing an ordinary proposition whose probability equals the conditional probability of consequent given antecedent and keep the rest of our semantic machinery intact. For each desideratum, give the case or phenomenon that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp. Where some set of desiderata is provably jointly unsatisfiable, say exactly which set, and which assumption a theory must surrender to escape. End with a brief scorecard of the main contender families — the material conditional, Stalnaker-style possible-worlds selection semantics, non-propositional or assertability views in the tradition of Adams, restrictor analyses in the tradition of Kratzer, and dynamic or expressivist semantics — against the list, but keep the scoring short; the list is the deliverable.

The desiderata should cover at least the following, though I expect you to add items I have missed:

1. The assertability datum. Competent speakers reliably judge "if A then B" probable to just the degree that they judge B probable on the supposition of A. The theory must explain why this equation is so robust without being refuted by the triviality results — it must say precisely what, if not the probability of a proposition, the equation tracks.

2. Validity and invalidity. Modus ponens must come out compelling, while the move from "A or B" to "if not-A then B", along with strengthening of the antecedent, transitivity, and contraposition, must come out bad for indicatives — and the theory must explain why these failures are not mere performance errors, given that the corresponding inferences are classically valid for the material conditional.

3. Embedding behavior. Indicative conditionals sound defective under negation, under Boolean combination, inside probability operators, and as the antecedents of further conditionals, whereas right-nested forms ("if A, then if B then C") are comparatively acceptable and seem interchangeable with "if A and B, then C". The theory must predict this asymmetry rather than merely record it, and must take a stand on whether the interchangeability is exact.

4. Restriction readings. In "if it rains, the match is usually cancelled", the if-clause appears to restrict an adverb of quantification rather than to connect two propositions. The theory must either unify such cases with the two-proposition cases or explain why no unification is required, and it must say how "if A, probably B" is compositionally derived.

5. Disagreement and retraction. A hearer who rejects "if A then B" typically cannot felicitously reply "that's false" while granting the supposition of A; dissent takes the form of refusing the step from A to B or of disputing B under that supposition. The theory must say what genuine contradiction between two speakers over a bare conditional could even consist in, and whether there is any fact of the matter they disagree about.

6. Interaction with epistemic vocabulary. "If A, might B", "if A, must B", and consistency judgments such as "it might be that if he's in London he's miserable" must fall out of the same account of epistemic modality that the conditional itself presupposes, rather than requiring a separate stipulation for each construction.

7. Sequence effects. "If Alice comes, the party will be fun; but if Alice and Bob come, it won't" is felicitous, while the reverse order is not. The theory must derive the asymmetry, and say whether it survives when the second antecedent is incompatible with the first consequent.

Then: identify the smallest set of your listed desiderata that is provably jointly unsatisfiable — state the triviality result in the vocabulary of the desiderata themselves, not in the notation of some particular formalism — say which single desideratum, if dropped, purchases the most coverage of the remainder, and which one does the most work in eliminating contenders. Flag explicitly any desideratum you suspect is an artifact of one research tradition rather than a genuine datum; I would rather have a shorter defensible list than a long ecumenical one.

The purpose that fixes adequacy: a semantics able to predict competent speakers' judgments of probability, validity, and felicity for indicative conditionals in ordinary English — not a logic for mathematical proof, and not a theory of counterfactuals.
