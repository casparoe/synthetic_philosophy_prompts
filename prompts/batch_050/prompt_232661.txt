When a region of an otherwise stable state holds an independence referendum, only the region's residents cast ballots: the rest of the country's citizens, whose territory, tax base, defence commitments and shared institutions would all be altered, get no vote, and this is widely treated as obvious. Yet when the same state's national legislature draws electoral boundaries, sets environmental standards, or authorises an airport whose noise falls across the border of a neighbouring municipality, it is treated as equally obvious that the affected outsiders have no claim to a vote there either. What principle for constituting the demos do these practices jointly presuppose? Consider the standard candidates — that everyone affected by a decision should have a say in it, that everyone subject to a law's coercion should, or that the demos is simply whoever existing states happen already to enfranchise — and ask which, if any, vindicates both practices at once, and which the practices quietly rule out. Reconstruct the implicit theory of democratic inclusion, say whether the pair of practices is coherent or merely defers to the territorial status quo, indicate where the practices are underdetermined and several rival theories would license them equally well, and describe what would concretely have to change if the 'all affected interests' principle were taken seriously. Please answer in British English.
