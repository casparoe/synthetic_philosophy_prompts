I'm revising for an exam on classical kalām and my friend hit me with this argument that's meant to demolish the Ash'arite position on divine command, and honestly I can't see where it slips, if it does.

(1) Calling something just only makes sense if there's some standard by which it's just.
(2) For creatures, that standard is God's command -- that's the whole Ash'arite point, and I grant it.
(3) But God can't be bound by his own command, because an obligation needs someone above you doing the obliging, and you can't oblige yourself.
(4) Nor can he be bound by a standard outside himself, since nothing uncreated exists apart from God, and if justice were a real thing over him he'd be subject to something and lose his aseity.
(5) So there's no standard by which God's own acts could be just or unjust.
(6) So when revelation calls God just, it's either false or completely empty -- it just means he does whatever he does, which would be true of a tyrant too.
(7) But the tradition absolutely insists God is just and that this means something.
(8) So the Ash'arite view collapses, and we're forced back to the Mu'tazilite line that justice is objective and known by reason even before revelation.

I really want to keep (3) and (4) since divine freedom and aseity feel non-negotiable to me, but I also can't live with (6). Which step do I actually reject, why did it look fine, and what's the price of rejecting it -- for both the Ash'arite and the Mu'tazilite? And if I'm forced to say God's justice isn't the same property as human justice, am I just left with a word?
