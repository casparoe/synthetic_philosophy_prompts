Exam question: Explain Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason. Your answer should identify the two principles whose conflict constitutes the dualism, explain why Sidgwick took each to be rationally grounded, explain why they cannot both be followed, and say how, if at all, Sidgwick thought the conflict could be resolved.

Student's answer: In the Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick surveys three methods by which people actually reason about what they ought to do: egoistic hedonism, the morality of common sense (intuitionism), and universal hedonism (utilitarianism). The dualism of practical reason, which Sidgwick presents in the book's concluding chapter, is the conflict between rational egoism and the demands of common-sense morality. Sidgwick argues that each side of this conflict rests on a self-evident principle. The egoistic side rests on the axiom of prudence: it is rational to aim at one's own greatest good on the whole, giving no privileged weight to nearer pleasures over later ones merely because they are nearer. The moral side rests on the deliverances of common-sense morality, which Sidgwick takes to be intuitively evident to reflective people. Because practical reason is a single faculty, its deliverances cannot genuinely contradict one another; so once we see that the axioms of benevolence and justice show common-sense morality to be at bottom utilitarian, the dualism dissolves, since egoism and morality are revealed to be the two faces of a coherent practical reason. Sidgwick therefore concludes that the dualism is only apparent, and that the person who grasps the self-evident axioms will see that rational egoism and the moral point of view are ultimately reconcilable without remainder. He adds that even if some residual tension were to persist, it would be removed by the religious hypothesis that a just God apportions happiness to virtue, and he treats this theological postulate as the definitive solution to the problem rather than as a hope ethics itself cannot underwrite. Sidgwick also defends egoism by appealing to psychological hedonism: since we can only desire our own pleasure, pursuing it is the only course of action reason can coherently prescribe.
