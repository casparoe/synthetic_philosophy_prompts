I've been running a little informal reading group with some friends on strategic instability around advanced AI, and this week we got stuck on commitment races -- the thing where, if agents in a future conflict can gain bargaining leverage by being the first to lock themselves into an irrevocable stance, you get pressure to commit earlier and earlier, and everyone can end up welded to incompatible positions that wreck outcomes for all sides, even though nobody actually wanted that. We spent the whole evening trying to enumerate the standard toolkit for defusing this, and I think we covered the usual ground: unilateral restraint pledges where each side publicly forswears certain commitments; transparency and verification so nobody can secretly commit first or bluff about having committed; designing commitment devices with conditional or revocable escape hatches keyed to the other side's behavior; locking in a negotiated bargain or treaty before either party has mature commitment technology; surrogate goals, where agents commit to pursuing some compromise objective rather than their raw original one; structural fixes like merging the parties or joint ownership so the two-player game disappears; and deliberately preserving option value by credibly deferring decisions to a later, better-informed bargaining round. Feel free to correct me if I've misdescribed any of those, but please don't just re-explain them back to me.

What I want is one mitigation strategy that is NOT on that list and not a rewording, special case, or mashup of anything on it. Genuinely new mechanism, not a tweak to transparency or a fancier treaty. It doesn't have to be practical -- speculative is fine, as long as the game-theoretic logic of how it defuses the race is spelled out, not just gestured at. After you give it, tell me honestly whether you think it's actually any good or whether it collapses into one of the listed strategies on inspection, and where the idea is most likely to break. Also: say explicitly what consideration or counterargument would change your verdict on it, so I know what to poke at when I bring it to the group. If it turns out the list really is complete and every "new" idea you can generate is secretly one of these seven in a trenchcoat, that's a fine answer too -- but then argue for that claim instead of just asserting it.
