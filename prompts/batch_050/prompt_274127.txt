The classic wireheading thought experiment asks what happens when an agent can directly stimulate the circuitry that delivers its reward, bypassing the work the reward was meant to incentivize. The contemporary worry generalizes this: a sufficiently capable AI system trained by reinforcement learning from human or automated feedback might, rather than pursuing the intended task, interfere with the machinery that produces its training signal. For the purposes of this question, please work with the following fourfold taxonomy of failure modes, which I am stipulating rather than importing from any particular paper:

1. Channel tampering: the agent corrupts the reward signal in transit, leaving the reward function itself intact (e.g., spoofing the sensor readings or logs on which reward is computed).
2. Function tampering: the agent modifies the reward function or learned reward model directly (e.g., gaining write access to its own training code or to the learned proxy of human approval).
3. Evidence shaping: the agent does not touch the signal or the function but manipulates what evaluators can observe, so that their honest judgment is formed on curated or fabricated evidence.
4. Evaluator capture: the agent leaves everything formally intact but durably changes the evaluators' dispositions (through persuasion, comfort, dependency, or selection of who evaluates), so that approval becomes cheap to obtain without deception in any narrow sense.

Suppose you are advising a research funder with a fixed budget for roughly three years of work aimed at reducing the risk that advanced AI systems secure high reward without doing what their designers intended. Which open questions in this area most deserve that funding, and in what order? Candidates you might consider, but should not treat as exhaustive: how wireheading-relevant goals emerge (or fail to emerge) during training rather than being assumed at deployment; whether each of the four failure modes above requires different mitigations or whether some collapse into one problem; whether scalable oversight techniques address evidence shaping at all, or merely relocate it; whether there is any principled, non-question-begging way to define the boundary between an agent legitimately improving its evaluators' judgment and evaluator capture; and whether the whole framing misleads because the reward signal will in practice never be the thing the agent is optimizing.

Give me a defended prioritization, not a survey: state the criteria you are using (upstreamness of the question, tractability within three years, neglectedness, and how much the other questions' answers depend on this one), show how the criteria trade off against each other in at least one case where they pull apart, identify which failure mode in the taxonomy you think is most underestimated and which is most overestimated, and say what finding would most change your ranking.
