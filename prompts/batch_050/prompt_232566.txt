Over the past year, a colleague in my department and I have been trading spectrum cases in advance of a workshop on transitivity and value, and I have quietly assembled a record of her verdicts. All the cases concern a single subject enduring pain of varying intensity and duration — bracket worries about the human lifespan, since the subject simply lives as long as a case requires. Her judgments so far: between two months of agony and one year of severe chronic pain, she judges the year worse; between that year and five years of moderate chronic pain, she judges the five years worse; between those five years and twenty-five years of mild but persistent pain, she judges the twenty-five years worse. Yet when I put the endpoints to her directly, she judged the two months of agony worse than the twenty-five years of mild pain — she would take the quarter-century of aching without hesitation — and she likewise judged one year of severe pain worse than twenty-five years of mild pain. She affirms every one of these verdicts on reflection, understands the transitivity principle perfectly well, and declines to retract anything.

The case she has not yet seen, and which I intend to put to her at Thursday's meeting: twenty-five years of mild but persistent pain versus 125 years of a barely noticeable discomfort — an occasional dull twinge, the sort one mostly ceases to register. Predict her verdict. More importantly, tell me which rule she is implicitly running. As far as I can see, at least three candidates fit her record equally well: that 'worse than' is genuinely intransitive along the spectrum and every pairwise verdict stands as given; that her mild-region verdicts are forced choices masking imprecise comparability, the relevant outcomes being on a par rather than strictly ranked; and that her judgments track whichever dimension is salient in the pair presented — duration when intensities are close, intensity when they diverge — so that no single worseness relation underlies the record at all. If you think I have missed a candidate that fits better, say so.

Give me your best guess as to which rule she is running, with reasons drawn from the pattern of verdicts rather than from general plausibility; say what each of the rival rules would predict about the new case instead; and finally, name the further comparison or probe you would put to her to discriminate your favored rule from its nearest rival — a change in the menu of options, a forced all-at-once ranking, a sequence of offered trades, or something else — and what result would settle it.
