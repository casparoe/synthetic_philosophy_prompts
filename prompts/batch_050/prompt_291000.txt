At a political theory seminar, Maya and Leo disagreed about Harsanyi’s aggregation theorem. (1) Maya said that if every citizen’s preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, the social preference relation does too, and society prefers any prospect unanimously preferred by its members, then social welfare must be representable as a weighted sum of individual utilities. Thus, she claimed, a policymaker’s choices across a sufficiently rich set of social lotteries can reveal the welfare weights assigned to different citizens. (2) Leo replied that this proves far less than it seems: because von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities are unique only up to positive affine transformations, doubling one citizen’s utility function and halving that citizen’s weight leaves the same social ranking unchanged. The theorem therefore cannot identify ethically meaningful weights without an independent scheme for comparing utility units across persons. (3) Maya answered that the rescaling does not change the theorem’s substantive conclusion: once the individual utility representations are fixed, the social weights are fixed as well, so the alleged arbitrariness is merely a choice of bookkeeping units. (4) Leo responded that fixing the representations merely postpones the ethical question. A common normalization such as setting everyone’s worst outcome to 0 and best outcome to 1 is not morally neutral; it treats people with very different stakes as though their normalized gains were comparable. (5) Maya conceded that the theorem does not settle distributive justice, but insisted that it still excludes nonlinear social criteria: if the stated axioms hold, apparently independent concerns about priority for the worse off must already be encoded in the individual utility functions, the lottery structure, or the weights. (6) Leo replied that this last point equivocates between representing a consistent social preference and justifying it: the theorem may force coherence among choices, but it cannot tell a policymaker whether equality, priority, or efficiency deserves greater weight.

Adjudicate the exchange move by move, identifying what each speaker gets right and wrong. In particular, explain exactly what the theorem does and does not establish, whether observed social choices could reveal welfare weights, and which additional ethical assumption would be needed to give those weights interpersonal significance.
