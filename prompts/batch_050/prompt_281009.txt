Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about Hegel's master-slave dialectic, and I lost, or at least I couldn't find the exit from his argument. I want to know whether the exit exists and where it is.

The argument turns on a sentence from the lordship and bondage section of Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit (in J. B. Baillie's translation). Hegel writes:

"Action from one side only would be useless, because what is to happen can only be brought about by means of both."

My friend's reading: recognition is worthless unless it is the recognizer's own deed, given from a position of independence rather than as a reflex of the recognizer's situation. From there he built this:

(1) Recognition of me counts only insofar as it is the other's own action — which means it must be the sort of thing that could have been withheld.
(2) A recognizer whose standing depends on me — an employee, a student seeking a grade, a servant, a flatterer, a child — cannot withhold recognition without cost to themselves; their recognition is therefore not fully their own action.
(3) So recognition from dependents is, to the degree of their dependence, not genuine recognition. (This, he says, is exactly the lord's predicament: he is recognized by a consciousness he has reduced to the status of a thing, and so remains unrecognized.)
(4) But nearly all recognition in any actual society flows through relations of dependence: salaries, grades, titles, promotions, invitations, inheritances.
(5) So almost no recognition anyone has ever received is genuine.
(6) Hence the everyday picture, in which people are genuinely recognized by colleagues, students, audiences, and children, is an illusion — and Hegel's dialectic shows it.

I tried to counter with the sentence two paragraphs later, where Hegel says each self-consciousness "is the mediating term to the other, through which each mediates and unites itself with itself," but my friend replied that this merely restates the criterion his argument exploits: mediation by a dependent is not the mediation Hegel demands. What I would most like to keep is (3), since the lord's hollow victory seems to me the true heart of the section; but I cannot accept (5), and I suspect something slipped between the quoted sentence and (2).

Please structure your answer with these sections, in this order: "Diagnosis" (roughly 150 words — name the single step you reject); "Why the step looked safe" (roughly 120 words); "What rejecting it costs" (roughly 120 words — including whether I can keep (3)); "Reading the passage" (roughly 150 words — what the quoted sentence, in its context, actually commits Hegel to, and whether my friend's gloss of it is licensed); "The runner-up" (roughly 80 words — the second-weakest step and why it survives); "Bottom line" (two or three sentences).
