My mother died four years ago, and since then a small industry has appeared offering to build a chatbot out of a dead person's texts, emails, and voice recordings so you can keep talking to them. I have no academic training in ethics, but I have strong reactions to these things, and I would like to know whether my reactions add up to anything coherent. Here they are, as honestly as I can report them.

Listening to her old voicemails: completely fine. I do it on her birthday. Reading her old letters and emails: fine. My brother doing her catchphrases at family dinners, in her exact intonation, to make us laugh: fine, even moving, though he is literally putting new words in a dead woman's mouth. A chatbot trained on her messages that answers questions in her writing style: not fine, and I cannot fully explain why, since in one sense it is only recombining things she actually wrote. A version of that chatbot that messages me first -- "thinking of you today" in her voice, on the anniversary of something: much worse, though the content is gentler than half of what my brother says at dinner. A company licensing a dead actor's face and voice to sell cars: obviously not fine, and that one at least seems easy. Here is the case that breaks my theory, whatever my theory is: if my mother, while alive and knowing she was dying, had sat down and helped build the chatbot herself, recorded training material, and told us she wanted us to have it -- I think I would say fine, or at least much more nearly fine. But then the wrongness of the ordinary chatbot cannot be about the technology or the simulation as such, since nothing about the software changes. And yet consent cannot be the whole story either, because if she had consented to the car commercial I would still find it grotesque.

So I am left with a pair I cannot reconcile -- my brother's impressions are fine, the chatbot is not, and both are new utterances in her style made without her input -- and a suspicion that my real principle involves some mixture of who is doing the speaking, whether she authorized it, and whether the thing is being used to comfort the living or to extract something from them, but every version I sketch either condemns the voicemails or permits the commercial.

Is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself? If it takes more than one clause, tell me which case motivates each clause. Tell me what it implies about cases I have not listed: a therapist suggesting I use the chatbot short-term as a grief exercise; my aunt wanting one built from her own late husband's data, over her children's objections; a historian in a hundred years doing the same to a long-dead public figure. And be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot save, because I would rather give one up than adopt a theory bloated with exceptions.

The case I actually need it for: my daughter is three and will never remember her grandmother. Part of me wants to let her grow up able to ask the chatbot about family stories, so that some version of my mother is there for her. I keep going back and forth. Tell me what your principle says, and if it says nothing clean, tell me which two of my existing verdicts are pulling against each other in it.
