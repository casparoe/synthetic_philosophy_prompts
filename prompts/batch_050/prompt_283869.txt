I administer a hardship fund for a large professional association. Members apply for emergency grants after financial shocks -- medical events, job loss, property damage, family crises -- and my three-person committee awards or denies roughly twenty-five applications a month, with amounts capped at a level that matters to the applicant but cannot fully replace what was lost. Our founding charter says the fund exists to compensate members for misfortune, not to underwrite the consequences of their own choices, and I am told this makes us, in effect, practicing luck egalitarians. I have read enough to know the core idea: it is unjust for people to be worse off through no fault of their own, but inequalities that trace to genuine choices are, at least on the strict version, not the fund's problem. What I lack is a way to apply that idea repeatedly, defensibly, and fast.

Please turn luck egalitarianism into a written procedure my committee can actually follow. Concretely:

First, give us an ordered set of tests for separating brute luck from option luck in a real application, where we have a two-page form, supporting documents of the applicant's choosing, and about fifteen minutes of discussion per case. The tests should tell us what questions to ask about foreseeability, available alternatives, insurance and precaution, and whether the applicant had a genuine opportunity to avoid the loss -- and they should say what to do when the record cannot answer a question, because in practice it often cannot.

Second, handle the cases where the theory and the charter pull apart or generate known problems: the applicant whose loss is partly choice and partly chance; the imprudent applicant (say, the member who could afford insurance, chose not to buy it, and now faces ruin) where strict application gives an answer that feels cruel -- tell us whether the procedure should incorporate the "harshness objection" as an explicit escape valve, and if so how to keep it from swallowing the rule; the member whose disaster results from a pursuit we have no wish to penalize (a religious commitment, caring for a parent) which philosophers discuss under the heading of expensive tastes; and the incentive problem that once applicants learn our criteria, every story will be framed as brute luck.

Third, give us three worked applications end to end: one clear award, one clear denial, and one where the procedure, correctly followed, yields an outcome a reasonable committee member will dislike -- I want to see the friction before it happens, not discover it in a meeting.

Be explicit about which steps of the procedure faithfully carry over the theory and which are administrative compromises; where a step is a judgment call dressed up as a rule, say so and say who on the committee should own it. Finally, state plainly what this procedure cannot do -- the categories of application where following it will produce results we should be prepared to defend in front of our membership anyway.
