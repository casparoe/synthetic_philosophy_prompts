So I was reading about perjury law (long story) and something struck me as genuinly weird. As I understand it, to convict someone of perjury the prosecution generally has to show the witness said something literally false under oath. If a witness gives an answer that's technically true but crafted to send everyone in the room in completely the wrong direction -- say, asked whether he ever had a personal bank account in Switzerland, he answers that the company had an account there, which is true but totally evasive -- courts have historically been very reluctant to call that perjury. In everyday terms the guy lied his face off, but legally he didn't.

Meanwhile the oath itself makes witnesses swear to tell the whole truth, which sure sounds like it's demanding more than mere literal truth. And elsewhere the same legal system is happy to punish misleading conduct -- fraud, false advertising, securities stuff -- without insisting on a literal lie.

I'd like you to answer as a philosopher who works on the ethics of communication -- the lying/misleading distinction, assertion vs implicature, Grice, that whole area. Think carefully and do a bit of brainstorming before you settle: maybe sketch two or three candidate reconstructions of what's going on in the doctrine, then tell me which you find most plausible.

Here's what I want to know. First: what theory of communicative wrongdoing does perjury doctrine actually presuppose? Does it only make sense on the view that lying (saying something you believe is false) is intrinsically worse than merely misleading, or could someone who thinks lying and misleading are morally on a par still justify the doctrine on other grounds -- ease of proof, fair notice to the witness, something about what the witness alone controls vs. what the listener infers? Second: is the doctrine internally consistent with the oath's demand for the whole truth, or does the law presuppose one thing in the oath and another thing in the enforcement? Third: if we fully adopted the view that misleading is just as bad as lying, what would concretely have to change about perjury law -- and can you imagine any practice-level reasons (not moral ones) to keep the literal-falsity requirement anyway?

Please distinguish, in your answer, between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with. I have a hunch the doctrine is more philosophically underdetermined than it looks, but I might be wrong.
