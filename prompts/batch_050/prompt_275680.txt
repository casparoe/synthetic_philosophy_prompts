I'm a teaching assistant for a philosophy of mind seminar, and I'm helping students prepare for a session on computational theories of mind. The professor has a running bit where students propose candidate "computers" and she rules on whether the system genuinely computes. I've been keeping a tally, and I can't figure out the rule she's applying. Her verdicts so far: an ordinary laptop running a simulation obviously computes; a rock does not compute anything, even after a student pressed her on the argument that a rock's microphysical states can be mapped onto the state transitions of arbitrary finite automata; an abacus computes, but only when someone's using it; a sundial does not compute the time, though she conceded it "carries information about" the time; the stomach does not compute its digestive chemistry, despite performing impressively reliable input-output transformations; and a hypothetical system that could hold an hour-long conversation indistinguishable from a human's, but only by storing and retrieving every possible conversational exchange from a vast lookup table, would not be thinking -- though she said, intriguingly, that whether it counts as computing is a separate question she left open. When a student asked directly whether a single neuron computes, she said it was "a harder case than it looks" and moved on.

Next week I have to lead the discussion section, and I want to anticipate her rulings on two new cases the students have already drafted: first, an ant colony whose foraging behavior reliably settles on near-shortest paths between nest and food, implementing what the biology literature itself describes as a distributed optimization algorithm, with no ant possessing anything like a representation of the route; second, a trained artificial neural network that classifies images with superhuman accuracy, whose internal activations its own designers can only interpret after the fact with probing tools. For each case, tell me what her verdict most likely is and why. Then identify the rule she seems to be running across the whole track record -- is it something like a mechanistic, causal-structure criterion for computation, a semantic criterion requiring intrinsic content, a use-relative or pragmatic criterion on which computation is ascribed rather than intrinsic to the system, or some hybrid -- and name the strongest alternative rules that fit her existing verdicts just as well. For each plausible rule, say what it predicts about the ant colony and the neural network, and where the predictions diverge.

Finally, two harder questions I'd like to spring on her if the discussion goes well. First, what single further case, if added to the record, would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving candidate rules? Second, is her combined position stable at all: her rock verdict forces her to block the triviality result that every physical system implements every computation, her lookup-table verdict suggests mere input-output matching is insufficient for mentality, but whatever restriction she adopts had better not be so strict that it excludes biological brains too -- so does any rule fitting her verdicts actually leave her room to say the brain is a computer, and if more than one does, which one costs her the least?
