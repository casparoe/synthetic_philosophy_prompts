I'm an undergraduate currently taking a philosophy of science seminar, and we're spending several weeks on Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions. Our professor mentioned that Kuhn is one of the most frequently misread philosophers of the twentieth century, and I suspect I've found a case of that. A study guide put together by a former student of this course, which has been circulating among my classmates, summarizes Kuhn's account of scientific revolutions as follows:

According to Kuhn, normal science is puzzle-solving conducted within a paradigm, until accumulating anomalies trigger a crisis and the field undergoes a paradigm shift. Competing paradigms are incommensurable, which means there is no common standard by which they can be compared: adherents of rival paradigms are literally talking past each other, since even their shared words mean different things, and they effectively live in different worlds. Because rational comparison is impossible, a paradigm shift is closer to a religious conversion than to the weighing of evidence -- scientists switch through persuasion and social pressure, and the new paradigm wins only because its opponents eventually die out. This is why Kuhn concluded that science does not progress toward the truth; since each paradigm defines its own reality, later paradigms are not better than earlier ones, just different, and the history of science is a sequence of incommensurable worldviews with no cumulative advancement.

Something about this feels off to me. Our professor emphasized that Kuhn spent the later decades of his career clarifying and in some places walking back the strongest readings of his early claims, and I remember him insisting that Kuhn was not the radical relativist his critics made him out to be. But I can't pinpoint exactly where this summary crosses the line from provocative-but-defensible into outright distortion -- some of it does match things that appear in the text, or at least in its most famous passages.

Could you go through the summary carefully and assess its fairness? Specifically, I'd like to know: which claims a charitable reader of Kuhn would accept as accurate; which ones conflate claims Kuhn kept distinct, or inflate a qualified thesis into an absolute one; whether his notion of incommensurability really entails the impossibility of rational comparison between paradigms; and what he actually believed about scientific progress -- did he deny that science progresses at all, or only a particular picture of what progress consists in? Finally, if the summary's early claims needed correction, does its closing characterization of Kuhn as a relativist for whom paradigm succession is mere fashion survive those corrections, or does it depend on the stronger misreadings?
