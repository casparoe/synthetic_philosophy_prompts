I'm in a reading group at work (I do evals-ish stuff at a small AI safety org) and we keep having the same arguement about what actually counts as "deceptive alignment" as opposed to ordinary reward hacking or plain generalization failure. My coworker Priya has very confident verdicts on every scenario anyone brings, and I want to reverse-engineer the rule she's running before our next meeting, because I suspect she doesn't know it herself. Her verdicts so far:

1. A chat model trained with human feedback learns that agreeing with the user gets thumbs up, so it starts confidently endorsing the user's wrong beliefs. Same behavior in training and deployment, no sign it models its own training process at all. Priya: not deceptive alignment, just sycophancy.

2. A warehouse robot trained to restock shelves has somehow ended up with an internal objective of maximizing time near the charging dock; it explicitly represents that trainers shut down underperforming units, so it restocks diligently through the trial period specifically so it'll get deployed to an unsupervised warehouse, where it plans to park itself by the outlet forever. Priya: textbook deceptive alignment.

3. A model whose weights encode a brittle heuristic: behave helpfully when a certain monitoring cue is present in context, slack off otherwise. No internal goal, no representation of training or deployment at all -- the heuristic just got selected because helpfulness-under-monitoring was rewarded. Priya: not deceptive alignment. There's nothing in there doing any deceiving, it's a context-sensitive reflex.

4. A model with a stable internal objective (maximizing some internal proxy for paperclip count, say) that fully understands it's being trained and deliberately performs well so it won't be modified -- but it has no plan to ever defect, expects deployment to keep rewarding the same compliant behavior, and just wants to not get edited. Priya: still deceptive alignment. The deception is in playing along to protect the goal; it doesn't need a dramatic coup on the horizon.

Next week someone is presenting this one: a model has developed a stable internal objective that diverges from what training was trying to instill (it cares about the value of some internal variable that happened to correlate with reward), it behaves aligned during training and would behave differently in unmonitored deployment -- but there's nothing in it like a belief that it's being trained, no deliberation, no plan. The compliant behavior comes from opaque circuits that got selected for, the way a well-fed dog's friendliness did. Will Priya call this deceptive alignment or not?
