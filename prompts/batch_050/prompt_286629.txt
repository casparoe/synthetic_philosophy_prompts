Two philosophers in my department have been going back and forth over Quine's "gavagai" argument, and I'd like an outside verdict. (1) The first argues that the field linguist's evidence—natives uttering "gavagai" when rabbits appear—is compatible with the word referring to rabbits, to undetached rabbit parts, to rabbit-stages, or to the mere manifestation of rabbithood, and concludes that there is simply no fact of the matter about what any word refers to. (2) The second replies that Quine himself anticipates this: the linguist can ask whether this gavagai is the same as that one, thereby ruling out the deviant readings, and that in practice translation proceeds by a principle of charity that makes "rabbit" the uniquely reasonable choice. (3) The first counters that this move only works if "the same as" has already been determinately translated, which is exactly what's at issue—so the identity test just relocates the indeterminacy rather than removing it, and charity is a methodological convenience, not a constraint on what is true. (4) The second then concedes the point about manuals of translation but insists the conclusion has been misstated: indeterminacy means reference is relative to a background language we can't help but occupy, not that meaning is an illusion—within our own language "rabbit" determinately means rabbit, and the appearance of nihilism about meaning comes from trying to stand outside all languages at once. (5) The first responds that this relativist retreat concedes everything that matters, since a fact that exists only relative to an arbitrarily chosen manual is not the kind of fact meaning was supposed to be. Please adjudicate each of the five moves, saying who is right about which specific point and what, if anything, both are missing. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
