I've been reading accounts of what it is for a physical system to compute, and I keep finding that the disagreement about whether the brain is a computer is really a disagreement about what question is being asked. Before I commit to any answer, I want the specification. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of concrete computation: what any theory of when a physical system performs a computation has to deliver before the question of the brain can even be posed non-vacuously.

At minimum, address the following, and for each give the case or argument that makes it a requirement rather than a prejudice: (1) the account must not trivialize -- the standard triviality results (Putnam-style arguments that every ordinary open system realizes every finite-state automaton, Searle-style observations that the wall in front of me can be mapped onto any program) mean that any account built on a bare state-mapping gets everything for free, so the account must say what blocks those mappings without making computation a matter of what an observer happens to find useful; (2) it must classify the easy cases correctly -- laptops, abaci, slide rules, thermostats, and a single neuron should not all come out the same way; (3) it must handle continuous and noisy systems rather than defining computation only for clean discrete state transitions, since the brain is not clean or discrete; (4) it must make room for miscomputation -- a machine can fail to do what it is computing -- without letting the normativity come from nowhere; and (5) it must explain why claiming that the brain computes is supposed to be explanatory in neuroscience rather than a relabeling exercise, if it is.

Then the harder part: identify any desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that requiring computation to be fully observer-independent sits badly with requiring the individuation of computations to track the level of description at which computational explanations in cognitive science actually operate, but I'd like that argued rather than asserted. Note any other pairs where satisfying one forces a concession on another, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate accounts.

Finally, and briefly, score the main contenders against the list: the simple mapping account, the counterfactual/causal-structure account associated with Chalmers's combinatorial state automata, mechanistic accounts of the Piccinini type, and semantic or content-involving accounts. Keep the scoring short; the requirements list is the deliverable I care about.
