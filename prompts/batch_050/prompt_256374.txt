So I write a column on big ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working up a piece about why utilitarianism keeps surviving its own refutations. A philosopher I interviewed made the classic separateness-of-persons point — the Rawlsian complaint that utilitarianism treats the losses of some as cancelled by the gains of others, as if society were one big person. Then over drinks afterwards a grad student spun it into an argument that feels like it proves way too much, and I want to use it in the piece but only if I can actually say where it breaks. Here it is as best I remember it. (1) The separateness objection works because a burden borne by A isn't compensated by a benefit to B — they're different individuals, so the gain doesn't reach the one who paid. (2) But the very same structure holds within a single life: the person-stage who suffers through dental school, or saves instead of spending, or quits smoking, is compensated by benefits that land on a different, later stage, decades away, with different memories, values, and projects. (3) On any view where personal identity over time is just a matter of degree — psychological continuity and connectedness, which fade — me-now and me-at-70 are less unified than two strangers who happen to share an apartment and a Netflix account. (4) So maximizing across a whole life ignores the separateness of person-stages exactly the way utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons. (5) So ordinary prudence — saving for retirement, enduring chemo, flossing — is morally on par with harvesting one patient to save five. (6) That's absurd, so something earlier has to go.

My editor's instinct (and mine) is that (2) is the weak link — the word "compensated" is doing something sneaky — but when I try to say exactly what, I keep slipping into just asserting that a life is one person, which feels like it's precisely what (3) denies. The grad student, fwiw, bites the bullet at (5) and says prudence is overrated, which is a great quote but not a great philosophy lesson for my readers. So: which step do you actually reject, why did it look plausible in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost you? And if the answer turns out to be that separateness only matters where there are whole persons and not stages, I want to know whether that's a principled line or just the objection's defenders saving their own argument from proving too much.
