This semester I began TA-ing an undergraduate course in set theory, and after our first lecture a student stayed behind to ask about Skolem's paradox, which she had read about over the summer. I gave her an answer I am no longer confident in, and since the topic will come up again when we reach the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems, I want to get it straight before then.

Her source was a chapter in a popular book on infinity. As best I can reconstruct it, the chapter's account runs as follows. In the early 1920s, Thoralf Skolem showed that any consistent first-order axiomatization of set theory has a model whose domain contains only countably many objects. This generates a paradox: set theory proves Cantor's theorem, including the uncountability of the real numbers, yet in such a model there are only countably many things around, so the model's "real numbers" are a countable collection and Cantor's theorem simply fails inside it. The model, the chapter says, is mistaken about its own contents. Skolem himself drew the conclusion that uncountability is not an absolute property of a collection but only relative to a model, so there is no fact of the matter about whether the continuum is really uncountable. The chapter closes by suggesting that the moral for working mathematicians is that first-order logic is too weak to express the concept of infinity, and hence that formal set theory should not be taken to capture what mathematicians actually mean when they call a set uncountable.

When the student asked, I told her the paradox is usually dissolved by distinguishing how the model looks from outside from how things look inside it, but when she pressed me on what that distinction amounts to, I found I could not articulate it cleanly. My questions are these. First, which parts of the chapter's account would a logician accept as accurate, and which parts misstate either the theorem or its significance? In particular, I am suspicious of the claim that Cantor's theorem fails inside the countable model, and of the leap from the theorem's existence to the conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about the continuum's size. Second, how exactly does the internal/external distinction resolve the apparent contradiction, and does that resolution vindicate Skolem's relativism about uncountability, resist it, or leave it as a further philosophical question the theorem itself does not settle? Third, is there anything true in the vicinity of the chapter's closing claim about the expressive limits of first-order logic, stated more carefully? I would like an answer I could give, honestly and without hand-waving, to a strong undergraduate who has not yet seen model theory.
