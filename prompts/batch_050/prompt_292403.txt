A colleague of mine defends what he cheerfully calls the Sculptor's Argument, and I want to know exactly where to push back. He states it like this.

(1) Before the sculptor begins, there is exactly one object on the pedestal: a lump of clay.
(2) Sculpting adds no matter to the pedestal and removes none; it only rearranges the clay's parts.
(3) Rearranging the parts of things that already exist never brings a new object into existence. When I rearrange six books on a shelf, I produce a new arrangement of the books, not a seventh object alongside them.
(4) So when the sculpting is finished, no new object has come into existence on the pedestal. There is still exactly what there was: the lump of clay.
(5) A statue, if there were one on the pedestal, would have to be numerically distinct from the lump, for the two have different properties: the lump would survive being rolled into a ball tomorrow and the statue would not; the statue would survive the gradual replacement of small bits of clay and the lump would not.
(6) But by (1), (2), and (4), nothing on the pedestal is numerically distinct from the lump.
(7) So there is no statue on the pedestal. There is clay arranged statue-wise, and our statue-talk is at best a convenient way of describing the clay.
(8) Nor is there anything special about sculpting. A table is lumber rearranged, a ship is planks rearranged, and a cat is, in the end, cells and nutrients rearranged. The same argument therefore shows there are no tables, no ships, and no cats — only matter, variously arranged.

He accepts the whole conclusion and advertises the economy of his ontology as a virtue. I am prepared to grant him the statues if I must — I gather serious philosophers have gone eliminativist about artifacts — but (8) seems to me to prove too much, and I would rather not learn from a metaphysics argument that my cat does not exist.

Two things I'd like. First, a diagnosis: which premise or step should someone who wants to resist actually reject? (2) seems undeniable, and (1) looks like common sense, so I assume the contest is between (3), the inference from (4) to (6), and the modal claims in (5) — but I can't tell which of these is carrying the weight, and I notice that (5)'s move from "different properties" to "numerically distinct" is doing quiet work that a four-dimensionalist or a friend of phase sortals might refuse to grant. Second, a price list. If I keep statues by rejecting (3) — saying new objects really do arise when matter falls under the right sortal — I think I'm committed to two exactly coincident objects sharing all their matter, and I'd like to know what the constitution view owes me by way of explaining what makes them two rather than one. If instead I reject the modal premises in (5), what does it cost to say the statue is identical to the lump, either contingently or as one stage of a longer space-time worm? And is there a principled way to accept the argument for tables and statues while blocking it for cats, without that looking like special pleading on behalf of organisms? Please rank the options by total cost, given that my firm preferences are: keep cats, keep ordinary statue-talk mostly true, and don't multiply entities beyond necessity.
