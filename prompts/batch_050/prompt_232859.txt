I recently came across the strategy-stealing argument in combinatorial game theory and I'm trying to understand why it hasn't been put to wider use. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows: in a game like Hex, suppose hypothetically that the second player possessed a winning strategy. The first player could then make an arbitrary opening move and thereafter play as though she were the second player, simply following the stolen strategy and treating her initial stone as irrelevant; since having an extra stone on the board can only ever help, she would win, contradicting the supposition. So the second player cannot have a winning strategy, and therefore the first player must have one.

What strikes me is how general this recipe looks. The reasoning seems to apply to any finite, symmetric, perfect-information game: open arbitrarily, adopt the hypothetical second-player strategy, and let the extra material work in your favor. So my question is this: why hasn't strategy-stealing been used to establish that White has a winning strategy in chess? Chess is finite and has perfect information, and the symmetry of the starting position seems to give White exactly the same kind of opening move that Hex's first player exploits. Is the obstacle merely practical — say, that we cannot yet compute what the stolen strategy would recommend — or is there something in the structure of chess that blocks the argument itself? If it's the former, I'd be curious whether strategy-stealing has at least been used to narrow down the expected outcome of perfect play, and if it's the latter, I'd like to understand exactly where the Hex reasoning fails to transfer.
