My philosophy of biology professor has spent the semester on naturalizing mental content, and she has a running habit of ruling on cases that come up in seminar. Her verdicts so far:

1. A toad's brain contains a state that fires when a small dark object moves across its visual field, triggering the tongue-flick; in the lab it fires equally at flies and at lead pellets flicked past it, since the mechanism cannot tell them apart. The state, she says, represents prey, not "small dark moving thing," notwithstanding the mechanism's indiscriminateness.

2. Certain anaerobic bacteria contain an internal magnetic structure that orients the cell along the earth's field; in their hemisphere the field lines angle downward toward oxygen-poor sediment, and swimming as directed is what keeps them alive. The structure's orientation, she says, represents the direction of oxygen-poor water, not the direction of magnetic north, even though the two never came apart in the lineage's history.

3. A sea slug has a withdrawal reflex; before conditioning, the internal state correlated with a formerly neutral touch to the siphon has no content, but after repeated pairings of that touch with electric shock, the state comes to mean danger. Function, she allows, can be acquired within a lifetime and need not be laid down by natural selection. Notably, she says the state means danger -- the shock side of the pairing -- rather than "touch."

4. A home thermostat, and likewise a plant's phototropic bending, have no representational content at all, despite reliably covarying with temperature and light direction. Bare correlation, she insists, never suffices; some history of selection or learning is required.

Next week she has promised to rule on this case: a toad of the same species as in case 1 is raised from a tadpole in a laboratory where the only small dark moving things it ever encounters are lead pellets flicked by an experimenter, and every tongue-flick at a pellet is promptly followed by delivery of a mealworm. After months of this regimen the toad snaps reliably at pellets and has never seen a fly. What does its speck-detector state represent -- prey, pellets, or food?

I'd like you to reconstruct her position before predicting. First, identify the two rules about how content gets fixed that are each fully consistent with verdicts 1 through 4 (there are at least two; say which standard option in this literature each most resembles). Second, state what each rule predicts about the lab toad, including which reading of the learning-friendly rule verdict 3 commits her to. Third, give your best prediction of her actual verdict, with your reasons. Fourth, describe one further case -- not the lab toad itself -- whose verdict would tell the two rules apart, and say which way she would have to rule on it to confirm each rule.
