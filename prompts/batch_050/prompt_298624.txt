The exam question was: "Set out Robert Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, stating its conditions carefully. Explain why the theory commits Nozick to denying that knowledge is closed under known implication, using the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis to illustrate. Then assess whether the denial of closure should count as a cost of the theory or as one of its attractions."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Nozick's idea is that knowledge is not a matter of having sufficiently strong reasons but of standing in the right counterfactual relation to the truth. S knows that p, via a method M, just in case: (1) p is true; (2) S believes, via M, that p; (3) if p were not true, S would not believe, via M, that p; and (4) if p were true, S would believe, via M, that p. Condition (3) is the sensitivity condition; condition (4) is often called adherence. A belief meeting both is said to track the truth.

To see the payoff, take the hypothesis that I am a brain in a vat. Consider first my belief that I have hands. In the closest worlds in which I lack hands — say, worlds where I have lost them in an accident — I would not go on believing I had them, since my perceptual methods would deliver a different verdict. So the belief is sensitive, and I know that I have hands. Now consider my belief that I am not a brain in a vat. If that belief were false — if I were a brain in a vat — I would still believe that I was not one, since the vat's controllers feed me experiences indistinguishable from ordinary life. So this belief is insensitive: if it were false, I would believe it anyway. I therefore do not know that I am not a brain in a vat.

But I do know that having hands entails not being a handless brain in a vat. So I know p, know that p entails q, and do not know q: closure fails. Nozick accepts this result openly, and it is worth stressing that the failure is narrowly confined. Closure breaks down only where the deduced proposition is the denial of a skeptical hypothesis or some equivalent heavyweight claim; ordinary deductions are unaffected. When Dretske knows at the zoo that the animal is a zebra and infers that it is not a mule cleverly painted to look like one, the inferred belief remains sensitive — if it were a painted mule, he would not believe it was not one — so the everyday closure principle is preserved even though closure against the skeptic is lost.

Should the denial of closure count against the theory? On the one hand, it validates an attractive package: we keep ordinary knowledge, refuse the skeptic's assumption that knowing p requires knowing the denials of skeptical hypotheses, and explain why skeptical arguments feel compelling — the skeptical hypothesis really is something we cannot know to be false. On the other hand, the abominable conjunction — I know I have hands, but I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat — strikes many as something no one can coherently assert, and a related dogmatism worry arises: if I know I have hands, it seems I can dismiss in advance any evidence suggesting I am a brain in a vat. A further consideration is that the rival safety condition — roughly, that S's belief could not easily have been false — secures the same anti-skeptical verdicts as sensitivity in the brain-in-a-vat case while preserving closure, since in nearby worlds where I believe I am not a BIV, that belief is true; so the closure-denying machinery may be unnecessary.

On balance, the denial of closure seems to me a genuine cost, but one that buys something real: a diagnosis of skeptical arguments as trading on a principle we have independent reason to doubt."
