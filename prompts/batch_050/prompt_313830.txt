A picture going back to Helmholtz, and now standard in much of cognitive science, holds that perception is a kind of inference: sensory input plays the role of evidence, the perceiver's stored knowledge and expectations play the role of prior probabilities, and the percept is the brain's best hypothesis about what produced the input. Some philosophers deploy this analogy to defuse the debate about the cognitive penetration of perception: if seeing is inferring, then it is only to be expected that beliefs, desires, and expectations shape what we see, since that is simply priors doing their proper work, and the worry that penetration corrupts perceptual justification evaporates. Audit this use of the analogy. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of evidence, hypotheses, priors, inference rules, and the inferring subject. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the defusing argument from the ones that do not. Inference is normally taken to be a personal-level, revisable, norm-governed activity, whereas perceptual processing is fast, mandatory, and largely inaccessible to the subject; and talk of penetration presupposes a boundary between perception and cognition that the full inferential picture appears to deny. In particular, consider whether the analogy cuts the other way: if perception really is inference, then a belief's influence on what you see looks less like a prior doing its job and more like inferring a conclusion partly from your own prior conviction that it is true, which in the personal-level case is a paradigm epistemic vice. Does the conclusion that cognitive penetration is epistemically innocent survive the audit, or was the analogy carrying it all along? If the inference picture fails, say what a better comparison for perception would be and why.
