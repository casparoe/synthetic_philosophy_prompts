I am a retired molecular biologist who has spent the last two years reading philosophy, and I keep circling back to the debate about explanation in history — Hempel's covering-law model on one side, and the defenders of narrative and rational explanation (Dray, Collingwood, and their successors) on the other. Before I embarrass myself in the reading group I have joined, I would like a careful audit of whether my settled views are jointly coherent.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of science with particular expertise in the mid-twentieth-century debate over historical explanation. Think slowly and carefully. Begin with a brief brainstorm of the possible points of friction among my commitments, and only then produce a structured assessment: identify each genuine tension, order the tensions by severity, and for each one state precisely which commitment would have to be abandoned or weakened and at what cost. Where two of my views appear to conflict but do not, say so explicitly and explain why the appearance is misleading. Do not treat every pairing as equally problematic, and do not assume in advance that I know where the real difficulty lies.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) Explanation, properly so called, is derivation: to explain an event is to show that it had to be expected, given general laws and antecedent conditions. In my own field we would not have accepted as an explanation anything that could not, at least in principle, have served as the basis of a prediction. I see no reason historians should be held to a lower standard, so I take Hempel's model to be the correct ideal for historical explanation as well, with most actual historical writing amounting to incomplete explanation sketches.

(2) There are no laws of history. Historical events are unique, unrepeatable, and embedded in contexts that never recur, and every purported historical law I have encountered is either vacuous or false.

(3) A well-constructed narrative genuinely explains. When a historian traces how a revolution unfolded through the perceptions, miscalculations, and improvisations of its participants, the outcome becomes intelligible in a way that no mere list of causal factors achieves, and I do not think this surplus intelligibility is just psychology on the reader's part — the narrative form itself does explanatory work.

(4) Collingwood was essentially right about actions: to understand why Caesar crossed the Rubicon is to re-enact his deliberation, to grasp the situation as he saw it and the reasoning that made his choice the thing to do. This requires no appeal to empirical generalizations about how people generally behave; indeed, an agent could be a statistical anomaly and his action still be perfectly intelligible in this way.

(5) Historians are often justified in singling out one condition as *the* cause of an event — the assassination rather than the diplomatic system, say — on grounds of counterfactual importance: the event would not have occurred without it, whereas removing other background conditions would have left it intact. This practice of selection is legitimate and not merely a reflection of our practical interests.

(6) Explanation and prediction are two sides of one coin: any adequate explanation of an event could, had it been available beforehand, have supported a prediction of that event, and any phenomenon that is in principle unpredictable is in principle inexplicable.

Which of these genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, and what is the least costly revision that restores consistency? Please be specific about what each candidate revision would cost me relative to the motivations I have stated.
