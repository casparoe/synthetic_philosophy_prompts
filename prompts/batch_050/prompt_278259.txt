I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am designing a workshop for engineers who already understand redundant implementations, quotient types, canonical forms, and gauge fixing in simulations. I want a decision tree for the following question: in a gauge theory, which parts of the mathematical formalism represent physical reality, and which parts are surplus structure?

Please construct the taxonomy as a decision tree rather than as a prose survey. Start from this neutral situation: two mathematically distinct models are related by an admissible gauge transformation and generate exactly the same observable predictions. The tree should determine whether that fact settles their physical identity and, if not, what further considerations do.

Follow these requirements:

1. Use 9–12 branch nodes. Every branch should be a question about a distinct commitment, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing the names of the philosophical positions. Do not use nodes equivalent to “Do you accept position X?”
2. First present the logical space generated by the answers; only afterward assign established position names to the occupied regions. If a logically possible combination has no standard defender, say so and briefly explain whether it appears incoherent, unmotivated, or merely underexplored.
3. The leaves should include, where the tree supports them:
   - fine-grained realism, under which gauge-related models can represent distinct physical states;
   - invariant-only reductionism, under which only gauge-invariant quantities are fundamental;
   - sophisticated symmetry realism, under which gauge-related descriptions represent one possibility even though the larger mathematical structure remains useful or explanatory;
   - relational structural realism, under which the fundamental facts concern relations between systems or subsystems rather than locally assigned intrinsic values;
   - representational deflationism or pragmatism, under which asking which redundant representation is real confuses a modeling choice with a metaphysical fact;
   - a boundary-sensitive position, under which transformations that are redundant in the bulk can correspond to physical degrees of freedom at boundaries.
4. Treat the following as independent dimensions where appropriate: whether empirical inequivalence entails physical unreality; whether symmetry-related labels are physically individuating; whether an entire gauge orbit can be the physical object; whether a structure may be nonfundamental yet still objectively instantiated; and whether redundancy is judged differently for global, local, internal, and spacetime symmetries.
5. Include a small battery of diagnostic cases. At minimum, use:
   - two electromagnetic potential configurations related by a gauge transformation and producing the same electromagnetic field;
   - a uniform transformation of every internal degree of freedom in a closed universe;
   - a local transformation affecting only an interior region while boundary data remain fixed;
   - a simulation that gauge-fixes variables before numerically evolving them;
   - a formulation using only invariant relational quantities, such as loops or correlation data;
   - transformations normally classified as redundant that act nontrivially at an asymptotic boundary.
6. For every leaf, provide a one-sentence definition, its answer pattern through the tree, its verdicts on the diagnostic cases, and its most awkward consequence. Do not define the views merely by listing which mathematical objects they call “real.”
7. Explicitly flag any leaves your questions cannot reliably separate—especially invariant-only reductionism and sophisticated symmetry realism if your tree treats their difference as purely presentational. Explain what additional dispute would be needed to separate them.
8. Distinguish three claims that are often run together: gauge choices are conventional; gauge-dependent quantities are not fundamental; and gauge-dependent quantities have no physical status whatsoever. Your taxonomy should not treat these as interchangeable.
9. Use electromagnetism or Yang–Mills theory only as illustrations, not as a source of technical results the reader must know. Briefly define any term that would not be familiar to a mathematically competent software engineer.
10. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or suggested readings; the tree should stand on its conceptual distinctions alone.
