So I work for a midsize organization that accredits museums, battlefield sites, and school history programs (think of us like a halfway house between a funding body and a standards board). Part of my job is reviewing proposals for commemorations -- plaques, exhibits, anniversary events, curriculum units -- and I keep running into the same problem: some of them are honest remembrance of a defeat, and some of them are lost-cause mythmaking, and I can't get my review committee to agree on where the line is. Recent cases on my desk: a donor-funded exhibit on Confederate soldiers framed entirely around their letters home, a state park audio guide about Masada, a town that wants a Dunkirk-style "miracle of deliverance" plaque about its own evacuation in a war most visitors have never heard of, and a proposal referencing the 1389 battle of Kosovo for a Serbian cultural center.

Here's what I'd like you to do. Historians who study lost causes seem to converge on a few markers: the defeat gets retroactively cleansed of its actual causes (slavery vanishes from the Confederate story), the losers get recast as morally superior to the winners, the defeat becomes a kind of secular sacrifice or martyrdom, and the myth ends up doing political work in the present that the original event couldn't have. I want you to turn that cluster of ideas into an actual review procedure my committee can apply in about 30 minutes per proposal, by people who are smart but not historians.

A few requirements. First, I want the procedure to hinge on a distinction I've been toying with, which you should refine or replace if its broken: some commemorations are *grief-facing* (they mourn the dead and let the dead be dead) and some are *cause-facing* (they recruit the dead into an ongoing project). A good procedure should tell us which one we're looking at, since grief-facing commemoration is usually fine and cause-facing is where the trouble starts -- but note the tricky bit, which is that the same artifact can be one or the other depending on framing, so the procedure needs tests applied to the text and context, not just the topic.

Second, give us the tests as an ordered sequence -- things like: does the narrative state why the war/battle happened at all, and does it do so in the first third of the text? If you removed every adjective, would the cause still look noble? Does the account mention any contemporaneous critic of the cause? Who is invited to identify with whom? That sort of thing, but done properly and grounded in what scholarship on lost causes actually says rather than my half-remembered version of it.

Third, work the procedure on at least three of my four cases above, and make sure at least one of them comes out with a verdict that will make me or a donor uncomfortable -- e.g., I suspect the Masada audio guide and the Dunkirk plaque are not as innocent as they look, or maybe the Confederate letters exhibit is more defensible than my committee assumes, whichever way the procedure actually cuts. Don't rig it to be even-handed; follow the procedure.

Fourth, mark the points where the procedure runs out and a human has to just decide -- I assume there are cases (maybe the Kosovo one, given the present-day politics) where no checklist settles it, and I'd rather you flag those honestly than pretend the rubric covers everything. Also tell me what the procedure will systematically get wrong -- who it will unfairly clear and who it will unfairly convict.
