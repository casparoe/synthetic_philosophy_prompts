I'm a TA for an undergrad ethics course and I'm drowning in grading this week, so I'm trying to streamline how I do comments on these short papers. The assignment was a 2-page response to this question:

"Jane English famously argues that the answer to the question of what grown children owe their parents is 'nothing.' Explain why she rejects the debt model of filial obligation and how her friendship model is supposed to work instead. Then consider the objection that her view makes filial obligations too easy to escape: if what's owed depends on the current state of the friendship, a child could dissolve her obligations simply by letting contact lapse. Can English respond to this? Is the response adequate?"

Here's the student's answer, which I need to grade out of 20 with written comments:

"English's answer is 'nothing,' by which she means grown children have no filial obligations as such. Her target is the debt model, the idea that parental sacrifices create a debt children must repay. She argues that no debt exists because the children never consented to or requested the care they recieved, and debts can only arise from voluntary transactions. In place of the debt model she proposes the friendship model: what grown children owe their parents are the duties of friendship — loyalty, staying in touch, spending time together, help when needed — and these duties exist only as long as the friendship does. Where the friendship has died, nothing is owed. English takes this to be a strength of the view, since unlike the gratitude model, which she endorses as a fallback for cases where friendship is absent, the friendship model explains why the obligation is ongoing rather than a finite debt that could in principle be paid off.

As for the objection, English can reply that friendship is not voluntary in the way the objection assumes: genuine friendships rest on a shared history and cannot be ended by a unilateral decision the way you cancel a subscription, so the duties persist for as long as any real friendship does. This response strikes me as adequate, since it shows filial obligations are exactly as robust as friendship itself, and nobody thinks the duties of friendship are optional."
