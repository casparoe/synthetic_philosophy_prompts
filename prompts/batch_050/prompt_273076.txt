Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about Aristotle's sea battle and parted without resolution. Her position, which she insisted was the common-sense one, was that a claim like 'there will be a naval engagement in the Strait of Gibraltar tomorrow' is, as of today, neither true nor false, so long as the matter is not yet causally settled; the future, she says, has to happen before anything can be true of it. I held that bivalence is non-negotiable and that the fatalist argument from present truth to necessity trades on a scope confusion between the necessity of the conditional and the necessity of the consequent. She ended the evening at roughly 0.2 that future contingents have determinate truth values right now; I ended near 0.8, though I concede my confidence outruns my command of the machinery.

I would like your own credence that future contingents — propositions about particular future events that are neither necessitated nor excluded by the present state of the world — are true or false at present. Begin by stating the proposition precisely enough to have a truth value, then assign your number, then decompose it. Among the terms I would expect, though you may restructure them: your probability that the classical fatalist argument is valid rather than equivocating; your probability that truth about the future requires a presently existing ground or truthmaker; your probability that the Ockhamist reply — the proposition has always been true, yet its having been true constrains nothing — dissolves the problem rather than merely restating it; your probability that some non-bivalent semantics (three-valued, supervaluationist, or branching-time) is coherent and does not covertly rebuild determinism on every branch; and an empirical term for whether the future is causally open at all, together with your judgment about whether the logical question even depends on that empirical one. Give each term a number and one line of justification, show how they combine into your final credence, identify the term to which the result is most sensitive, and say where, in your decomposition, my friend's 0.2 and my 0.8 most likely diverge.
