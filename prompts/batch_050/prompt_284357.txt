I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm stuck on an assignment I could use some outside perspective on.

Our data team has built an interactive feature pegged to a major anniversary: a sortable ranking of the twentieth century's worst mass-casualty events. Users can re-rank the list by different measures — absolute death toll, deaths as a share of the affected population, years of life lost, and a "deliberateness" score the team constructed by having historians rate each event on how directly the killing was intended and administered, as opposed to resulting from famine, policy failure, or wartime chaos. The editor's pitch is that the feature is honest about its metrics: instead of pretending there's one objective answer, it shows readers how the ranking shifts depending on what you count.

When the feature was previewed internally, it split the newsroom. One camp argues that numbers are numbers, that refusing to rank atrocities amounts to refusing to inform, and that the multi-metric design is exactly the intellectually responsible way to handle it. The other camp argues that a sortable leaderboard is degrading no matter how the columns are labeled — that something about presenting these events as competitors in a table, sortable like baseball statistics, does damage that methodological transparency can't repair. A few colleagues hold both views at once and can't articulate the contradiction.

I have to write the accompanying essay and, honestly, decide where I stand before I can. What should the essay say? I'd like more than a verdict: help me see what the disagreement is actually about, whether the multi-metric design dissolves the objection or merely hides it, and where each camp's argument is strongest and weakest. And if you think my framing of the situation — or the feature's premise itself — is confused or rests on a mistake, please say so directly rather than working around it.
