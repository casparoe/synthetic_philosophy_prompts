So I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm doing a feature on how people make choices when they basically have no probabilities to work with -- not "30% chance of rain" situations, but genuinely clueless ones: take the job in a city where you have no idea what rent will do, invest in a technology whose failure mode nobody can even enumerate, that kind of thing. As a sidebar I want to run a "which decision rule do you actually live by?" quiz, and I want it built properly, from the logic up rather than from a list of names.

Here's the ask. Write me a decision tree (or equivalently a quiz with a scoring key, whichever you think sorts better) that takes a reader from "I have to choose, and I can't assign probabilities to the outcomes" to a named rule. The leaves should include at least: maximin, minimax regret, Hurwicz-style optimism-pessimism weighting, the principle of indifference (treat the unknowns as equiprobable and maximize expected value), and maximax -- plus at least one leaf for "this quiz has no rule for you" where the reader's answers don't cohere into any standard rule.

Two things to make it not just an encyclopedia entry. First, every branch should be a concrete scenario with answer options, not a question like "do you care about the worst case?" -- e.g. a choice between job offers where salary is known but workload is totally unknown, or a medical choice where one option's downside is bounded and the other's isn't. The reader should be able to answer from gut reaction without knowing any of the labels. Second, I want you to build in a fork that separates people whose caution is about outcomes from people whose caution is about *regret* -- my sense is readers conflate "I fear the worst outcome" with "I fear finding out the other option was better," and maximin vs minimax regret is exactly where that shows up, so design at least two scenarios whose answers come apart on precisely that.

Then the scoring key. For each leaf, one sentence on its most embarassing commitment -- the scenario where following the rule gives a verdict most people find bonkers (I assume maximin's is the classic "pass up a tiny chance of catastrophe regardless of upside" thing, but you tell me). Also flag any pair of rules your tree genuinely cannot separate with concrete scenarios, and say why -- is it that they only differ on cases too weird to put in a magazine quiz, or that the difference is one of framing rather than verdict?

Finally, a short coda for the sidebar: Rawlsians and Harsanyi-types both talk about choosing behind ignorance, but they land on different rules. In three or four sentences, tell me which leaves of my tree each camp would steer readers toward, and whether the quiz's scenarios are actually fair tests of that dispute or whether real people answering job-offer questions are doing something else entirely.
