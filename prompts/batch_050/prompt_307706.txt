In my day-to-day work as a software engineer, intentional vocabulary is unavoidable: the load balancer decides where to route traffic, the compiler expects a semicolon, the retry logic assumes the failure is transient, and our customer-support chatbot is evaluated each sprint on whether it understood the user's request. Yet nobody on my team would say, except as a joke, that the office thermostat believes the room is too cold. I recently came across Dennett's two-bitser -- a machine built to sort US quarters that would keep doing its job identically if shipped abroad and used to sort a different coin of the same size and weight -- together with his claim that there is simply no fact of the matter about which coin it really detects, and that the same is ultimately true of us. What theory of meaning and intentionality does my professional practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat the chatbot's understanding as the same kind of thing as a human's, or as something merely borrowed from its designers, trainers, and users? Is the practice internally consistent -- can it justify grading the chatbot on understanding while denying beliefs to the thermostat, and on what principled basis? Finally, say whether the practice strictly requires the theory it seems to assume or could survive under rivals, and what would concretely have to change in how we build and evaluate AI systems if Searle is right that artifacts can only ever have derived intentionality, or if Dennett is right that intentionality is everywhere just a stance we adopt toward things whose behavior it predicts.
