I'm a novelist working on a book where one of the main characters, an older guy who teaches tai chi in a community center basement, has to get the idea of wu-wei across to the fifteen-year-old protagonist over the course of one lesson. The kid is stressed out of his mind, over-prepares for everything, and is deeply allergic to anything that smells like mysticism -- he's the kind of teenager who hears a word like wu-wei and immediately checks out. In the draft I have now, the instructor just lectures him and it's dead on the page, obviously.

So before I rewrite the scene, help me figure out how a real, good teacher would actually build this explanation in something like forty minutes of talking and moving around. Don't give me the lecture itself -- give me the plan. What idea has to be in place before "effortless action" even makes sense as a thing to want, and what order do the pieces have to come in? I'm guessing the paradox part (that trying to stop trying is still trying, so telling someone to do it seems self-defeating) has to go somewhere specific in the sequence, not just be sprung at the end, but I can't figure out where.

Also: one example has to carry the whole thing. I've been considering three -- learning to ride a bike, learning to swim, and a cook who used to measure everything and one day stops needing to -- and I'd like you to pick which one should do the heavy lifting and say what the other two lack (I have a feeling swimming is secretly the wrong choice but can't articulate why). Whatever example we use has to work for a kid who has no interest in Chinese philosophy and will seize on anything cheesy.

Then the misconceptions. I'm sure the kid will form some predictable wrong versions -- like "so it just means go with the flow and do whatever feels good" or "so practice is pointless, the masters are just lazy" -- and I want the full list in the order he'd probably land on them, plus where in the sequence each one should get headed off so the scene doesn't turn into whack-a-mole at the end.

Two more things. First, the paradox: ideally the lesson itself shouldn't commit the thing it's warning about, i.e. a teacher droning "stop striving" at someone is itself a kind of striving. If there's a way to structure the plan so the teaching enacts the point rather than just describing it, I want that flagged, because that's the thing that would make the scene actually work as fiction. Second, tell me what to leave out entirely at this level -- the scholarly disputes, the religious dimensions, whatever -- since the instructor is a practical guy and I need to know what he would defensibly skip without teaching something wrong.

And give me one thing the kid could say or do at the end of the scene that would show me (and the reader) that it landed, rather than him just nodding politely. Something concrete, not him saying "I get it now."
