Earlier this year the small grant-making foundation where I work began using a large language model for the first-pass screening of applications, and because our board requires a documented reason for every rejection, I fell into the habit of asking the system to explain each of its verdicts. Over several months I have noticed a recurring pattern that I cannot find a name for, and I would like to know whether the phenomenon is established, under what label, and with what boundaries.

Three instances, in the order I noticed them. First, when I feed the same batch of applications to the system in a different order, a few of its verdicts flip, yet the explanations it produces never mention order; each reads as though the verdict were compelled entirely by the text in front of it, and the explanations on either side of a flip are equally fluent and equally confident. Second, when I ask it to verify a budget table, it sometimes volunteers its working, and on two occasions I have redone the stated steps by hand and found they do not yield the number it gave; the number appears to have come from somewhere else, with the steps arranged around it afterward. Third, when I challenge a verdict, the system concedes and immediately produces a new, equally assured explanation for the opposite verdict, which suggests to me that the explanation trails the conclusion rather than generating it.

I recognize a resemblance to what psychologists call confabulation in humans, where a person sincerely reports reasons that were not in fact operative. But the human case presupposes a hidden process plus a sincere but mistaken report of it, and I am unsure whether the analogy survives in a system that produces the report and the verdict in much the same way, nor whether the relevant literatures — in philosophy of mind, in cognitive psychology, or in the study of these systems themselves — treat this as one phenomenon or several. I also want the phenomenon distinguished from its neighbors: from lying, which seems to require an intention these systems arguably lack; from so-called hallucination, which as I understand it concerns false content rather than the relation between a process and a report of that process; and from ordinary human rationalization after the fact.

So my question is not merely terminological, and I will not be satisfied with a bare label. Please tell me whether this configuration — where a system's report of how it reached an output and the actual source of the output come apart, in systems that generate both by the same means — has an established name and literature, what the nearest concepts are, and where the edges of each lie. More importantly, I want a committed verdict rather than a survey: take a definite position, with reasons, on whether self-reports of this kind can ever count as evidence of what produced the output, or whether my board's documentation requirement is, in this context, epistemically worthless and I should say so. Write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables.
