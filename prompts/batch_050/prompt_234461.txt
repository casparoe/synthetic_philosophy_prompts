So I'm running a reading group for grad students on philosophy of set theory, and last week we did the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem and Skolem's paradox. It went fine but also badly, in the specific way that everyone had memorized the standard dissolution (the model is countable, the missing bijection just isn't in the model, nothing to see here) and treated it like a full stop. I tried to push them with the passage where Skolem himself raises the worry, in "Some remarks on axiomatized set theory" from 1922: "How can it be, then, that the entire domain B [a countable model of Zermelo's axioms] can already be enumerated by means of the finite positive integers?" And what struck me is that Skolem is asking this *after* he has the technical resolution -- he knows perfectly well why there's no contradiction, and he still thinks the question needs an answer. My students couldn't really say what that further answer could even be, which told me they don't actually have positions, they have a slogan.

So here's what I want: a decision tree handout I can give them before next session, titled something like "what do you actually think Skolem's paradox shows." Six to eight leaves. Each internal node should be a question someone can answer without knowing any of the labels -- e.g. whether "uncountable" names an absolute property of a set or only a relation to a background stock of functions, whether the metatheory where the theorem is proved is itself subject to the same result (and if not, what privileges it), whether anything about our practice or intentions could single out an intended model, whether the first-order sentence that formally "says" there are uncountable sets really does say that. The leaves should cover the main real options: Skolem's own relativism about set-theoretic notions, the textbook dissolution (paradox is mere appearance, countability is absolute from the external standpoint), the view that first-order languages just can't express uncountability so you move to second-order logic with full semantics, the view that the axioms don't fix an intended interpretation but our use of the language does, a Putnam-style anti-realist reading where the moral generalizes to all reference, and a pluralist or multiverse picture where each model is its own legitimate universe. Each leaf gets the name of the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two constraints that matter to me. First, the textbook dissolution must NOT be a terminal node: anyone who lands there has to answer at least two more questions about where the external standpoint comes from before the tree lets them stop, because that complacency is exactly what I'm trying to break. Second, build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names -- so note any unoccupied combinations of answers and say briefly whether they're empty because they're incoherent or just unattractive, and flag any pair of positions your questions can't pull apart (I suspect relativism and the multiverse view will be tricky to seperate, and I'd rather have that flagged honestly than papered over with a fake distinguisher). If a question doesn't split any of the six positions, cut it -- I'd rather have five sharp nodes than nine decorative ones.
