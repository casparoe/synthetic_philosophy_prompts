Next month I'm giving a twenty-minute paper at our university's history of science colloquium, and I've talked myself into a corner I'd like help defending. My claim is that Newton's theology was not a private eccentricity bolted onto the Principia in old age, but did real work inside his natural philosophy -- that his voluntarist, dominion-centered conception of God is bound up with his absolute space and time and with his refusal to treat gravity as mechanically explicable. The audience is mostly historians of early modern science, some of them committed contextualists who will sympathize with the general project but who know the Newton manuscript literature far better than I do, plus two philosophers of physics who I suspect hold the older "separability" view (Newton the physicist can be cleanly extracted from Newton the theologian), plus a church historian who works on anti-Trinitarianism and will know exactly how much I'm relying on published texts versus the Yahuda manuscripts. Nobody there is a Newton specialist full-time, but several have read Westfall and Dobbs and will push back from that reading.

The hinge of my argument is a passage from the General Scholium, which I'll project during the talk. Newton writes: "He is not eternity or infinity, but eternal and infinite; he is not duration or space, but he endures and is present. He endures for ever, and is every where present; and by existing always and every where, he constitutes duration and space." My reading is that this is not pious decoration: Newton is deriving absolute space and time from God's mode of existence, which means the metaphysics of Book I's definitions is downstream of a theology, and the theology is doing explanatory work a purely mechanical philosophy couldn't do. I also use the passage to argue Newton's God is a God of dominion rather than a world-soul, which I connect to his insistence that gravity's cause is not to be feigned mechanically.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way this particular audience would phrase them -- historians' objections sound different from philosophers' objections, so mark who raises what. For each, tell me what I should concede without damaging the thesis, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objections based on things I've left out: the Latin versus the Motte translation, the chronological problem of when Newton wrote the Scholium relative to the doctrine of absolute space, and whether "constitutes" can bear the weight I'm putting on it. And tell me honestly which single question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I have a suspicion it's the one about the manuscripts I've never read, but you may see a worse one.
