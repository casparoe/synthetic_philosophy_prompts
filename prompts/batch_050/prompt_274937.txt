I'm putting together a reading group on measurement and optimization, and we keep running into the same muddle: everyone in the group gestures at Goodhart's law, but when pressed we can't agree on what a satisfactory theory of it would even look like -- what such a theory should explain, which cases it must cover, and which phenomena it should deliberately leave out. Before we read any more proposed frameworks, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata for a theory of proxy failure: what an adequate account of why metrics degrade under optimization pressure must be able to deliver. Include, for each desideratum, the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a camp-specific preference.

To give you the lay of our disagreement, so the list engages with it: one member insists the theory must sharply distinguish at least four failure modes that all get lumped together -- a proxy that was only ever correlated with the goal and drifts apart under selection pressure; a proxy that tracks the goal at moderate levels but breaks at the extremes of its range; a proxy that was causally upstream of the goal until optimizing it directly disrupted the causal pathway; and a proxy being actively gamed by an agent who knows it is being measured. Another member thinks that taxonomy misses the point and that the real requirement is explaining why legible, quantifiable proxies seem to fail faster than holistic, judgment-based ones. A third wants any adequate theory to say something about mitigation -- not just diagnosis -- and thinks a framework that doesn't distinguish recoverable from unrecoverable metric collapse is useless to practitioners. Meanwhile, I'm unsure the whole enterprise shouldn't be subsumed under more general results about distribution shift, adversarial optimization, or the difference between selection and control, and I'd like the desiderata list to address whether Goodhart-type phenomena deserve their own theory at all.

So: give me the requirements list -- the cases an account must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse (for instance, between gaming a metric and honestly optimizing a bad one, or between a measure failing as a measure versus failing as a target), the results from statistics or economics it must remain consistent with -- with the motivation behind each. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement would knock out the most naive versions you encounter in practice. A closing paragraph briefly scoring two or three familiar framings against the list is welcome, but the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard.
