So I help run a local rationality-adjacent reading group and we're doing a month on behavioral economics classics, and I want to kick off the loss aversion session with a "what do you actually believe?" sorting instrument rather than the usual thing where everyone just nods along to Kahneman. My frustration with the standard discourse (and honestly with a lot of LW-adjacent discussion too) is that "loss aversion is a bias" and "loss aversion is a legitimate preference" get treated as two teams, when the real action is in subtler positions: that the endowment effect is just transaction costs plus rational uncertainty about why someone wants to trade with you, that reference dependence is a framing artifact that disappears once you fix the description, that losses genuinely loom larger hedonicly and that's fine, that it's a bias but a useful one we should keep, that it's a bias and we should train it away, etc.

Could you write me a decision tree or taxonomy that sorts people into positions on this? Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names: start from questions someone can answer without knowing any labels, like concrete cases (the mug experiment, refusing a coin flip that's win 200 / lose 100, refusing to sell a concert ticket for 10x what you paid, being more upset about a $30 parking ticket than happy about finding $30). Then each branch should be the claim that actually separates positions — e.g., "is the asymmetry in the feelings themselves or in how we report/predict them?", "would the effect survive if the subject fully understood the setup and had no reason to distrust the experimenter?", "is stability of preference itself a value?" Each leaf should get a position name (real if one exists, 'unnamed' with a note on whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored if not) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Requirements: roughly 8 to 12 leaves, and I need at least the following to appear as distinct leaves: pure-bias-to-be-elimininated, legitimate hedonic asymmetry, rational-response-to-asymmetric-information, and "the effect is real but mostly an artifact of how the experiments frame 'yours' vs 'not yours'." Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't cleanly separate with a diagnostic question, rather than pretending it can. End with a short note on how confident you are in the overall structure and single out the single branch or leaf you're least sure you've carved correctly.
