I run a small reading group that pairs philosophy papers with novels, and our next session matches Warren Quinn's puzzle of the self-torturer with Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray. For those in the group who haven't done the reading, I usually circulate a one-paragraph gloss, and this is what I've drafted: Quinn imagines a device fitted to a person's body with a long row of settings, where each step up delivers a payment but also increases the pain by an amount too small to feel at the moment of turning. At every individual step it seems rational to turn the dial -- the money is real and the difference is imperceptible -- yet the agent who keeps turning ends in agony he would never have chosen outright. Quinn's lesson, as I understand it, is that rationality cannot be assessed choice-by-choice against adjacent alternatives; the agent must at some point reason about the trajectory, adopt a stopping rule not grounded in pairwise comparisons, and treat his future selves as parties he can betray.

I want to write up a critical reading of the novel in these terms, and I'd like your help developing it. Dorian's corruption looks incremental: there is no single evening on which he becomes cruel, and the narrative repeatedly stresses that he himself cannot detect the change -- only the portrait registers it, one barely visible alteration at a time. That suggests the portrait is doing the work Quinn says the rational agent must do internally: it makes the cumulative trajectory visible precisely because the increments are not. But I'm unsure how far this goes. Dorian, unlike the self-torturer, is given an external record of exactly the information his situation hides from him, so is his failure actually the same failure -- or is it something worse, or something different in kind, like weakness of will or simple refusal to look? The murder of Basil also seems like a discontinuity that breaks the incremental pattern, and Wilde's moralism (corruption has a price; aesthetic detachment is punished) may sit awkwardly with Quinn's puzzle, which is about rationality rather than vice. Please give me a reading that engages the scenes a reader would actually cite -- the wish before the portrait, the abandonment of Sibyl Vane, the opium dens, Basil's murder, the late attempt to be good by sparing Hetty Merton, and the final stabbing of the canvas -- and that says where the novel resists the self-torturer frame rather than forcing it to fit.

Please take a definite position rather than surveying possibilities: commit to a verdict on whether the novel genuinely dramatizes the self-torturer's structure or instead depicts a distinct failure of agency, and argue for it. I'd also like you to commit on the harder question of what the novel contributes that the philosophical literature lacks -- my own hunch is that Wilde understands something about outsourced self-observation (we are increasingly like Dorian, letting external records track what we decline to feel incrementally) that Quinn's armchair setup misses -- and if the reading leans on a contested interpretation of either the novel or Quinn's argument, flag it explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
