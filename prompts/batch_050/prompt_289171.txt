Background: our team was picking between two vendors for a monitoring service, one we'd used before (known outage rate) and one new (no data), and Marcus said preferring the known vendor for that reason alone is literally the Ellsberg fallacy. Dana disagreed and they ended up setting up the actual urn version on a whiteboard. Rough transcript of the four moves:

(1) Marcus: whatever credence Dana assigns to "red from the unknown urn," symmetry forces it to 50% -- there's no argument for red over black that isn't equally an argument for black over red. So the known-urn bet and the unknown-urn bet have identical expected value, and preferring the known one is just an unforced error. A bias, same as loss aversion.

(2) Dana: the symmetry argument only pins down her credence, it doesn't make the bets identical. With the unknown urn she can't rule out that the proportions are unfavorable or even that someone rigged it. If the urn was stocked by anyone who gets to see her bet, betting on it is a guaranteed loss, so avoiding the ambiguous bet is rational caution, not a fallacy.

(3) Marcus: that's smuggling in an adversary the setup explicitly excludes -- the urn was filled by a random process, stated up front. And anyway the objection proves too much: by that logic she should refuse any bet where she didn't personally verify the mechanism, which would paralyze basically every real-world decision. Also, over repeated plays the ambiguity washes out, so single-shot aversion is incoherent with how she'd act across many trials.

(4) Dana: "it washes out in the long run" is irrelevant for a one-shot choice -- this is one vendor decision, not a thousand draws. And Marcus's point in (3) actually concedes hers: he treats a 50% credence with zero evidence behind it as interchangable with a 50% credence backed by actually knowing the urn. A coin she knows is fair and a coin she found on the street are not the same bet even if her credence in heads is 0.5 for both.
