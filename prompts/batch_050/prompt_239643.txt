I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about mind-uploading and machine consciousness. While reporting, I interviewed a philosopher of mind who walked me through an argument against the view he called functionalism, and I've been unable to find the mistake — or to decide whether there is one. I'd like a careful diagnosis. Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it in my notes, using a thought experiment I gather has circulated in philosophy-of-mind anthologies since the early 1980s:

(1) What makes a physical system have a mind is its functional organization: the pattern of internal states it passes through in response to inputs, and the way those states produce outputs. Any two systems that pass through the same sequence of functional states, given the same inputs, have the same thoughts and experiences, whatever they're made of.

(2) Imagine that Einstein's brain, shortly before his death, was exhaustively mapped: every relevant state it could occupy, and for every state and every possible sensory input, the next state the brain would enter. Imagine all of this printed as an enormous set of lookup volumes — the Book. A patient clerk who reads questions aloud, looks up the current entry, and moves a marker to the indicated next entry thereby passes through, step for step, exactly the functional organization Einstein's brain passed through while holding a conversation.

(3) So by (1), the book-and-clerk system, while being operated, genuinely hosts Einstein's mind. Talking to it is talking to Einstein; it thinks, remembers, and feels as he did.

(4) But nothing about the Book itself changes when the clerk works or sleeps. Ten clerks in ten libraries would yield ten simultaneous Einsteins; a clerks' strike would yield none. So on this view, whether Einstein's mind exists — and how many of him there are — is decided entirely by librarians' schedules. Facts about who is conscious become facts about other people's hobbies.

(5) Worse: given enough scratch paper and a flexible enough scheme for reading the entries, the same volumes could be operated so as to trace the state-transitions of any mind whatsoever. So the Book, sitting shut on the shelf, already contains every possible mind — and a theory on which a static object contains all minds has lost the ability to say what makes anything conscious.

(6) The consequences in (4) and (5) are absurd. Hence (1) is false: functional organization is not sufficient for mind. Consciousness must depend on the material substrate itself — something about living neurons, not the abstract pattern they implement.

I should disclose my priors: my piece is broadly sympathetic to substrate-independence, and I'd like to keep (1), or something close to it, intact. But I want the strongest honest analysis, not an apologetic. Please identify which step or steps you reject, explain why each rejected step looked plausible on first reading, and state what rejecting or qualifying it actually costs — including, where relevant, what it costs for (1). If you think the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to surrender, say so and argue for your choice.

After your prose diagnosis, please summarize your analysis as a table with the following columns: Step (the step number); Verdict (one of "accept," "reject," or "accept with qualification"); Source of Plausibility (why the step initially seems fine); Cost of Rejecting (what a theorist gives up by denying or qualifying it).
