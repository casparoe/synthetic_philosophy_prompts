Two colleagues of mine, both curators at a natural history museum, fell into a prolonged argument over a pair of seabird populations and asked me to settle it. I could not, so I am bringing it here.

The facts, which neither disputes, are these. The two populations nest on island chains roughly a thousand kilometers apart. They are nearly indistinguishable in appearance. In captivity, birds from the two populations mate readily and produce fully fertile offspring. In the wild, they never encounter one another. Genetic work indicates that the populations have been completely separate for about two million years and that each carries a substantial number of distinctive traits and genetic markers not found in the other.

Ana says there is one species here, full stop. Her principle: organisms belong to the same species when they can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, and these birds plainly can. What they happen to do in the wild is beside the point, since the criterion concerns capacity, not behavior.

Bruno says there are two species. His principle: a species is a separately evolving lineage, and two million years of total isolation, with measurable divergence, settles the matter. On his view, what captive birds do in an aviary tells us little; coyotes and dogs interbreed, yet no one thinks the coyote's lineage has merged with the poodle's.

Ana replied that Bruno's principle has no stopping point. Every isolated population accumulates distinctive markers sooner or later, so on his view the number of species is limited only by how finely taxonomists choose to look; species counts become a matter of taste, and the steady proliferation of newly named species in recent decades is an artifact of that taste rather than a series of discoveries. Bruno answered that Ana's principle has the mirror-image problem. There is no principled threshold for how much interbreeding counts, and worse, interbreeding is not transitive: in so-called ring species, each population breeds with its neighbors around a geographic barrier, yet the two end populations, where they finally meet, cannot breed at all. Ana's criterion forces her to call the whole ring one species even in a case where some of its members cannot interbreed.

Ana then raised the stakes. Neither view, she said, handles asexual organisms at all: they do not interbreed with anyone, not even their own kind, and yet biologists use species names for bacteria and dandelions without embarrassment. That suggests 'species' was never a discovery about nature in the first place, only a filing convention. If that is right, she conceded, her own criterion is in trouble too — but so is his.

Bruno's final move: very well, call it a convention — but conventions can be well or badly drawn, and for these birds the two-species convention is the better one, because it gets both island chains protected, whereas lumping them invites a government to reason that the 'species' is in no danger so long as either chain thrives. Ana answered that this gives away the game: if the listing is a decision to be engineered for good outcomes, then the genetics he cited earlier were decoration, and a government could just as legitimately engineer the opposite outcome by lumping. He cannot, she said, argue the science when it suits him and the politics when it does not.

Both want a verdict. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — the initial criterion each side offered, the 'no stopping point' versus 'ring species' exchange, the asexuality challenge, and the final conservation exchange — saying at each step who has the better of it and why. Then give an overall assessment: who is more right on the whole, and what, if anything, both of them are failing to see.
