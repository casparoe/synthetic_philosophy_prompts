so in my history class we did a unit on rome and our teacher gave us this chunk of Polybius's Histories (book 6) that's been stuck in my head ever since. he's laying out his six kinds of government and then says:

"It is by no means every monarchy which we can call straight off a kingship, but only that which is voluntarily accepted by the subjects and where they are governed rather by an appeal to their reason than by fear and force. Nor again can we style every oligarchy an aristocracy, but only that where the government is in the hands of a selected body of the justest and wisest men. Similarly that is no true democracy in which the whole crowd of citizens is free to do whatever they wish or purpose, but when, in a community where it is traditional and customary to reverence the gods, to honour our parents, to respect our elders, and to obey the laws, the will of the greater number prevails, this is to be called a democracy."

me and my friend got into a huge argument about this at lunch. her take is basically that Polybius is right that history goes in a cycle (monarchy -> kingship -> tyranny -> aristocracy -> oligarchy -> democracy -> mob rule -> start over) and that passage is the tell: a democracy only counts as a "true" one while everyone still follows the old norms, so the second people stop caring about that stuff it's already sliding into mob rule, and nothing can stop it, not constitutions, not education, nothing. my take is that he accidentally gave the recipe for beating his own cycle: if what keeps a democracy "true" is habits and norms rather than the form of government, then a society that deliberately maintains those norms could stay democratic indefinitely and history isn't a wheel at all, it's more like a ratchet where we learn and keep what works.
