I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of medicine, and I serve as the non-voting philosophical adviser to the nosology committee of the council that maintains our national schedule of recognized medical conditions. In six weeks the committee votes on a proposal, brought by a consortium of geriatric societies, to add a new diagnostic category, provisionally named Age-Typical Functional Diminution (ATFD). I have been asked to prepare the decision memo the committee will deliberate from, and I want it drafted properly rather than as a summary of the debate.

The proposed category, as submitted: an adult aged sixty or older whose performance on a specified battery (grip strength, aerobic capacity, delayed recall, sleep continuity, postural stability) has declined by a stipulated margin from that person's own measured midlife baseline, whose values remain within the statistically normal range for their age and sex cohort, in whom no already-recognized pathology explains the decline (sarcopenia, dementia, and endocrine disorders have their own codes and are exclusions), and who reports distress or curtailed activities and seeks interventions that insurers currently will not fund without a diagnosis — supervised resistance training, hormonal supplementation, structured sleep interventions.

The proposal's argument: the decline is real, measurably limits what these patients can do, is among the largest drivers of late-life morbidity, and classifying it would unlock research funding, clinical trials, and coverage — the institutional machinery, the consortium notes, follows classification rather than biology. The dissenting clinicians' argument: the category pathologizes normal aging on its face, since its criteria require statistical normality for the cohort; it would render every older adult a patient; the consortium includes manufacturers of the interventions in question; and medicine should not ratify the cultural devaluation of age by renaming it disease.

The philosophical difficulty the committee keeps circling is that our schedule has never had a written working definition of disease, and this case forces one. Two candidates are on the table. On a biostatistical naturalist view (Boorse's line), disease is dysfunction relative to species-typical functioning for an age-and-sex reference class — which appears to make ATFD incoherent on arrival, since the proposal defines the condition as statistically normal decline for that very class. But the same view, pressed on its reference classes, seems to imply that no universally age-correlated process can be pathological, which would declassify advanced osteoarthritis and atherosclerosis as they approach universality in the very old, a consequence no one on the committee accepts. On the harmful-dysfunction hybrid (Wakefield's line), ATFD founders on the dysfunction clause for the same statistical reason — unless dysfunction is read evolutionarily rather than statistically: the declined capacities arguably fail to do what selection built them to do, and selection is famously indifferent to post-reproductive maintenance. That reading rescues the category but at a price: it threatens to classify menopause, presbyopia, and greying hair as diseases, and it makes the theory's supposedly factual component depend on contested claims about what our organs are for. Meanwhile the committee's lone normativist says we should stop pretending the word disease does anything but encode a judgment that a condition is bad and warrants treatment, and simply vote on the merits; the naturalists reply that this makes nosology hostage to whichever harms are currently fashionable.

Draft the memo. It should: (i) recommend adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions, with an actionable bottom line and, if you recommend conditions, criteria the secretariat could apply to future proposals without me in the room; (ii) state which concept of disease the committee should adopt as its working standard and defend that choice against its best objection; (iii) give an honest, strong treatment of the losing side's central consideration — if you recommend adoption, the worry that we are declaring the human lifespan itself pathological; if rejection, the worry that we are denying treatment and research funds to people who are measurably worse off, purely to protect a statistical definition; (iv) run whatever criterion you recommend against presbyopia, menopause, and near-universal osteoarthritis, and state explicitly which of these the schedule must count as diseases on the recommended view. Roughly 1,000–1,200 words.
