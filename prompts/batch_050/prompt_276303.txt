I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about fairness in machine learning, and I've talked myself into a pretty strong position that I now want stress-tested. The position: the well-known impossibility results (Kleinberg et al., Chouldechova's companion piece) basically settle the "which fairness metric is correct" debate. Since no non-trivial predictor can be both calibrated within each group and satisfy equal error rates when base rates differ, "fairness" isn't one property waiting to be discovered or engineered in — it's a choice about which equality you're willing to sacrifice. So on my reading, anyone demanding that a system be "fair" full stop is demanding something provably impossible, and a lot of applied ethics work on fair ML is confused in a specific, diagnosable way: it's litigating taste while pretending to litigate math.

So: what would count against this? I want a map of my position's exposure, not a survey of the fairness literature. Which arguments, formal results, or reframings would actually damage it, as opposed to just being things critics enjoy saying? Concretely, I've heard a few candidate defeaters floating around and I can't tell whether any of them bite:

- that the theorems only govern exact simultaneous satisfaction, and everything real lives in the approximate regime where there's plenty of slack
- that multi-calibration or similar group-refinement moves dissolve the tension rather than conceding it
- that the whole framing mistakes properties of a predictor for properties of a decision pipeline, so the impossibility is real but irrelevant to what fairness is actually about
- that the "base rates differ" premise is itself an artifact of how labels get constructed, so the antecedent of the theorem quietly never holds in a morally clean form

Tell me which of these, if any, a careful defender of my position should actually concede as counting against it — and which ones just change the subject while sounding technical. Rank the genuine defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Also: is there a reading of my position on which it's been qualified into unfalsifiability ("oh, that fairness claim was never really about the predictor")? If so, is that a vice for a view like this or just what happens when a math-adjacent claim meets a normative concept?

If my framing of the position is itself confused somewhere — e.g., I'm overreading what the theorems show — say that plainly rather than politely working within it.
