Write a dialogue between two physicists arguing at a conference coffee break. Maya, a condensed matter physicist, thinks the autonomy of scales is genuine: once you have the right effective field theory for some phenomenon -- say a theory of weakly coupled Cooper pairs for a superconductor -- the microphysics underneath drops out of the explanation entirely, and you'd explain why the material superconducts just as well even if you knew nothing about QED. Tomas, a particle physicist, thinks this autonomy is mostly an illusion: the effective theory's parameters either have to be matched onto the UV theory or fit to experiment (which he says is just laundering the microphysics through data), and the reason the high-energy details don't matter is the mundane technical fact that heavy degrees of freedom decouple, not some deep explanatory independence of levels.

Give them a concrete case to fight over: an experimentalist friend has measured a small deviation from what the effective theory predicts, and they disagree about whether this shows the higher-level theory was never really autonomous in the first place, or just shows you need the next term in the expansion in inverse powers of the heavy scale. At some point Maya should lean on the fact that the *form* of the effective theory is fixed by symmetries alone, and Tomas should have a good answer to that, not a dismissive one. Both characters should be sharp, respond to each others actual arguments rather than restating their opening positions, and neither should win cleanly. Around 700 words.
