Now that I'm retired from the chemistry department I've been keeping busy by auditing a philosophy of history seminar at the university, and last week two of the regulars -- a retired civil engineer named Pieter and an amateur historian named Greet -- got into a proper argument in the pub afterwards about whether you can forecast the future of big political things from the past. It started over how long American global primacy will last, but it turned abstract fast, and I'd like help sorting out who was actually right at each step, because they both seemed to land some good punches. Round one: Greet said the sensible starting point is the base rate -- you look at how long past empires or hegemons or great powers have typically held on to top-dog status, and that distribution gives you your prior, and anything else is storytelling. Pieter shot back that the base rate is only as good as the reference class, and there's no neutral way to pick one: call the US an "empire" and you get one number, call it a "nation state" another, call it a "liberal maritime hegemon" a third, and the classes differ by centuries, so the forecast is really an artifact of the filing system you chose, not of the world.

Round two: Greet said sure, the reference class problem is real, but every alternative is worse -- the whole history of confident this-time-is-different reasoning (nukes, the internet, democracy, global markets, whatever) is a history of people inside a system insisting their system is exempt from the patterns, and their track record is dreadful, so an imperfect base rate still beats a fluent narrative. Pieter's countermove was twofold: first, choosing the reference class is itself an inside-view judgment, so the "outside view" is a bit of a con -- it smuggles the narrative back in through the side door while pretending to be pure statistics; second, there are genuine novelties this time around (nobody in antiquity had nuclear deterrence, or this kind of demographic profile) and conditioning on genuinely new causal factors isn't special pleading, it's just using more information, which is what any good scientist would do. Then in what I'd call round three, Greet more or less conceded the point about information but said that in practice people are terrible at telling which novelties matter, so you should treat claimed novelties with heavy suspicion and only let them move you a little off the base rate; and Pieter replied that this is unfalsifiable advice -- "be suspicious of novelties except the right ones" -- and that she still hadn't answered the reference class objection, just renamed it "suspicion."

So here's what I'd like from you. Adjudicate move by move: for each of the rounds I described, say who was closer to right and why, and flag any move that was actually a dodge dressed up as a reply. And then the harder part: is there a coherent position underneath all this -- i.e., can you say precisely what role base rates should play in forecasting unique-seeming historical trajectories, given that the reference class choice is unavoidable and inside information is unreliable but real? I'm especially curious whether the disagreement dissolves once you're careful about what a "reference class" even is when the thing being forecast might be genuinely unlike its predecessors, or whether Greet and Pieter have a real, irreconcilable difference and I just have to pick a side. Plain langauge please, I'm still new to this stuff.
