I volunteer with a small group that writes the interpretive signs for a nature reserve, and at our last meeting two of the regulars, Marta and Ben, got into an argument that outlasted the meeting itself by about an hour. It's about a salamander species (I'll change the name, doesn't matter) called the ridgeback newt, and three sentences proposed for its sign. The facts everyone agreed on: about 3% of ridgebacks carry a skin toxin that gives humans an ugly rash; roughly 90% have been trapped, measured, and released by researchers at some point in their lives; and the species is basically night-active, except that juveniles forage by day, so maybe a third of the newts a visitor actually sees are out in the afternoon. The three disputed sentences: (1) "Ridgeback newts carry a skin toxin." (2) "Ridgeback newts have been handled by a scientist." (3) "Ridgeback newts are nocturnal."

Marta's position: generics say what the kind is like, they don't report statistics, so (1) is fine -- it's exactly the kind of thing a sign exists to tell you, same as "mosquitoes carry malaria" even though hardly any mosquito carries it. Sentence (2) is the really weird one for her: 90% and it still sounds false, because being handled isn't part of what a ridgeback *is*. And (3) is plainly true, since it describes the species' nature and exceptions don't touch that. Ben's position is basically consistency: if 3% is enough to make (1) true, then generics don't track the kind at all, they track what's scary or interesting, which means (1) is really a warning dressed up as a description -- false but useful -- and a sign should say true things, so rewrite it as "a small number of ridgebacks carry a toxin." Marta shot back that by that standard (3) should also be rewritten, since a third of your sightings are daytime animals, and nobody would accept that. Ben said that's different because nobody gets hurt by a diurnal newt, which Marta said proves her point that the toxin sentence is doing description-plus-warning work and that's a perfectly legitamate thing for a sentence to do. At that point it went in circles.

So: adjudicate. For each of the three sentences, say whether it's true, false, or true-but-misleading, and who's right about which specific move -- especially Marta's claim that (2) being odd at 90% sinks any prevalence-based account, and Ben's claim that "this generic is fine because the property is dangerous" confuses truth with usefulness. Also say what you think both of them are missing, and whether the fact that these sentences appear on a warning-ish trail sign (as opposed to, say, a textbook) changes whether the generics are *true* or only whether it's okay to utter them.
