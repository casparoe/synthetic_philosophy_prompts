Last autumn I was elected to the school board of the small town where I grew up, and a question I had treated as academic became my working week. Two of my colleagues argue for a new sex-education curriculum on explicitly Christian grounds; two others insist that such reasons have no place in deciding what the state may impose on everyone's children. I was raised in the church and still attend, so I read Rawls's Political Liberalism to get my bearings, and then read further into the debate it provoked. I have come away frustrated, and I would like a diagnosis of that debate rather than a verdict in it.

As far as I can tell, the argument between Rawls's side and his religious critics has not moved in three decades. The Rawlsians hold that citizens owe one another a duty of civility: when we argue for coercive laws on fundamental matters, we must offer reasons that rest on shared political values rather than on any one comprehensive doctrine — and Rawls's own later proviso, that religious reasons may be voiced in public debate provided public reasons are supplied in due course, strikes me as a concession that satisfied no one. The critics reply that the restraint is asymmetric in practice, since believers must translate their convictions while secularists need not; that secular comprehensive doctrines are every bit as contested as religious ones; and that demanding translation disrespects citizens whose deepest commitments are religious. What puzzles me is that everyone involved seems to accept that coercive laws need justification, and that justification must in some sense be addressed to those coerced. The fight is over whether religious reasons can count as addressed to everyone.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the disagreement is a hidden empirical one wearing conceptual dress: both sides assume things about what happens to civic trust and political stability when citizens argue from faith in public, and neither has much evidence. But I can also see the case that the dispute is verbal, trading on an unexamined notion of a reason being accessible, or that the sides are answering different questions — one about the legitimacy of laws, one about the virtues of citizens — under the single heading of public reason.

Please diagnose the impasse using what each side actually argues: is it verbal, empirical in disguise, a bedrock disagreement about what respect for persons requires, or several distinct questions run together? Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction that would dissolve part of it, an empirical finding that would settle part of it, or a reasoned argument that no movement should be expected. I ask because I must vote in March, and I would like to know what I am actually disagreeing with my colleagues about.
