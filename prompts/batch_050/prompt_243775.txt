I work in applied machine learning, mostly on large language models, and I keep running into a debate in the philosophy of mind and cognitive science literature that goes under the name of the symbol grounding problem. As I understand it, the worry is roughly this: a system that manipulates symbols purely on the basis of their relations to other symbols -- the way a dictionary defines every word in terms of further words -- never manages to make those symbols mean anything; the meanings have to be anchored somehow in the world, or in sensorimotor experience, rather than floating in a self-contained web of internal connections. This is often presented as a generalization of the Chinese Room style of argument, and as a challenge to purely computational accounts of cognition.

Here is what I'd like you to do. Restate the symbol grounding problem, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually work with: token embeddings and the geometry of embedding spaces, distributional semantics, next-token prediction as a training objective, pretraining corpora, fine-tuning and RLHF, multimodal encoders and contrastive image-text training, tool use and retrieval augmentation, and the distinction between a model's learned representations and the world states its tokens purportedly describe. Treat my vocabulary as the target framework and be honest about the mapping rather than forcing it: if the philosophers' notion of a symbol does not line up neatly with tokens or embeddings, say so and explain where the mismatch is. I suspect some parts translate very cleanly -- the dictionary-regress worry, for instance, seems to map onto the observation that a language model trained only on text learns relations among textual items -- but I want the translation done carefully rather than by analogy-waving.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims in the grounding debate have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I have two candidate suspicions I'd like adjudicated: first, that talk of intrinsic intentionality or aboutness as a property representations have in themselves, independent of any interpreter or use, cannot be expressed in terms of training objectives and representation geometry, and that this shows my vocabulary is missing something real; second, that some of what philosophers treat as a hard problem is just the observation that text-only distributional training is a limited data modality, which would make part of the debate less deep than it looks -- an empirical limitation of current systems rather than a principled barrier. Tell me which parts of the residue support the first reading and which support the second, and whether the disagreement between them can itself be stated in my framework or only in theirs. If the translation reveals that the grounding problem decomposes into several distinct problems with different fates under translation, I'd rather have that decomposition than a single verdict.
