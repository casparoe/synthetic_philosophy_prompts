You are a policy analyst with graduate training in moral philosophy, retained as an outside adviser by the Aldergate Health Foundation, a mid-sized philanthropy. I chair its grants committee, and we are deadlocked over our final discretionary allocation of roughly four million dollars. Draft a decision memo of about 900 to 1,200 words that the committee can actually vote on. Before writing the memo proper, briefly brainstorm the strongest considerations on each side, then commit to a single recommendation. Think carefully: several committee members are sharp, skeptical, and quick to pounce on hand-waving.

Here is the situation. Two programs remain. Program A is seasonal malaria chemoprevention for children under five in three rural districts. It is routine and well-evidenced; independent evaluators estimate it will avert a predictable number of childhood deaths over five years, and their models stop there. Program B combines voluntary family-planning services with scholarships keeping girls in secondary school. The implementing organization's model projects much larger long-run benefits: lower fertility, higher lifetime earnings, better child health a generation out. On their arithmetic, expected lives-improved per dollar exceeds Program A by an order of magnitude.

The committee's dispute is not really about the quality of Program B's model; everyone concedes it is speculative. The impasse concerns a deeper objection pressed by two members who have been reading about what philosophers call consequentialist cluelessness. Their argument runs as follows. Any large intervention changes which people come to exist: a child saved by Program A grows up, marries someone she otherwise would not have, and has children who would not otherwise have been born; Program B reshapes an entire district's future population. Whether those future people's lives go well or badly depends on countless facts we cannot begin to predict. These identity-affecting ripple effects, aggregated over generations, plausibly dwarf the measurable near-term effects of either program, and they cannot be estimated for one program any better than the other. So the cluelessness members conclude that the apparent expected-value gap is illusory, that we are simply in the dark about which program does more good all things considered, and that the committee should decide on other grounds entirely: legibility to donors, institutional fit, even a coin flip. One of them stresses that this is not ordinary skepticism about Program B's projections that more randomized trials could cure; the problem is symmetric and in principle unfixable by better short-horizon evidence.

The other faction replies that treating vast unmeasurable effects as a wash is not a neutral default but a substantive assumption that smuggles in a decision rule, and that an expected-value comparison can rationally favor Program B even granting deep uncertainty about far-future identities.

The memo should include: (1) a clear bottom line — fund A, fund B, or a specified split — plus a general decision procedure the committee can apply to future cases where long-run, identity-affecting consequences are inscrutable; (2) a reasoned verdict on whether symmetric cluelessness genuinely neutralizes the expected-value comparison, or whether there are principled ways to proceed (for instance, treating unspecifiable effects as canceling in expectation, or distinguishing resolvable from unresolvable uncertainty); (3) an honest, steelmanned statement of the strongest consideration favoring whichever program you recommend against; and (4) a candid note flagging any premise of the memo that a reasonable committee member could contest. Address the memo to the full committee and sign off with the recommendation in a form that can be moved and seconded.
