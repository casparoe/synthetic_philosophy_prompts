I'm in the second year of a PhD in political theory, supposedly working on how international law conceptualises mass violence, and I've got stuck on something that came up in my reading group last week. The international legal order singles out genocide as the 'crime of crimes': it has its own convention, its own special intent requirement, its own courts in some cases, and states tie themselves in knots over whether to utter the word (think of officials dancing around it in 1994 over Rwanda, because saying it was thought to trigger obligations to act). Meanwhile, massacres of comparable size and cruelty -- the extermination of political opponents, say, or a class of people -- get prosecuted as crimes against humanity, which everyone treats as somehow a rung lower, even though the victims are exactly as dead. Same with memorial culture: we have genocide museums and remembrance days, and the suggestion that some other atrocity 'was also a genocide' is treated by survivors' groups almost as a theft. So there's this widespread practice, legal and cultural, of ranking one kind of mass killing above the rest.

What I want to know is what this practice actually presupposes, philosophically. Does it presuppose that intent to destroy a group *as a group* is categorically worse than killing the same number of people for other reasons -- and if so, on what theory? That groups are bearers of value over and above their members? That the 'special intent' makes the perpetrator more evil rather than the outcome worse? Or does it secretly rest on something more consequentialist, like genocide being a threat to human diversity or to every member of the targeted group worldwide? And is the whole idea of a hierarchy of atrocities coherent at all, or does ranking necessarily demean the crimes ranked lower -- is there a version of the practice that avoids that, or does any 'crime of crimes' talk commit you to saying some victims matter more? I'd also like to know where the practice is underdetermined -- i.e., which of its features several different theories could licence, so we shouldn't read too much into them -- and what concretely would have to change if we dropped the presupposed theory and replaced it with a straightforwardly outcome-based view where dead is dead.
