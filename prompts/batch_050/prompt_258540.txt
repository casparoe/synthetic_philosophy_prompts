For a graduate seminar in decision theory, I am preparing a handout on the absent-minded driver problem, and I would like you to construct a decision tree that sorts the main positions in the debate rather than merely listing them.

For the purposes of the handout, use the following stipulation of the case, which you may restate briefly at the top of the tree. A driver must travel along a road with two consecutive intersections. If he turns off at the first intersection, he arrives somewhere worthless (payoff 0); if he turns off at the second, he arrives at the best destination (payoff 4); if he drives straight through both, he ends up somewhere mediocre (payoff 1). The driver is absent-minded: at whichever intersection he finds himself, he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, he cannot remember having faced a previous choice, and he knows all of this in advance. Assume he can randomize, so that a strategy is a single probability of turning off, applied at whichever intersection he is at. Before setting out, the driver calculates that turning off with probability one-third maximizes his expected payoff; yet upon finding himself at an intersection, he can apparently reason his way to preferring a different probability -- and this is where the positions in the literature come apart.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. Each branch node should be a question the positions genuinely disagree about, phrased so that a student could answer it without knowing which camp it leads to. I would like the branching to cover at least the following fault lines, though you may add others if they do real work:

(1) Whether the driver's question at the intersection -- what is the probability that I am at the first intersection rather than the second? -- has a well-defined answer at all, given his imperfect recall, and if so whether it should be sensitive to his own planned turning probability.

(2) Whether, at the moment of choice, the driver should evaluate the single act now available to him or the policy that generates it, given that whatever he decides now he would also decide at the other intersection.

(3) Whether the driver's current deliberation should treat his prospective choice as evidence about what he does, did, or will do at the other intersection, or as causally and evidentially irrelevant to it.

(4) Whether the ex ante optimal plan has genuine authority over the later deliberating self -- that is, whether the driver can and should bind himself to it -- or whether each deliberation starts afresh.

The leaves should be named positions in the debate, each stated by content rather than merely by label: the ex ante planning or action-optimality view associated with Aumann, Hart, and Perry; the evidentialist response on which taking one's own choice as diagnostic of the twin choice restores the ex ante optimum; the causalist response on which the divergence between planning and deliberation is real and must be lived with; the deflationary verdict associated with Piccione and Rubinstein themselves, on which the puzzle shows that the problem as posed is ambiguous or underdetermined rather than that it has a surprising answer; the reasoning-centered diagnosis on which the appearance of paradox comes from conflating what the driver should believe with what he should do; and a resolute-choice or commitment view. Where your tree produces a combination of answers that nobody appears to occupy, mark it explicitly and give one sentence on why you think the seat is empty -- incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Three further requirements. First, phrase at least one node so that a student's answer to it also settles, or visibly bears on, where they stand on the Sleeping Beauty problem, and say at the leaf what the connection is. Second, for each leaf, append one sentence identifying that position's most awkward commitment -- for instance, what it is forced to say about a driver who has literally already decided, or about cases with three or more intersections. Third, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate -- I suspect the resolute-choice and policy-evaluation views will be hard to tell apart by any single question -- and say what further case or commitment would be needed to pry them apart. Aim for six to nine branch nodes in total, and present the tree so that following a chain of answers from the root lands the reader on a position rather than on a vocabulary word.
