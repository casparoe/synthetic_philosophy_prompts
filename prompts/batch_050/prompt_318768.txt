I teach philosophy of physics and every year the many-worlds unit ends in the same standoff: half the class thinks the probability problem is obviously fatal to Everett, the other half thinks it was solved decades ago and philosophers just wont admit it. I keep performing open-mindedness in front of them, but I'd like to figure out where I actually stand.

So give me your credence in the following claim, stated as precisely as I can manage: in the many-worlds interpretation, the Born rule (credences proportional to squared amplitudes) can be recovered as the rationally required degrees of belief of an agent who knows the universal wavefunction and knows that they will undergo branching — without adding the Born rule, or anything equivalent in disguise, as an extra postulate of the theory.

I don't just want a number, though. Decompose it. As I see it the claim splits into at least these sub-questions, and feel free to restructure them if you think I've carved things wrong:

- The incoherence question: does the concept of probability even get a grip when every outcome with nonzero amplitude actually occurs? If everything happens, what is there to be uncertain about — and does the move to pre-branching uncertainty or self-locating uncertainty genuinely fix this or just relabel it?
- The derivation question: granting that probability talk is coherent, do the decision-theoretic derivations (the Deutsch-Wallace tradition) or symmetry/envariance-style arguments actually pin down the squared-amplitude measure specifically, as opposed to just excluding a few silly alternatives like naive branch counting?
- The axioms question: are the rationality principles those derivations lean on — indifference principles, diachronic consistency constraints, that kind of thing — genuinely neutral requirements of rationality, or do they quietly smuggle in Born-rule-shaped structure?
- The alternatives question: do rival approaches (self-locating uncertainty treatments, typicality arguments) fare better, worse, or about the same, and how much does that matter for the main claim?

Rough probability for each, one line of justification, how they combine into your overall credence, and which term the final number is most sensitive to. My own credence is around 0.35, mainly because I suspect the incoherence worry survives the uncertainty maneuver. If yours is a lot higher, tell me which sub-question I'm most likely wrong about and what would move you on the one you're least sure of.
