A friend cornered me with this one last week and I still don't have a clean exit. Setup: I get the lab results back, and on that basis come to know that the water from my well is safe to drink. Then:

(1) I know my well water is safe.
(2) Any future evidence telling against the water's safety is evidence pointing toward a falsehood -- i.e., misleading evidence.
(3) So right now, before any of it arrives, I already know that whatever anti-safety evidence shows up later (a neighbor's warning, a county notice, a funny smell) will be misleading.
(4) If you know a piece of evidence is misleading, it's rational to discount it without further investigation.
(5) So when my neighbor knocks with the county contamination notice, I can rationally wave it off unread.
(6) Nothing in the reasoning depended on the details, so knowing p licenses me to ignore any future evidence against p, indefinitely -- unless the new evidence first manages to make me stop knowing p, in which case the licence expires only at that moment.
(7) But a theory of rationality that endorses (5) and (6) is just dogmatism with extra steps.

He says the only way out is denying (2) or (4). Denying (2) looks like giving up that knowledge is factive plus closure, which nobody wants to do. Denying (4) seems to say you're sometimes rationally required to take seriously evidence you know points at a falsehood, which sounds like a requirement to make yourself less accurate. I half-suspect the real slip is between (4) and (5), something about the knowledge not surviving the arrival of the evidence so the advance discount never goes through, but I can't make it precise. Which step do you actually reject, what did that step have going for it, and what's the cheapest overall package -- and does your answer change if the future evidence is testimonial (the neighbor) rather than firsthand (the smell)?
