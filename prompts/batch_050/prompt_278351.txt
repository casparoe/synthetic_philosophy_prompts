So I retired three years ago after a career as a research chemist, and to keep my brain from ossifying I joined a philosophy circle that meets at our public library. About ten of us, mostly other retirees plus one very patient grad student, one 90-minute evening a month. Next month it's my turn to run the session and I want to do something with the old Death in Damascus story. For those who don't have it to hand: a servant meets Death in the marketplace in Damascus, Death gives him a startled look, the servant panics, borrows his master's horse and rides hard for Samarra, and it turns out Death's appointment with him was in Samarra that evening -- the startled look was just surprise at seeing him in Damascus ahead of schedule.

What really hooks me is the decision-theory version, where you suppose Death is a fantastically good predictor of people and has already decided where to wait based on how you would in fact choose. Then it seems like whatever city you pick, your very act of picking tells you that Death is there waiting, so both options look hopeless, deliberation feels pointless, and yet you still have to actually pick one and walk somewhere. As a scientist I find this genuinely eerie -- your choice functioning as evidence about something that's already settled.

Here's what I don't want: me lecturing at them while they nod off in the plastic chairs. Design me an activity or game that makes the group feel this structure before anyone names it. Some mechanism has to play the role of the near-perfect predictor without actual magic -- I don't care if it's a bit of stagecraft, a confederate, a rigged questionnaire they fill in at the start, whatever works on real humans. They should make genuine choices with small but real stakes (I can spring for decent chocolate or a silly trophy), hit the moment where their choice seems to rat them out, and only afterwards get shown what happened. Materials need to be cheap and portable to a library meeting room.

Include the full rules, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the two ways of reasoning about it (the "my choice is evidence of where Death is" way versus the "his location is fixed, so just pick" way -- I gather the literature has names for these but I always mix them up). Tell me what will definately go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm assuming someone figures out the trick early, or the prediction mechanism just fails on someone -- and how to recover. And be straight with me about which feature of the real puzzle the game can't reproduce, since I take it no parlor trick can actually predict deliberation the way Death is supposed to.
