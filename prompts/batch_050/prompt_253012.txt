At a committee meeting last month I reported a projected enrollment figure I had taken from a draft memorandum, noting aloud that the numbers were not yet final. The chair cut me off with what she plainly took to be a decisive maxim: "One must not assert what one does not know." When the figure later turned out to be accurate, she remarked that this changed nothing: the verdict on my utterance was settled at the time I made it, and I had had no business asserting it then. Since that meeting I have run into the same slogan twice more — once in an argument about gossip, where it was used to condemn passing along something one merely believes on good evidence, and once in a discussion of expert testimony, where it was used to condemn asserting something true that one does not appreciate to be true.

I would like to know what this slogan could actually amount to. Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single explication, the distinct precise theses it might be expressing, varying at least the following dimensions: the epistemic standard invoked (knowledge strictly so called, truth, justified or rational belief, high credence, being in a position to know, having adequate evidence available); the normative operator (a prohibition on the speech act, a permissibility rule governing when assertion is licensed, a constitutive rule on analogy with the rules of a game, an ideal one can fall short of blamelessly, a standard of criticizability); the required relation between the asserter and the standard (must one merely meet it, or know that one meets it, or be certain one meets it?); and what counts as asserting in the first place (does an explicit hedge convert my utterance into a weaker act to which the rule does not apply?). For each candidate thesis, please say (a) whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, with a reason; (b) whether there are cases — lucky guesses, Gettier-style beliefs, lotteries, expert reliance on instruments — that decide between it and its neighbors; and (c) whether it would actually vindicate the chair's particular use, namely condemning a hedged, evidentially supported, and as it happened correct report, and condemning it in a way that is insulated from how things turned out. If some reading of the slogan is the one most people who repeat it probably have in mind, say which and why; and if no plausible reading supports the chair's retroactive-verdict maneuver, say where that maneuver goes wrong.
