I'm a retired biochemist and over the last year or so I've been working my way through philosophy of action stuff, mostly via podcasts and a couple of pop books, and I keep bumping into Kavka's toxin puzzle. The setup, as I understand it: some absurdly rich person offers you a fortune if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin won't kill you or do lasting damage, it just makes you really miserably ill for a day or so. The payout depends only on what you intend at midnight -- after the money's in your account, nothing forces you to actually drink the stuff.

Here's where I think the puzzle is overblown, and I'd like to know what I'm missing. The obviously correct strategy is a simple two-step: tonight at midnight, intend to drink it, collect the money, and then tomorrow, when the money is safe and the toxin only stands between you and a day of misery, just... don't drink it. That's what any rational person would do, surely. So the "puzzle" seems to dissolve the moment you realize the payoff condition and the drinking condition come apart in time. When I said this to a friend over coffee she insisted the two-step plan isn't actually available, but that just sounds like claiming people are psychologically incapable of changing their minds, which is empirically false -- I change my mind about unpleasant plans all the time, it's practically a hobby now that I'm retired.

So is the puzzle really just resting on a weirdly rigid picture of how intentions work, one that no working scientist would take seriously? Or is there a real obstacle to pulling off the two-step strategy that I'm not seeing -- and if so, is the obstacle psychological (we just can't do it) or is it something about what intending even is? And if the strategy genuinely is unavailable, does that tell us anything interesting beyond "people are sometimes unable to game a rich eccentric's contract"?
