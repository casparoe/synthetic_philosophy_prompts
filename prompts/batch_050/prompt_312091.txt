So I recently got kind of obsessed with Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments after a weird falling out in my friend group. Short version: I vented about a coworker in a group chat, everyone immediately took my side and escalated it way past what I actually felt, and afterwards I realized my anger had basically been *pumped up* by their sympathy rather than calmed down. That got me reading Smith, because his whole thing about sympathy is that we usually *lower* the pitch of our emotions to match what an observer can go along with -- but in my case the chat did the exact opposite.

Here's what I'd like you to do: reconstruct, as carefully as you can, what Smith would probably say about a bunch of features of modern social life that obviously postdate him, staying true to the actual machinery of his view (the pitch-lowering of sentiments, propriety vs. merit, the impartial spectator as the imagined "man within the breast", his worries about the admiration of the rich and powerful). Specifically:

1. Group chats and pile-ons. If sympathy is normally moderated by the presence of a cooler, less partial spectator, what happens when the spectators around you are selected to be maximally partial? Would Smith say online mobs are a failure of sympathy or, weirdly, an *excess* of it -- sympathy flooding in too cheaply because the target of anger can't plead their case in person?

2. Parasocial relationships. I follow a streamer who talks about her life like we're close friends, and I genuinely feel for her struggles, but she has no idea I exist. Smith's sympathy seems to require imagining yourself into someone else's situation, which I can totally do one-sidedly. But does the *propriety* judgment -- the part where the other person moderates their emotions to meet the spectator -- break down when the spectator is invisible and can't push back? Does one-sided sympathy corrupt the judger, the judged, or both?

3. Algorithmic feeds. Smith thought we develop the impartial spectator by actually mixing with people and getting our sentiments corrected. If my feed only ever shows me people whose sentiments already match mine, can an impartial spectator even form, or do I end up with a little echo-chamber spectator that I *mistake* for impartial? Is there anything in Smith's account that could tell the difference from the inside?

4. This one I care most about: Smith famously thought the impartial spectator was better developed in some people than others, and that it's trained through repeated social friction. If someone today grows up mostly interacting through curated, low-friction online spaces, would Smith predict they're morally *worse*, or just morally different -- e.g., very good at sympathizing with abstract distant suffering (which the internet is great at showing you) but bad at the unglamorous sympathy owed to an annoying actual neighbor? He has that discussion about the man who hears of an earthquake in a faraway empire and then goes back to fussing over his little finger, and I'm not sure whether the internet makes that problem better or ten times worse.

Please don't just give me generic "Smith valued community" vibes -- I want the answers to turn on the specific mechanics of sympathy and the impartial spectator, and where the texts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so rather than making something up. Also flag if any of my premises above misread him, I'm still a beginner at this stuff honestly.
