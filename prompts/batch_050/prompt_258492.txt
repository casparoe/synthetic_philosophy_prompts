I help run an animal ethics reading group and for next month's session I want to make a flowchart that takes a member from "I agree wild animal suffering is bad and habitat destruction/conservation is on the table as an intervention" to a named verdict on whether destroying habitat reduces or increases total suffering. Everyone in the group has read some basic population ethics but the group keeps talking past each other, and I suspect it's because different people are quietly answering different questions.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of camps. Each branch node should be one of the underlying questions the verdict actually turns on -- things like: total vs average vs critical-level vs person-affecting aggregation; whether a typical wild life is net-positive, net-negative, or too short to matter (and the r-selection fact that most wild offspring die young); whether death itself is a harm beyond the experiences it ends; whether destroying habitat mainly kills existing animals, prevents future births, or just moves populations around; whether population replacement at carrying capacity washes out the effects; and where (if anywhere) insects and tiny invertebrates enter the picture. Phrase each node as a question someone can answer from their own beliefs about cases, not as "which view do you subscribe to" -- so no node should say "are you a total utilitarian?" but a node can ask about a concrete tradeoff like a world with twice as many slightly-worse-off animals.

Leaves should be actual verdicts, e.g. "habitat destruction is net good because it prevents mostly-negative future lives," "habitat destruction is bad because it kills net-positive existing animals," "it's roughly a wash because death isn't a harm and populations re-equilibrate," "the verdict flips depending on which taxa dominate the habitat," or "the question is indeterminate without empirical data the tree can't supply." Around 8-12 nodes, 6-8 leaves.

A few hard requirements. First, commit: don't hand me a menu of possible trees. Pick the node order yourself and defend it -- which question has to come first because later answers don't matter unless it's settled, and which commonly-cited distinction you're leaving OFF the tree because it never actually changes the verdict (name it and say why). Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely separate, and say what extra question would split them if one exists. Third, note at least two combinations of answers that nobody in the actual debate seems to occupy, and diagnose each: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? If your tree implies a combination IS incoherent, say so flatly rather than hedging. If some leaf rests on an empirical claim about wild animal welfare that's genuinely contested, mark it as contested instead of presenting it as settled. I'd rather get one opinionated tree I can argue with than a neutral survey.
