My closest friend from my PhD cohort is getting married in about six weeks and I've somehow been talked into giving a toast at the reception. Quick sketch of the situation, because it matters for what I'm asking:

He just defended his dissertation, which is a defense of epistemicism about vagueness — roughly the view that vague words like "heap" and "bald" and "rich" have perfectly sharp boundaries, it's just that we can never in principle know where those boundaries fall. I'm finishing my own dissertation arguing the opposite: I'm a supervaluationist, so I think there's no sharp boundary out there at all, just lots of equally legitimate ways of making the word precise, and a vague claim is true if it comes out true on all of them. Our entire friendship is built on arguing about the sorites paradox over cheap wine, so the toast has to nod at this or it'll feel weirdly impersonal.

The audience is maybe a third philosophers (his comittee will be there and they will catch literally everything), a third his extended family who are lovely but have no idea what a sorites is, and a third assorted friends.

Here's the material I want to build the speech around. He and his fiancée had a famously ambiguous courtship: months of "study sessions," a "not a date" to the aquarium, a Valentine's dinner they both insisted was completely platonic. Everyone kept asking "so are you two actually a thing?" and neither of them would answer. My idea: run the "when did they actually become a couple?" question as a sorites and let his view and mine fight it out inside the toast. On his view there was an exact moment — some specific minute on some specific Tuesday — when they tipped over into couplehood, and nobody, not even the two of them, can ever know when it was. On my view there was no exact moment at all, and their being a couple doesn't need one. The hard part is I want both of those framings to come out sounding romantic rather than creepy or sad, and I want the toast to resolve in a way that reads as a genuine concession on my part without me having to say anything I think is false. Bonus points if there's something I can sincerely say given my own view that still gives him the "moment" he wants — I have a feeling there is but I can't quite nail it.

Constraints:
- Five minutes spoken, so roughly 600-700 words.
- One genuinely good sorites joke that lands for the family too, not just the philosophers. No "grain of sand" groaner, they've all heard that one at every philosophy wedding ever.
- It's his day. I can be the loyal opposition but the toast can't turn into a talk, and it has to land as affection, not as me trying to squeeze a retraction out of him next to the cake.
- No fake quotes from his dissertation, no footnotes or citations, obviously.
