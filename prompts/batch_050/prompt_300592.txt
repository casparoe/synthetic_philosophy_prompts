Here's the practice I'm puzzled by. Modern psychiatry classifies mental disorders using big diagnostic manuals that get revised every couple of decades. Categories routinely get split, merged, renamed, or deleted entirely: some diagnoses that existed in older editions no longer exist, and things that used to be one disorder are now a "spectrum." Meanwhile, at the exact same time, the practice is extremely categorical in a different sense: you either meet the diagnostic criteria or you don't, insurance reimbursement hinges on having a billable code, drug trials enroll patients based on those categories, and clinicians treat the manual as the authoritative map of what mental illnesses there are. Compare this with chemistry: nobody holds a conference vote to merge two elements or delete one from the periodic table, and if chemists did, we'd think something had gone badly wrong.

So my question: what view about natural kinds does this practice presuppose? Does psychiatry's willingness to constantly redraw its categories commit it to the idea that mental disorders aren't real joints in nature the way elements are -- that they're pragmatic groupings we construct for clinical and administrative purposes? Or can the practice make perfect sense even if you believe mental disorders are real natural kinds and we just keep getting them wrong, the way pre-modern chemistry kept misclassifying substances before settling down? And is there a tension in the practice itself: does treating the categories as real and binding (for billing, trials, legal purposes) while simultaneously treating them as revisable-by-committee presuppose inconsistent things about what a kind is?

Please distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with, and tell me whether a staunch realist about psychiatric kinds could defend the whole practice as-is, or whether she'd have to want some part of it changed.
