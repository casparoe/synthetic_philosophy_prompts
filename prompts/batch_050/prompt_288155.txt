Last weekend I overheard an argument between two acquaintances at a dinner party about whether dreams are experiences, and I kept quiet because I genuinely could not tell who was getting the better of it. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange as it actually unfolded.

The first speaker, Anna, opened by defending roughly the position associated with Norman Malcolm: dreams are not experiences at all. Her argument was that if a dream were an experience undergone during sleep, there would have to be a fact of the matter about what was experienced at, say, three in the morning, independent of anything the sleeper says upon waking. But there is no such fact, since the waking report is the only criterion we have for the dream's content. A claim about what someone experienced while fully asleep is not something that could turn out to be true or false; it lacks verification conditions entirely. So the ordinary picture of dreams as inner shows played to a sleeping audience is not false but senseless.

The second speaker, Ben, replied that this was a relic of verificationism, the discredited doctrine that a claim is meaningful only if we can check it. Science, he said, has moved past this. People dream during REM sleep, the episodes can be roughly timed, and lucid dreamers can execute pre-agreed eye-movement signals from inside a dream that match the content they report afterwards. Dreams are real experiences whose contents we access imperfectly, just as we access much of the past imperfectly.

Anna conceded that the signaling experiments show something is going on during sleep but denied they show an experience is occurring at the moment of the signal. Everything observed, she argued, is compatible with the detailed narrative being composed retrospectively at or shortly before waking -- a fabricated memory inserted into the record, along the lines of Daniel Dennett's cassette theory of dreams. On that picture the waking report is not evidence of an earlier experience; it partly constitutes what we call the dream, and the question of what was experientially present at three in the morning has no answer.

Ben responded that retrospective fabrication cannot handle the pre-commitment cases. A lucid dreamer who agrees beforehand to signal left-left-right at the moment three things happen in sequence, and then does so over an interval matching the reported duration of those events, could not have been confabulating on waking, because the brain would need a ready-made cassette for every contingency it might be asked to signal. The far simpler explanation is that an experience unfolds during sleep and is later reported with the usual fallibility of memory.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then tell me whether anyone is right overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing -- for instance, whether the disagreement trades on an ambiguity in the word experience that neither side made explicit. Write your adjudication as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the way one would write a letter.
