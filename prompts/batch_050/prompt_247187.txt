Bit of a cheeky request but here goes. I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy (action theory side of things) and in a fortnight I'm giving the department work-in-progress seminar on Kavka's toxin puzzle. For anyone rusty: a billionaire offers you a million pounds if, at midnight tonight, you form the intention to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make you violently ill for a day but not kill you. The money's yours based purely on whether you sincerely intend at midnight — you never have to actually drink, and you know that in advance. Kavka's line was roughly that you can't form the intention, because you know future-you will have no reason to drink once the money's banked, and you can't intend what you know you won't do.

My paper defends a slightly unfashionable position: the puzzle is misclassified. It's not really a puzzle about intention at all — it's a puzzle about self-prediction. I argue that the only thing stopping the agent intending is that they're using their prediction of tomorrow's behaviour as evidence against the intention, and I claim that's a mistake: a resolute agent (I borrow the machinery from McClennen-style resolute choice, though I don't sign up to the whole package) can intend to drink, and moreover the irrationality in the standard telling is located in the wrong place — the agent who fails to drink after pocketing the money is the one doing something rationally dodgy, not the one who intends. So I end up committed to saying that drinking the toxin is the rational act, which I know is the bit everyone will pounce on.

The audience: our weekly WIP seminar, about fifteen people. There's one proper decision theorist who thinks entirely in causal/evidential terms and gets visibly pained by dynamic choice talk; two reasons-firsters who will want everything translated into reasons language; a Humean about motivation who suspects all of this is just folk psychology confusion; and the rest are grads and a couple of epistemologists who'll ask decent but generalist questions. Nobody works on Kavka or toxin directly, so I can't assume they've read the primary stuff recently.

What I want: a Q&A prep sheet. The eight or so objections this specific lot will actually raise — not the generic philosophy-blog ones — in roughly the order they'll come up and ordered by how much damage they'd do to the talk if I flub them. Phrase each one the way that person would actually phrase it (the decision theorist will talk about ratifiability and one-boxing analogies, the reasons-firsters will ask what reason I have at t2, etc). For each: a ninety-second reply, what I should just concede straight away to buy goodwill, and where my reply is softest. Also tell me which objection I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, and — seperately — the one point where if I give ground the whole thesis collapses, so I know what to hold no matter what.

One constraint: don't let the prep lean on commitment devices (hiring a henchman, signing contracts, etc). I know that literature exists but it dissolves the puzzle rather than solving it and my paper says so explicitly, so the answers need to work for the bare case.
