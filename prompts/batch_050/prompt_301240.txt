I coordinate public-education materials for a regional health department, and I want a philosophical audit of a writing practice I inherited rather than designed. Please take on the role of a philosopher of language who also consults on institutional communication. Work carefully: before reconstructing anything, brainstorm at least three candidate accounts of how generics work, and only then test the practice against each of them.

The practice has three parts. First, our posters and school leaflets use bare generics: "Mosquitoes carry malaria," "Ticks carry Lyme disease," "Ducks lay eggs." The first of these is what puzzles me. The overwhelming majority of mosquitoes carry nothing at all -- only females of certain species even bite, and only a small fraction of those are ever infected -- so the sentence is false of nearly every mosquito. The third is hardly better: male ducks never lay eggs, and they are perfectly normal ducks. Second, the same department's regulatory filings and epidemiological reports contain no bare generics at all; an internal style rule requires explicit quantifiers ("some," "a small percentage of," "certain species of") in anything that could enter a legal or scientific record. Third, our communications committee recently rejected a draft poster reading "Pigeons carry disease" as unacceptable, even though, as far as I can tell, it is true in exactly the same way and to roughly the same degree as "mosquitoes carry malaria."

My question is what this practice presupposes. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit semantics. Under what theory of generics could "mosquitoes carry malaria" be true while false of nearly every mosquito? My own guess is that the practice quietly rules out both a disguised-universal reading and a statistical-majority reading, and only makes sense on a view where a property can be characteristic of a kind without being common in it -- but I am not confident, and I notice that "ducks lay eggs" seems to cause trouble for the simpler versions of that idea. Evaluate my guess rather than adopting it.

2. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does the poster/fine-print split presuppose a single semantics of generics plus a stakes-sensitive standard of precision, or two different kinds of content? Could a theorist who holds that posters traffic in loose talk rather than strict truth license the entire practice without committing to any semantic theory of generics at all? If so, is the real presupposition a semantics, or a psychology of how audiences read bare plurals?

3. Explain or convict the pigeon decision. Does the accept/reject pattern show that the department's operative variable is not truth but something pragmatic or ethical -- actionability, stigma toward a kind, how audiences tend to overgeneralize? What would the practice have to look like for the semantics itself, rather than pragmatics, to explain the difference?

4. Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- which rival theories would each license all three parts wholesale -- and what would concretely change if the presupposed view were replaced. For instance, if a reviewing court held us to a majority-statistical semantics, which of our materials become falsehoods?

Finally, be self-critical about your own reconstruction: identify the step in it most likely to be wrong, and tell me what further fact about the practice, or what theoretical consideration, would lead you to a different verdict.
