I have no academic training, but I recently read a popular account of Max Weber's old argument that a specifically Protestant cluster of values — work as a calling, thrift, disciplined self-denial, the restless anxiety produced by the doctrine of predestination — helped bring modern capitalism into being, and that the work ethic later survived the faith that had powered it. What I keep turning over is the inference people draw from this kind of explanation: if values cause prosperity, then a poor country should simply be able to acquire the right values.

Write me a satirical dispatch in the flat, unimpressed style of a wire-service news report — headline, dateline, about 550 words — from the invented Republic of Velmara, whose government has launched an official program of mass conversion to Calvinism as a growth strategy: a Ministry of Vocation, state-radio sermons on predestination, tax rebates for demonstrated sober industriousness. The satire should land because the underlying sociology is exactly right: officials concede that the keenest converts were already the wealthiest merchants; an economist on the record notes that so far the economy seems to be producing Calvinists faster than Calvinists are producing economic growth; a theologian complains that the work ethic without the dread is merely being tired; the ministry, finding the theology hard to install, begins issuing citizens the outward forms directly. The target is the culture-as-cause style of explanation and the leap from it to policy — not any real church or country. Keep the irony deadpan but legible, include at least one skeptical foreign expert quoted by name (invented), and by the final paragraph the reader should feel the genuine question of how anyone could ever establish whether the values made the wealth or the wealth made the values.
