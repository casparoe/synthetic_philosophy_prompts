I’ve somehow ended up on the advisory committee for a democracy-reform nonprofit that wants to use the Condorcet jury theorem as more than a slogan. They’re considering things like large citizens’ assemblies, expert-plus-lay panels, and AI tools that give voters information before a binary policy vote. The obvious problem is that the classroom version of the theorem assumes independent judgments, a common notion of competence, and voters who are right more often than not, while the real cases involve groupthink, uneven expertise, strategic behavior, and deliberation. We have enough money to fund a small group for two years, mostly conceptual and mathematical work rather than large experiments, and I’ve been asked to help set the research agenda.

Which open questions about the jury theorem should be prioritized, and in what order? Candidates include: how to model correlated errors without making the theorem trivially useless; whether competence can be estimated without circularly treating agreement with the majority as evidence of competence; when deliberation improves individual competence and when it merely increases correlation; whether abstention or “don’t know” votes should count as information about competence; and how much of the binary-choice result survives for ranked choices or multi-option decisions. Feel free to replace or add questions, but please defend the ranking using criteria such as how many other questions depend on it, how tractable it is in two years, how much it would change the nonprofit’s institutional design, and how neglected it seems. I want a genuine agenda, not a survey: include the dependencies between the questions, say which fashionable question you would deprioritize, and explain what discoveries would cause you to reorder the list.
