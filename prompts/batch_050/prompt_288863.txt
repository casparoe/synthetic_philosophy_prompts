I co-teach a graduate seminar in aesthetics that has spent a term split between two camps: the intentionalists, who hold that a literary text's meaning is fixed by what its author actually intended to convey, and the anti-intentionalists, who hold that meaning is fixed by what the text supports when read against its public linguistic and historical context, whatever was in the author's head. Write the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, worked out against three cases: a recovered typescript in which the author wrote "hostile" where her drafts and letters show she meant "hospitable," though the sentence reads naturally either way; a novelist who, thirty years after publication, announces that her celebrated book encodes a confession no reader has ever found, while her newly surfaced diary leaves it unclear whether the code was planned during composition or invented afterward; and a poem produced by an unguided computer shuffle that is indistinguishable from deliberate human work. Structure the document as a numbered list of claims both sides accept, then a table of residual disagreements — each phrased so its own side would accept the wording, with merely verbal disagreements explicitly separated from genuine ones — then the cruxes: the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side, each paired with a case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Commit throughout rather than offering menus: state definitely which disagreements are real, name one crux as decisive, and give your reasons. Begin with the statement itself, with no preface or commentary.
