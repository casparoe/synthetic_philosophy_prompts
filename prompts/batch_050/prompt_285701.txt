You are a philosopher of perception serving as the neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over photographic transparency — the question of whether we see through photographs, or only ever at them. Think carefully and take the time this deserves: the value of the deliverable lies entirely in the precision of its formulations.

The first camp defends the transparency thesis (the view most closely associated with Kendall Walton): photographs are prosthetic aids to vision. When you look at a photograph of your grandmother, you literally, though indirectly, see your grandmother. Photographs belong in a class with mirrors, telescopes, and periscopes, not with paintings and drawings, and what marks the boundary is counterfactual dependence: had the scene in front of the lens looked different, the photograph would look correspondingly different, and this dependence routes around anyone's beliefs about how the scene looked. Even a photographer convinced the subject is honest will capture the pickpocket mid-theft.

The second camp, the skeptics, holds that photographs are representations through and through. What you see is a flat, patterned surface; your access to the depicted scene is inferential or imaginative, exactly as with a painting. The mechanical causal chain makes photographs superb evidence, but evidence is not perception, and seeing an object requires the possibility of locating it egocentrically — of its being the sort of thing that could, in principle, occupy a place relative to your own body here and now. That condition fails for the dead and the remote, which is why the transparency theorist's cheerful consequence that we see long-dead people in their portraits should be treated as a reductio, not a discovery.

Before drafting anything, brainstorm for your own working purposes: list candidate agreements, candidate cruxes, and the strongest version of each side's core argument. Then produce the joint statement itself, in three sections.

Section 1 — Agreed claims, numbered. Include whatever both camps can genuinely sign concerning the evidential difference between photographs and paintings, what the comparison with mirrors and telescopes does and does not establish, whether either view threatens the ordinary practice of trusting photographs in journalism and criminal trials, and what would count as seeing at all. Every entry must be signable by both camps without crossed fingers; if a candidate agreement can only be stated vacuously, omit it and note the omission.

Section 2 — Disagreements table. State each residual disagreement in two columns, with each side's position phrased in vocabulary its own proponents would accept — no strawmen, no question-begging terms. Where a disagreement turns on the meaning of the word see rather than on anything about photographs, mark it as verbal and state what, if anything, survives once the ambiguity is resolved.

Section 3 — Cruxes and test cases. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp, then record, for each of the following cases, the verdict each camp is committed to and whether the case bears on any crux: (a) a live closed-circuit video feed with a ten-second delay; (b) an ultrasound image of a fetus, watched by the expectant parents in real time; (c) a photograph taken by an unattended camera on a timer and developed entirely by machine, never seen by any human until this moment; (d) a photorealistic image generated by a computer model depicting a scene that never existed, indistinguishable from a photograph by any available test; (e) a photograph of a person who has since died; (f) a live video feed from a camera mounted on a robot exploring another planet.

Close with a short statement, voiced for each camp separately, of what that camp now concedes it must explain — a burden it had not fully acknowledged before the collaboration. Neither camp may be weakened for narrative effect; if one side's position is easier to state vividly, compensate by giving the other side the sharper argument.
