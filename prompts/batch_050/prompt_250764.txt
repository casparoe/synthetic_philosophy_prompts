In debates over humanitarian intervention — Kosovo, Iraq, Libya — defenders repeatedly lean on the domestic-rescue analogy: sovereignty is like the walls of a private home, and intervening to halt mass atrocities is like breaking down a door to stop a parent beating a child, where no one thinks the homeowner's consent is required. Critics answer with a competing mapping, on which intervening powers are less like Good Samaritans than like armed intruders whose earlier rescues keep ending with the house burned down. For a seminar on the ethics of war, please audit the rescue analogy as it actually functions in this debate: give the correspondence table (head of state as homeowner, the victim population as child, the Security Council as police, and so on); identify where the mapping breaks — for instance, that the interveners answer to no dispatcher, that the "rescuer" often has interests in the property, and that it is the homeowner rather than the child whose consent is at issue; and assess which conclusion, if any, the analogy can support once the disanalogies are priced in. If some weaker or better analogy would do the argumentative work more honestly, propose it. At most 400 words.
