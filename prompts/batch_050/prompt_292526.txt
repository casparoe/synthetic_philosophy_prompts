In a graduate seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry we spent a session on Ian Hacking's essay "Making Up People" (London Review of Books, 2006), and the following passage has stayed with me:

"We think of these kinds of people as definite classes defined by definite properties. As we get to know more about these properties, we will be able to control, help, change, or emulate them better. But it's not quite like that. They are moving targets because our investigations interact with them, and change them. And since they are changed, they are not quite the same kind of people as before. The target has moved. I call this the 'looping effect'."

I want to turn this into something more systematic before I let the seminar debate the usual realism-versus-constructionism question. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of psychiatric classification must deliver if it takes the phenomenon in this passage seriously — the cases it has to get right (for instance, that hysteria and multiple personality flared and faded while something like schizophrenia seems to recur across eras and cultures; that homosexuality could be a diagnosis and then cease to be one without the people concerned changing), the distinctions it cannot afford to collapse (between a classification being socially formed and the distress being unreal; between classifying people and classifying chemical elements; between a label reshaping behavior and merely permitting its expression), and the facts it must remain consistent with (that suffering under a transient label can be genuine, that diagnoses do institutional work — insurance, law, access to treatment — beyond describing symptoms). Give the consideration behind each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable and which look like the prejudices of a particular camp, and flag any pair or subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for example, whether a theory can both hold that classifications track stable underlying kinds and explain why the targets move. If one requirement in the list knocks out more of the going positions than any other, say which and why.

Finally, and only briefly — a short scorecard, a few lines each — test four contenders against the list: a naive medical-realist picture on which the DSM approximates a natural taxonomy; a strong social-constructionist picture on which diagnoses manufacture their objects; Hacking's own dynamic nominalism as gestured at in the passage; and a causal-network picture on which disorders are mutually reinforcing symptom clusters rather than common-effect entities. I am more interested in the requirements and their motivations than in the verdicts, so don't let the scoring take over.
