In a working group on trade policy, our recurring dispute about sweatshops keeps collapsing into a fight between two analogies, and I would like the fight adjudicated properly rather than rhetorically.

The defender of non-interference argues as follows. A sweatshop's wage offer is like the offer of a merchant who, passing through a desert, finds a traveler dying of thirst and sells him water at ten times the usual price. The traveler pays gladly and leaves better off; nobody supposes he would be helped by forbidding the transaction, or by the merchant driving on without stopping. A mutually beneficial, freely accepted offer does not wrong its recipient, and if anything deserves blame, it is the desert — the background poverty — not the person who made it slightly less deadly.

The critic counters with a different source situation. The case, she says, is more like the owner of the region's only oasis fencing it off and charging the thirsty for entry. Here the scarcity is not an innocent backdrop but something the offerer maintains and profits from, the consent to pay is manufactured by the fence, and what presents itself as a rescue is actually the collection of rent on a need the collector helps preserve.

Audit both analogies as arguments rather than as images. Give the correspondence table for each: what plays the role of the water, the price, the desert, the merchant's merely passing by, the fence, the traveler's freedom to refuse. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for each analogy's intended conclusion — non-interference in the first case, license to regulate in the second — from those that are idle. In particular: the first analogy describes a one-shot transaction while employment is an ongoing relationship in which the employer acquires standing power over the worker; assess whether that difference changes what consent can license. The second analogy assumes the offerer bears responsibility for the background conditions; state what would have to be true of multinational employers and their home governments for that premise to hold, and note where it is empirically contestable. Each analogy also encodes a baseline against which "benefit" is measured; make both baselines explicit and identify which analogy begs which question. Finally, examine the step from "the transaction makes no one worse off" to "the transaction is not wrongful," which the first analogy needs and quietly performs. Does either policy conclusion survive the audit, and if neither does, what is the better analogy this debate should be using?
