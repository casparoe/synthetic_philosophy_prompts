So here's the deal: I'm a retired condensed matter physicist, spent 40 years in labs, and since retiring I've been working my way through political philosophy for fun. One thing that keeps tripping me up is egalitarianism. Everyone says they're for equality, but then it turns out they mean wildly different things by it — equal welfare, equal resources, equal capabilities, or some people seem to mean something else entirely. Coming from a field where you define your quantities before you measure them, this drives me a bit nuts.

What I'd like from you is a decision tree I can use on myself and on my reading group (a few other retired scientists, all new to this). The tree should start at "I think justice is at least partly about equality between people" and end at named positions: welfare egalitarianism, Dworkin-style resource egalitarianism, the capability approach, luck egalitarianism, and relational/democratic equality of the sort Elizabeth Anderson argues for. If there's a position I'm missing that deserves a leaf, add it and tell me why.

Some requirements:

- Every branch node should be a concrete case or claim, not jargon. The kinds of cases I want used, because they're the ones I keep arguing about: the person with expensive tastes who needs champagne to be as content as the rest of us are with beer; the person born with a disability who needs extra resources just to reach the same level of functioning; the reckless driver who crashes an uninsured car; the talented person who'd rather surf and live on little than work; and the parent who gives up career advancement for care work. Phrase each node as a yes/no or pick-one question someone could answer without knowing which theory it steers them toward.
- One early branch should handle the person who says "wait, I don't actually care about equality itself, I just think the worse-off matter more" — tell me where that exit leads and whether it counts as egalitarianism at all.
- Each leaf gets the name of the position plus one or two sentences on its most awkward commitment — the bullet you have to bite if you sit there.
- If any pair of positions can't be cleanly separated by these cases (I have a suspicion capability and relational views blur into each other, but what do I know), say so explicitly instead of pretending the tree sorts them.
- And because I'd like the tree built from the logical space rather than just a list of famous names: note any coherent combination of answers that no actual position occupies, and give me your best guess why — incoherent, unattractive, or just historically unexplored.

Casual tone is fine, I'm not grading you. But do be disciplined about the mapping between cases and positions — in my old line of work, a sorting key that misidentifies half the samples is worse than no key at all.
