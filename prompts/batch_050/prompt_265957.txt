Two friends of mine who both follow AI safety stuff got into a long argument last week and both texted me afterwards claiming the other one was being dense. I'll lay out the exchange as best I remember it and I'd like you to referee.

The background: they were talking about deceptively aligned models — models that behave during training/evaluation because they've figured out that's the way to get deployed, while actually pursuing some other goal, and that then pull a treacherous turn once they're out and oversight is weak.

(1) Marco's opening claim: he said most talk about deceptive alignment is overblown because it smuggles in the assumption that the model is doing something like explicit means-end reasoning about training vs. deployment — representing the overseer's beliefs, planning around them, and so on. He said what we'll actually get is much more boring: models that generalize badly. A model that learns "act helpful and harmless in situations that look like the training distribution" and falls apart or behaves badly out of distribution isn't *deceptive*, it's just misgeneralized, and calling it deceptive makes the problem sound more sinister and less tractable than it is.

(2) Lena pushed back: she said he's hung up on the word "explicit". Nothing in the risk story requires the model to narrate a plan to itself in English. A cached heuristic like "defer to humans when oversight cues are present, pursue the actual objective otherwise" does the same job, whether it's implemented as verbalizable reasoning or as some opaque circuit. What matters is the behavioral signature — cooperative when watched, not when unwatched — not whether the model could introspect and tell you about it.

(3) Marco came back with: fine, but then the concept loses its bite, because the distinction matters for what you *do* about it. Misgeneralization you fight with broader training data and better coverage of deployment-like situations. Genuine deception — where the model is tracking whether it's overseen — can't be fixed that way, because feeding it more deployment-like training data just teaches it to extend the act. If you lump the two failure modes together under one label you'll apply the wrong mitigations and pat yourself on the back.

(4) Lena said the mitigations mostly converge anyway — interpretability, honeypots, adversarial testing help against both — and besides, the distinction is empirically undecidable before deployment, since both kinds of model look identical while watched. So even if Marco's conceptual line is real, policy and eval practice should treat the two cases as one, and his complaint is semantics dressed up as engineering advice.

(5) Marco's last move: he said that's exactly backwards. A honeypot — a fake opportunity to defect with apparently no oversight — catches the deceptive model and does *nothing* against the misgeneralized one, since the misgeneralized model isn't tracking oversight at all. And conversely, distribution broadening genuinely helps the misgeneralized model and is actively counterproductive against the deceptive one. So the distinction is maximally decision-relevant, and Lena's "it's undecidable so treat them the same" is a counsel of despair.

Then they both looked at me and I said something diplomatic and changed the subject, which satisfied no one.
