Between October 2018 and March 2019, two new Boeing 737 MAX aircraft — Lion Air Flight 610 and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — crashed in similar fashion shortly after takeoff, killing 346 people. The investigations converged on MCAS, flight-control software added to compensate for the aerodynamic effects of the MAX's larger, repositioned engines. The salient facts, for our purposes: Boeing was under intense schedule and cost pressure because Airbus had launched the competing A320neo; a clean-sheet redesign was never seriously on the table; keeping the MAX within the existing 737 type certificate, and in particular avoiding simulator-training requirements for pilots transitioning from earlier 737s, was a central commercial objective; MCAS as originally certified drew on input from a single angle-of-attack sensor; and the FAA had delegated substantial portions of the certification work to Boeing's own engineers. Please treat these facts as given.

Analyze this episode through Herbert Simon's framework of bounded rationality and satisficing. I want the mapping done carefully rather than gestured at: identify the agents whose rationality was bounded (individual engineers, engineering teams, Boeing management, the FAA's oversight structure), state what each agent's aspiration level plausibly was and where it came from, and describe the search processes that were terminated early — both the search for design alternatives and the search for evidence that the design already chosen was unsafe. In doing so, please work with a distinction I want taken seriously: call it search satisficing versus evidence satisficing. Search satisficing is stopping the hunt for options once an adequate one appears; evidence satisficing is stopping the hunt for disconfirming information about an option already adopted. Which of the two does the MCAS story primarily instantiate, at which level of the organization, and does the standard theory even mark this distinction, or does it silently lump them together?

Then tell me where the framework breaks. Bounded rationality is a theory of cognitive limitation, but much of what happened looks less like agents bumping against the limits of their cognition and more like agents operating inside incentive structures and information channels arranged so that the bar for "good enough" sat where catastrophe could walk under it. Can a satisficing model distinguish adaptive economy of effort from motivated non-search, or does it absorb both and thereby explain neither? And whose boundedness is doing the explanatory work — if the binding constraints were institutional (delegation of certification, schedule pressure, the type-certificate regime) rather than cognitive, is this a case of bounded rationality at all, or of something for which Simon's vocabulary is a polite misdescription?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode do to the theory? Satisficing is usually offered as both descriptively accurate and normatively defensible — the rational response of finite minds to real limits. Consider whether a safety-critical engineering regime, in which the costs of a missed failure mode fall on third parties who never consented to anyone's aspiration level, shows that satisficing needs an account of who may set the bar, at whose expense, and with what verification before stopping — a component the theory arguably never had. Close with a verdict: does the 737 MAX episode confirm satisficing as a description of how organizations actually decide, discredit it as advice for high-stakes design, or reveal that the theory was never really about decisions like these in the first place?
