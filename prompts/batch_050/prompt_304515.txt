I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a magazine feature on the contested border between legend and history -- the point at which a narrative preserved in tradition (the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, the Exodus) may responsibly be treated as evidence for real events. My editors want a house standard, something like a "legend audit," that the desk can apply uniformly to any tradition claiming a historical kernel, so the criteria cannot be tailored to whichever case is under discussion. Write the list of requirements such a standard must meet to count as adequate: the phenomena it must handle (narratives with one surviving source, centuries of oral transmission, archaeological silence where a story implies a large footprint, and so on), with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps -- the minimalists and maximalists of biblical archaeology, Homerists who read Hisarlik optimistically and those who don't, historians of sub-Roman Britain and their skeptics. One requirement I insist you address: the standard must not be calibrated after the fact to vindicate or to debunk a favored tradition, so say what that demand actually rules out in practice. And if some subset of plausible requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance, demanding independent corroboration while still wanting to affirm any event that left no surviving contemporaries -- make that explicit and say which requirement you'd sacrifice; that tension is the part I most want for the piece. Skip the scorecard on Troy, Arthur, and Exodus themselves; I'll apply the standard in the article.
