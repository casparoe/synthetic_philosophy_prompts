For my ethics class I am writing about the doctrine of double effect, and I want to stress-test its most controversial clause: the idea that a harm brought about as a means to a good end is forbidden, while the very same harm merely foreseen as a side effect can be permissible. Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The setting must be realistic (a mine, a flood, a demolition site, or something similar; no runaway trolleys and no transplant surgeons, since those have been done to death). The scenario must involve a single action that satisfies three of the doctrine's four conditions: the act itself is not intrinsically wrong, the agent intends only the good outcome, and the good achieved is proportionate to the harm done. But it must appear to fail the remaining condition, because one person's death or injury is causally productive of saving the others, not merely incidental to it. Then, the hard part: supply a redescription of the very same physical events under which that harm counts as a mere side effect, so that the doctrine permits the act after all. I want to know whether the means/side-effect line tracks something real in the world or only the words we choose to describe what happened.

Please structure your answer in five sections, in this order: "The scenario" (one paragraph); "Four conditions, three satisfied" (about 150 words, walking through each condition and showing where the trouble lies); "The redescription" (about 150 words, same events, different bookkeeping); "Does the line track the world or the words?" (about 100 words, your verdict); and "The doctrine's best reply" (about 100 words, the strongest way a defender of double effect could argue that one of the two descriptions is simply the correct causal analysis).
