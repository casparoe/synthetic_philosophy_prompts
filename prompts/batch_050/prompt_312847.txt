My therapist (who I like, no beef) trotted out the line everyone uses: depression is an illness just like diabetes is, there's a chemical thing wrong in your brain the way a diabetic's pancreas doesn't make insulin, and taking an SSRI is no different from taking insulin. It was clearly meant to do some work on me -- stop me feeling guilty about needing meds, stop me thinking I could just willpower my way out, and I think nudge me toward compliance with the prescription.

Then I made the mistake of reading some Szasz, who argues that the mind can't be sick except metaphorically -- the way a joke or an economy or a society can be called "sick" -- because illness properly applies to bodies, and what psychiatry calls mental illness is really problems in living dressed up in medical vocabulary. And now I can't tell who's doing the metaphor.

So: audit the diabetes analogy properly. Give me the full mapping -- what plays the role of the pancreas, the insulin, the blood-sugar measurement, the diagnosis, the "it's not your fault" -- and then show me where each mapping actually breaks. Some disanalogies seem load-bearing and some don't: e.g. diabetes has a biomarker test and depression doesn't, but does that matter for the analogy's intended use or is it incidental? Also tell me which conclusions the analogy is silently smuggling in via its source domain (diminished blame? the appropriateness of lifelong medication? the idea that treatment targets a mechanism rather than a person?) and whether each of those survives once the breaks are on the table. And Szasz's counter-picture needs the same treatment -- he leans on an analogy too (mind-sickness as a metaphor like sick-joke-sickness), so map where HIS comparison breaks and what it quietly assumes about what counts as a real disease. End with: if neither analogy can carry the anti-shame conclusion my therapist wanted, can anything -- or does that conclusion actually depend on the medical framing after all?
