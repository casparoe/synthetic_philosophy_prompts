I am a retired population geneticist who has lately been spending his mornings with philosophy books, and I keep tripping over a slogan I first heard decades ago in conservation circles: "extinction is forever." What brings it to mind is that I recently heard it deployed twice in the same week, for what seem like opposite purposes. At a public lecture, a speaker used it to wave away de-extinction projects -- the woolly mammoth proxies, the passenger pigeon, the thylacine -- as a childish refusal to accept loss. Two days later, an old colleague used the same sentence to argue for extreme caution about CRISPR gene drives in wild mosquito populations: whatever we release, we cannot recall, so we should treat every such intervention as permanent. It struck me that the slogan cannot mean the same thing in both mouths, since in the first case its force is that extinction cannot be undone and in the second that an intervention cannot be undone -- and if the first use is right, the second speaker's premise about what gene drives risk seems oddly easy to satisfy, since we apparently already know how to make permanent changes.

So here is my request. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this one sentence could be expressing. I can see the outlines of several myself: a metaphysical claim that a species is essentially its historical lineage, so that any engineered population resembling the mammoth would be at most a convincing copy and never the species itself; an empirical claim about the limits of foreseeable biotechnology; an ecological claim that a species is partly constituted by its niche, its co-evolved partners, and its learned behaviors, all of which vanish even if a genome survives; a claim about information loss, on the analogy of entropy, where the degraded DNA of a long-dead species is simply unrecoverable; and a normative claim that policy should treat extinction as irreversible regardless of the science, lest the promise of resurrection erode the motive for protection. There are no doubt others I have missed.

For each reading, I would like your verdict: which are trivial or near-tautological, which are substantive and defensible, and which are simply false. Then the harder question -- which readings would actually support each of the two uses I witnessed? Does the lineage-essentialist reading, for instance, undermine de-extinction in a way the ecological reading does not, and does anything in the menu justify my colleague's caution about gene drives without relying on a premise his own slogan seems to deny? If the slogan turns out to do no honest work in either argument, I would like to hear that too.
