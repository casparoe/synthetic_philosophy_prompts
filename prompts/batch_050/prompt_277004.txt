I cover behavioral science and public policy for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about government "nudge units" — the behavioural-insight teams that dozens of countries now maintain, which redesign official forms and enrollment procedures to steer people toward outcomes the teams judge better: auto-enrolling employees into pension plans, making organ donation opt-out rather than opt-in, sending tax reminders worded to invoke social norms. The intellectual justification is always some version of the same story: people's actual choices are distorted by inertia, present bias, and framing effects, so the choice architect steps in to correct the distortion and deliver what people "really" want. In interviews, though, I've never gotten a straight answer to the basic question of how the architect knows which choices count as distorted and which count as authentic. The teams often treat non-enrollment under an opt-in regime as evidence of bias rather than preference — but then, when most people stick with a default after it's introduced, they cite the low opt-out rate as evidence that the new default serves people well. I can't tell whether that's a discovery or a circularity.

My question is what this practice presupposes about the distinction between a preference and a bias. A nudge architect must be operating with some standard for when observed choice reveals what a person values and when it reveals a malfunction — what is that standard, and does the practice actually have a coherent one? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can: perhaps choices count as biased when they are unstable across frames, or when people later disavow them, or when they diverge from what a well-informed deliberator would pick. Then stress-test it against the practice's own details. In particular: if the test for a genuine preference is what people stick with, the opt-out rate can't simultaneously be the error being corrected and the evidence of success; if the test is instead people's self-reported goals, it's unclear why self-reports get epistemic authority that choices lack; and if the test is what an ideally rational agent would choose, say whose substantive judgments about the good life are being smuggled in. I'd also like to know whether the practice could survive taking choices as sovereign — whether anything recognizably remains of "nudging" if we drop the presupposition that the architect can tell bias from preference better than the chooser can.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
