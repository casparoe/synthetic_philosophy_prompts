The socialist calculation debate has been declared settled several times over: by Mises and Hayek's side after the collapse of the planned economies, by Lange's defenders who note that he answered the calculation argument as it was originally posed, and more recently by commentators who argue that the internal planning apparatus of firms such as Amazon or Walmart -- which coordinate vast numbers of non-priced internal transfers across supply chains rivalling small national economies -- shows that Lange's trial-and-error proposal was simply ahead of its computational time. Yet the participants keep talking past one another. One side treats the question as whether a planner could in principle gather the data and solve a system of equilibrium equations; the other treats it as whether any institution lacking rivalrous bidding among independent owners of capital could generate, in the first place, the knowledge those equations would need as inputs.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the two sides measure different things -- computational tractability on the one hand, knowledge generation on the other -- and that the modern claim that big data and optimisation software have solved planning conflates the two, since a firm's internal logistics optimises against prices generated by markets outside the firm and so cannot straightforwardly be generalised to an economy with no outside. But I am not confident in this diagnosis. Perhaps the disagreement is really empirical, concerning how much dispersed, tacit knowledge matters relative to what can be extracted from transaction data; or perhaps it concerns what counts as success -- static allocative efficiency versus adaptability under unforeseen change -- in which case neither side could win on the other's terms even in principle.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it: is it verbal, empirical, formal, or a clash over what an economic theory must explain? State what each side actually claims, identify where exactly they cease to engage, and assess my hypothesis against the alternatives. Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress -- a sharper distinction, a formal result, an empirical test, a new kind of case, or abandoning the question as posed -- or argue, with reasons, that no progress should be expected.
