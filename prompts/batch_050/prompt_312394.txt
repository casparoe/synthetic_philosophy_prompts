I'm TAing a second-year political philosphy course and next Friday I run a 50-minute tutorial on paternalism. I want to build the whole session around this passage from Mill's On Liberty (chapter 5, "Applications"):

"The maxims are, first, that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself. Advice, instruction, persuasion, and avoidance by other people if thought necessary by them for their own good, are the only measures by which society can justifiably express its dislike or disapprobation of his conduct."

Don't explain paternalism to me -- plan how I should teach it. Concretely:

- What has to be in place before the passage can do any work. I'm guessing they need the harm principle first, and a grip on the idea that what makes an interference paternalistic is the *reason* for it (your own good) rather than the fact that it restricts you. But tell me the actual order and what depends on what. For instance, does the soft/hard paternalism distinction come before or after the passage, and why?
- The passage does something sneaky: it parks "advice, instruction, persuasion" on the permitted side of the line. I want the session's hinge to be where nudges fall relative to that list -- cafeteria layouts, pension auto-enrolment, opt-out organ donation. Is a nudge persuasion, or a fourth thing Mill didn't have a box for? Plan the arc so this lands as the payoff, not the opener.
- The one carrying example. I'd default to seatbelt laws but I suspect that's stale and lets them pattern-match to a cached answer. Suggest something better and say what the example must have and must lack -- presumably it should be self-regarding-ish but contestable, so they're forced to actually fight over whether it "concerns no person but himself."
- The misconception map, in the order the misconceptions will actually show up. My predictions: (1) "paternalism = any state action I dislike", (2) "soft paternalism isn't really paternalism so it's automatically fine", (3) "Mill's basically a libertarian so he'd oppose all of this" (the passage itself is the corrective here), (4) conflating "affects others" with "harms others". Tell me which I've got right, what I'm missing, and exactly where in the sequence each one should be headed off -- and whether that's before or after the relevant distinction is introduced.
- One end-of-session check: a single question or case whose answer shows me whether they've got the structure (the reason-for-interference test plus Mill's permitted list) rather than just the vocabulary. Ideally something where the right move is noticing the case is underdescribed as stated.
- What to deliberately leave out at this level and say so -- Kantian dignity objections, the empirical literature on whether nudges even work, whatever you think doesn't fit in 50 minutes.

Keep it concrete and sequenced. I want a plan I can run on Friday, not an essay about teaching.
