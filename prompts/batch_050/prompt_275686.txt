So I just got home from like a three hour argument with my friend Dan and I'm still annoyed, so sorry if this is rambly. Here's the thing we kept going in circles on. Dan insists that if you can't point to any difference between two things, then they aren't actually two things, they're one thing you're thinking about twice. And he used this move in a bunch of different cases over the night and I want to know if the move has a name, because he kept saying "this is just basic logic" and I don't buy it.

Cases it came up in:

1. We were talking about whether a perfect atom-for-atom copy of you would be you or just a copy. Dan said if there's genuinely no difference, not even one, then the question "which one is the real you" is meaningless because there's only one thing.

2. Later it was something about electrons, like how physicists say all electrons are exactly alike. Dan's take was that "two electrons" is really just a way of talking.

3. Then I tried to construct a counterexample: imagine a universe that's completely empty except for two metal spheres, same size, same color, same everything, floating some distance apart. Surely that's two things, not one! Dan said no, because anything you say about one sphere is also true of the other, so there's still nothing to distinguish them.

The part where I think he's cheating: when I said "well, sphere A is over HERE and sphere B is over THERE," he said location doesn't count as a real difference, it's not a property of the sphere itself. And honestly I couldn't say why it should count, but it also seems like a move he just invented to win. Like, his principle sounds fine until you notice that "being to the left of the other sphere" is something true of sphere A and not sphere B... except then he says the reverse is true of sphere B so it cancels out?? It felt symmetric in a way I couldn't break.

So my questions: does his principle actually have a name and a history, or is he bluffing? Has anyone famous defended the "two identical spheres in empty space" scenario as a counterexample, and does it work? And the thing I really want: is there a principled way to say which differences count (location? relations to other things? just plain being numerically distinct?) without it being circular, i.e. without defining "difference" as "whatever makes them two." Because if the only thing seperating them is that they ARE two, the whole argument feels rigged from the start.
