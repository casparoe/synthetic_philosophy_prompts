I manage a family-owned property business, and last month I was negotiating the sale of a small commercial building. The prospective buyer asked me directly, toward the end of a meeting, whether we had received any other offers. In fact, we had received none -- but a few weeks earlier another party had toured the building, spoken enthusiastically, and said they were prepared to pay close to the asking price before going quiet. So I answered: there has been serious interest at essentially full asking price. Every word of that was true, but I chose it deliberately, knowing the buyer would hear it as there being a live competing offer, and I believe it moved him to raise his bid by a meaningful amount. Afterward I told a friend about it, and she said I might as well have just made up an offer outright -- that what I did was dishonest in exactly the same way, and that congratulating myself for staying technically truthful was a self-serving legalism. I pushed back: I didn't assert anything false, and the buyer had every opportunity to ask follow-up questions; surely there is a real difference between asserting a falsehood and saying something true in a way you expect to be misread, and surely that difference should matter to how blameworthy I am. She's unpersuaded, and now I'm less sure of my footing than I was. Is there a genuine moral difference between these two things, or is the distinction I was relying on one that collapses under scrutiny? If the difference is real, what exactly grounds it -- and does it survive the fact that my intent, the buyer's resulting false belief, and my gain were identical in both versions of the story? I'd also like to know whether the negotiation context changes the analysis: buyers presumably expect a degree of strategic opacity in such settings, but I don't know whether that expectation licenses what I did or merely explains why people do it. Please give me a principled assessment, not just practical advice about deal-making, and be willing to tell me my friend is right if she is.
