Right, I need help settling something. My mate Dan and I were in the pub til closing last night arguing about whether 2 is an element of 4. Sounds mad, but hear me out. His line: on the standard von Neumann construction of the natural numbers, 2 is {0, 1} and 4 is {0, 1, 2, 3}, so 2 ∈ 4 is simply true, end of. My line: that's just an artefact of one particular way of coding numbers as sets — use Zermelo's construction instead and it comes out false, so whatever is going on, it can't be a fact about the numbers themselves, only about a modelling choice. We both ended up agreeing the question itself is somehow dodgy, but could not for the life of us pin down where exactly the dodginess lives.

So build us a decision tree that walks someone from the starting point "2 and 4 are perfectly respectable mathematical objects, and '2 ∈ 4' is at least a grammatical question" to a named position on what, if anything, fixes its answer. Some requirements:

- Each fork should be a concrete claim or case you'd put to someone cold — about whether numbers are objects at all or just positions in a structure, whether set-theoretic constructions identify numbers or merely model them, whether 'the natural numbers' picks out one thing or a whole family of isomorphic things — so they answer on instinct rather than reverse-engineering which label they're heading for. No forks of the form "do you side with Benacerraf?", that defeats the whole purpose.
- Leaves must include at least: the reductionist who says 2 ∈ 4 is simply true; the reductionist who says it's simply false (different construction, same confidence); the structuralist who says there's no fact of the matter because the question treats positions as if they were objects; the eliminativist who says number-talk generalises over systems so the question never even arises; and a nominalist leaf where 'true' and 'false' both misfire because there are no abstract objects to begin with. Add whatever other leaves you reckon belong in the space.
- Where two leaves answer every fork identically (I suspect the ante rem and in re structuralists might, differing only in vibes), flag them as inseperable by this tree rather than pretending you've split them.
- Where a combination of answers has no named occupant, mark it and say in a sentence whether it's empty because the view is incoherent or just unfashionable.

Keep it to maybe six or seven forks deep, no more. Then finish by telling me which single fork you think Dan and I actually diverge at, and which leaf each of us lands on.
