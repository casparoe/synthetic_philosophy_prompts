A friend of mine read Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness last year and never really recovered — she now judges people's lives by what she calls the du Châtelet test. Her verdicts so far: the retired guy down the hall who lives for his weekly poker nights and his firm belief he's still improving — happy, she says; a Stoic-ish coworker who's trained himself out of every strong passion and is never disappointed — not happy; a woman who's convinced her mediocre poems are brilliant and whose whole social life runs on that belief — happy; a rich neighbor with every pleasure money buys but who's riddled with superstition and snobbery — not happy; and an activist whose single ruling passion forces her to lie and manipulate people — not happy, apparently virtue is a hard constraint.

Here's the new case we're arguing about: a self-made businessman whose animating passion — the thing that gets him up at 5am and that he clearly loves — rests on his conviction that the world is a pure meritocracy and he earned everything himself. That conviction is both the illusion that fuels his passion and, pretty clearly, a prejudice of exactly the kind du Châtelet says blocks happiness. Predict my friend's verdict, tell me which rule she seems to be running across her earlier calls and which other rules fit them just as well, and give me the follow-up case that would actually settle which rule it is. And if you think the setup is confused somehow — e.g. I've garbled what du Châtelet actually held, or there's no fact of the matter here — say that instead of playing along.
