For an internal discussion document at my lab, I've been asked to state my working view of the alignment problem and have colleagues stress-test it. Here's where I've landed: (1) Alignment is fundamentally one problem -- getting a capable system to reliably pursue what its operators actually intend -- and outer alignment, inner alignment, and corrigibility are best understood as subproblems of that single problem, not as separate problems in their own right. (2) Working out "human values" or the good is not part of the alignment problem; it's a downstream moral-philosophy question that alignment research can and should remain neutral on. (3) Deceptive alignment is a genuine risk even under a perfectly specified training signal, because goal-directed inner optimizers can emerge whose objectives bear no relation to that signal. (4) A fully aligned AI should sometimes refuse or override its operator's explicit instructions -- for instance, when the operator is clearly mistaken about their own interests, or when an unauthorized user is issuing commands. (5) Failures of competence or world-modeling are safety problems, not alignment problems, and shouldn't be folded into the definition. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and where tensions are real, what is the least costly revision to each? Please rank any tensions by severity and tell me which single amendment would make the whole set cohere.
