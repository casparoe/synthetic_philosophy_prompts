I direct dining services at a mid-sized university, and twice a year we make sourcing decisions that a skeptic could dismiss as rounding errors: whether to keep chicken on the menu (we buy roughly 40,000 pounds a year), whether to pay a premium for certified-humane beef, whether to shift one dining hall to plant-based entrees by default. The standard worry is the inefficacy objection -- our purchases are too small to change what producers do, so the ethical weight of these choices is zero. I suspect that's too quick: supply chains respond to demand in lumpy, threshold-driven ways, so a small buyer can have a real expected effect even when no single purchase visibly tips anything, but I have no usable way to turn that thought into policy.

Write a decision procedure my procurement staff (not philosophers) can run on any sourcing proposal in under an hour, operationalizing what the philosophy of individual contributions to collective harms actually establishes: how to estimate the expected marginal effect of a purchase when effects pass through thresholds; how to distinguish cases where the expected effect is genuinely negligible (one buyer in a vast fungible market) from cases where it is merely small; how the reasoning changes when the harm runs through greenhouse gas emissions rather than animal production; and at which points the procedure must stop and hand the question to a value judgment (e.g., how animal welfare weighs against dollars) that no procedure can settle -- flag those handoffs explicitly, and mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) making one dining hall plant-based by default; (2) certified-humane beef at a 15% premium; (3) a student group proposal to replace beef with chicken on animal-welfare grounds -- work this one honestly, since the per-animal arithmetic may yield a conclusion the students will not like. Answer in American English, and end with a single line of the form \answer{case 1: [adopt/reject/escalate], case 2: [adopt/reject/escalate], case 3: [adopt/reject/escalate]} giving the procedure's verdict on each proposal.
