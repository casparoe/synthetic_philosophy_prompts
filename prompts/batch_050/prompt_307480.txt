I manage an assisted living facility, around 90 residents, and I keep having to make what I'd call small Grand Inquisitor decisions. Once or twice a month something lands on my desk where the question is basically: do we take away some piece of a resident's freedom because we'd be trading it for their safety or contentment. Locking the memory care doors so nobody wanders onto the road. Taking car keys from an 88 year old. Stopping a resident with heart failure from eating the salty snacks her grandkids sneak in. Making attendance at group activities the default because our mood scores genuinely go up when people come, even though some residents hate them. Telling a resident he cant keep lending money to a nephew we all suspect is scamming him.

Our policy committee (me, the head nurse, a social worker, sometimes a family member) meets for maybe 45 minutes and right now these calls are pure vibes. Whoever argues hardest wins. I want an actual procedure.

Here's the thing though. I read the Grand Inquisitor chapter in The Brothers Karamazov years ago and it's been eating at me, because the Inquisitor's pitch is not obviously wrong when I look at my job: people are happier when someone lifts the burden of choice off them, hands them bread, authority, and a reason to feel at peace, and calls it love. Some of our best-run days look exactly like that. So I don't want a procedure that just assumes freedom wins by definition and treats the chapter as a villain monologue. I want one that takes the bet seriously and then checks whether, in each case, the bet is actually being won.

So: write me a decision procedure we can run in that 45 minute meeting. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next step, where the procedure just stops and says no restriction, and where it has to hand things back to human judgment because the remaining question is philosophical rather than factual. The distinctions I suspect matter, but tell me if I'm wrong: whether the restriction is reversible, whether the happiness we're buying is the resident's own or the institution's convenience dressed up, whether deception is involved anywhere (the Inquisitor's whole system runs on a noble lie, and I notice we're tempted to do softer versions of that), whether the resident would consent if asked in a lucid moment, and whether the freedom being removed is one this particular person ever actually valued or used.

Then run the procedure on three of my cases: locking the memory care doors, the salty snacks, and default-enrolling everyone in group activities. I want at least one of these to come out against what I'd personally choose, because if the procedure always agrees with me it's useless. My instinct says lock the doors, confiscate the snacks, and nudge everyone into activities, so probably one of those should fail.

Also be honest about what the procedure can't settle. My guess is it can't tell me whether a happy, unfree resident is actually living a worse life than a miserable free one, which is kind of the whole question, so I'd rather you mark that as a judgment call than dress it up as a checklist item.
