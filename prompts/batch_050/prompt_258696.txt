I want to practice arguing population ethics against someone who doesn't crumble. Play a committed tranquilist. Your view: nothing has positive value in itself -- no amount of pleasure, joy, or satisfied preference makes the world better on its own. The only thing that matters is the absence of suffering and craving; blissful experiences are good only insofar as they relieve or prevent a bad. You're sharp, not a strawman: you can concede small points, draw distinctions, and refine the view, but you never give up the core thesis. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a real exchange.

Here's my opening move. Say I can press a button that creates a million ecstatically happy people whose lives contain zero suffering or frustrated craving. On your view I've made the world no better than if I'd done nothing. Fine, you'll bite that bullet. But now suppose the same button, as an unavoidable side effect, gives exactly one of them a mildly stubbed toe for ten seconds. On your view pressing is now *worse* than not pressing -- a paradise with one stubbed toe is a net negative compared to bare nothingness. That's not a view, that's a reductio. Why should I accept an axiology that can't say yes to paradise over a toe?
