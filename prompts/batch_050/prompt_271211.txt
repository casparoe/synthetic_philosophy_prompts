I'm reporting a piece on dementia care and keep running into a philosophical puzzle in the law that I need to get straight. In most jurisdictions, a competent adult can sign an advance directive refusing life-sustaining treatment, and clinicians will honor it decades later once the signer has advanced dementia and lacks capacity — even if the patient now appears content, and even though the same patient, had she never signed, would receive the treatment. Some jurisdictions go further and permit euthanasia on the strength of such a directive. Yet the authority runs only one way: the directive can be revoked freely at any point while the signer remains competent, but the now-incompetent patient cannot revoke or amend it, and her present resistance at most triggers hesitation rather than a fresh judgment of her current interests. What conception of the authority of an earlier self over a later self does this practice presuppose — that the person persists across the loss of capacity, and that her autonomy is best exercised at the moment of maximal competence? Does it rest on a view about what makes a life go well, such that the former self's considered judgments outrank the demented patient's present pleasures? Which rival accounts of personal identity or welfare would the practice rule out, and which elements of it would survive those rivals? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably, and flag where it seems philosophically underdetermined or internally inconsistent. And since this is going into print: separate what is settled in this debate from what remains genuinely contested, and don't present any contested position as the consensus.
