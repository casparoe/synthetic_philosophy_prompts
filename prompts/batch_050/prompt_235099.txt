Got into a long argument at a pub quiz (of all places) and I want either ammunition or to be talked out of my position. Someone dropped the line "consent obtained by deception isn't consent" and used it to argue that a guy who lies about being married in order to sleep with someone has done something in the same moral family as assault. My gut says the slogan is doing way more work than it's entitled to, but when I tried to push back I realised I couldn't actually say what the principle behind it is suppose to be.

So: what are the different precise principles that one-liner could be standing for? I want a menu, not a lecture. Some things I'm confused about that your menu should hopefully sort out:

- Does it mean ANY lie voids consent? Surely not — people lie about their job, their age, their taste in music all the time and we don't call the resulting sex nonconsensual.
- Or is it only lies about what the act itself is (like the old cases where a doctor does something sexual under the pretense of performing a medical exam) versus lies about the surrounding circumstances (marital status, wealth, intentions)? And is that distinction actually principled, or is it just a line that got drawn somewhere for practical reasons?
- Or is it about what would have changed the person's mind — a lie voids consent iff the other person wouldn't have agreed had they known the truth? That seems to capture the married case, but it also seems to capture an absurd amount, like someone who wouldn't have gone home with you if they'd known you voted differently than they assumed.
- Or is it about whether the lie concerns something the agreement itself was about, versus background assumptions one party happened to bring along?

For each reading, tell me whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or just false, and — this is the important part — which readings actually get my pub quiz opponent his conclusion that lying about being married makes the sex nonconsensual, and which don't. I suspect the readings that get him his conclusion also prove far too much, but I want that shown, not asserted.

And if the honest answer is that the slogan has no single defensible precisification and people just slide between a strong reading (for the punchy cases like the fake doctor) and a much weaker one (for the married guy), then say that outright and describe exactly how the slide works, because naming that move is what I actually want to walk away with.
