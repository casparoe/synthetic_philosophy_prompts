Last week a friend and I argued late into the night about police reform. Her position was that every serious proposal in the current debate -- from modest procedural reforms, to "defund," to outright abolition of the police -- already accepts Max Weber's definition of the state and merely haggles over how the monopoly on legitimate force should be staffed, supervised, or distributed. My position was that abolitionists, at least, must reject the definition itself. We stalled over a passage from Weber's 1919 lecture Politics as a Vocation that I had looked up mid-argument:

"Specifically, at the present time, the right to use physical force is ascribed to other institutions or to individuals only to the extent to which the state permits it. The state is considered the sole source of the 'right' to use violence."

I would like this passage tested against the actual allocation of coercive force in an ordinary modern state, so let me describe that allocation concretely. A police officer may use force proactively and affirmatively -- to compel an arrest, to disperse an unlawful assembly, to execute a court order -- in circumstances where the identical act by a private person would be a crime. Private persons may use force only defensively, transiently, and under after-the-fact review. And a large penumbra of non-state force -- licensed security guards, bail enforcement agents, armed bodyguards -- exists only under revocable state permission, while its unlicensed equivalents, such as a self-organized neighborhood patrol carrying batons, are prosecuted.

What theory of legitimate violence does this allocation presuppose? Read strictly, Weber's sentence says the state is the sole source of the right, so that all private force is delegated and revocable in principle -- a claim about authority, not merely about who happens to wield force. But the same practice also fits a weaker rival view on which citizens hold an independent right of defensive force that the state regulates and supplements but does not grant. Does the practice actually decide between these, or only appear to? Consider a tension within it: defensive force is treated as a right the state cannot withdraw, while licensed private force is treated as a privilege it can. Does one coherent theory cover both treatments, or does the institution quietly run on two incompatible ones? Finally, adjudicate the dispute with my friend: which of the standard proposals -- unarmed civilian crisis-response units, routine patrol without firearms, replacing police functions with community institutions -- could be enacted while leaving the practice's presupposition intact, and which require denying that the state is the sole source of the right to use violence? Say also where the practice is simply underdetermined, so that both my friend's reading and mine remain available.
