I write a long-form science and philosophy column, and I've been asked to give the closing talk at a weekend ideas festival -- forty-five minutes, a general audience of curious adults (book-club types, a few engineers, no one with formal philosophy training). My assigned topic is the illusionist view of consciousness: the claim that the seemingly obvious "inner glow" of experience -- the what-it's-likeness that feels so clearly in need of explanation -- is a kind of introspective misrepresentation, and that the hard problem arises from taking that misrepresentation at face value. I understand the position well enough to defend it in print, but I've never had to build it up live, from scratch, for people who will arrive assuming that their direct acquaintance with their own experience is the one thing that can't possibly be an illusion.

So, acting as a professional philosopher of mind who also has real experience teaching skeptical audiences: don't explain illusionism to me -- plan how I should explain it. Work out the dependency structure first: what has to be in place before the audience can even hear the thesis as a thesis rather than as a bizarre denial that they feel anything (I'm particularly worried about the slide from "your introspective picture of experience is inaccurate" to "you are not conscious," and where in the sequence that conflation must be severed). Then give me the one carrying example the whole talk should be built around -- something from ordinary perceptual or introspective error, vivid enough to do real work, but please reason explicitly about what the example must have and what it must lack (e.g., why a standard optical illusion might be pedagogically perfect or might secretly load the wrong moral, since it implies a veridical backstage view that illusionism denies). Map the misconceptions I should expect in the order the audience will generate them -- I'm anticipating at least "an illusion of experience is itself an experience, so the view is self-refuting," "this is just behaviorism with better branding," and "you're explaining away the one datum we know best" -- and tell me precisely where each should be headed off, whether before it forms or as it forms.

Think carefully and, before writing the plan, spend a paragraph brainstorming which structural choice matters most: leading with the science (change blindness, confabulation) versus leading with the philosophical puzzle, and what each opening costs me. End the plan with a concrete check for understanding -- one question or prompt I can pose to the room that would show they've grasped the view's actual shape rather than merely absorbed a provocation -- and a candid list of what I should openly refuse to explain at this level (the illusionist's positive account of why brains generate this particular misrepresentation, the metamers of phenomenality, and so on), with one sentence each on why deferring it is a feature, not an evasion.
