I run a philosophy reading group for undergrads at my university, mostly second-years who've done one intro ethics course. Next week we're doing prioritarianism, and I get one 90-minute session on it. They've all just spent the previous week on utilitarianism and the repugnant conclusion, so they'll arrive thinking the only question in population/welfare ethics is "how do we add up wellbeing," and equality talk makes them reach straight for their political intuitions about income.

Plan the session for me. Specifically:

- The order the ideas have to come in. I think they need to feel the pull of egalitarianism before prioritarianism can be introduced as an alternative to it rather than a variant of it, but I'm not sure whether the leveling-down objection should land before or after I've defined the priority view properly. Tell me what depends on what and why, not just a list.
- One carrying example. I need a single case (or a matched pair of cases) that does the heavy lifting: it should make leveling-down feel genuinely bad, not just weird, and then let prioritarianism show off. I was considering some version of a divided-world setup where two populations never interact, or else a case about taking sight from the sighted when it can't be given to the blind. Tell me which you'd build on, what features the case must have (e.g., should the worse-off be identifiable people? should the loss be large or small?), and what it must not contain or I'll spend twenty minutes on objections that are beside the point.
- The misconceptions, roughly in the order they'll surface, and where in the arc to head each off. Ones I'm already dreading: "prioritarianism is just egalitarianism with extra steps," "giving priority to the worse-off means we always help the worst-off person no matter how small the benefit," and someone will definitely say the leveling-down objection just shows equality is a weird thing to care about impersonally and therefore prioritarianism wins by default, which skips the actual hard part. Also at least one misconception I haven't thought of that students reliably form.
- A check at the end that shows they got it -- like, a novel case I'd put to them where the right answer distinguishes someone who understood the view from someone who memorized the slogan "worse-off people's wellbeing counts more."
- What to deliberately leave out at this level (I assume the person-affecting stuff and infinite-population headaches), and one sentence each on why it's fine to omit without distortion.
