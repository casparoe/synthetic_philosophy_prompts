I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of language, and for my term paper I'm cataloguing the systematic differences between indicative conditionals ("If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did") and subjunctive/counterfactual conditionals ("If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have"). So far my list has four entries:

1. The minimal-pair contrast itself: swapping indicative for subjunctive morphology can flip the apparent truth value of otherwise parallel conditionals, as in the Oswald case.
2. Counterfactuals famously fail contraposition and transitivity, whereas the corresponding inferences with indicatives sound far better.
3. Counterfactuals permit backtracking readings (roughly, reasoning from a counterfactual antecedent back to how the past must have been different); indicatives don't.
4. Indicatives embed smoothly under epistemic modals ("It might be that if she comes, he'll leave"); counterfactuals resist this and require the modal to appear in the consequent instead.

I'd like a fifth entry that is genuinely distinct from these — not a restatement, special case, or downstream consequence of any of them. Please state the difference precisely, give a minimal pair that illustrates it, and say whether you think the contrast is robust or whether it can be explained away by one of the four items already on my list.
