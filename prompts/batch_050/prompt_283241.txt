I teach a second-year undergraduate metaphysics module, and next term I have a single 75-minute seminar with sixteen students on the sea battle argument: the idea that if it is true today that there will be a naval battle tomorrow, then tomorrow's battle was never really open, and since the same goes for every truth about the future, nothing is. Students reliably nod along with the standard replies on the page without ever feeling why the argument tempted anyone, so I want an activity that makes them feel the trap close before anyone names it.

Design a structured game with the following arc. In an early phase, small groups must write down precise predictions about events that will occur later in the session and seal them in envelopes — events that depend partly on other participants' choices, so that nobody can dismiss the predictions as concerning mere dice. Your payoff scheme (points, not money) should reward predictions that are both early and specific, so that groups are pushed to commit to determinate claims about a future they do not control. In a middle phase, after the events have occurred, run an audit: groups open the envelopes and must collectively answer, for each successful prediction, the question of when it became true — at the moment of writing, or only at the event. The disagreement this provokes is the point of the exercise. Include one further round that stages what is sometimes called the lazy argument: a designated player learns the content of a prediction about their own future action and may then choose whether to deliberate about that action at all, or to let things happen as they will, and the group must reckon with what the prediction's truth would imply about the pointlessness of deliberating.

Specify rules and roles precisely, the materials needed, the payoff structure, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Then give me a debrief plan that maps concrete moments of the session onto the steps of the fatalist argument — from truth about the future, to the future's being settled, to the apparent futility of deliberation — and shows where each step can be resisted (denying that the prediction was true in advance, distinguishing a truth's holding from its being necessitated, and so on), with what each resistance costs. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — for instance, groups colluding to make their own predictions come true — and how to handle it. Finally, be frank about which feature of the original problem the game cannot reproduce: the events here are chosen or rigged by participants, whereas the sea battle concerns a future nobody controls, so say explicitly what the facilitator must supply in words.

Write no more than 800 words, in British English.
