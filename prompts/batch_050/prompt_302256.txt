I work with a small group developing mechanistic anomaly detectors for neural networks. Our problem is not merely finding unusual outputs but deciding when two unusual-looking processes instantiate the same mechanism. We use two internal diagnostics. A causal silhouette records which components materially change an output under a standardized battery of interventions, while ignoring activation magnitudes that make no difference. A compensation shadow records which dormant components take over when the original pathway is damaged. The disagreement concerns three cases: a network that performs the same algorithm after moving it from two attention heads to one wider head; a network that normally uses one pathway but has a backup pathway with a different algorithm; and two networks that compute the same function through genuinely different intermediate concepts but produce identical responses to every intervention in our current battery. Half the group calls the first two cases one mechanism with variable neural packaging, but not the third. The other half says the causal silhouettes differ in the first case and the compensation shadows differ in the second, while the third may simply reveal the limits of our tests.

This argument has survived several rounds of new experimental results because both sides can redescribe every result in their preferred vocabulary. My hypothesis is that we are conflating at least three notions: mechanism as a causal graph, mechanism as an abstract algorithm, and mechanism as a family of pathways connected by reliable compensation. Diagnose the disagreement rather than merely choosing a definition. Is it primarily verbal, empirical, or a disagreement about the purpose of mechanism identification—prediction, explanation, intervention, or assigning responsibility? Explain which proposed distinction would make progress, what observations or intervention experiments would discriminate among the positions, and whether there is any principled answer to what counts as the same mechanism independently of the detector’s purpose.
