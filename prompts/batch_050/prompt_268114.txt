I am helping to set the research agenda for a small philosophy-of-science working group — three people, roughly eighteen months — that has been asked to supply conceptual foundations for a project on mechanistic anomaly detection: flagging, in biological and artificial systems alike, when some observed behavior does not issue from the mechanism we take to be operative. Every such judgment presupposes an account of mechanism-identity — of when two systems, or two runs of the same system, count as instantiating the same mechanism. Existing frameworks (mechanist accounts of the Machamer–Darden–Craver style, causal-model approaches, and functionalist treatments inherited from philosophy of mind) each gesture at a standard of sameness, but none was designed for this purpose.

For the sake of argument, take the field to revolve around three candidate standards: (i) sameness of component parts and their organization, at some fixed grain of description; (ii) sameness of counterfactual behavior under interventions, regardless of which parts realize it; (iii) sameness of functional-computational role, regardless of causal detail. My working hypothesis is that the most important open questions concern the relations among these standards — where they converge, where they come apart, and what to do when they come apart — rather than the choice of a winner among them. I want that hypothesis tested, not assumed.

Which three or four open questions about mechanism-identity should the group prioritize, and in what order? For each question, explain: why it is upstream of other questions, including applied ones (for instance, whether a trained artificial network that matches a human subject's input–output profile but arrives at outputs by a different internal route should count as running the same mechanism for anomaly-detection purposes); how tractable it is by philosophical rather than empirical methods within our eighteen months; and what developments — conceptual or empirical — would move it up or down the ranking. Also identify at least one question that is frequently treated as central in this area but that you think the group should deliberately deprioritize, and say why its resolution would change little downstream. Defend your final ordering explicitly against the most obvious rival ordering.
