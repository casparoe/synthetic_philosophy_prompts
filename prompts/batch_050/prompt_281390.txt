For a graduate seminar on weakness of will, I want the specification in hand before my students evaluate any particular account. Write the desiderata a theory of akrasia must meet to count as an adequate theory of it: the phenomena it must explain (acting freely against one's own all-things-considered judgment, not merely acting and then regretting it); the cases it must get right (the Socratic puzzle about how such action is possible at all, temptation and addiction, cases where apparently akratic action turns out on reflection to be rational); the distinctions it must preserve (weakness of will versus a genuine change of mind, weakness versus compulsion, judgment versus intention); and the constraints it must respect (it may not simply deny the phenomenon's possibility without explaining away the appearances, and it must cohere with both a defensible philosophy of action and the empirical literature on preference reversal and hyperbolic discounting). Give the consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of particular camps. Then flag any pair or subset of desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — in particular, the tension between treating hyperbolic discounting as a full explanation of akrasia, which risks redescribing every case as rational preference change over time, and treating akrasia as genuinely irrational action, which is what the theory was supposed to be about. Say briefly which single requirement excludes the most contenders. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; score Davidson's, Holton's, and Ainslie's accounts against the list only briefly at the end, if at all.
