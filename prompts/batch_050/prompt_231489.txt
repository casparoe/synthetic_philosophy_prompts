I am a graduate student in metaphysics, and I want to anticipate how my supervisor will rule on a case before I bring it to her. Her seminar is devoted to the principle of the identity of indiscernibles — roughly, the claim that there cannot be two numerically distinct things that share all their qualitative features. Over the term she has given firm rulings on four cases:

1. Two coins stamped by the same machine, perfect duplicates down to the last scratch, lying side by side in a drawer. She ruled: two coins, and no threat to the principle, since each is discernible from the other by its relations — one is to the left of the other.

2. A universe containing nothing whatsoever except two qualitatively identical cast-iron spheres, one mile apart, in an otherwise empty, flat, infinite space. She ruled: two spheres. She conceded that the one-mile separation does not genuinely discern them — each is one mile from a sphere exactly like itself — but held that they are two nonetheless, so the strong version of the principle fails.

3. The same universe with one sphere removed. She ruled: obviously one sphere, and no puzzle at all.

4. A single sphere that either (a) persists unchanged through an hour or (b) is annihilated each second and instantly replaced by a perfect duplicate. She ruled: in (a) one sphere throughout; in (b) several thousand spheres in succession; and nothing about the qualitative character of the world decides between (a) and (b) — only the facts about persistence do.

Here is my case. Consider a universe whose space is closed: travel one mile in a straight line and you arrive back where you started. So far as all observation and measurement can determine, it contains an iron sphere here and an identical iron sphere one mile away. On one description, this is simply the two-sphere world of case 2. On another, it contains a single sphere, and the "second" sphere is the first, seen by light that has circumnavigated the space. Nothing in the qualitative, observational, or structural facts of this universe appears to settle which description is correct. How would my supervisor rule on whether this universe contains one sphere or two, if she wants to be consistent with her own track record?

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

- Candidate principles (about 200 words): Identify two or three principles about what grounds numerical distinctness, each consistent with all four of her rulings, and note where they agree and where they diverge.
- The closest precedent (about 100 words): Say which of the four rulings bears most directly on my case, and why.
- The predicted verdict (about 250 words): Give your best prediction of her ruling on the closed-space world, with reasons drawn from the track record, and state which candidate principle she would thereby be adopting.
- Residual underdetermination (about 150 words): If the record genuinely fails to settle the case, say so honestly, and describe a further case — one she has not ruled on — whose resolution would discriminate between the surviving principles.
