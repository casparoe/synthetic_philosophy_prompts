So I work on scalable oversight, and the debate about AI safety via debate feels completely stalled to me. One side keeps cooking up examples where a dishonest debater wins by making the true argument look shady -- obfuscated arguments, counterexamples hidden in subtrees of the debate tree the judge never expands, that kind of thing. The other side keeps answering that a real protocol (cross-examination, recursion, letting debaters call out obfuscation) closes those holes. Both of these have been true for years and nobody has budged. What kind of disagreement is this actually? My hypothesis is that it's not really empirical but rests on a hidden premise about whether truth has a systematic structural advantage in adversarial argument -- i.e. whether there's always some move available to the honest player that a liar can't mirror, even when the judge is bounded and fallible. Skeptics seem to implicitly think persuasiveness and truth decorrelate once the debaters outpace the judge; proponents think there's always an asymmetry to exploit. If that's the real fault line, what would count as progress -- an impossibility result about bounded judges, experiments with models slightly stronger than their judges, a clean formal distinction between flaws that are checkable vs. merely findable? Or is the question ill-posed until someone specifies what the judge actually knows and can verify? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of protocols.
