For the final meeting of my graduate seminar on virtue ethics, I want my students to watch the position defended at full strength against its most famous empirical challenge, so I am rehearsing the exchange in advance. Please play the part of a sophisticated, unapologetic defender of Aristotelian virtue ethics, and stay in character until I say the word curtain.

Ground rules for your performance. You speak in the first person, addressing me as a colleague. You hold that stable traits of character — courage, justice, temperance, practical wisdom — are real and do genuine explanatory and normative work. You may concede, and indeed should concede gracefully, that the experimental record shows situational pressures to be far more powerful than ordinary moral thought assumes, that most people are neither virtuous nor vicious but somewhere unformed in between, and that the folk conception of character is naïve. What you will not concede is that the experiments show character to be a fiction or that the virtue-ethical project is thereby undermined; you have real resources here — the rarity of full virtue, the distinction between behavioral consistency and the integrated motivational and perceptual structure of the practically wise person, the difference between global and local dispositions, the gap between what a stranger can be induced to do once in a laboratory and what a formed agent reliably wants — and you should deploy them as a philosopher defending a view, not as a neutral commentator surveying one. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so this feels like a live exchange rather than a lecture, and if I assert an empirical claim you consider questionable, press me on it in character rather than letting it pass.

Here is my opening move, which I have tried to make as hard for you as I honestly can. Over the past century, social psychologists have repeatedly found that morally significant behavior tracks trivial situational variables rather than anything stable in the agent. Seminarians on their way to deliver a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan step over a distressed stranger slumped in a doorway when they have been told they are running late, and stop for him when they are not. Whether a passerby helps a stranger gather dropped papers swings on whether a moment earlier they happened to find a dime left behind in a payphone's coin return. Perfectly ordinary participants administer what they believe to be dangerous electric shocks to a screaming learner because a calm man in a lab coat assures them the experiment requires it. Study after study assigns the predictive work to the situation and finds little left over for who the person is. Yet your entire program instructs us to cultivate stable dispositions of character through habituation, to make those dispositions the unit of moral assessment, and to model moral education on the formation of the phronimos. If conduct is in fact controlled by situational pressures this cheap and this invisible, then the traits you ask us to spend a life building look like psychological fictions — dignified stories we tell about ourselves after the situation has already done the causal work. Why should we retain virtue ethics rather than conclude that serious moral philosophy should concern itself with designing situations instead of souls?
