I'm on the dining services committee at my university (about 9,000 students, one big dining contractor). We're voting in two weeks on whether to drop beef and lamb from all campus menus and go chicken/plant-based for the hot entrees. The sustainability office pushed this on emissions grounds.

Here's the wrinkle. One of the committee members, an econ lecturer named David, circulated a one-pager last week arguing we should vote no, and honestly it's better argued than anything the sustainability office has sent us. His core point: our beef purchases are a rounding error in the national market, so whether we buy or not, the same number of cattle get raised and slaughtered, and the same emissions happen. The switch changes nothing in the world, it just costs us (the contractor quoted roughly an 8% price increase, and some students will be unhappy). He says symbolic gestures are fine for individuals who get warm fuzzies from them, but a committee stewarding a budget doesn't get to spend other people's money on warm fuzzies. And then -- this is the part that made me laugh and then stop laughing -- he added that since the vote will probably be something like 6-1 either way, his own vote won't change the outcome, so he's planning to abstain, by his own logic.

I want to vote yes, but I don't want to win on vibes. Draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the committee before the vote. It should:

- Give a clear recommendation (yes/no/yes-with-conditions) on both the menu change and, if you think it needs addressing, David's abstention.
- Take his argument apart fairly. Don't just assert "every little bit helps" -- engage with the actual mechanism, i.e. whether small purchasers really have zero effect on supply, including the possibility that suppliers respond in lumpy thresholds rather than smoothly, and what that does to the expected impact of our decision even if we can't tell ex ante where the threshold is.
- Say something honest about the case where he's right that our specific purchase changes nothing: is there still a reason for an institution, as opposed to an individual, to act? (complicity, norms, what the university is for, whatever you think survives scrutiny)
- Address the abstention move directly, because if his logic defeats the menu change it also seems to defeat his voting no on it, and if it doesn't defeat his voting then maybe it doesn't defeat the menu change either.
- Include the strongest version of his side you'd want on record if the opinion loses, so the minority report doesn't misrepresent him.

Keep it under 900 words, committe-appropriate tone, no academic name-dropping -- half the committee is facilities and catering staff and they'll tune out if it reads like a philosphy seminar.
