Last week over beers I got into it with two friends — one does contract law, one studied philosophy — about why promises actually bind, and we just kept circling the same three positions without anyone budging. There's the convention/practice story in the spirit of Hume and Rawls: promising is a social institution that only works if participants play along, so you ought to keep your promise for roughly the same reason you shouldn't cheat at chess. There's the expectation story associated with Scanlon-style views: when you promise, you deliberately give someone a reason to expect performance, and it would be wrong to let them down having knowingly created that expectation. And then there's the normative-powers camp, which says promising is simply the exercise of a capacity we have to create obligations at will, the way signing a deed transfers property. Every camp has a stock counterexample against the others — the promise made to a dying hermit that no one will ever learn about, where no expectations survive and no practice is harmed (bad news for the first two stories), or cases where breaking the promise would actually produce better overall expectations, or the worry that the normative-powers view just redescribes the datum instead of explaining it.

My working hypothesis is that the debate looks stalled because the sides are answering different questions under one label: one explains why the institution exists, one explains when and how breach wrongs the promisee, and one just restates that promises obligate. Is that right, or is the disagreement genuinely substantive — do the camps differ about which cases count as data (intuitions about weird thought experiments vs how the actual practice works), about standards of what an explanation of a norm has to do, or about starting intuitions nobody can argue for? And is there maybe a hidden empirical question doing the work, like what promisors and promisees actually understand themselves to be doing? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey and not a verdict about which theory wins — engage with what each side actually says, including the strongest version of each rather than the easy targets. And tell me what progress would even look like given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, a piece of evidence that could settle part of it, a changed question — or an argument that the impasse is permanent and why that wouldn't be a scandal.
