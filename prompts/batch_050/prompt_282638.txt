I had a long argument with a friend last night about Mill's The Subjection of Women, and although it ended amicably I came away with the nagging sense that I had lost to something other than a position. Mill's claim, in rough paraphrase, is that what passes for the nature of women is largely an artificial product of the circumstances in which women have been raised and confined, so that existing social arrangements cannot be justified by appeal to a nature those arrangements themselves produced. My friend defended the appeal to nature; I pressed Mill's objection. What I want to ask about, though, is not who was right. It is a pattern in how my friend's side of the argument behaved — one I only saw clearly afterward — and I suspect it is common enough to have a name.

The pattern had, so to speak, two settings. Whenever some observable fact fit his picture — the sex composition of a particular profession, an average difference in temperament or preference — he cited it as straightforward evidence about what men and women naturally are: the world is telling us something, and social arrangements merely register it. But whenever I argued that the observation was contaminated, that we have never once observed women or men outside arrangements that push them toward precisely those outcomes, he did not defend the inference. He shifted to a different claim: that since nobody can know what the sexes are like independently of upbringing and expectation, the question of nature is unanswerable, and that an unanswerable question is itself a reason to leave existing arrangements as they are. So nature was legible when the evidence suited him and illegible when the objection landed — and I noticed that the illegibility was never treated as neutral. Ignorance about nature, in his hands, always counted in favor of the status quo, never against it. I should add that none of this seemed deliberate; he appeared to experience himself as holding one steady view throughout.

The nearest patterns I already have names for do not quite fit. A simple appeal to nature — inferring how things ought to be from how they supposedly are — describes only the first setting and misses the oscillation. A motte-and-bailey, where someone alternates between a bold claim and a timid one, is closer, but in the cases I have heard discussed the timid position is a retreat, held only until the challenge passes, whereas here the modest claim that nature is unknowable was doing positive argumentative work: it was recruited as a premise for keeping things as they are, not merely as shelter. So: is there an established name for this pattern, in rhetoric, informal logic, or anywhere else? If there are several near neighbors, how exactly does what I have described differ from each of them, and which fits best? And if nothing fits, I would rather have a coined term with a real definition than a forced identification: necessary and sufficient conditions, whether the asymmetry of the ignorance (its counting only for one side) is essential to the pattern or merely typical, and a few boundary cases — for instance, whether the same pattern occurs when the inscrutable thing appealed to is not nature but tradition, or divine intention, or what an unregulated market would produce, and whether it still counts when the speaker is perfectly sincere, as I believe my friend was. A bare label without the carving would not answer my question.
