I teach metaphysics at a small liberal-arts college, and I am designing the final session of my seminar on verbal disputes and metaontology. I want to anchor the session in the record of a colleague who has spent her career sorting famous philosophical disputes into the merely verbal and the genuinely substantive. Her published and classroom verdicts on six cases are as follows:

First, two hikers argue about whether a tree falling in an unobserved forest makes a sound, one meaning an auditory experience by "sound" and the other a pattern of pressure waves. Her verdict: merely verbal. Second, astronomers and laypeople argue about whether Pluto is a planet, with all parties agreeing on every orbital and physical fact. Verdict: merely verbal. Third, two diners argue about whether a hot dog is a sandwich. Verdict: merely verbal. Fourth, a compatibilist and an incompatibilist argue about whether anyone has free will. Verdict: substantive. Fifth, two metaphysicians argue about whether the gradually rebuilt Ship of Theseus remains numerically the same ship, both granting every fact about its planks, provenance, and history. Verdict: substantive. Sixth, two philosophers argue about whether a statue is identical to the lump of clay that composes it, agreeing on all the physical and historical facts. Verdict: substantive.

The case for the final session is this: a platonist and a nominalist, both fully informed about mathematical practice and both competent speakers of the shared language, argue about whether numbers exist. The platonist holds that abstract objects belong to reality's basic inventory; the nominalist denies this while cheerfully asserting, in ordinary mathematical contexts, that there are infinitely many primes.

I am less interested in the correct classification than in reconstructing my colleague's method. Please structure your response in four sections, in this order: "Her likely verdict" (roughly 80–120 words: a prediction, plainly stated); "Candidate rules" (roughly 250–300 words: state two or three principles each consistent with all six verdicts, show the fit, and give each rule's prediction for the numbers dispute, noting also which natural-seeming rule her record already eliminates); "The best guess and its grounds" (roughly 150–200 words: which rule she is most plausibly running, and why it should be preferred over its rivals); and "The case that would settle it" (roughly 80–120 words: one further dispute, described concretely, on which the surviving candidate rules diverge, so that her verdict on it would reveal her principle).
