I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of action (14 students, one 75-minute session). Design a structured in-class activity that makes students feel, in their own case, the tension between deliberating about what to do and predicting what they will do — the sense in which treating your own future action as an object of prediction seems to crowd out treating it as a question to be settled by reasons.

The activity should work roughly as follows in spirit (improve on this skeleton as you see fit): students face a sequence of small, real decisions with modest stakes (e.g., points convertible into a trivial prize, snack allocation, or exemption from a minor course chore), and at various points they are asked to write down a probability that they themselves will choose a particular option before they choose. I want the design to engineer at least three distinct moments:

1. A moment where prediction and deliberation coincide comfortably — where stating a probability about one's own upcoming choice feels like just another way of making up one's mind, so students can later be shown that the tension is not omnipresent.
2. A moment where prediction becomes genuinely self-undermining or self-fulfilling — where the payoff structure means that whatever probability a student assigns changes what it is rational for them to do, so that honest prediction becomes unstable and they feel the floor give way.
3. A moment of asymmetry — where predicting another student's choice in an identical decision problem is easy and natural, while predicting one's own is not, so that the first-person/third-person gap shows up as an experience rather than as a claim on the board.

Specify the rules precisely enough that I could run the session from your document alone: the exact decision problems, the payoff scheme (no real money; points should map onto something students mildly care about), the timing and order of predictions versus choices, whether predictions are sealed or announced, and how to keep students from coordinating or gaming the setup. Include at least one rule whose purpose is not obvious and that you explain only in the facilitator notes.

Then provide a debrief plan: questions that connect each of the three moments to the underlying philosophical issue (e.g., whether deliberation requires treating one's own action as open in a way prediction forecloses; whether the instability in moment 2 shows that some probabilities about one's own actions are unknowable in principle or merely awkward; whether the asymmetry in moment 3 supports the idea that agency involves a distinctive first-person stance). Students should encounter the problem through the activity before hearing any vocabulary, so indicate where in the session terms like deliberation, prediction, and the first-person perspective are allowed to appear.

Finally, tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (including at least one failure mode specific to students who have taken a course covering Newcomb-style problems and will try to perform sophistication), and which feature of the philosophical problem the activity structurally cannot reproduce — in particular, whether a game with stipulated payoffs can really capture the case that matters most, namely weighty decisions where the reasons are not manufactured by an instructor.
