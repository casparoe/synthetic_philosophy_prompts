Next month I'm giving a colloquium talk in a philosophy department where I'll defend the claim that liquid markets are basically our best epistemic technology -- that when a question has a market on it, the price is a better guide to the truth than any panel of experts, and institutions should defer to prices the way they currently defer to peer review. My argument leans on the idea that traders with private information are paid to reveal it, so prices end up pooling dispersed knowledge no individual holds.

The audience: mostly epistemologists (a few formal ones who know their Bayesianism, several social epistemologists who work on peer disagreement), plus two behavioral economists who were invited because the department chair thought it would be fun, and one grumpy financial economist. Nobody there works on prediction markets specifically, but the formal people will definitely know the no-trade theorems.

A few things I specifically need covered:

- The Grossman-Stiglitz style worry that prices can't be fully revealing or nobody gets compensated for gathering information, so there's a built-in limit to how much the price can know.
- Something the social epistemologists will raise about peer disagreement -- if two equally informed rational people can persistently disagree, why think a market settles anything rather than just averaging the disagreement.
- Whatever the behavioral economists throw at me (overconfidence, noise traders, bubbles). I assume they'll be harder on the rationality premise than the philosophers will.
