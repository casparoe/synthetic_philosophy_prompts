I have been working through Chapter IV of Charles Darwin's The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871), where Darwin considers how conscience could have arisen from social instincts. He writes:

"In the same manner as various animals have some sense of beauty, though they admire widely different objects, so they might have a sense of right and wrong, though led by it to follow widely different lines of conduct. If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering. Nevertheless, the bee, or any other social animal, would gain in our supposed case, as it appears to me, some feeling of right or wrong, or a conscience."

I have built the following argument on this passage, and I am suspicious of it precisely because the conclusion seems to ruin the chapter it came from.

(1) On Darwin's account, conscience is fully constituted by social instincts together with reflection on the conflict between enduring and transient impulses; there is no further moral faculty behind it.

(2) The content of a creature's duties — which acts it recognises as sacred duties — is therefore wholly fixed by whichever instincts selection happened to favour in its lineage.

(3) Hence, had our evolutionary history matched that of the hive-bees, the killing of brothers and fertile daughters would have been duties in precisely the same sense in which parental care is a duty for us.

(4) If which acts are duties depends wholesale upon contingent evolutionary history, then no act is a duty simpliciter; the word "duty" can only ever mean "what our instincts prompt us to approve of".

(5) But Darwin himself plainly does not use the word in this deflated way. Within the passage itself he says only that the bee-reared humans would think fratricide a sacred duty, which presupposes a gap between what is thought a duty and what is one; and he opens the chapter by describing the moral sense as the most noble of man's attributes, language that sits very oddly with a theory on which moral judgement is merely the residue of local instincts.

(6) Darwin's account therefore either proves too much — it debunks its own subject matter and cannot support the supremacy he claims for the moral sense — or it quietly presupposes a standard of duty independent of the very instincts it says exhaust morality.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would like to keep (2), since the historical contingency of moral content seems to me the passage's whole point, and I would like to resist the conclusion that Darwin debunks himself. But I suspect the cost of doing so is hidden somewhere between (3) and (5). Please identify the step or premise you think should be rejected, explain why it looked plausible given the text, and set out what each of the available repairs would commit me to — including the repair that concedes the argument and simply accepts the relativist reading of Darwin.
