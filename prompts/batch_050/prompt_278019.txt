So I'm a software dev, zero philosophy training, and I've been reading Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" (it's in his Four Dissertations) because I got into a dumb argument about whether "good code" is objective and someone told me to read it. I hit this passage early on and I can't quite square it with the rest of the essay:

"There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliant: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions. In all matters of opinion and science, the case is opposite: The difference among men is there oftner found to lie in generals than in particulars; and to be less in reality than in appearance."

Here's what's confusing me. He says in science people fight over general claims but the disagreement is fake and dissolves once you define your terms, whereas in taste people agree on the general words ("elegant", "affected") but the disagreement is *real* and hides underneath. That seems backwards from my experience — when two engineers agree "code should be readable" but then fight for 200 comments on a PR, it feels like there IS a real underlying disagreement, same as the critics. So is Hume's generals/particulars distinction doing actual work here or is it just a linguistic observation? And the bigger thing: isn't he undercutting himself? If everyone secretly means something different by "elegant", that sounds like an arguement *against* there being a standard of taste, but he spends the rest of the essay arguing there is one. How does he get from this passage to that conclusion without contradicting himself — or does he?
