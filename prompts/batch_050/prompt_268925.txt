I teach a third-year undergraduate course on later Wittgenstein, and for next year's syllabus I need to design the two-week unit on the private language argument (roughly §§243–315 of the Investigations, though I do not expect students to have read all of it). My students will have completed one prior course in philosophy of language and will arrive having absorbed, secondhand, the vague idea that Wittgenstein "showed private languages are impossible." I want a teaching plan, not an explanation of the argument itself — I know the material; what I need is the pedagogy thought through.

Specifically, I want the plan to settle and justify the following:

1. The order of ideas. There is a genuine fork here: one can enter through the rule-following considerations and treat the sensation diary as a downstream application, or enter directly through the diary at §258 and let the rule-following material emerge from it. State which order you would use and why, identifying what each stage depends on — i.e., what must already be in place for the next stage to land, and what goes wrong if a stage is skipped or reversed.

2. The carrying example. The diary case at §258 is the canonical text, but in my experience students find marking a calendar too thin to feel the pull of the private linguist's position. Either defend the diary as the carrier or design a better example (a made-up case is fine — say, someone tracking a recurring twinge to report it to a physician, or a meditator developing a private taxonomy of mental states), and specify what the example must contain — a putative ostensive definition, a practice of reidentification over time, a distinction the subject insists on between seeming right and being right — and what it must not contain if it is not to preempt the argument's conclusion.

3. The misconception map. Tell me which misunderstandings the students will predictably form, and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected afterward. I anticipate at least these, but correct me or add to the list: that the argument is simply verificationism in disguise; that it rests on the fallibility of memory; that it is behaviorism about sensation; and that it shows there is no "inner" at all, rather than something narrower about what could count as a language of it. For each, say whether it is a misreading students bring in or one the argument itself invites if taught in the wrong order.

4. A check for understanding: one question or short exercise I can pose at the end of the second week that would discriminate students who have grasped the argument from students who have merely learned to recite "no criterion of correctness."

5. What to leave out explicitly at this level — I assume the Kripkean skeptical reading and the community-versus-individual debate are candidates — and how to flag the omission honestly without making the students feel they have been given a simplified toy.

Because the sequencing claims here are judgments about how misunderstanding works rather than provable conclusions, end the plan by stating where its reasoning is most likely to fail: which pedagogical choice is most contestable, and what evidence about how these students actually stumble — the sort of thing I could observe in the first week — should lead me to reorder or redesign it.
