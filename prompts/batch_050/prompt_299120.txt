i'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book whose main character, clare, is a talented illustrator who has spent four years not starting the graphic novel she keeps telling everyone (and herself) she'll begin once things settle down. i'm trying to get the psychology of her stalling right rather than just gesturing at laziness, and i've fallen down a rabbit hole about weakness of will.

could you compare two ways of understanding what's going on in clare's head: (1) the traditional philosophical picture of akrasia, where she genuinely judges that sitting down to work today is what she has most reason to do, and yet doesn't do it; and (2) the behavioural-economics picture of hyperbolic discounting, where her preference for starting is perfectly stable while the start date is far away, but flips as the day approaches, because the immediate cost of opening the sketchbook looms larger and larger relative to the distant rewards. what i'm really after is whether (2) explains (1) or quietly dissolves it. if her preferences genuinely reverse as the deadline nears, is she failing to act on her best judgement at all, or just changing her mind on a predictable schedule? does anything remain of the weakness in weakness of will once the discount curve is doing all the work?

this matters practically, not just theoretically. my first draft wrote clare's interiority as a kind of courtroom: reason delivers its verdict, desire overrules it, and she watches herself disobey. but lately i keep wondering if the truthful version is more like an internal marketplace, where future clare keeps getting sold off cheap by present clare, and where resolutions only ever stick if she can find ways to bind her later self — deadlines imposed by other people, public commitments, deleting the apps, that sort of thing. which of the two pictures gives me the better machinery for writing her inner monologue? and is the courtroom picture just the marketplace picture as seen from the morning after, when the losing bidder has sobered up?

two more questions, if you can bear them. first, if hyperbolic discounting does dissolve classic akrasia, is there any residue left over — some way a person can act against her own current, considered judgement rather than merely against the judgement of an earlier self — that a novelist should definately keep in the character? second, is there a risk that the discount-curve story flatters people like clare, making procrastination look like rational time-inconsistency rather than the failure of anything at all, and would a serious book have to push back against that? feel free to be opinionated throughout — i want a view i can argue with, not a survey.
