Our philosophy society puts out a satirical zine each term, and this issue's theme is "great villains of the canon, given a fair hearing." I got assigned the Grand Inquisitor from Book V, chapter 5 of The Brothers Karamazov, and instead of writing a straight essay I want to run him as a piece of modern bureaucratic communication: an internal all-staff memo (or newsletter, whatever fits) from the Office of the Grand Inquisitor in Seville, sent the morning after the incident in the cathedral square -- the arrest of the silent Visitor, the late-night interview in the cell, and the Visitor's release. Roughly 600-700 words, with a subject line, a couple of section headings, maybe a bullet-point action items list at the end.

The thing that makes it funny (I hope) is that the memo never breaks character and never concedes anything. The Grand Inquisitor genuinely believes he is the last humanitarian on earth, so the memo should run his actual argument in HR-speak: freedom is a burden the flock was never strong enough to carry; people will always end up laying their freedom at someone's feet in exchange for bread and certainty; miracle, mystery, and authority are simply the three pillars of any functioning organization; the millions get to be happy precisely because the unhappy few take on the terrible responsibility of deceiving them. The comedy should come from the collision between that argument and corporate language -- the auto-da-fé described as a staff reduction event, the Visitor's silent kiss logged as a non-verbal acknowledgment of receipt, the release processed as an exception granted out of policy. But he has to stay *sincere* the whole way through: no twirling mustache, no moment where he admits it's all power for its own sake, because that's the one reading that lets the reader off the hook. If anything he should come across as quietly exhausted and hurt that nobody appreciates the sacrifice.

One more constraint: I don't want the satire to depend on cheap anachronisms (no jokes about wifi or synergy for their own sake), every joke should land on something that's actually in the chapter. And at the end, out of character, add a short note -- maybe 100 words -- flagging where the piece took interpretive liberties: e.g. whether your Inquisitor is a secret atheist or a believer who lost his faith in people rather than God, whether he's lying to the prisoner or to himself, and which contested reading of the chapter would, if it turned out right, force the biggest rewrite of the memo. I'd rather know where the satire is most likely to be unfair than have it pretend to be neutral.
