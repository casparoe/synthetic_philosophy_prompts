I'm a health economist, and last month an ethics committee reviewing one of my cost-effectiveness models put me on the spot with a question I answered badly. My model implied, as such models do, that a sufficiently large number of averted minor harms — brief headaches, mild nausea, a day of lost sleep — could outweigh a single death or a single case of severe suffering, since everything sits on one cardinal scale and aggregates. A committee member asked whether I really believed that, and gestured at the well-known thought experiment comparing one person undergoing decades of excruciating torture to an astronomical number of people each receiving a single dust speck in the eye. I mumbled something about threshold effects, but the truth is I don't know how the philosophical debate around this case maps onto the formal machinery I actually think with: cardinal utility, separability across persons, lexicographic orderings, incomplete preferences, vague thresholds, transitivity of pairwise comparisons. That machinery is what I'm fluent in — more fluent than in the vocabulary of moral philosophy.

So here is my request. Take the dust-specks-versus-torture debate and restate it systematically in the vocabulary of welfare economics and decision theory, piece by piece. In particular: render the "additive aggregation" verdict as a claim about a cardinal, separable social welfare function; render the "lexical threshold" verdict as a lexicographic ordering over kinds of harm; render the spectrum argument (each step down the sequence of harms looks negligible, so by transitivity the conclusion follows) as a claim about a chain of pairwise comparisons and what must give for it to fail; and render the vague-threshold and incommensurability positions in whatever formal vocabulary fits them best, even if that vocabulary is non-standard. For each rendering, tell me explicitly whether the translation is clean, distorted, or impossible — and where it is distorted, name exactly what was bent.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What claims or intuitions in the philosophical debate have no expression in this vocabulary at all? I have my suspicions — for instance, whether the relevant distinction is between harms aggregated within one life versus across lives, whether the phenomenological character of torture resists placement on any common scale with trivial harms, and whether the indeterminacy of the threshold is epistemic or constitutive — but I want these assessed, not assumed. End with a verdict on the residue: does it point to something genuine that welfare economics cannot say (and if so, does that indict the framework for practical purposes like mine), or does it reveal that the philosophical debate was trading on fuzziness that the formalization usefully exposes? Please don't quietly import my framework's commitments and present them as translations — I want to be able to see the seams.
