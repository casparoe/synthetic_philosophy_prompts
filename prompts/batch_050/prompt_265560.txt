I am considering a strong open-borders position: every competent adult has a defeasible right to enter, reside, and work in any state; border agents may verify identity and exclude people posing a credible, individualized threat of serious harm, but may not exclude anyone because of expected fiscal costs, labor-market competition, cultural difference, or effects on social trust. What would count against this position? Distinguish objections to the claimed right from objections to implementing it in an otherwise closed-border world, and assess cases involving institutional overload, migration-induced political backlash, loss of the administrative capacity needed to enforce the right, and unequal burdens on particular localities. Which findings or scenarios should be decisive rather than merely awkward, and which would a defender reasonably treat as calling for compensation, sequencing, or temporary safeguards rather than abandoning the position? Please also say whether the position makes any risky empirical prediction that would differentiate it from a more conventional presumptive-freedom view, or whether its qualifications leave it insulated from refutation.
