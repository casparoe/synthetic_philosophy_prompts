For a term paper in my philosophy elective, I am writing about arms races in which every participant's individually sensible choice leaves the whole group worse off, and no one can safely be the first to stop. My central example is my own school's sleep schedule. Almost everyone I know studies past midnight, and most of us would rather not. But colleges evaluate us relative to one another, so the student who unilaterally starts sleeping eight hours does not become well-rested and happy; she becomes well-rested and outcompeted. My paper's organizing claim is a distinction I have not encountered in my reading, and I would like a probability estimate on whether it survives scrutiny.

I propose that these traps come in two kinds. Call the first a hollow trap: no one benefits from the bad equilibrium itself, everyone would prefer the escape if everyone escaped together, and the trap persists solely because of the risk of moving first and the difficulty of confirming that others have moved too. Call the second a trophy trap: some party profits from the status quo and would actively work to restore it if the participants tried to coordinate their way out. In my school example, candidate profiteers might be tutoring companies, or selective colleges whose applicant pool becomes ever more differentiated by extreme effort.

My conjecture is that this distinction is not merely descriptive but predictive: hollow traps can be dissolved by the participants themselves, through a mutual commitment that is public, cheap to verify, and covers enough of the group (something like a widely signed pledge to cap study hours), whereas trophy traps cannot be dissolved from inside and require an external enforcer with coercive power.

The precise proposition I want your credence on is this: for traps of the hollow type, a participant-designed mutual commitment mechanism that is public, cheap to verify, and initially adopted by a large majority of the group succeeds in moving the group to the better equilibrium, and holds it for a meaningful length of time, in at least half of real-world attempts. My own credence is about 0.35, and my teacher's is about 0.7.

Please give me your own number, and, more importantly, the decomposition behind it. The sub-questions I see are these. First, whether the hollow-versus-trophy distinction tracks a genuine causal difference or is just a relabeling of how difficult each trap feels. Second, whether verification cost really is the binding constraint in hollow traps, or whether it is usually dominated by other problems, such as genuine heterogeneity among participants (some students truly want to study longer) and the temptation to defect secretly. Third, whether there is a selection effect: perhaps pledges only get attempted in groups already close to escaping, which would inflate observed success rates. Fourth, whether a dissolved trap simply reconstitutes on a neighboring dimension — if study hours are capped, does the competition migrate to extracurriculars, leaving nothing gained.

Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, explain how they combine into your final credence, identify which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and tell me what evidence or argument would move it most. Finally, given the decomposition, where does the disagreement between my 0.35 and my teacher's 0.7 most likely live?
