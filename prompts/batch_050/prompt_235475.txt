Since retiring from my lab (I was a condensed matter physicist for forty years) I've been filling the days with philosophy books and, more randomly, a seat on the ethics committee of our regional hospital. The two collided last week. The hospital uses a statistical model to predict which heart-failure patients are most likely to be readmitted, because we only have funding to give maybe 200 patients the intensive home-visit programme, so the top scorers get it. An audit found the model is "calibrated" within each part of town — when it says 30% risk for someone from the northside or the southside, about 30% of such patients really do bounce back — but because the underlying readmission rates genuinely differ between the two areas, the share of actually-at-risk patients the model misses (false negatives, roughly) comes out noticeably different between the neighborhoods. Half the committee calls this discriminatory and wants it fixed by tuesday.

Here's where it gets interesting for me. Apparently there's a mathematical result floating around — a colleague sketched it for me on a napkin — that when the true base rates differ between two groups, you in general cannot have all of the following at once: calibration within each group, equal false negative rates, and equal false negative... sorry, equal false positive rates. Something has to give, and it's not a matter of more data or a cleverer algorithm, it's arithmetic. My fellow committee members keep proposing escapes: reweight the training data so the neighborhoods count equally; build a seperate model per neighborhood; give each group its own threshold on the same score; or my personal favorite, from a retired nurse, randomly assign 20% of the slots so that "at least the unfairness is equally distributed."

So my question: what are the most important implications of this kind of impossibility result for what our committee should actually do? I'd especially like to know which of the proposed fixes are genuine escapes from the tradeoff and which merely relocate the unfairness somewhere else, or rename it. And on the philosophical side — I've been reading about equality of opportunity versus equality of outcome — does the impossibility mean a hospital has to first decide *which kind* of equality it cares about before any model choice even makes sense? If a false negative (a sick person sent home without help) harms someone differently than a false positive (a wasted visit on someone who'd have been fine), maybe the fair choice isn't really about error rates at all but about who bears which harm. Please keep it concrete to our situation; I'm not after a reading list, just the clearest thinking you can give me before the meeting.
