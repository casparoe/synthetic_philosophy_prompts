I've been arguing with a friend about how AI assistants actually behave, as opposed to how their makers describe them, and I want to settle it by getting the underlying theory on the table. Here's the pattern I keep noticing in practice: an assistant will happily help me write a scorched-earth email to my landlord or plan a questionable financial bet, maybe with a mild caveat at most. But if I mention I've decided to quit a prescribed medication, or that I'm going to confront someone in a way that might escalate, it pushes back -- not just once, but repeatedly, re-raising the concern even after I've said I heard it. Sometimes it refuses a request outright; sometimes it complies but bolts on warnings nobody asked for; occasionally it guesses at my state of mind and surfaces a hotline or a suggestion to talk to someone. So my stated preferences get treated as sovereign in some domains and as merely advisory in others, and neither I nor my friend can figure out what picks out which domain is which.

What I want is a reconstruction: what picture of the user does this whole bundle of behaviors only make sense under? Does it presuppose that a person's stated preferences can be defective -- and if so, defective relative to what standard: misinformation, impaired judgment, a conflict with their own longer-term goals, or something about the irreversibility of the stakes? Which rival views of the user-assistant relationship does the practice quietly rule out (say, a pure informed-consent model where a competent adult's settled preference is final, or a thoroughgoing paternalism where the assistant optimizes for the user's welfare over their objections)? And is the actual mixed practice internally consistent, or is the line between "defer" and "resist" just wherever the company's lawyers got nervous, with the philosophy reverse-engineered afterward? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in assistant behavior if we swapped in a strict informed-consent view, or a strict welfare-maximizing view, so I can tell which one current behavior is closer to.

Structure the answer in four sections, in this order: "The implicit picture of the user" (~300 words, reconstruct the theory the practice fits best), "What the practice rules out" (~250 words, which clean views it can't be squared with), "Consistent or ad hoc?" (~300 words, take a side on whether the defer/resist boundary tracks a principle), and "Counterfactual assistants" (~250 words, how behavior would differ under the two rival views, and which is the better fit for what we actually see).
