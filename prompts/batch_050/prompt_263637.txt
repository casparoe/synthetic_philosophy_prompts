Ok so background first: I'm a retired condensed matter physicist, got into philosophy about a year ago via the rationalist blogs and then actual papers, and I've fallen down the rabbit hole of Aumann's agreement theorem. My reading group has asked each of us to write up where we stand so the others can attack it, so before I embarrass myself I want a consistency check on the following.

Here are my commitments, roughly in the order I care about them:

(1) Aumanns result is basically sound as a piece of math and as philosophy: if two rational agents share a prior and their posteriors are common knowledge, then they can't keep disagreeing. Same evidence in the deep sense means same conclusion.

(2) Real life basically never delivers common knowledge of posteriors, and that's the escape hatch that explains why rational disagreement between honest people persists all the time in practice.

(3) Me and an old colleauge from the lab have been arguing for about twenty years about whether our field's flagship theory was a dead end. I know all his arguments, he knows all mine, we've read the same papers, we've each explained exactly how confident we are and why, more times than I can count. Neither of us has budged much. And I maintain we are both being perfectly rational.

(4) When someone I take to be roughly my epistemic peer disagrees with me, that disagreement is itself evidence, and I should shift my own confidence at least somewhat in their direction. I hold this as a general principle, not just about this one argument.

(5) In this particular case there's no private evidence either of us is hoarding -- after twenty years of swapping papers and arguing over beers I'm confident our information pools overlap almost entirely, and each of us knows that about the other.

Now, I have a vague worry (2) is doing some sneaky work for me, letting me endorse the theorem in the abstract while excusing the one disagreement I actually care about. But I might be wrong that there's a real problem. So: go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like it, and rank the real ones by how bad the damage is. For each genuine tension, tell me the cheapest fix and what it costs. And don't just flag everything as contradictory to look thorough -- if two of these actually cohere fine, say so.

Then, since the group will ask me for a bottom line, finish with a machine-readable final line in the form \answer{...} containing the number of the single commitment I should revise first (or the word none if the whole set hangs together).
