I am a retired molecular biologist who, over the past two years, has been working my way through philosophy of mind with a reading group. Last week two members of the group — I'll call them Ruth and Gerald — got into a dispute about Molyneux's problem, and it ended inconclusively enough that I'd like an outside adjudication.

For context, the problem as I understand it: would a person blind from birth, upon gaining sight, be able to tell a cube from a sphere by vision alone, without touching them? Ruth and Gerald both knew of research, reported within the last couple of decades, involving patients in whom congenital cataracts were removed or corneal damage repaired, and who shortly after surgery performed poorly when asked to match shapes across touch and vision, though they improved within days or weeks.

Gerald opened by declaring the question settled: the empirical answer is "no." The newly sighted patients fail the matching task, so tactile and visual shape representations are built separately and only become unified through learned association. Molyneux asked a factual question, and facts have now answered it.

Ruth replied that the experiments settle nothing of the sort. A patient whose visual system was deprived of input for decades will have a degraded cortex and blurry, disorganized images in the first days after surgery. Failure to match shapes under those conditions shows the visual input is poor, not that shape concepts are modality-bound. To test Molyneux's actual question you'd need a subject whose vision was immediately perfect, which no surgery can deliver — so Gerald's "settled" verdict rests on an artifact of the testing conditions. She added that the rapid recovery of cross-modal matching within days actually cuts against him: if the two modalities used wholly separate shape concepts, how could the link be forged so quickly? More plausibly there is a single underlying shape concept that merely needs recalibration to the new input channel.

Gerald's response was that Ruth had immunized the question against any possible evidence. If every failure is blamed on degraded input, and the required "pristine" conditions are physically impossible, then her version of Molyneux's question makes no testable prediction at all — and a question with no testable content isn't a deep conceptual puzzle, it's empty. On the learning point, he said she had it backwards: the fact that days of experience are needed is precisely evidence that the association is acquired rather than given; a genuinely shared concept would require no learning period at all.

Ruth's final move was to concede that his version of the question is testable but deny it is Molyneux's. The interesting question, she said, is whether shape concepts are constitutively tied to one sensory modality — a question about how concepts are individuated, which behavioral experiments can inform but never decide, because any matching task presupposes an interpretation of what the subject's success or failure expresses (conceptual capacity, as opposed to attention, confidence, or mere perceptual noise).

At that point dinner arrived and the argument was abandoned. I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it, and I noticed I keep flip-flopping depending on who spoke last.

Could you go through the exchange round by round and say who is right about each specific point: whether the cataract-removal studies bear on Molyneux's question at all; whether the initial matching failure or the rapid subsequent improvement is the more telling datum; whether Gerald is right that Ruth's position is untestable, and whether that would be fatal if he were; and whether Ruth is right that a conceptual residue survives any empirical result. Finally, is there anything important that both of them are missing — some distinction, assumption, or consideration that would dissolve or reframe the dispute?
