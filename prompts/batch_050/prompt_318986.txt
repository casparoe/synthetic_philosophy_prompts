I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I wrote this short essay trying to explain (mostly to myself) the daoist idea of wu-wei and the whole paradox of trying not to try. I'd like comments before I show it to anyone. Here's the essay:

---
Trying to not try sounds like a contradiction, but I think it's only a contradiction if you picture one agent doing one thing at one time. Software doesn't work like that and neither do people. When I practice piano scales, the practicing is effortful and deliberate, but the point of it is that later, at the recital, my fingers just go. The trying happened earlier so that the not-trying can happen now. Think of it like compile time and runtime: you do all the expensive work once, up front, so that the program runs cheap and fast later. Nobody says a compiler is paradoxical because it works hard so the program doesn't have to.

This, I think, is what the story of the cook who carves the ox is getting at. His blade stays sharp for years because he follows the natural seams instead of hacking. But he wasn't born doing that; the story itself says it took him years to get there. The effort was real, it was just spent in the right place and at the right time. So wu-wei isn't the absence of effort, it's effort that has been relocated -- from the moment of action into a long prior process of training, and from the conscious level down into habit. The paradox dissolves once you notice that "try" and "not try" are being predicated of different stages of the same system.

A bonus from this view: it explains why you can't skip the training. If someone tells you to "just be spontaneous," they're handing you a runtime instruction with no compile step, which is why that advice is useless and why forcing relaxation never works.
---
