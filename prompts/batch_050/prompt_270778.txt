I'm on the finance committee of a small community land trust in my town, and we've been fighting about the same thing for three meetings now and I want an outside opinion. The trust has an endowment, and the question is basically how much of it we should spend on stuff now (trail repairs, a new barn for the farm program, staff salaries) versus how much we should leave invested for the future. Our treasurer, who's an accountant, keeps saying that a dollar of benefit in 2075 is worth less than a dollar of benefit today, and that any responsible organization counts future benefits at a steep discount -- he has spreadsheets where things that happen fifty years from now barely register at all. He says this is just standard practice, not an opinion.

One of the other members, who is not a numbers person at all, got genuinely upset at the last meeting and said the people who will be using this land in 2075 are going to be just as real as we are, their needs will feel just as urgent to them as ours do to us, and treating their interests as worth a fraction of ours is just favoritism toward people who happen to be alive right now. She said if the trust is supposed to last forever, its decisions shouldn't have a built-in bias toward the present. The treasurer pushed back that it's not bias, it's arithmetic: money we don't spend gets invested and grows, so one dollar today can do what three dollars would have to do later, and we'd be fools to ignore that.

Here's what I can't untangle: it feels like both of them are right but also like they're talking past each other, because the treasurer's point about money growing seems like a real constraint, yet I'm not sure it actually answers her point -- she never denied that money grows, she denied that future people's wellbeing should count for less, and those seem like different claims that keep getting smushed together in the meeting. Am I right that there are two separate arguments being run as one? If so, which one should actually drive how we set our spending rate, and does the treasurer's spreadsheet quietly smuggle in a value judgment while wearing the costume of math?
