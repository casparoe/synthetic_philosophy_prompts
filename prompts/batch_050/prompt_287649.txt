I'm putting together a unit on the aggregation of harms for my undergrad normative ethics seminar -- we just finished the torture vs. dust specks stuff and my students (and, if I'm honest, my colleague across the hall who I was arguing with over coffee) pushed me on whether I actually have a consistent view or just a grab bag of intuitions. So let me lay out my gut verdicts on cases and you tell me what principle, if any, I'm committed to. (1) One person tortured horribly for fifty years versus any number of strangers each getting one dust speck in the eye: I save the one from torture, and I mean ANY number, the number doesn't matter. (2) Five strangers will die versus one will die, all else equal: save the five, obviously, numbers count here. (3) One person loses both legs versus a thousand people each losing a finger: I save the legs. (4) One person endures a week of severe flu versus ten billion people each enduring a single hour of a moderately bad headache: I find myself saying avert the headaches, which bothers me because an hour of headache is, what, maybe three dust specks' worth of bad, and if specks never aggregate up to a week of flu I don't see why headaches should. (5) One person dies at 40 versus a billion people each dying one week earlier than they otherwise would have: I go with the billion. (6) And here's an asymmetry I can't shake: one person suffering a hundred headaches over a year is clearly worse off than one person suffering one headache, harms aggregate just fine WITHIN a life, so I need the principle to explain what's supposed to change when the headaches are distributed across a hundred heads.

My tentative principle going in was something like: harms below some threshold of seriousness never aggregate, no matter the numbers, to outweigh a harm above the threshold -- but (4) and (5) seem to break that immediately, since a lost week of life and an hour of headache both feel speck-adjacent. So: state a principle (or two or three candidates) precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing work for which verdict. I want to know which of my six verdicts a coherent principle has to overturn -- I suspect it's either (1) or (4), but tell me. Tell me what the best candidate predicts for cases I haven't listed: everyone alive getting a papercut versus one person losing a hand; a million people with permanent low-grade background aches versus one person with a month of acute agony; and a case where the many each suffer a harm that is slightly MORE than half of what the one suffers. Also, is my within-a-life versus across-lives asymmetry in (6) defensible on any view that keeps most of the rest, or is it the tell that something's gone wrong? Please don't just describe the standard positions at me -- I want to know what *I* think, given what I've told you.
