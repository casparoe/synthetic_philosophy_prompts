I'm a PhD student in political theory and I keep running into the slogan "your vote is your voice" — most recently at a reading group where someone used it to shut down a defense of strategic lesser-evil voting, as in: if your vote is your voice, voting for a candidate you dislike is basically lying, so you should vote your conscience or not at all. That struck me as smuggling in a lot.

Then I came across this in Mill's Thoughts on Parliamentary Reform (1859), which muddied things further:

"The possession and the exercise of political, and among others of electoral, rights, is one of the chief instruments both of moral and of intellectual training for the popular mind; and all governments must be regarded as extremely imperfect, until every one who is required to obey the laws, has a voice, or the prospect of a voice, in their enactment and administration."

So here's what I want: lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims "your vote is your voice" could be making — from the trivial (voting transmits preference information) to the radical (voting is literally a speech act with sincerity conditions, so strategic voting is a kind of lie). For each reading give a verdict: trivial, defensible, or false. Then tell me (a) which readings would actually support my reading-group friend's anti-strategic move, and (b) whether the Mill passage helps or hurts that move — he seems to be giving an educative/developmental story about the vote rather than an expressive one, but I could be misreading.

Keep it under 450 words, not counting the Mill passage.
