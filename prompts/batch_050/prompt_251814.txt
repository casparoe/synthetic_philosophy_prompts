At a department mixer I overheard two graduate students, Nora and Eli, arguing about two drivers. Both drive home equally exhausted after identical late shifts, both know they are nodding off, both decide to continue; in one case a child steps from behind a van and is killed, in the other no one is there and the driver arrives home. Nora insists the drivers are morally identical: blame should track the quality of the choice and the risk knowingly imposed, since the child’s presence is outside either driver’s control, and punishing or despising the unlucky one more confuses evidence about what happened with desert. Eli answers that once someone has actually killed a child, new moral facts arrive: duties to confess, compensate, apologize, and submit to blame, and the parents are not irrational to hate that driver in a way they could not hate the mere risk imposer. Nora replies that those are causal and reparative consequences, not greater culpability; the lucky driver has the same vice and merely lacks a victim to repair. Eli says this turns morality into a private scorecard of intentions, whereas agency is answerability to a world that can make our choices terrible. They asked me to referee and I could not. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about outcome luck versus circumstantial luck, about the control principle, about the difference between blame, punishment, and duties of repair, and about the parents’ resentment; then say who is more right overall and what both are missing. Answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
