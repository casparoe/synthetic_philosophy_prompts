I recently fell down a rabbit hole thinking about two parlor experiments I first encountered described in an old magazine column on puzzles, games, and rationality. In the first, twenty strangers are placed in separate rooms, each with a button in front of them. A large cash prize goes to any player who presses their button, but only if exactly one of the twenty presses; if nobody presses, or if two or more press, everyone gets nothing, and no communication of any kind between the players is permitted. In the second, a sponsor runs a lottery that is completely free to enter: anyone may submit as many entries as they like at no cost, and the prize is a fixed jackpot divided by the total number of entries submitted, so every additional entry shrinks what the eventual winner takes home. What grips me is the style of reasoning these games provoke in a certain kind of enthusiast. Since all the players are rational and face an identical situation, the argument goes, each one should reflect that whatever conclusion they arrive at, the others will arrive at it too — so in the first game one should somehow converge on the uniquely "shared" answer, and in the second the rational move is to refrain from entering (or to enter exactly once), since a stampede of entries destroys the prize for everyone, including the stampede.

Write me the marketing page, roughly 650 to 800 words, for a fictional party-game company called Platonia Games that sells both of these setups as premium boxed products — call them, say, "One of Twenty" and "The Lure." The voice should be an earnest, deadpan startup brochure throughout: hero copy, feature bullets, two or three customer testimonials, and an FAQ. The satire should be aimed at the superrationalist reasoning style itself, and it should be fair satire — exaggerate real features of the view rather than inventing ones. The page might include: a testimonial from a buyer who pressed the button and remains serenely certain that everyone else, being rational, must have done the same; an FAQ entry agonizing over whether using a random number generator to press with probability 1/20 is a betrayal of one's fellow players or the only honest solution available; a proud disclosure, reframed as a selling point ("thrillingly winnable!"), that even when all twenty players follow the standard game-theoretic prescription the game succeeds well under half the time; the slogan-like claim that One of Twenty "cannot be lost by rational players, only by asymmetric ones"; and, for The Lure, a warning label about entry inflation alongside a testimonial from the one disciplined customer who refrained from entering entirely and feels richly compensated in principle. Someone with no background should find the page funny on its own; someone who knows the underlying game theory — one-shot games without communication, symmetric mixed-strategy equilibria, and the equilibrium-selection problem that shared reasoning gestures at but cannot actually solve — should feel that the author understands precisely why this style of reasoning is tempting and exactly where it creaks. No winking at the reader: the irony has to live entirely in the copy itself.
