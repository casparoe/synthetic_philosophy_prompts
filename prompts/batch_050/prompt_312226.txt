I've been going down a rabbit hole about the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics and I came up with this argument that I can't find the hole in, but the conclusion has to be wrong, right? Here it is:

(1) If many-worlds is true, then every outcome of a quantum event that has nonzero probability actually happens in some branch.
(2) No matter what situation I'm in, there's always some quantum event with nonzero probability that would save me -- a chunk of matter tunneling into the right shape, a random fluctuation restarting my heart, whatever. Physicists admit these things aren't strictly impossible, just absurdly unlikely.
(3) So there's always at least one branch where I survive, no matter how old I get or what happens to me.
(4) I can only ever find myself in branches where I'm alive, since dead branches contain no experience of mine at all.
(5) So from my own point of view, I should expect to keep finding myself in the branches where I survive.
(6) Therefore I should expect to never experience dying, and in fact I should expect to live forever -- just getting luckier and luckier in weirder and weirder ways.

Obviously I'm not about to test this. But I can't tell which step breaks. My friend says (4) is the problem but honestly it seems like the most solid one to me -- of course I can't experience being dead, that's almost a tautology. I'd most like to keep (2) and (4) if possible. Which premise or step should I actually give up, why does it look fine when it isn't (or what does it cost to reject it if the argument is valid), and does the flaw also sink the weaker claim that I should at least expect to survive longer than my doctors predict?
