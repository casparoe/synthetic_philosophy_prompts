I am presenting a paper at a philosophy colloquium next month defending what I call the affect-exhaustion thesis about pain's badness: drawing on pain asymbolia -- the condition, most often associated with damage to the insular cortex or produced by cingulotomy, in which patients report feeling pain, locate and describe it accurately, yet appear wholly untroubled by it -- I argue that everything bad about pain is exhausted by its affective-motivational dimension, and that the sensory-discriminative residue left over in asymbolic patients is not bad at all. On my view, asymbolic pain is evaluatively more like an itch or a tickle: unpleasant to no one, rationally ignorable, and not something a benevolent observer has any reason to eliminate. I support this with a test I call the disinterested guardian: imagine you hold proxy authority over an asymbolic patient and must decide whether to spare them a brief episode of their asymbolic pain at a small cost to their other interests; if you cannot justify paying anything to avoid it, the badness was never in the sensation.

The audience will be roughly ten value theorists and philosophers of well-being, several of whom are sympathetic to desire-based or objective-list accounts and will resist any purely hedonic picture of badness; two philosophers of perception who treat pain as fundamentally representational and will dislike my clean separation of components; and one neuropsychologist who knows the clinical literature and will, I suspect, press me on how tidy the dissociation really is -- on general emotional blunting in lesion patients, on morphine reports, on whether asymbolics genuinely retain the full sensory phenomenology.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections this specific audience will actually raise, phrased the way each group would phrase them, ordered by likelihood and by how much damage each would do to the paper. For each, give me a reply deliverable in two minutes, flag what I should concede openly and what I must hold, and note where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and a fallback framing for the disinterested guardian test if the room rejects it.
