A colleague who writes for a general audience has asked me to vet a passage from a draft article on experimental philosophy before it goes to press. I know this literature passably well but not well enough to mark the corrections myself with confidence, and I would value a careful audit. The passage reads:

"Knobe's 2003 experiment presented subjects with a chairman who, told that a new program would increase profits but also harm the environment, replied that he did not care at all about the environment and started the program. Most subjects said he intentionally harmed the environment; in a parallel case where the program happened to help the environment, most said he did not intentionally help it. Knobe concluded that the asymmetry arises because the folk concept of intentional action is itself evaluative: whether a foreseen side effect counts as intended depends on whether it is good or bad. This interpretation is now the consensus among experimental philosophers, the principal alternative — that subjects say 'intentionally' merely to signal that the chairman deserves blame, without really meaning it — having been ruled out by later experiments. The result therefore shows that there is no purely descriptive, value-free fact about what anyone intends, and it is widely regarded as having undermined the traditional project of analyzing intentional action in terms of belief, desire, and foreknowledge."

My inclination is that the first two sentences are essentially right and that the passage drifts thereafter, but I cannot say precisely where. Please work through it claim by claim and distinguish what a careful philosopher working on the side-effect effect would accept, what counts as a permissible simplification for a lay audience, and what misrepresents the state of the literature — attending in particular to the claims about consensus, the fate of the blame-signaling alternative, and the morals drawn about value-free facts and traditional analyses. For each misrepresentation, indicate how my colleague should amend it, and whether the amendments weaken the conclusions she wants the passage to support.
