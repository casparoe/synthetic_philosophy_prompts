Analyze the rise and collapse of the Soviet Union (1917–1991) as a test case for Ibn Khaldun's cyclical theory of political orders. On Khaldun's account, regimes are founded by groups bound together by asabiyyah — intense solidarity or group feeling — seize and consolidate power, and then decay as successive ruling generations grow accustomed to comfort and privilege, solidarity dissipates, and the dynasty exhausts itself, typically within three to four generations. Take the Bolshevik party as the solidarity-bearing group and the roughly 74-year lifespan of the Soviet state as the datum to be explained.

Work the mapping carefully first. What plays the role of asabiyyah in a modern ideological party, where membership is chosen rather than ascriptive: revolutionary comradeship forged in illegality and civil war, party discipline, class consciousness? Does the nomenklatura's trajectory from austere cadres to a privileged, self-reproducing caste fit Khaldun's luxury-and-decay mechanism? And can Gorbachev's reform attempt be read as a Khaldunian renewal of group feeling — and if so, why did it accelerate the collapse rather than arrest it?

Then press the case against the theory. Khaldun wrote for a world of tribal conquest dynasties; the USSR had an institutionalized succession mechanism, an industrial economy, an atomized rather than kin-based population, and faced no external conqueror at the end. Which of these differences are superficial and which break the model?

Finally, run the inference the other way: if a modern party-state does trace a recognizably Khaldunian arc in roughly the predicted time, is that evidence for the theory, or merely a coincidence that a sufficiently elastic cyclical theory could accommodate no matter what happened? State what observable outcome would have falsified the Khaldunian reading.
