In our department's reading group on conceptual engineering, we have spent the term on ameliorative projects: proposals to revise what we mean by words like "woman," "race," or "disability," rather than to analyse the concepts those words already express. At the last session a senior colleague dismissed the whole enterprise with a familiar line: this is just semantics. His thought, as far as I could reconstruct it, was that redefining a word cannot change anything that matters, and that the disputants on both sides of these debates are merely talking past each other. I suspect something is wrong with that, but I struggled on the spot to say exactly what. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that "it's just semantics" might be making -- for instance, that disputes over definitions are pseudo-disputes because each party could state its position in the other's vocabulary; that meaning is conventional and therefore renegotiating it leaves the world untouched; that the world sorts itself however it does regardless of how we classify it; or that a concept, once engineered, is no longer the concept we started with, so nothing has been discovered or improved? For each claim, I want a verdict: whether it is a platitude, substantive but false, or defensible; which reading the people who wield the slogan most plausibly mean; and, crucially, which reading his remark needed in order to land as an objection to ameliorative analysis, and whether he could defend that reading under pressure. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables -- the way one would write an email to a colleague.
