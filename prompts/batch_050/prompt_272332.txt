Two policy scholars — call them the futarchist and the parliamentarian — have argued for years about governance by prediction markets and have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration. I am chairing it, and I need the joint statement they will both sign, drafted to a standard that neither can later accuse of slant.

To keep the collaboration concrete, it concerns a specific proposed charter rather than the idea in the abstract. A mid-sized democracy would continue to legislate its objectives democratically — a national welfare index weighting median income, healthy life expectancy, self-reported life satisfaction, and verified emissions, revisable only by supermajority once every seven years — but would delegate a defined class of choices (infrastructure, public health, and education spending, excluding constitutional questions and individual rights) to conditional markets. For each covered proposal, markets would open on the index's value five years hence, once conditional on adoption and once conditional on rejection, and the policy would be adopted if and only if the adoption-conditional price exceeds the rejection-conditional price by a margin fixed in advance. The futarchist holds that even this limited scheme would outperform committees and ministries on those institutions' own terms. The parliamentarian holds that it would fail in ways its advocates systematically underdescribe, and that conventional institutions, for all their documented forecasting failures, remain the better instrument.

The statement should contain the following.

First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign, none of them vacuous. It should include, at minimum, what both accept about the demonstrated weaknesses of expert and electoral forecasting; about the conditions under which markets aggregate information well and whether those conditions plausibly obtain here; about the point that a price is a conditional expectation relative to a resolution structure, not a verdict on a policy; about the fact that rational traders with common information and common priors have no motive to trade, so liquidity must come from subsidies, hedging, or genuine disagreement, each of which can bias prices; and about the comparison being between two imperfect institutions rather than between a market and an idealized deliberator.

Second, a table of residual disagreements, each phrased so that the side it describes would accept the wording. I expect it to cover at least: whether the accuracy record of thick, short-horizon, repeated markets transfers to thin, one-shot, five-year policy markets; whether the welfare index is a serviceable proxy that can be iteratively repaired or a target that optimization pressure will corrupt faster than a seven-year revision cycle can fix; whether residual government discretion — emergency overrides, imperfect implementation — contaminates adoption-conditional prices with expectations about politics rather than policy efficacy, and whether that is fatal or merely quantifiable; whether the scheme can ever learn from its vetoes, given that rejected policies never resolve and so never yield outcome data; and whether a decision justified by a price can command the legitimacy that justifies coercion, independently of its accuracy.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a test, record, or case both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance — for example, what each expects a survey of documented manipulation attempts in existing markets to show; what result in a pre-registered pilot (a small jurisdiction running subsidized decision markets on low-stakes policies under a published metric) would count as vindication and as defeat for each; and which historical episodes of governance-by-target each side offers as evidence on the index question.

Fourth, a verdict on which disagreements, if any, are verbal rather than substantive, with reasons.

Format: the numbered agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with recorded predictions, and finally a short list of the commitments each side makes about what would change its mind.
