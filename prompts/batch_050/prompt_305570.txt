"In On Certainty Wittgenstein's main target is Moore's famous proof of the external world, where Moore held up his hand and claimed to know with certainty that it was a hand. Wittgenstein shows that Moore's proof fails, because Moore uses 'I know' where he really only means 'I feel absolutely convinced,' and knowing requires the possibility of doubt being removed by checking. But Wittgenstein then gives his own answer to skepticism: at the bottom of everything we do and say there are 'hinge propositions,' like the proposition that the earth existed long before I was born or that I have a body. These are a priori truths that no rational person can doubt. They aren't based on evidence and can't be justified, but we know them anyway, since without them the whole structure of knowledge would collapse. Doubt has to stop somewhere, and Wittgenstein's point is that the skeptic is refuted, because genuinely universal doubt is impossible for any rational agent. So in the end knowledge rests on an unshakeable foundation of indubitable hinge beliefs, and this is why On Certainty is usually read as defending a kind of common-sense foundationalism in the spirit of Moore."
