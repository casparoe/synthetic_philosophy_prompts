Here's an argument I wrote up for my grad seminar on causation a while back, and I've never settled whether it's clever or broken, because the conclusion seems unavoidable and also crazy. Setup: my fern died while I was away at a conference because the student I'd paid to water it never showed.

(1) The student's not watering the fern caused its death. That's just a true, ordinary causal claim, no different in kind from saying my overwatering the previous fern killed that one.
(2) If x caused y, then x actually happened. A causal relation needs two relata, and you can't have a relatum that doesn't exist.
(3) But the student's omission isn't an event. Nothing happened at my apartment that week -- that's the whole point of the tragedy. There was no x to stand in the causing.
(4) So (1) is false, or at best lose talk. Strictly speaking, nothing caused the fern's death.
(5) Now the trouble: this generalizes horribly. Starvation is the absence of food, dehydration the absence of water, a brake failure is brakes omitting to brake, heart failure is a heart omitting to beat. A huge share of our causal talk -- including basically all of negligence law -- runs through absences.
(6) So the little argument in (2)-(4), which looked airtight, apparently proves that starvation has never killed anyone and no brake failure ever caused a crash. It proves too much.
(7) Fine, so omissions can be causes after all.
(8) But then run it the other direction. If absences cause, then every absence involving a would-be preventer causes. The Queen never watered my fern either. Neither did you. So the Queen, you, and roughly eight billion strangers all caused my fern's death.
(9) Eight billion causes of one fern is exactly as absurd as zero causes of starvation.

So the very same pressure shoves me to zero causes in one direction and eight billion in the other, and I can't find a resting place that doesn't slide into one of them. If I had to pick, I'd rather keep (1) than give up that starvation kills, but I'd like to know what I'm paying. Which step should I actually reject, why does it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? And if your fix ends up making causation partly a matter of norms or expectations -- of who was supposed to do what -- tell me whether you take that to be a bug or a feature, because my seminar split right down the middle on it.
