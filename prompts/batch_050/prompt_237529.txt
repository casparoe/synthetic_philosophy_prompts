I'm putting together a handout for my second-year metaphysics class and I want a decision tree that sorts students into positions on the sorites paradox. Take the role of someone who's taught vagueness for years and has graded a thousand essays where students say "it's all just fuzzy" without knowing what that commits them to.

Here's the constraint that makes this hard: the tree has to be built from the logical space, not from a list of famous views. So think carefully through the branching questions first -- things like: does classical logic survive vague language? is there a sharp cutoff somewhere even if we can't know it? does "heap" change its boundary as the sorites argument runs? -- and only then locate the actual positions in that space. Every branch node should be phrased as a yes/no (or three-way) question a student could answer without knowing which view it lands them on.

Required leaves: epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree/fuzzy theory, contextualism about the boundary, nihilism (there are no heaps), and the view that vague predicates are just incoherent or inconsistent. Each leaf gets one sentence on the commitment its defenders find most embarassing.

Format the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. "Brainstorm: the branching questions" (~150 words) -- which questions carve the space, and why these ones rather than the obvious "is there a sharp boundary?" as the first split (or argue that it should be first)
2. "The tree" (the main deliverable) -- each node a question, each leaf a named position plus its awkward commitment
3. "Pairs the tree can't separate" (~100 words) -- e.g. I suspect supervaluationism and its truth-glut dual are hard to tease apart with assumption-questions alone; flag anything like that honestly rather than papering over it
4. "Empty corners" (~100 words) -- any coherent-looking combination of answers nobody defends, with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored)
