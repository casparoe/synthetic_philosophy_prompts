In my course on archives and historical method, I keep running into a professional norm that seems philosophically loaded, and I want help reconstructing what it actually presupposes. The norm is the default priority of the contemporaneous written record: when a written document produced at the time conflicts with an oral account transmitted later, historians and journalists treat the document as the primary evidence and the oral account as needing corroboration from it, and events attested only orally tend to be described with hedges like "according to tradition" or treated as unrecoverable altogether. The consequence everyone in the course notices is that people who left few written records — enslaved people, colonized populations, the illiterate poor — appear in history almost entirely through records produced by the institutions that controlled them, while the oral traditions their descendants maintained get discounted. The standard defense of the norm is that it isn't a theory, just method: memory is demonstrably reconstructive and prone to error, documents can be checked, and distortions in documents can be corrected by reading critically, against the grain, for the author's interests.

So here is my question. What theory of evidence does this default actually presuppose? Is it committed to the view that proximity in time is what makes testimony reliable — that writing freezes testimony at the moment of the event while memory degrades a copy of it? Does it presuppose that the only thing wrong with a biased source is distortion, which critical method can in principle correct, whereas silence — what was never recorded at all — is not evidence of anything and so can be set aside? Which rival views does the norm quietly rule out, for instance the view that the archive's exclusions are themselves produced by power and are therefore data rather than mere absence, or the view that oral transmission preserves a different kind of evidence (about meaning, collective experience, what a community thought worth remembering) rather than a decayed version of the written kind? I also want the distinctions drawn carefully: what does the practice strictly presuppose, such that it would collapse without it, versus what it merely sits comfortably with? Could a historian fully accept that archives are constituted by what institutions chose to record and still keep the written-word default on purely pragmatic grounds — in which case the practice is philosophically underdetermined and I've been looking for a theory that isn't there? And finally, concretely: what would historical method have to look like if one did take absence and oral transmission seriously as evidence in their own right, and would the result still be recognizable as the same discipline?

One request about how to answer: please don't reconstruct an elaborate implicit theory out of politeness if the honest answer is that the norm presupposes nothing deep. If you think the default is just a tractability heuristic that several rival theories of evidence would license equally well, say that plainly and show why, rather than manufacturing commitments the practice doesn't have. And if I've misdescribed the norm itself — if historians don't actually work this way — correct the premise first.
