My reading group just finished Shusaku Endo's Silence (some of us watched the Scorsese film instead, which caused its own argument), and I want to frame next week's discussion around the philosophy of divine hiddenness. The obvious hook is that Schellenberg's argument is about nonresistant nonbelief -- people who don't believe, are open to believing, and get nothing -- while Rodrigues has the opposite problem: he believes intensely, suffers for it, prays constantly, and God is silent anyway. So read the book against the hiddenness literature: is God's silence to a fervent believer a different problem from hiddenness-to-the-nonbeliever, philosophically, or does it actually make things worse for the theist, since here we seemingly have someone in a loving relationship with God who is still getting the silent treatment? Then the hard part: the novel's climax, where the voice finally speaks precisely as Rodrigues tramples the fumi-e, seems to suggest that God breaks silence only at the moment of betrayal. Is that a theodicy (silence as the condition for a certain kind of faith or solidarity with the suffering), a concession that the hiddenness argument basically wins and Christianity has to relocate God into the believer's own act of love, or something else? Ferreira's "swamp of Japan" speech raises another question the philosophers mostly don't touch: is the problem that God is hidden, or that the whole conceptual transplant failed? Which scenes would you actually assign the group to re-read, where does the hiddenness lens distort the book, and what do you think Endo understands about religious silence that the analytic literature, with its focus on belief states and evidence, tends to miss?
