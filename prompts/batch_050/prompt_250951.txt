I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I also sit (long story) on our eight-person style and standards commitee. We've deadlocked 4-4 over a word, and I've been stuck writing the decision memo before Thursday's vote. Here's the case.

We have a big feature in production about a gene-therapy trial for HIV. One participant, "Patient 6," got a CRISPR treatment designed to snip out integrated viral DNA, and he's now been off antiretrovirals for over three years with no replication-competent virus showing up in the standard assays. The fancy ultra-sensitive research assay did turn up a few intact proviruses in a small sample of his cells -- the sponsor calls that noise, while the trial's lead clinician calls it exactly why she only ever says "long-term remission" and would want a decade of clean data before saying anything stronger. The writer's draft describes Patient 6 as "functionally cured." The sponsor's press release says "cured," full stop. And a patient advocate on the trial's board hates cure headlines on principle, because the community got burned by hype in the past.

A philosopher friend tells me our split is basically an old fight about vagueness. Half the committee are de facto epistemicists: "cured" means something definite -- the virus can never rebound, period -- and Patient 6 either is or isn't, whatever we happen to know. So our job is to pick the best operational test available, apply it, and print the verdict. The other half are de facto supervaluationists: this is a penumbral case, any cutoff we announce is a stipulation dressed up as a discovery, and honest copy should state the determinate facts (40 months off treatment, the assay results, the clinician's dissent) and refuse the forced either/or.

So: write the memo. It has to rule on this specific sentence in this specific piece, set a standing house rule on cure language that a copy editor can actually apply at 11pm, say which picture of vagueness the rule encodes, and give the losing side's strongest case fairly, since four committee members will be reading for exactly that. Two wrinkles you should wrestle with, not wave at: the epistemicist half concedes we may never *know* the fact of the matter here, so aren't they licensing confident print claims nobody can actually check? And the supervaluationist half's own logic apparently makes "Patient 6 is either cured or he isn't" come out true while each disjunct stays indeterminate -- philosophically respectable, and a completely useless sentence in a magazine.
