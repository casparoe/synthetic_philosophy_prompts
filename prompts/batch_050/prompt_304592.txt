At a party last weekend two friends of mine got into a weirdly heated argument about a donation one of them had just made, and I couldn't figure out who was right, so I'm bringing it here. Sam had just given $500 to a highly effective malaria charity and was feeling good about it, when Maya pointed out that the kids whose lives are saved will grow up and have kids of their own, who'll have kids, and within a few generations the total effect of his donation could easily be net-negative or net-positive by a huge margin, and he has literally no idea which -- so "it saves a life" is just the tiny visible tip of an unfathomable causal iceberg. Sam's first reply was that this is true of every action anyone ever takes, including doing nothing, so if it undermines his donation it undermines morality as a whole and therefore has to be wrong. Maya said that's an appeal to consequences and not an answer -- maybe morality as a whole IS in trouble, and anyway the point isn't that you should do nothing, it's that you should stop pretending you have reasons.

Then Sam tried a different move: he said the unknown long-run effects are symmetric -- the donation is just as likely to make the distant future better as worse, so it all cancels out and he's left with the near-term good as a tiebreaker. Maya pushed back hard on this, saying "you don't know it's positive" is not the same as "you know it's symmetric," and that assuming symmetry is exactly the kind of epistemic laziness he wouldn't accept anywhere else -- nobody says "I'm clueless about whether this mushroom is poisonous, 50/50, bon appetit." Sam's last stand was that you have to make decisions somehow, and "expected value over the consequences we can actually estimate" is the only workable policy, so even if it's philosophically dodgy it's the right practical stance. Maya said fine, but then he should stop describing his donation as doing good and start describing it as "acting on the only inputs my decision procedure can see, while knowing they're probably swamped by inputs it can't" -- and that this rebrand would take all the motivational wind out of effective altruism, which is really what he's resisting.

So, can you adjudicate? Go through the exchange move by move -- Maya's opening iceberg point, Sam's "this would paralyze morality" response, her reply to that, his symmetry tiebreaker, her mushroom analogy, and his final practicality retreat -- and say for each whether it's sound, mistaken, or half-right and why. And if there's something both of them are missing (I have a vague sense there might be a difference between being clueless about which option is best and being clueless about whether an option is good at all, but I can't articulate it), spell that out too.
