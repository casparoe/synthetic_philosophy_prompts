So my friend and I stayed up til like 2am last night arguing about this and I'm still annoyed because I think he walked me into a corner I can't get out of. The setup: our roommate just found out her boyfriend cheated on her, told him he's a selfish coward, and my friend pointed out that she did basically the same thing to her ex two years ago, so she's a hypocrite and has no business being angry. I said fine, ok, maybe she lacks the *standing* to blame him, whatever that means exactly. He then turned this into a whole numbered argument and it seems to prove that basically nobody can ever blame anybody, which can't be right, but I honestly can't find the bad step. Here it is, as close to his version as I can reconstruct:

1. To blame someone for a moral wrong — as opposed to just noting, in a detached way, that they did it — you need standing: some kind of entitlement to press the complaint, the thing a person guilty of the same wrong lacks.
2. You lose standing to blame someone for a fault if you're guilty of that same fault yourself and haven't repudiated it. That's the hypocrisy condition, and it's what explains why "look who's talking" genuinely stings instead of being a mere insult.
3. "The same fault" has to be individuated broadly. Lying, cheating, flaking on people, quietly keeping the extra change — these are all just ways of putting your own interests ahead of other people's, and it would be arbitrary to say you keep your standing to blame liars merely because your own lies were about different subject matter.
4. Everyone has put their own interests ahead of someone else's, repeatedly, and almost nobody has repudiated every instance.
5. So nobody, or close enough to nobody, has standing to blame anyone for any moral failure.
6. And note this applies to blaming the hypocrite as well — so by my friend's lights even calling someone a hypocrite requires a standing that nobody actually has.
7. But that's absurd. It can't be that our roommate did something even slightly out of line just by being furious at her boyfriend, or that all the moral anger everyone feels every day is improper.

His takeaway was that the argument is airtight and the real lesson is that everyone should stop moralizing and mind their own business. I refuse to believe that, but when I pushed on the premises individually they all seemed... fine? That's what's driving me nuts. So: which premise or step do you think I should reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? I'd really like to keep (2) — the hypocrisy intuition feels like the one solid thing here — and I definately want to keep ordinary blame from being illegitimate across the board. If dropping some premise means giving up on standing-talk altogether, or means hypocrites get a free pass, I want that priced in explicitly rather than hand-waved. And if you think some word (my suspicion is "fault," maybe "standing") is quietly doing two different jobs in different premises, say where and say what the argument looks like once the readings are disambiguated — I have a wierd feeling the slide happens around (3) but I can't pin it down and I'd rather be told I'm wrong about that than keep staring at it.
