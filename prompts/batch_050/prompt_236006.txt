For the creative assessment in my second-year philosophy of law module (we are halfway through the unit on retributivist versus consequentialist theories of punishment), I have to produce a piece of satire grounded in our set text, and I would like your help writing it.

The set passage is from Jeremy Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (first published 1789), Chapter XIII, "Cases Unmeet for Punishment":

> But all punishment is mischief: all punishment in itself is evil. Upon the principle of utility, if it ought at all to be admitted, it ought only to be admitted in as far as it promises to exclude some greater evil.

Our seminar leader's gloss was that this single sentence contains the germ of the standard objection to purely consequentialist punishment: if the only warrant for inflicting pain is the greater evil it excludes, then the principle itself appears to permit deliberately punishing an innocent person whenever the deterrent arithmetic comes out right (the so-called telishment problem).

Please write a Swiftian modest proposal of roughly 600-700 words (the Bentham passage does not count towards this length) in the voice of an invented early-nineteenth-century reform society -- say, the Society for the Preventive Application of Punishment -- which adopts the passage as its founding charter and calmly proposes a scheme of exemplary punishment: persons privately known to the authorities to be innocent of the crimes in question are to be selected, tried, and punished in public, wherever the mischief thereby excluded exceeds the mischief produced.

Requirements:

- Stay strictly deadpan throughout. The register is committee prose: sub-headings, costings, selection criteria, protocols for keeping the victims' innocence secret, perhaps a pension scheme for their dependants. The horror must emerge from the arithmetic and the paperwork, never from any wink at the reader.
- Keep the society internally consistent with the principle in the passage. It should have a ready utilitarian answer to every scruple (it treats talk of natural or imprescriptible rights with the same contempt Bentham himself showed for such talk), and it must never slip into retributivist language -- desert may figure only as a superstition to be managed in the public mind, not as a reason for anything the society does.
- Enact the standard objections rather than stating them. A reader should recoil at the scapegoating; should notice the structural tension the society is forced to manage (deterrence requires publicity, yet the scheme requires secrecy about innocence); should feel the absence of any constraint of desert or consent; and should see the moment where a rule-consequentialist member resigns from the committee in protest -- together with why the society regards her resignation as half-hearted, since she has already granted the premise that punishment is justified only by its consequences.
- Include at least one joke that only lands for a reader who knows the retributivist alternative (for instance, the society's costings might treat Kantian desert as a luxury commodity too expensive for the public purse -- though I leave the precise gag to you).
- End with a Secretary's Note of about 80-100 words (outside the word count) in which the society sincerely denies that its proposal is a reductio of Bentham's principle, insisting it is merely an unpopular application of it.

Finally, after the satire and clearly separated from it, give me a short paragraph of roughly 120 words explaining which feature of consequentialist punishment the piece puts the most pressure on, and whether you think a consequentialist has a principled, non-question-begging reason to refuse the society's scheme.
