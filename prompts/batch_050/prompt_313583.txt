Over the past couple of years I have worked through the free will and moral responsibility literature, and I keep returning to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument as he presents it in his 1994 paper The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility. In my own paraphrase, the argument runs like this: to be truly, desert-entailingly responsible for what you do, you would have to be responsible for the way you are, for the character, values, and motives from which your choices flow; but you could be responsible for those only in virtue of earlier choices, which themselves issued from a way you already were; and the regress this generates cannot be completed by any actual human life. Strawson concludes that no one is ever morally responsible in the basic-desert sense, and, crucially, that this holds whether or not determinism is true. Here is roughly where I have landed, and I want to know whether it hangs together:

(1) I accept the Basic Argument and its conclusion. I also remain genuinely undecided about determinism, and I take this indecision to be irrelevant to my acceptance of the argument, since the argument is supposed to go through either way.

(2) Because retributive justification is unavailable, punishment can be justified only on forward-looking grounds: incapacitation, deterrence, rehabilitation, roughly on the model by which we quarantine carriers of dangerous diseases. A society that punished people for the sake of desert would be committing a categorical injustice, not merely getting the amounts wrong.

(3) All the same: when two people drive home equally drunk and equally recklessly on the same night, and one happens to strike a pedestrian while the other arrives home safely, the one who killed genuinely deserves more blame and a harsher sentence. The difference is not just that there is now a corpse to deal with; the killer has made himself more blameworthy. I find I cannot give this verdict up.

(4) Moral assessment of persons remains perfectly legitimate. It is simply true of some people that they are cruel, cowardly, or generous, and saying so is not a placeholder for some weaker claim. Judgments of character do not presuppose that anyone chose to have the character they have.

(5) I deliberately work on my own character, and I take it that such effort can really succeed, and that success of this kind is admirable in a way that mere good fortune of upbringing and temperament is not.

(6) The reactive attitudes are not useful fictions. Gratitude toward someone who went out of their way for me, and resentment toward someone who deliberately wronged me, are fitting responses to what those people did. A person who felt only mild disappointment where I feel resentment would be missing something morally real, not seeing the situation more clearly than I do.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Tell me whether this set of commitments is jointly tenable. If it is, show why each apparent conflict dissolves on inspection. If it is not, identify the genuine tensions, order them by severity, and for each one say which commitment has to go, what the cheapest repair would look like, and what that repair costs. Be specific: if two of these only appear to conflict, say so and show the dissolution; if two genuinely conflict, do not let me paper over it. I am not asking which commitments are independently plausible; I am asking what my own set commits me to.
