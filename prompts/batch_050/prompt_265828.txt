I am a novelist, and metaphor is the tool I reach for most, so I have more than an academic stake in what metaphor actually is. Lately, while preparing a series of craft lectures, I have been reading the philosophy of language on the topic, and I have found myself persuaded by Donald Davidson's position, as developed in his essay What Metaphors Mean: roughly, that a metaphor means only what its words literally mean -- nothing more -- and that all the richness we associate with metaphor belongs not to its meaning but to its effects. On this view a metaphor is typically false if taken flatly, or trivially true, and its real work is causal and open-ended: it jolts us into noticing things, into seeing one thing as another, and any paraphrase we offer is not a translation of a hidden content but an attempt to set down in prose what the metaphor provoked us to notice. I will confess this resonates with my experience. When readers or students ask what a particular image in one of my books means, anything I say in reply feels less like a recovery of something the sentence contained and more like a pale substitute for it, and the fact that good readers draw indefinitely many different things from the same image seems, on the Davidsonian picture, to be a feature rather than a failure of interpretation.

What I want to know is what would count against this view. Please do not just rehearse the standard complaint that it is counterintuitive; I want a map of where the view is actually exposed. For instance: does the empirical and theoretical success of conceptual metaphor theory -- the Lakoff and Johnson tradition, with its systematic cross-domain mappings, its claims about embodied motivation, and the psycholinguistic work suggesting that metaphorical processing is fast, automatic, and structured -- genuinely bear on Davidson's claim, or does it operate at a different level, about cognition rather than semantic content, so that the two could both be true? Would a successful compositional semantics that assigned determinate metaphorical contents refute Davidson, or would his defenders simply redescribe whatever such a theory captures as pragmatics? What about phenomena like dead versus live metaphor, the apparent translatability of metaphors across languages, or the fact that audiences converge on broadly shared readings of novel metaphors rather than scattering arbitrarily? Which of these would a committed Davidsonian concede as genuine pressure, and which would they wave away? And please address honestly whether the position has been qualified into near-unfalsifiability -- whether, that is, the Davidsonian can always deflect any apparent metaphorical content by relabeling it an effect -- and if so, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or merely a sign that it is a claim about where to draw the semantics-pragmatics boundary.

Finally, two requests about calibration. First, please keep clearly separate what is genuinely settled in this area from what remains contested among philosophers of language and linguists who work on figurative language, and do not present a contested reading of Davidson -- or of the empirical literature -- as though it were the consensus. Second, tell me whether the phenomenological datum I leaned on, the felt inexhaustibility of a good metaphor, actually supports Davidson specifically or is equally compatible with his rivals, so that I know whether my novelist's intuition is doing real argumentative work or is neutral between the theories and I have been giving it more weight than it deserves.
