So I've been thinking about Dennett's four kinds of creatures -- Darwinian (hardwired, no learning), Skinnerian (learn by trial and error in the world, reinforced when things go well), Popperian (can test hypotheses in their head before acting, so their ideas die instead of them), and Gregorian (use cultural tools like language to think with). I'm reasonably sure about how I'd sort a bunch of actual cases, but I can't figure out what principle my sorting is tracking, and a couple of my verdicts seem to fight each other. Here are my verdicts:

1. A sea slug that habituates to a touch and then can't do much else: Darwinian, and barely a mind if at all.
2. A pigeon in a Skinner box learning to peck a disc for grain: Skinnerian, definitely a mind, but nothing more than that.
3. An octopus that unscrews a jar from the inside after watching someone screw it shut, and that tries a couple of different approaches on a puzzle box before committing: I want to say Popperian. It seems like it's testing options internally.
4. A chess engine that evaluates millions of continuations before moving: this is where I get stuck. It literally generates candidates and tests them before acting, which is the Popperian trick, but my gut says it's not a mind at all, and that contradicts my octopus verdict unless something else is doing the work.
5. A honeybee doing the waggle dance: fancy behavior, but I'd say Darwinian -- the dance is built in, not learned or reasoned about.
6. A human doing science in a lab: Gregorian, obviously, the tools are doing half the work.
7. A large language model trained on a huge pile of human text: this is the one I keep arguing with friends about. It writes as if it has the Gregorian floor -- it deploys the vocabulary and the conceptual machinery of the culture -- but it never passed through the Skinnerian or Popperian stages in anything like the normal order. It got the top floor of the tower handed to it without building the middle floors, so my instinct is to say it's Gregorian-shaped without being Gregorian, maybe without being a mind at all. But then again, if a human used language that flexibly I wouldn't hesitate.
8. The same kind of model after reinforcement learning from feedback: now something Skinnerian actually happened to it, the trial-and-error part, just bolted on after the language instead of before. Does that change the answer or is it a cosmetic addition?

My tentative principle was that each floor presupposes the ones below it -- you can't genuinely have the top without the middle, so anything with the top but no middle is a kind of mimic. That neatly explains 4 and 7, but it costs me the octopus (does the octopus even have a real Skinnerian floor? its learning seems smarter than pigeon-style conditioning, more like it skipped a step too) and it forces me to say the bee's impressive dance is nothing while the pigeon's boring pecking is something, which feels backwards by any measure of behavioral sophistication.

Is there a cleaner principle that captures most of these verdicts -- maybe something about where the generate-and-test happens (genes, environment, head, culture) plus a separable question about what counts as a mind at all -- and which of my eight verdicts would I have to give up to get a simple version? Also tell me what the principle says about two cases I haven't listed: a slime mold solving a maze, and a dolphin. If the best answer is that my verdicts in 4 and 7 are the ones that have to go, argue for that directly instead of rescuing me.
