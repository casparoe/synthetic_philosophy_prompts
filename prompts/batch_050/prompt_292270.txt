I'm in a philosophy of cognitive science group and we've been stuck for three sessions on whether multiple realizability actually refutes mind-brain type identity, and I can't tell if we're even disagreeing anymore. The identity-theory fans keep saying that once you fix the grain of description, every apparent case dissolves: octopus and human pain only look like the same mental state because we describe them at a coarse level, and if we describe the function finely enough each species has its own function, so there's no multiply realized kind to worry about. The functionalists keep answering that this move makes functions so fine-grained that nothing is ever shared across species at all, which would wreck comparative psychology, and that anyway we can just point to cases: the same chess evaluation function implemented in a lookup table vs a trained network, or a thermostat built around a mercury switch vs a bimetallic strip. The identity people say those are trivially different kinds because they work by different mechanisms, which is exactly the grain question again. My hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than adopted, is that the debate isn't about brains at all but is a disguised methodological disagreement about how science should individuate its kinds, and that both sides have agreed to pretend it's an empirical question about neural diversity because that sounds more respectable. So diagnose the dispute: is it verbal, factual, about standards of evidence, or are the sides answering different questions under one banner, and what would actually count as progress given your answer. Give me your whole response as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion.
