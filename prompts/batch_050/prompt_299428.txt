I help run a political philosophy reading group at my university (mix of final-year undergrads and a couple of masters students, usually about 14-16 people, and we get the seminar room for 90 minutes). We've been slogging through historical materialism for a few weeks and the group keeps treating the base-superstructure claim as either obviously false ("the law isn't just a puppet of the economy, look at human rights!") or trivially true ("sure, incentives matter"). Nobody seems to feel the pull of the interesting middle position, the one where legal rules, moral intuitions and even religious ideas genuinely reorganise themselves around changes in how production is organised, and where people sincerely *believe* the resulting norms rather than cynically adopting them.

Design an activity that makes them feel this in their guts rather than just nod at it. I want the core mechanic to be something like: small groups each face a different material setup for producing whatever the game's currency is (different degrees of scarcity, different requirements for cooperation, maybe one group where hoarding is physically possible and one where it isn't), and they have to collectively write their own rules and justify them, ideally inventing little myths or principles along the way. Then partway through there should be some kind of technological shock that changes the production logic, and the interesting bit is watching whether their *justifications* shift along with their rules, not just their behaviour. The debrief should then map what happened onto the actual claims -- I'd like it to clearly seperate the trivial reading (people respond to incentives) from the stronger one (people internalise norms suited to the relations of production), and to say something about functional explanation, i.e. the idea that the superstructure persists *because* of what it does for the base rather than because anyone designed it that way.

Please be concrete about the runnable stuff: materials needed, exact rules, a timeline for the 90 minutes, how to stop groups copying each other, and what predictably goes wrong (e.g. a group whose norms stubbornly refuse to shift after the shock -- which is actually interesting, so how do I use that rather than treat it as a failure?). Also be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: presumably something about real class interests, generational timescales, and the fact that in the game I *am* the base, pulling the levers, whereas the actual claim is that nobody is. Don't lecture them beforehand -- they shouldn't hear the phrase "base and superstructure" until the debrief.
