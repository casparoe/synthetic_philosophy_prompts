I'm a high school junior in an independent study on value theory, and my current paper is on what's sometimes called the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the worry that pleasures are too different from one another for any single account to say what they all have in common. Before I commit to a position, I want the specification spelled out first, since I suspect half the disagreement in this literature comes from people assuming different requirements.

So: write the list of desiderata a theory of pleasure must satisfy to count as an adequate answer to the heterogeneity problem. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Here are the things I want the list to address at minimum, though please add anything I'm missing:

- The unification demand itself: the theory has to cover the full spread — bodily pleasures (a warm bath, scratching an itch), pleasures of achievement (finally solving a problem), aesthetic pleasures, anticipatory pleasures, and quiet contentment — without either gerrymandering the category or going so abstract that the account becomes empty.
- Which distinctions the theory must not collapse. I have in mind at least: pleasure vs. the mere satisfaction of a desire, pleasure vs. the belief that something is good, and intensity vs. duration vs. some notion of depth. Are there others?
- Hard cases any theory must take a stand on: pleasures taken in things that didn't actually happen (say, joy at a false report), pleasures that intuitively seem worthless or worse (taking pleasure in another's misfortune), and cases where pleasure and motivation come apart, like compulsive behaviors people don't enjoy. I think these constrain the theory in different directions — please check whether I'm right about that.
- Whether the theory owes us an explanation of why pleasure is good (and displeasure bad), or whether it's allowed to take that as a bare datum. This strikes me as a fault line between camps rather than a neutral requirement, and I'd like your verdict on which it is.

For each desideratum, mark it as non-negotiable for any theory, or as a commitment of a particular camp, and justify the marking. If some subset of the genuine requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — so that no theory can meet the whole list — that's the result I most want to see, and I'd like it stated explicitly rather than hinted at. Also tell me which single requirement, if treated as non-negotiable, eliminates the most live candidates, and why.

After the list, briefly score the two main families of views — theories that locate pleasure in a shared felt quality or hedonic tone, and theories that treat pleasure as an attitude (liking, or taking pleasure in something) toward its object — against your own requirements. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are the deliverable.

Please also flag, for each major claim, how confident you are in it — especially the judgments about which requirements are non-negotiable, since I expect those to be the most contestable — and end by singling out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
