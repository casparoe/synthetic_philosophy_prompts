I am a PhD student working at the boundary of statistical mechanics and philosophy of physics, and I have been trying to articulate what I actually believe about the thermodynamics of information by collecting my verdicts on particular cases. I would like to know whether there is a single principle behind them, and if not, which verdict I should surrender.

Here are the cases. First: erasing one bit from a memory register in contact with a heat bath at temperature T dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat, no matter how cleverly the erasure is engineered. I take this to be a genuine, inescapable cost. Second: measuring the position of the particle in a Szilard engine need cost nothing, thermodynamically speaking, provided the measurement apparatus starts in a standard state -- the real cost of the whole cycle is paid later, when the demon's record of the outcome is erased to close the loop. Third: inserting and removing the partition in that same engine slowly and without friction costs nothing, and the subsequent isothermal expansion of the one-molecule gas yields exactly kT ln 2 of work. Fourth: a demon equipped with an inexhaustible supply of blank memory can, I think, extract work from a single heat bath indefinitely without ever paying an erasure cost -- but this does not violate the second law, because the pristine memory is itself a thermodynamic resource being consumed, akin to a battery draining. Fifth: merely copying a bit, or any logically reversible manipulation of information, is free. Sixth: two observers who differ only in what they have recorded about a gas will correctly assign it different entropies and different extractable work; entropy, in this sense, is partly a function of what one knows.

The pair I cannot reconcile is this. On the one hand, I accept that the second law is statistical, so that over short times and small scales fluctuations can transiently run entropy downhill, and a demon could occasionally beat the kT ln 2 bound on a single trial. On the other hand, I stated my first verdict as an absolute floor on erasure cost, and I am reluctant to give it up, because without it I do not see what protects the second law from systematic exploitation rather than mere lucky fluctuations.

Is there one coherent principle -- presumably something in the family of Landauer's bound, the mutual-information accounts of feedback control, or the fluctuation theorems -- that delivers all of these verdicts, or the largest defensible subset of them? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause of it each of my verdicts motivates, and tell me honestly which verdict it cannot accommodate. Then tell me what the resulting view predicts for two cases I have not settled: a demon that, instead of erasing its growing memory, reversibly compresses it; and a genuinely quantum demon whose measurements disturb the system it observes.
