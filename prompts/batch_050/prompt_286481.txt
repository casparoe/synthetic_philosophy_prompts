I spent forty years doing condensed matter physics and in retirement I've fallen down the philosophy of biology rabit hole. Right now I'm stuck on the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique of adaptationism, and I want a test case to beat up in my reading group.

Construct a hypothetical biological case (plausible to an actual working biologist, no fantasy creatures) with three stages: a trait that arises purely as a byproduct of selection on something else, then gets co-opted for a function of its own, and then the originally selected trait is lost, so the former byproduct ends up carrying the functional load alone. Spell out the trait, the developmental or structural chain that makes it a byproduct in the first place, the co-option, and the loss. Then tell me: at which stage, if any, does the adaptationist explanation become the right one? And when the adaptationist gets it wrong at stage one, is that a factual mistake or a mistake about what counts as *the* explanation of the trait?

One more constraint: the byproduct explanation can't just be "we don't know why this trait exists." Give the actual mechanism. I want the case to pry apart origin-explanations from maintenance-explanations, because my suspicion is that's where the whole fight really lives and both sides talk past each other. Finish by saying which single feature of the case does the work — what happens to your verdicts if you delete the co-option stage, or the loss stage?
