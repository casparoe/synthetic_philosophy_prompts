I work in network science — mostly historical trade and migration data — and a colleague in our history department recently roped me into a reading group on Eurocentrism in global history. The group's core worry, as I understand it, is roughly this: standard narratives of the past few centuries put Europe at the center of the story and treat everyone else as reacting to it, and the proposed remedies go under names like connected histories, multiple modernities, and the project of "provincializing Europe" — retelling the past so that the causal and intellectual action is distributed across many regions at once, with Europe as one node among others rather than the protagonist. I kept having the feeling that almost everything the historians were saying had a precise counterpart in the vocabulary I use every day: centrality measures and hub nodes, edge weights, sampling biases in how a network gets constructed from archival traces, preferential attachment and path dependence, the way a metric computed on a graph silently depends on which nodes and edges were recorded in the first place.

So here is what I'd like. Restate the Eurocentrism critique of global historiography — as both a descriptive thesis about how the past actually unfolded and a methodological thesis about how histories get written — in the vocabulary of network analysis, as faithfully as you can. Which parts of the critique translate cleanly (my suspicion: a lot of the "Great Divergence" material about when and why northwest Europe pulled ahead looks like claims about when one region became a hub, and under what network conditions hub status is fragile or contingent)? Which parts only translate with distortion? And which parts, if any, have no expression in graph-theoretic vocabulary at all? The reading group kept insisting that the problem isn't just that Europe gets counted as too central, but that the historian's own standpoint — being trained in European categories, writing in European languages, using archives built by European states — distorts the picture before any counting begins. I genuinely cannot tell whether that claim is a deep point that my framework can't express, or a loose way of gesturing at measurement and sampling biases that my framework expresses better than theirs does.

Treat the residue as the interesting part of the exercise: for each claim that fails to translate, say whether the failure indicts the network vocabulary or suggests the historiographical claim was less precise than it sounded. Be honest rather than diplomatic in either direction — I don't want the translation to quietly smuggle in network-theoretic assumptions and call the result faithful. And at the end, tell me what would change your verdict: which argument, example, or consideration would lead you to conclude instead that the untranslatable residue is the whole point of the critique, and where in your own reasoning you think you're most likely to be wrong.
