I am a PhD student in moral philosophy working on the ethics of blame. In a recent seminar on standing to blame, I criticized a public figure's infidelity, and a colleague shut me down with 'people who live in glass houses shouldn't throw stones' — an allusion, I gather, to rumors about my own conduct. I want to understand what that proverb could actually be claiming, because the conversational move seemed to work without anyone articulating why. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it might express: for instance, that my blame is likely false or unwarranted; that hypocrites forfeit a right or entitlement to blame even when their blame is apt; that blaming someone expresses a claim to a kind of moral authority the hypocrite lacks; that it is impermissible for the hypocrite to voice the blame though a third party could blamelessly voice the identical words; or merely a prudential point about inviting retaliation. For each thesis, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; whether it targets the content of the blame or the person doing the blaming; and whether it would actually vindicate my colleague's move of silencing me, rather than merely changing the subject to my character. Conclude by telling me which reading best fits the proverb's ordinary use and whether standing, on that reading, requires that I be guilty of the same wrong I condemn or of any comparable wrong at all.
