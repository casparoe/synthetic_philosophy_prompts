I chair our university's animal research ethics committee, and we've got a proposal that's split us down the middle in a way I keep going back and forth on myself. A comparative cognition lab wants to run a food-caching study with a colony of jays: the birds get to hide food in two different trays on different days, one tray containing stuff that perishes quickly and one with stuff that keeps, and after a delay the birds are allowed back to recover what they cached. The researchers claim that if the birds preferentially go back for the perishable food early but not late, this shows the jays remember not just where food is but what they cached and when, i.e. some kind of episodic-like memory, and a related follow-up arm would test whether the birds cache more in a compartment where they've learned they'll be hungry tomorrow, which the lab describes as planning for the future. The welfare side is benign -- no deprivation beyond normal mild food restriction before sessions, standard housing -- so the fight isn't about harm to the birds at all.

The fight is about Morgan's canon. Half the committee, mostly the animal behaviour people and one very vocal philosopher, say the whole proposal is built on exactly the move the canon forbids: interpreting behaviour as the product of a higher faculty (episodic memory, future planning) when a lower one (associative learning, innate caching routines, plain stimulus discrimination) could account for whatever the birds do. They want us to reject it as methodologically unsound, or at minimum force the lab to strip the cognitive framing from the grant text. The other half says the canon is a rule about how to interpret results, not a rule about which hypotheses you're allowed to test, that parsimony cuts both ways (one planning mechanism might be simpler than six bolted-on associative rules), and that refusing to let anyone test cognitive hypotheses is itself a bias dressed up as rigour. Both halves have quoted the canon at me and, as far as I can tell, it says something like: don't explain an action by a higher psychological faculty if it can be explained by one lower down the scale. I'm honestly not sure that settles anything here.

Draft the decision memo I'll bring to next month's meeting: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. Sections in this order, roughly: Recommendation (lead with the bottom line, ~100 words), The proposal and the dispute (~150 words), What the canon actually requires here (~250 words -- this is the heart of it; say what the canon does and doesn't rule out as a methodological principle, and whether the two experimental arms stand or fall together), The strongest point on the losing side (~150 words, and it has to be the real version, not a strawman), and Conditions and next steps (~150 words -- concrete things the lab could actually do, e.g. what result would count as ruling out an associative explanation, pre-registration of rival predictions, that sort of thing). Whole memo around 800 words, in British English please.
