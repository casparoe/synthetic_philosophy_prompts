Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about what makes pain bad. She defended the view that suffering is essentially a matter of aversion — that an experience is bad for you insofar as you have an intrinsic desire that it not continue — while I kept insisting that pain's badness is right there in how it feels, prior to any attitude we take toward it. Neither of us conceded much. Afterward I wrote down where I think I've landed, and before I go another round with her I'd like an audit of whether my own position actually hangs together. Here it is:

1. What makes pain bad is its phenomenal unpleasantness — the felt character of the experience itself — and not the subject's occurrent or dispositional aversion to it. An aversion can be absent, irrational, or satisfied, and none of that settles whether the experience was bad.

2. Unpleasantness is itself a phenomenal property. It is not analyzable as "being such that the subject wants it to stop," even in sophisticated dispositional terms. If it were so analyzable, my friend would be right, and I don't think she is.

3. People with pain asymbolia genuinely feel pain. They are not merely claiming to, and their condition shows that the sensory dimension of pain can occur without its normal affective-motivational dimension.

4. Nevertheless, when a pain asymbolic's body is injured, the pain they feel is still intrinsically bad for them, in some measure — not merely bad because unattended injuries tend to worsen. It would be wrong to needlessly inflict such pain on them even setting all instrumental considerations aside.

5. For purposes of comparing how bad two pain episodes are for a person's welfare, the only relevant variables are intensity and duration. Other factors matter for other reasons, but the badness of the pain itself is fixed by those two.

6. The pain of torture is worse than a phenomenally identical pain of equal intensity and duration experienced in wanted labor or childbirth, because of what the pain means — helplessness, violation, the intentions of another person. The meaning makes the suffering worse, not just the surrounding circumstances.

7. Part of the ordinary concept of pain is that it hurts: an experience that nobody minded in any way whatsoever wouldn't count as pain at all.

8. Suffering requires consciousness. A deeply anesthetized patient whose body mounts stress responses to surgery is not thereby suffering.

Please treat this as an audit, not a survey of the literature. Tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; in particular, check 4 and 6 carefully against the principles I've stated, since I have a suspicion one or both of them are verdicts my theory can't actually deliver, but I'm not sure. Rank any genuine tensions by severity, and for each one state the cheapest revision and what it costs. If you think a tension is merely verbal or rests on an equivocation — I have my eye on 3 and 7 there — say so and explain why, including whether the resolution is entirely free or quietly concedes something. Above all, be committed: don't lay out a menu of possible repairs and leave me to choose. Tell me which single revision you would actually make if these were your views, and defend it against the strongest alternative, even if the revision you'd recommend is giving up the claim I most want to keep.
