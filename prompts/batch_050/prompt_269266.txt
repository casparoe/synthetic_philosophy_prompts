In Book 3, Part 2, Section 5 of the Treatise of Human Nature, Hume argues that no natural act of the mind could constitute a promise: "Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions." His conclusion is that promissory obligation is an artificial product of social convention. I'm trying to figure out what I actually think grounds the obligation, and my verdicts on cases seem to presuppose something Hume claims is impossible. Here are the verdicts:

- I promise my neighbor I'll water her plants while she's away; it binds even if she'll never check whether I did.
- My aunt on her deathbed extracts a promise that I'll keep her garden going; it binds after her death, though she can never know, rely, or be disappointed.
- I say "I promise" into the phone after the line has already gone dead, and the other person never hears it; that doesn't bind, however sincere my willing.
- A mugger forces me to promise to leave my door unlocked tonight; that doesn't bind.
- I promise a friend I'll drive the getaway car for his insurance scam; no obligation to do it results.
- I promise my sister, unprompted, that I'll attend her dinner party; she privately dreads these parties and would be quietly relieved if I skipped. Breaking this one feels barely wrong -- not nothing, but not the plant-watering case either.
- A stage actor says "I promise" in character; obviously it doesn't bind.

The pair I can't reconcile: the deathbed promise binds with no possibility of reliance or even awareness on the promisee's side, yet the dinner-party promise seems to lose most of its force precisely because nothing is at stake for the promisee. Both look like cases of "no expectations riding on it," but I judge them differently. And the dead-phone case -- full sincere willing, no obligation -- seems to vindicate Hume's point that the will alone can't manufacture a duty; but then whatever does the work in the plant-watering case isn't the will either, and I don't know what it is.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts? State it precisely, flag which verdict motivates each clause, and say whether it answers Hume's argument -- by identifying the binding "act of the mind," or by showing why none is needed. If the simplest adequate principle forces me to give up one of my verdicts, say which one and why that price is worth paying. Finally, what does the resulting view predict about a case I haven't listed: two castaways with no prior exposure to any practice of promising, one of whom says "I promise" to the other -- can that bind, and what would Hume have to say about the answer?
