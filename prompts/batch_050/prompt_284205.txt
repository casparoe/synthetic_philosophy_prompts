My flatmate has a doomsday-argument variant that feels as if it proves far too much, but I keep losing the thread. Suppose demographers can establish that I am approximately the 120-billionth human ever born, while leaving open whether there will eventually be trillions of biological descendants, vast numbers of uploaded minds, both, or neither.

His argument goes like this:

1. Given a history containing exactly N observers in my reference class, and ignoring all other evidence for the moment, the probability that a randomly selected observer has my approximate birth rank is about 1/N.

2. So a 200-billion-person history predicts my actual rank much better than a history containing 10^15 observers, by a factor of several million.

3. Evidence should be assessed by liklihood ratios. My rank therefore produces an enormous update in favour of the smaller history, whatever the prior plausibility of a stable interstellar civilisation happens to be.

4. Since this conclusion follows from my rank alone, it cannot be overturned by empirical arguments about safer technology, better institutions or reduced extinction risk; those are merely features of the rival histories being compared.

5. Including future uploads in the reference class makes the long-future population still larger, so that possibility makes the long future even less probable rather than rescuing it.

6. Armchair selection reasoning can therefore establish with very high confidence that neither mass biological expansion nor mass uploading lies in our future.

7. But this seems crazy: it apparently lets any sufficiently early observer rule out every sufficiently large future, even when all the ordinary evidence for that future is excellent.

Which numbered move should I reject first, and why does it look legitimate? Do not just call it a reference-class problem. If the culprit is the sampling assumption in (1), explain what should replace it. If (1)-(3) are defensible, explain why an enormous likelihood ratio need not produce an enormous posterior probability. Then assess whether adding uploads really strengthens the argument in (5), rather than changing either the evidence or the relevant reference class. Finally, compare the costs of rejecting random self-location with the costs of restricting what indexical evidence can tell us. I would prefer to preserve ordinary Bayesian updating if possible. Please answer in British English.
