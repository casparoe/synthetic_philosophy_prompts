I work in a symbiosis lab (mostly aphid-Buchnera stuff) and our journal club has been stuck on the same question for over a year now: is the holobiont an organism, or is it just the host plus some tenants? The weird thing is that nobody in the room actually disagrees about any of the biology. We all know Buchnera can't survive outside the aphid, we all know the aphid can't make essential amino acids without it, we all know the bacteria are transmitted through the egg. And yet one half of the room keeps saying "this is obviously one individual, it's a metabolic whole, it's inherited as a package" and the other half keeps saying "it's an ecological interaction, a very intimate one, but the partners have separate evolutionary interests and you can pull them apart in a way you'd never do to a kidney." We've read the immunological-boundary people, the units-of-selection people, the "it's all just loose ecological associations" skeptics, and the arguments just bounce off each other. My advisor has even proposed what he calls the swap test as a tiebreaker: if you can replace the symbiont with an equivalent strain from another host lineage and the whole system carries on fine (which, annoyingly, we've basically done in the lab with engineered aphid lines), then the holobiont was never an organism, because organisms don't survive wholesale replacement of parts of themselves. Half the room says the swap test proves the skeptics right, the other half says it just shows individuality is about functional organization rather than token parts, the same way you're still you after your cells turn over.

So my question is not actually "which side is right" but: what kind of disagreement is this? I've started to suspect it's not a factual disagreement at all and maybe not even a straightforward verbal one (both sides know what the other means by "organism"), but something more like a dispute about what job the concept of an individual is supposed to do -- bookkeeping for natural selection, policing metabolic boundaries, grounding research programs -- and the sides talk past each other because they've tacitly picked different jobs. But I might be wrong, maybe there IS a hidden empirical question in there that would settle it, or maybe the whole thing is ill-posed and "is X an organism" is just the wrong shape of question. Please diagnose the structure of the impasse: point at where exactly the arguments stop engaging with each other, say what each side would have to concede to move, and tell me what genuine progress would even look like here -- a sharper distinction (between what and what?), an experiment or formal result that could in principle change minds, or an argument that no such thing exists and why. Also feel free to tell me if the swap test is as decisive as my advisor thinks or if it's sneaking in the very criterion it's supposed to test.
