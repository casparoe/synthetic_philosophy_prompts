I'm a graduate student in psychology, and for two years now I've watched the same fight replay in our department's methods seminar: one of our faculty, a dyed-in-the-wool frequentist who thinks the replication crisis is a crisis of incentives, perverse publication practices, and statistical illiteracy, keeps clashing with a Bayesian colleague who thinks the crisis is downstream of null-hypothesis significance testing itself — that a framework licensing binary decisions from p-values against a point null was always going to produce a literature full of noise, no matter how well-behaved the researchers. They've argued past each other enough times that the department chair finally asked them to do an adversarial collaboration and produce a document both can sign. They have not gotten past the first meeting, and I'd like you to draft for them the document they should be aiming at.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: I expect these to include things like the fact that underpowered studies inflate effect-size estimates under either framework, that optional stopping and analytic flexibility corrupt nominal error rates as typically reported, that a p-value is not the probability the null is true and a posterior is not a guarantee of replication, that preregistration constrains researcher degrees of freedom regardless of one's inferential philosophy, and that neither framework by itself dictates what studies get run or published. State each claim precisely enough that both would sign it, without smuggling in either side's framing. Second, a disagreements table stating where they still part ways, each position phrased so its own side would accept the wording: I suspect the real fault lines are whether the crisis is attributable to the logic of NHST or only to its misuse, whether a Bayesian workflow with reasonable priors would have prevented the pattern of non-replicating results or merely redescribed it, and whether concepts like type-I error control retain their value once one abandons a behaviorist, long-run-error rationale for inference. Third, for each disagreement, identify the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — and specify a test, dataset, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. For example, both might accept that the outcomes of large-scale replication projects, or of prediction markets in which scientists bet on which findings replicate, bear on the dispute — but say exactly how, and what each side must predict for the result to count against its own position.

Please write in American English. If you conclude that some portion of their dispute is merely verbal — for instance, if the two are using "evidence" or "error" in different senses — say which portion and explain why, rather than papering over it. The goal is a text both statisticians could actually put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact, not a verdict on who is right.
