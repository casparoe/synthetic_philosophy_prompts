I work as a grants officer at a mid-sized foundation that funds international development programs, and over the past few years I have become increasingly puzzled by what looks like a double standard in how my sector treats evidence. I cannot tell whether the underlying position is principled or simply confused, and I would like help reconstructing it. On one side, for program-level interventions — deworming pills, mosquito net subsidies, cash transfers, textbook distribution — we and most large funders now demand randomized controlled trials, or at minimum a credible quasi-experimental design. An observational study, however careful, is treated as suggestive at best; the whole identity of the evidence-based movement in development is built on the claim that intuition, expert judgment, and before-and-after comparisons mislead, and that only a deliberately constructed counterfactual earns belief. Proposals are routinely rejected for resting on nothing more than a compelling story about a success somewhere else. On the other side, the very same institutions publish confident flagship claims about the deepest determinants of national prosperity — that impartial courts, secure property rights, and constraints on executive power are what made some countries rich — on the basis of evidence that could never survive our own review process: a handful of unrepeatable historical episodes, cross-country correlations, and comparative narratives about pairs of countries that diverged after acquiring different institutions. These are, by the standards we enforce for a two-dollar pill, exactly the confounded, uncontrolled, one-off comparisons we dismiss as storytelling.

The puzzle sharpens when I look at how the second half of the practice actually operates. Nobody can randomize court systems across countries; history ran once. And yet our reports do not respond to this by staying silent about why nations are rich — they respond by quietly applying a different, lower standard to the biggest question than to the smallest ones, as though the stringency of our epistemology tracked how answerable a question is rather than how much rides on it. Meanwhile the same documents insist that context is everything and that institutions cannot be transplanted, while their practical advice consists almost entirely of transplants: independent central banks, property registries, and anti-corruption commissions modeled on successes elsewhere. One chapter will attribute a region's poverty to geography — landlockedness, disease burden — and the next to its institutions, even though the two are historically entangled, since which institutions were implanted where depended heavily on climate and disease in the first place.

So here is what I would like from you. First, reconstruct the implicit epistemology of causation that this dual practice presupposes, if there is one: what view of causal knowledge licenses demanding an experimental counterfactual for a mosquito net while accepting comparative-historical argument for the claim that courts cause growth? Second, tell me whether a single coherent view could make sense of both halves — for example, the idea that knowledge of mechanisms (how institutions channel incentives) travels across contexts in a way that measured effects do not, or the idea that we are entitled to lower our standards precisely where higher ones are unattainable — and say what exactly such a view commits us to believing about what can be learned from a history that ran only once. Third, say which rival epistemologies the practice rules out, and where it is merely underdetermined — compatible with several views rather than committed to any. Finally, tell me what would concretely have to change if the standard were unified in either direction: if the evidentiary bar we apply to bednets were applied to claims about why nations are rich (does honest application end in near-silence, and is that humility or abdication?), or if the comparative-historical bar were applied to program funding (does that license decisions on plausible stories?). I am less interested in whether any particular institutional claim is true than in what a practice like ours is entitled to claim to know, and on what grounds.
