A colleague and I keep circling the same dispute, and I would like a referee. Here is his argument, stated as fairly as I can manage, that deontological constraints are irrational:

(1) If it is wrong of me to kill one person even when doing so is the only way to prevent five other people from being killed, then the wrongness of my act must be grounded in something bad about killings, and that bad-making feature is present in each of the five killings just as it would be present in my one.
(2) Where a bad-making feature is present in several events, an outcome containing one instance of that feature is, in that respect, better than an outcome containing five, whoever produces the instances.
(3) A practically rational agent must act to bring about the outcome that is better in the respects she herself cites to justify her duties.
(4) So if I accept the constraint in (1), rationality requires me to kill the one, and any view that forbids this asks me to act against the very values that ground the prohibition. Constraints are therefore incoherent.
(5) Nor is there anything special about killing. Substitute keeping one's promises, refraining from lying, or giving priority to one's own child, and the argument runs unchanged: any agent-relative duty or permission faces the same fork, on which either its ground is an impartial feature present in every instance (in which case minimizing violations is rationally required) or it has no ground at all.
(6) Hence there are no rationally defensible agent-relative reasons: morality is either impartial maximization or caprice.

Here is where I stand. I want to keep both that I may not kill one to save five and that I may save my own child rather than two strangers. I cannot accept (6). What I want from you is a diagnosis, in three parts.

First, name the premise or step you would reject and explain why it looked compelling. I want an account of the argument's grip, not merely the application of a fallacy label, since each premise strikes me as something I have asserted in other contexts.

Second, price the rejection. Does abandoning your chosen step leave me able to say what, if anything, is bad about the five killings? And does it expose me to the charge that a constraint amounts to caring more about the cleanliness of my own hands than about the victims — the objection that it is self-indulgent to let five die so that I am not the one who kills?

Third, test your repair against the generality claimed in (5). Does your diagnosis vindicate the parental permission as well as the constraint against killing, or only one of them — and if both, does it vindicate so much that the view collapses back into my colleague's conclusion? In particular, if your repair works by making the relevant values or rankings agent-relative, explain why this is not simply impartial maximization refitted with an agent-centered scoring rule: why, that is, the observation that any constraint can be represented as the maximization of some agent-relative value does not show that my colleague was right all along and had merely stated his argument badly.
