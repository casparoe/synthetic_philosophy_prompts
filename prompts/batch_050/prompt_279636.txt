Last night I had a long argument with a friend and came away defending a position I now want stress-tested rather than vindicated. My view is epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: phenomenal properties -- the felt character of pain, of seeing red, of tasting coffee -- are real, non-physical byproducts of physical processes, but they cause nothing. Everything we do and say, including everything we say about our experiences, is fully explained by the physical states that produce the experiences, and the experiences themselves are causal dead ends, along for the ride like a whistle on a locomotive.

My friend's main line of attack was the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experiences cause nothing, then my judging, reporting, remembering, and reasoning about my experiences cannot be causally connected to the experiences themselves, and it seems to follow that my beliefs about my own consciousness are at best lucky accidents and at worst self-undermining -- I have no justified claim to know that I am conscious, even though it is the one thing I seem most certain of. I think the standard reply survives this: my judgments are caused by the physical basis of the experience, which covaries reliably with the experience, so the judgments track the experiences without needing causal contact with them, the way a reliable gauge tracks a temperature without the temperature's phenomenal feel doing any work. My friend says this rescue makes the phenomenal properties epistemically idle in a way that amounts to giving up the view's motivation.

Here is what I want: a map of what would count against epiphenomenalism, sorted by how decisive it would be. Distinguish what would be a clean defeater from what would merely be awkward. Include philosophical arguments (the paradox above, and anything stronger I may be missing), but also consider whether any empirical result could in principle count -- for instance, evidence that phenomenal character as such does causal or informational work that no underlying physical state duplicates -- or whether the view is so constructed that no observation could touch it. On that last point, be honest: if epiphenomenalism has been qualified into unfalsifiability, say whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or just what a metaphysical position looks like. Finally, say what its own serious defenders would concede as counting against it -- not only what critics like my friend claim -- and whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment is genuinely the sharpest exposure or whether the view's soft underbelly lies elsewhere.
