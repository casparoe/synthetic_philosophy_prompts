In discussions of monetary policy I keep running into the same move, and I'd like a careful audit of it rather than another restatement. The move goes back at least to Berkeley, who in The Querist suggested that the true idea of money is not the metal or the paper but something like a ticket or counter: what matters is not what the token is made of but the power it conveys to set other people's industry in motion. Modern versions are everywhere. A columnist I read recently argued that fiat currency is essentially a system of points in a game the state administers -- tokens whose value comes entirely from the rules and conventions of play -- and concluded that much public anxiety about money printing and government debt is therefore confused: if money is just counters, the issuer can create more counters, and the only real question is how the points get distributed. Critics reply that the analogy proves too much and that the points-in-a-game picture is precisely why people underestimate inflation.

Audit the analogy as an argument, not as a slogan. First, give the explicit mapping: what in a national economy corresponds to the game, the counters, the rulebook, the game administrator, the players, and the score. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the columnist's conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Candidates I'd want assessed include: game tokens are usually non-scarce and costlessly reissuable in a way that currency is not, at least not without distributional side effects; points in a game rarely have private debts, wages, and long-term contracts denominated in them; tokens typically cannot be saved across games or traded for tokens of rival games, whereas currencies face exchange rates and capital flight; and the administrator of a game does not usually rely on players' willingness to hold tokens as the store of their life savings. Third, say what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks -- in particular whether it builds in the neutrality of money and a kind of value-nominalism, and whether Berkeley's own version (counters as claims on labor and industry, not mere scorekeeping) is closer to or further from the modern points version than the columnist assumes. Finally, deliver a verdict: does the conclusion that printing-money anxiety is mostly confusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the analogy can't carry that weight, what would a more honest comparison for fiat money be?
