The question on the exam was:

"Peirce and James both call themselves pragmatists about truth, but they mean quite different things by it. Explain each view, making clear how they differ, and say which view better survives the objection that pragmatism just confuses truth with usefulness. Your answer should go beyond defining the two positions."

Here's the student's answer:

"Peirce's account of truth is sometimes called the convergence view: the truth about a question is whatever the community of scientific inquirers agrees on about it. So 'true' means, roughly, 'currently accepted by science', and as inquiry proceeds our beliefs get truer. James takes a different line. For him, truth is a matter of an idea's practical consequences -- an idea is true insofar as believing it is useful or expedient for the believer. This is his famous 'cash-value' metaphor: true ideas are ones you can cash in for successful experience. The key difference is that Peirce ties truth to what a community of inquirers settles on, whereas James ties it to what works for the individual.

The objection is that pragmatism confuses truth with usefulness, since a false belief (e.g. a comforting illusion) might be very useful. This objection hits James much harder than Peirce. James's view seems to allow that a belief could be true for me simply because holding it benefits me, which collapses truth into wishful thinking. Peirce is safe from the objection because on his view truth is fixed by what science says at a given time, and science is not in the business of validating comforting illusions. So Peirce's view better survives the objection.

One might worry on James's behalf that 'useful' shouldn't be read as 'comforting' -- he means ideas that lead us successfully through experience, putting us in touch with reality rather than just pleasing us. But even on this reading, James still defines truth in terms of consequences for believers rather than in terms of correspondence, so the objection stands."
