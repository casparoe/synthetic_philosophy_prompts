Years ago I came across a description of a promotional contest: the prize was a large fixed sum divided by the number of entries received, entries were free and unlimited, and if nobody entered there was no prize at all. I never forgot it, and over the years, with no formal training in decision theory or game theory, I have accumulated a set of intuitions about variants of this setup. I would like to know what principle, if any, unifies them — or, failing that, which of my intuitions I should abandon. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. The symmetric one-shot. A million people, myself included, are each told: you may enter at most once, entry costs nothing, and a prize of $1,000,000 will go to a randomly chosen entrant — but only if exactly one person enters. If zero or several enter, no one gets anything. My verdict: the right move is to enter with probability of roughly one in a million, settled by private randomization; simply entering, and simply abstaining, both feel like mistakes. My reasoning, such as it is, is that everyone else is working through the identical problem, and whatever conclusion I reach, most of them will reach as well.

2. The dissimilar pool. Same contest, but I am told the other million were selected for impulsiveness and a distrust of lotteries — nothing like me. My verdict: now simply entering seems fine. Whatever held me back in case 1 evaporates.

3. The replica. A two-player version, one prize, awarded only if exactly one of us enters. I am told the other player is a perfect copy of me — same memories, same reasoning — deciding independently, with no communication. My verdict: randomize fifty-fifty, and this time the thought that the other will do as I do feels not merely available but decisive.

4. The selfish round. A different structure: each of two strangers may either take $100 for themselves or give $300 to the other; choices are simultaneous, one-shot, anonymous. My verdict: take the $100. The other person will reason as they will, and I cannot control them; forgoing money in the hope that a stranger mirrors me feels like superstition. I notice this sits awkwardly with case 1, where the thought that others reason as I do did real work for me.

5. The published history. The one-winner contest from case 1 is run monthly among the same hundred people, with all past entries made public. My verdict: after a few rounds, simply entering on an understood rotation, or staying out when the record suggests others are due to enter, is perfectly rational — no coin flips required. Public information seems to license coordination that private symmetry alone cannot.

6. The dividing prize. The original contest: $1,000,000 divided by total entries, unlimited free entries. My verdict: send exactly one. Zero feels like passing up a free ticket; a thousand feels wrong even though each additional entry strictly raises my chance of winning and costs me nothing. I cannot fully justify that last part, and it is the verdict I trust least.

My tentative principle, if I have one, is something like: choose as you would want everyone relevantly like you to choose, on the assumption that they will settle where you settle. But I can see it struggles with case 4, and I am not sure what work "relevantly like" is doing between cases 1 and 2.

What I want: a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply them mechanically; for each clause, the case above that forces it; the principle's verdicts on cases I have not asked about — say, a version where I can watch entries accumulate in real time before a deadline, and one where I may coordinate with exactly one friend beforehand; and an honest account of which of my six verdicts no reasonable principle can accommodate and I should therefore drop. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether there is a coherent principle behind my verdicts and, if so, what it is — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
