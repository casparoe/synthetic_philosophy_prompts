I am a novelist preparing to interview a philosopher of mind at a literary festival event tied to my next book. My guest defends a view she calls symbol-level realism, built on Hofstadter's careenium image: picture the brain as a vast pool table on which countless tiny marbles (the simms) and a smaller number of huge, symbol-inscribed balls (the simmballs) endlessly careen off one another. Her claim is that the big balls genuinely shove the small ones around -- that symbol-level patterns, and not merely the neural substrate they ride on, do real causal work. My novel turns on precisely this: its narrator maintains that a character she invented gradually came, over years of drafting, to determine her own decisions, and I want the philosophy underneath the fiction to be respectable rather than decorative.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. The picture is easy; I want her pushed onto the hard part. Include at least:

- one question on the causal-exclusion worry: if the physics of the small balls already fixes every trajectory, what work is left for the big balls to do -- with a follow-up keyed to the predictable reply that one motion can admit two causal stories at different levels of description;
- one question on the gap between a symbol-level description being predictively indispensable and its being literally causal, with a follow-up for the reply that predictive grip of the right kind is all causation ever amounted to;
- one question on what it would even mean, inside the careenium, to check who shoves whom: is there an intervention or counterfactual one could run that would settle it, or is the view so framed that no arrangement of the balls could count against it;
- one question pressing her on my novelist's case: can a pattern that exists only across a writer's habits, notebooks, and readers have done the shoving, or does her view confine symbol-level causation to single skulls -- and if the latter, what principled line excludes my narrator?

Keep the whole set under 400 words, with each question at most two sentences plus a one-sentence follow-up.
