Okay so this is going to sound odd but I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a craft problem that has become a philosophy problem and I can't separate them anymore. I got obsessed with this idea of Hofstadter's (Gödel, Escher, Bach guy, and the later book where he talks about his wife dying) that a person's "I" isn't confined to one skull -- that when you know someone deeply, a coarse-grained copy of them lives in you, running on your neurons, so after they die part of them literally persists in the people who knew them. Not as a metaphor, he means it. In my current book the main character is a widow whose dead husband keeps "answering" her in arguments, predicting what people will say in his voice, making jokes only he would make. My problem, three drafts in, is that I can feel the difference between scenes where the husband is genuinely present in her (the Hofstadter thing) and scenes where he's just being remembered -- the memory scenes feel flat, sentimental, dead in a way the other ones don't. But I can't articulate the difference, which means I can't write toward it reliably, and this is my whole book so I need to.

So: turn Hofstadter's idea into a procedure I can run on any scene. Give me an ordered set of tests -- like, the questions I should ask about a scene in sequence, what a passing answer looks like for each, and where a failure means "cut or rewrite, this is nostalgia not presence." Some distinctions I suspect matter and you should decide whether they survive the translation: whether the character in the widow's head produces responses she couldn't have generated as herself (novelty vs. replay of stored recordings), whether the inner-husband has enough grain/resolution to count as a copy at all versus a cartoon, whether persistence needs the copy to still be updating or whether a frozen one counts, and whether it matters that the copy lives in a brain that's already running someone else. For each test say honestly where it's going to misfire or where it hands things back to my judgment because it's an aesthetic call not a conceptual one.

Then run the finished procedure on these two scenes from my draft, because I need to watch it work: (1) the widow, at a dinner party, hears someone make a comment and suddenly knows exactly what her husband would have muttered in response, word for word, and has to leave the room; (2) she keeps his voicemail greeting on her old phone and calls it when she can't sleep. I suspect your procedure will say scene 1 passes and scene 2 fails, but I've been attached to the voicemail scene since draft one, so if it kills that scene, tell me anyway and tell me what the procedure can and can't be trusted on. I'm not precious, just stubborn.
