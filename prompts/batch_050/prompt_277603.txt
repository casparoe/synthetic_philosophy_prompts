So my two roommates got into it after we watched this biopic about a famous architect who died like fifteen years ago (not naming him, doesn't matter, but he's real and the movie used his real name and everything). The movie has him take credit for a building design that a junior colleague of his actually came up with, and it's a big dramatic scene, and it never happened. It also merges his two wives into one character and shuffles the timeline so it looks like he walked off a project out of cowardice when really the funding fell through. Roommate A says the movie is basically a lie about a real person. Roommate B says it's a drama, not a documentary. They went at it for an hour and I want a referee.

A's opening move: if a newspaper ran that theft scene as reporting, it'd be straight-up defamation. Slapping "based on a true story" on it doesn't change the content, it just changes the legal exposure, and the only reason they get away with it is the guy is dead and can't sue, which if anything makes it slimier. The studio borrows the real man's name and reputation to sell tickets, then hides behind "it's fiction" the second anyone complains about accuracy.

B: come on. Nobody walks into a biopic thinking every scene is documentary footage. Compressed timelines and invented scenes are just how the genre works, it's been like this forever, and if you demand factual accuracy you don't get better movies, you get no movies. The director's whole defense was that the scene is emotionally true -- the guy really was ruthless and ambitious, the scene just dramatizes a trait he actually had.

A: then why not invent a fictional architect who's ruthless and ambitious? Because the movie *wants* the epistemic authority of a true story -- it's marketed as the real man's story, people leave the theater thinking they learned something about him -- while wanting the creative freedom of fiction. You can't have both. And "everyone knows it's dramatized" is just empirically false, walk outside and ask people what they think the guy did, they'll repeat the movie.

B: changing the name defeats the whole point, which is to reflect on this actual person and his era, not some made-up guy. And look, there *were* real attribution disputes about his buildings. The theft scene is a compression of real stuff, not an invention from nothing.

A: I'd actually grant that distinction and I think it sinks you. Compression preserves the verdict and changes the packaging. But if the real disputes were small and mostly settled in the colleague's favor, then "compressing" them into he-stole-the-building flips the verdict. That's not compression, that's reversal.

B: okay, but then give me the principle. Every film ever made merges characters and moves events around -- the merged-wives thing doesn't bother you at all, and it should, by your lights. "Any selection shapes the verdict" cuts against your test, because literally every editing choice shapes what the audience concludes. So either all historical fiction is defamation or you owe me a line between permissible shaping and impermissible falsifying, and I don't think you have one.

Then B also tried: even if the movie is inaccurate, no harm was done, because the man is dead and the dead can't be harmed. A said we wrong dead people all the time -- if you promise someone on their deathbed you'll burn their diaries and then you publish them, you've wronged them, full stop -- so "he's dead" can't do the work. B said that might show duties about the dead exist but not that a movie anyone can ignore is a wrong on the level of breaking a deathbed promise.

So: go through it round by round. Who's right about whether the movie makes actual claims about the guy, whether "audiences know it's fiction" works as a defense, whether the emotional-truth / compression-of-real-disputes defense survives A's reversal point, whether A actually owes a principled line between the merged wives and the stolen design (and if so, where is it), and the dead-can't-be-harmed exchange. And is there anything they're both missing?
