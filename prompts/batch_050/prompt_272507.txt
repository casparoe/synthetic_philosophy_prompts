I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I'm working on a piece about whether individuals actually shape history. The Hitler case keeps coming up as the litmus test: would something like Nazi Germany have happened without him? And the weird thing is, this argument has been going for over a century -- the Carlyle-style Great Man camp vs the Tolstoy/Marx-flavored structural camp -- and as far as I can tell nobody has budged and both sides just keep restating their opening positions. Great Man people point to how contingent the Nazi rise was (a few elections, backroom deals in 1932-33, Hitler's personal obsessions driving specific policies), structuralists point to Versailles, the Depression, German nationalism, and say someone was going to fill that slot.

So: is this a real disagreement or is something fishy going on? My hunch is the two sides aren't even answering the same question -- one is asking about which events occur, the other about their character and timing -- but maybe there's a deeper problem, like that counterfactuals about whole societies just can't be tested the way you can test, say, whether a bridge would have collapsed without a particular bolt. Diagnose the impasse for me: what kind of disagreement is it really, what does each side treat as evidence that the other side doesn't, and what would genuine progress even look like here? A better method? A clarified question? Or should we expect it to stay stuck forever, and if so why?
