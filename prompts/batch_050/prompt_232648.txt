I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting an explainer on the psychology of humour. While reporting, I asked a psychologist why audiences so often greet jokes about recent disasters with the complaint that it's "too soon," and she waved the question off with the old line that comedy is just tragedy plus time. Since then I've heard the same formula from a stand-up comic defending a bit about a celebrity death that happened last week, and from an editor explaining why our satire desk could run a piece about a medieval massacre but not one about last month's flood. The phrase clearly does real work for people — it ends conversations — but when I tried to write one precise sentence stating what it asserts, I found I couldn't, and I'd like to fix that before filing.

Please separate the distinct claims the slogan could be making, from the trivial to the radical, and give me a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible, or simply false. I can see candidate readings — that distress decays into amusement as a psychological law, that temporal distance makes a violation feel benign and therefore safely mockable, that the claim is really about cultural memory rather than any individual's feelings, that it's a disguised norm of etiquette about when joking becomes permissible rather than a fact about humour at all, and that it's a radical thesis that all comedy is recycled suffering — but I don't trust my own taxonomy. For each reading, say whether the evidence from the main theories of humour (superiority, incongruity, relief, benign violation) would count for or against it, and note where the theories disagree among themselves about what "time" is even doing.

Two wrinkles from my reporting that I'd like the analysis to engage with. First, a comedy writer told me the formula has it backwards: his best material comes when the wound is fresh, and what audiences call "too soon" is often when jokes land hardest in the room. Second, a thought experiment I want to pose to my sources: if immersive media could make a centuries-old atrocity feel psychologically present — make viewers feel as if it happened to them, yesterday — would the slogan predict that jokes about it stop being funny? If so, the operative variable seems to be felt distance rather than calendar time, which I'd think changes which readings survive. Finally, tell me which reading my psychologist source most plausibly meant, and whether that reading actually licenses dismissing the "too soon" question or instead makes it more pressing. Lay the whole thing out as a numbered menu of readings with verdicts, so I can use it as the skeleton of the piece and as a checklist for re-interviewing my sources.
