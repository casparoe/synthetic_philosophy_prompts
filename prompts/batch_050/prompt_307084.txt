help me put together a handout for my grad seminar on infinite ethics. we're past the basic "infinity breaks utilitarianism" week and i want a worked example the students can actually chew on rather than just nod at.

Construct a thought experiment to these specs:

- The setup is two policies some community has to choose between, where the consequences play out across an infinite sequence of generations (or seasons, or whatever frame works). Keep it earthy — a village council deciding how to run the grain reserve forever, that kind of thing, not a galactic utilon maximizer.
- Every person in the story has a finite, positive welfare level. Nobody is miserable, nobody is infinitely blissful, no negative numbers anywhere.
- The two principles i want pitted against eachother: (1) a dominance rule — world A is better than world B iff everyone in A is at least as well off as their counterpart in B and at least someone is strictly better off; and (2) a limit-of-averages rule — compare the limit of average welfare across the first n members of the sequence as n grows without bound.
- The engineered outcome: your two worlds have to come out incomparable under the dominance rule (neither dominates the other) but strictly ranked under the limit-of-averages rule. And both limits have to actually exist — no oscillating averages that fail to converge, that's cheating at this stage.
- The scenario itself should be statable in one short paragraph, and the verdicts have to fall out of the numbers, not be stipulated.

Then, after the scenario:

1. Walk through both verdicts, showing the arithmetic for the limit rule explicitly.
2. Here's the kicker: take the world the limit rule ranked better and reorder the same people across the sequence — same multiset of welfare levels, same lives, just arranged in a different order — so that the limit of averages stops existing or lands somewhere different. Then explain what this shows about whether the limit rule is secretly leaning on an arbitrary ordering of people who don't actually come labeled 1, 2, 3, ... in the world.
3. Finish with a short note on how someone who wants to defend the limit rule anyway would push back (e.g., insisting the generations really do arrive in a fixed temporal order, so the reordering move is illegitimate), and whether that defense generalizes to cases where the infinite population is spread across space instead of time.

Write the whole thing in American English.
