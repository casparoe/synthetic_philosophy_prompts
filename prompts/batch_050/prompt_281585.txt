Got into it with my friend again last night (she's the Burke person in our group, I'm the one who keeps reading rationalist-adjacent stuff and getting yelled at for it) and we landed on Chesterton's fence, specifically whether the Four Pests campaign in Mao's China is actually the slam-dunk case for it that everyone treats it as. Quick recap of the episode as I understand it, correct me if I have it wrong: late 1950s, sparrows get declared a pest because they eat grain seed, the population gets mobilized to kill them en masse, sparrow numbers crash, locusts and other insects the sparrows had been eating explode, harvests get wrecked and this contributes to the famine, and eventually sparrows have to be brought back in from elsewhere. She says this is exactly what the fence principle predicts: nobody bothered to figure out what the sparrow was *for* before destroying it, and the function only became visible once it was gone. I said the case is too easy and proves almost nothing, because even a fence-skeptic concedes you shouldn't do things for literally no reason with zero investigation. We stalled there.

So: take the episode and run the analysis properly. First, the mapping -- what exactly is the "fence" here (a species? an ecological role? the accumulated agricultural practice around it?), who occupies the position of Chesterton's reformer, and what would "understanding the fence before removing it" have concretely required in 1958? Was the relevant knowledge available but ignored, or genuinely absent? That matters and I want it treated separately, because "the knowledge existed and was suppressed for political reasons" is a very different lesson than "the knowledge couldn't have been had." Second, my worry: is this case actually evidence for the fence principle, or is it the principle's equivalent of a free throw -- a case where literally any decision procedure except pure vandalism gets it right? If the principle only ever gets tested on catastrophes, how would we ever catch it being wrong, i.e. cases where fence-preservation kept a harmful practice alive for decades?

And third, the part my friend refused to engage with: Chesterton's fence seems to smuggle in the assumption that the fence is old and was built by people with situated knowledge responding to real conditions. But the Great Leap era was itself tearing up old arrangements at speed -- collectivization was fence-demolition on a massive scale, and by 1958 a lot of what counted as "the existing order" had been standing for about five minutes. So what does the Burkean heuristic say when the status quo is itself the product of a recent rupture? Does it tell you to conserve whatever happens to be in place now, which would have meant conserving the revolution, or does it smuggle in a deeper preference for whatever is genuinely evolved versus imposed, and if the latter, doesn't the principle need a whole extra theory to tell those apart before it can advise anyone about anything? Tell me where the theory is actually silent here instead of letting it mumble.
