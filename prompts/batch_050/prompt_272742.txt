I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about the research tradition that distinguishes tight cultures (strong norms, low tolerance for deviance) from loose ones (weak norms, high tolerance). The literature tends to argue that tightness is a functional response to chronic threat -- dense populations, disease pressure, resource scarcity, conflict -- and that neither pole is simply better. Fine. But I keep noticing that when I go case by case, my own judgments don't line up cleanly with any single version of that story, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, is actually generating my verdicts. Here they are. Heavy fines for littering in a flood-prone city where blocked drains routinely drown neighborhoods: justified, and I want them enforced. Zero-tolerance ticketing for jaywalking on quiet residential streets: oppressive, a bad use of the same machinery. A rail system where drivers are disciplined for thirty-second delays: on reflection I think this is good, because the network effects are real, even though it sounds extreme out of context. A community enforcing strict rules about how women dress in public: bad, and not redeemed by being sincerely held or widely supported. A hospital kitchen with draconian hygiene rules: obviously good. Street food vendors in a poor city shut down for failing the same hygiene rules: my gut says this is cruel, even though the contamination risk is real. Employees who break explicit protocol to report a safety hazard up the chain: I want them protected and praised, and I want the protocol to make room for that. And one more: a loose, individualistic society that handles everyday life beautifully but cannot coordinate a pandemic response versus a tight society that handles the pandemic beautifully but crushes eccentrics -- I find I can't say which is better without knowing what threats are coming, which feels like either an insight or a dodge.

My tentative principle, when I try to state it, is something like: tightness is justified in proportion to the severity and probability of the harms that deviance imposes on third parties, and unjustified when the rule protects only the rule itself, or tradition, or the sensibilities of the majority. That handles the littering, the jaywalking, the train drivers, and the dress codes. But I don't think it handles the food vendors -- by my own principle I should endorse shutting them down, and I don't -- and I'm not sure it handles the whistleblowers, since praising protocol-breaking seems to endorse looseness exactly where the harms of deviance are largest. So: is there a single principle, or a small set of them, that captures most of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which work? Please say which of my verdicts your best candidate principle has to overturn or strain to accommodate, rather than quietly saving them all at the price of vagueness, and what it predicts about cases I didn't list -- mandatory evacuation orders, say, or open-plan offices with rigid meeting etiquette. I'd also like to know where this reasoning is most likely to go wrong: what evidence or argument would lead you to a different principle, and whether my inability to rank the two societies in the last case is the tell that my whole approach is situation-relative in a way no tidy principle will survive.
