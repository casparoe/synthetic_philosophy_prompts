I'm the volunteer treasurer of the residents' management company for our block of flats (24 flats, shared freehold, I'm one of three directors). Our communal boiler is six years old and running fine, but the firm that installed it went bust last month and the model's been discontinued, so there's basically no failure data out there. The heating engineer's survey, which cost us £400, says only that units of this type typically need full replacement somewhere between 10 and 26 years from installation. So: remaining life somewhere between 4 and 20 years, and that's literally all we know.

On Thursday we have to vote on whether to buy a five-year breakdown-and-replacement contract a broker is offering, a one-off £11,000 out of the reserve fund. Replacing the boiler ourselves would run to about £30k, plus emergency callout costs and 24 furious households if it died mid-winter.

Here's my problem. Two of us have already "done the maths" and reached opposite conclusions, and as far as I can tell they've made the same assumption:

- Priya spread the probability evenly over the remaining lifespan -- every year from 4 to 20 equally likely -- and gets roughly a 6% chance it fails inside the contract period, so £11k is a rip-off.
- Martin spread the probability evenly over the annual breakdown risk instead, treating the yearly failure chance as the unknown quantity, anywhere from 1-in-20 up to 1-in-4, and gets the boiler more likely than not to fail within five years, so £11k is a bargain.

Martin reckons this is literally that Bertrand paradox thing, where "equal ignorance" gives different answers depending on how you carve up the possibilities, and the principle of indifference (principal?) can't tell you which carving is correct. Priya says hers is obviously the natural reading because the engineer talked about lifespan, not risk rates, and Martin is overcomplicating it. Our third director wants to flip a coin, which is presumable the one option worse than both.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting -- a page or so, British English please. It needs:

1. An actual bottom line -- buy the contract or don't -- because "this is philosophically interesting" doesn't keep 24 flats warm.
2. A clear explanation of why both spreadsheets count as "being neutral about what we don't know" and yet disagree. I want the other directors to genuinely get this, not just be told it.
3. Your honest take on whether either carving is better principled -- there's been muttering about "constant risk" being special, and something about spreading it evenly over the log of a quantity, whatever that means -- or whether neutrality just isn't neccessarily achievable here at all.
4. The strongest argument for the option you recommend against, taken seriously, because the losing side will be sitting across the table.
5. Whether any tie-break that doesn't pretend we know more than we do (worst-case reasoning, regret minimising, whatever) should genuinely sway us, or whether that's a cop-out dressed up as rigour.

Don't pad it and don't give me a reading list -- I need something I can defend out loud on Thursday.
