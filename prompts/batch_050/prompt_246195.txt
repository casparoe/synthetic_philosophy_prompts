I work as a senior claims assessor for a property and cargo insurer. Several times a month I handle contested claims in which our policies exclude losses attributable to the nature of the insured goods themselves — goods that spoiled, corroded, overheated, or shattered, as we say, on their own — while covering the very same losses when an external cause is responsible. Each disputed claim is worth enough to be fought over, my determination has to be made within ten working days, it has to survive arbitration, and the lawyers on both sides will attack whatever reasoning I commit to the file. The recurring difficulty is that the exclusion clause asks, in effect, whether the lost goods possessed a certain disposition — perishability, brittleness, a tendency to self-heat — and whether the loss was a genuine manifestation of it. At present I have no consistent method for answering that question, and different assessors on my team resolve similar files differently.

Please turn what philosophy knows about dispositions into a procedure my team could actually run on such a claim. Concretely, I need: (a) an ordered list of questions to ask of a file, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each before moving on; (b) a translation of the relevant distinctions into claims language, preserving at least the differences between a disposition being present while its manifestation is blocked by something extrinsic (the well-packed glass that survives the fall), the disposition being removed at the very moment it would have manifested, and the loss occurring when the goods never had the disposition at all (perfectly sturdy goods broken by extraordinary handling) — because each of these lands on a different side of our exclusion, and collapsing them is exactly what the opposing lawyers exploit; (c) guidance for the common situation in which the only test we can run is on the post-loss remains of the goods, where the loss event itself has altered the very thing being tested, so that the test may be incapable of revealing whether the disposition was there beforehand — when such a test result should count as evidence at all, and what to do when it cannot; and (d) explicit stopping points where the procedure hands the file back to a human because the remaining question is one of contractual wording or fairness rather than of what the goods were like. Mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three claims: a container of fruit that arrived rotten, where the shipper claims the refrigeration unit failed and our own surveyor reports that it ran within specification; a cargo of coal that self-heated and ignited mid-voyage despite being stowed exactly as such cargo is normally stowed; and a crate of laboratory glassware that shattered after being dropped by a forklift, where the shipper argues that any glassware would have broken. Include at least one case in which the procedure delivers an outcome I will not like — paying a claim I expected to exclude, or excluding one I expected to pay — and close with a section on what the procedure cannot do, especially the points where the metaphysics of dispositions and a court's interpretation of the exclusion clause are liable to come apart.
