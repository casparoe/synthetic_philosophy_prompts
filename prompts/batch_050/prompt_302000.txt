I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and last week I had two sources on one call for a piece about whether we should intervene in nature to reduce wild animal suffering. They basically stopped talking to me and started arguing with eachother, and I've been stuck on who actually won ever since.

Marta's a welfare biologist. Her opening: the raw numbers swamp everything else. Most wild animals die within days or weeks of being born, so suffering in nature plausibly dwarfs factory farming, and at minimum we should fund serious research into gentler interventions — oral vaccines, fertility control instead of culls, that kind of thing.

Dev's a conservation ecologist. His reply: the history of ecosystem intervention is a graveyard of good intentions. Remove or repress one species and effects show up three trophic levels away that nobody modeled. And anyway, an ecosystem isn't a sum of welfare points — predation and struggle are partly constitutive of what makes it valuable in the first place.

Marta: valuable to whom? That sounds like human aesthetic preference dressed up as ecology. And refusing to act isn't a neutral option — we already manage habitats, run culls, spread disease around. The doing/allowing distinction doesn't get him off the hook.

Dev: there's still a difference between managing an ecosystem for stability and re-engineering it for welfare, and her whole case leans on welfare measurements in wild animals that are mostly guesswork. Cortisol levels don't tell you what it's like to be a vole. Also, r-strategists dying young isn't obviously tragic — a short life isn't automatically a bad life.

Marta's last move before they both had to run: his epistemic humility is suspiciously selective. He's confident enough to keep managing ecosystems as usual, just never confident enough to manage them kindly.

So — referee this for me. Go point by point: the "ecosystems have non-welfare value" claim, the doing/allowing move, the measurement objection, the short-lives-aren't-bad-lives point, and the selective-humility accusation. Say who's right on each, who wins overall, and what both of them are missing. Verdict up front, and keep the whole thing under 400 words since it's going in my notes.
