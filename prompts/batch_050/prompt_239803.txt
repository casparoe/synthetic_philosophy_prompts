Before I take a side on the actual infinite, I want to know what I should be asking of a view. Write the desiderata for an account of infinity in mathematics: what any adequate account must deliver — which established results it has to preserve (Cantor's diagonal argument, the uncountability of the reals), which puzzles it has to dissolve rather than merely tolerate (Galileo's paradox that the squares are both a proper subset of and equinumerous with the naturals, Hilbert's fully occupied hotel that always has room for more guests), and which distinctions it has to respect (potential versus completed infinity, mathematical existence versus physical realization) — with the case or argument motivating each requirement. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp, whether Cantorian set theorists, constructivists, or strict finitists. In particular, I suspect that the principle that a whole is always larger than any of its proper parts and the principle that collections that pair off one-to-one are the same size cannot both be honored once infinite totalities are admitted: if that subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and say what each choice costs, because that is the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, assess how orthodox Cantorian set theory, a potentialist picture on which infinity is only ever a process, and strict finitism fare against the list.
