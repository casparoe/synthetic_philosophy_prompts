I've been working through Confucian role ethics in a reading group on comparative moral theory, and last week we got stuck trying to figure out whether my own case-intuitions hang together or are just a pile of culturally inherited reactions. I want to outsource the reflective equilibrium. Here are my verdicts, stated as plainly as I hold them. First: a son discovers that his father has been evading taxes for years; he neither reports him nor confronts him, but quietly arranges for the arrears to be repaid out of family savings. Defensible, I think — this seems like a descendant of the famous exchange in the Analects where a governor boasts of an upright villager who testified against his own sheep-stealing father, and Confucius replies that where he comes from uprightness consists in fathers and sons shielding one another. Second: a daughter reports her physically abusive father to the police. Also defensible — though I've never been able to articulate to myself why this differs from the first case. Third: an engineer sees his manager falsifying safety reports, remonstrates privately twice, is rebuffed, and then goes to the regulator. Clearly right, and the order matters. Fourth: the same engineer goes straight to the newspapers without any private remonstrance. Permissible, maybe, but something is lost — though I can't say whether what's lost is moral or merely aesthetic. Fifth: a young man declines a prestigious job abroad to remain near his ailing mother. Admirable, but I don't think it was obligatory, and I'd hesitate to call someone a bad son for leaving. Sixth: a capable minister serves a cruel ruler faithfully and efficiently for a decade, reasoning that his role as minister requires loyalty; he never remonstrates and never resigns. Not fine — I have a strong sense that Confucianism has the resources to condemn him, and that a role ethic that can't condemn him has collapsed into mere job description.

So: is there a single principle — one that could plausibly be called Confucian role ethics rather than generic partialism or virtue ethics in Confucian dress — that captures all six verdicts? Please state it precisely, clause by clause, with each clause motivated by the case that forces it. I'm especially interested in how it handles the first-versus-second pair, since the shielding norm and the reporting verdict seem to pull apart, and in whether the remonstrance requirement in cases three and six is one principle or two (does the employee owe the same duty of graduated remonstrance as the minister, or is hierarchy-load-bearing here?). If no principle saves all six, tell me which verdict is most expendable and why it should be the one to go rather than another.

Finally, two further asks. Once you have your best principle, run it against the case I keep circling without resolution: a son who is also a physician is asked by his father to backdate an insurance document, where the son's professional role and his filial role issue directly opposed verdicts — does the principle say which role wins, and does it have anything to say about whether professional roles count as roles in the Confucian sense at all, or only family and political ones? And then the meta-question: does your principle genuinely make role-relationships the fundamental unit of moral analysis, or does it quietly install an impartial backstop — harm to third parties, say, or some threshold of gravity — that is doing the real work in cases two, four, and six, with the Confucian machinery as decoration? If the latter, I'd rather hear that said plainly than have the principle dressed up as more distinctive than it is.
