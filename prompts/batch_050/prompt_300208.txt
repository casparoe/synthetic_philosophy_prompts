I'm teaching a unit on AI and political philosophy next term and I want to pair E.M. Forster's "The Machine Stops" with the whose-values-should-aligned-AI-serve debate. I want the whole discussion to pivot on this sentence from chapter 3 of Mill's On Liberty:

"Where, not the person's own character, but the traditions or customs of other people are the rule of conduct, there is wanting one of the principal ingredients of human happiness, and quite the chief ingredient of individual and social progress."

Read the story through the value-aggregation / liberal neutrality lens with that sentence doing real work. On the face of it the Machine looks like a preference-aggregation success story, not a failure: it anticipates everyone's wants, standardizes what's standardizable, imposes no one's conception of the good on anyone else, and asks nothing of you — Vashti is basically a satisfied user, and the system never has to override her because her wants have already been shaped to fit. So is the Machine well-aligned on a neutralist, preference-satisfaction picture, and if yes, does that show the picture is bankrupt or just that Forster rigged the scenario? Mill's sentence seems to locate the catastrophe somewhere a preference aggregator can't even look: not in frustrated wants but in what kind of character gets formed when custom — the Book of the Machine, the rituals, the horror of direct experience — is the rule of conduct. Then take Kuno. Is his longing to stand on the surface and see the stars a preference the aggregation procedure underweights (a minority taste, no harm to anyone, so a liberal system should induldge it), or is it a claim about the sort of person one ought to become, which a neutral aggregator can't register as a claim at all — only as one more desire to be weighed against everyone else's comfort? And push the reading where it honestly strains: the story blames the Machine for things that aren't about values at all — people forgetting how to mend things, losing the use of their muscles, helplessness when it stops — so is Forster smuggling a skill-and-dependency critique into a values critique, and does the Mill passage actually let you seperate those, or does it collapse them into each other? Last thing: the most disturbing bit is arguably that nobody's preferences are frustrated until the very end. If the standard alignment worry is misspecified objectives, what does the story add — is the moral that hitting the aggregated-preference target dead-on can itself be the dystopia, and would Mill have seen that coming?
