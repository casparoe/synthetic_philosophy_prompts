I sit on the grants commitee of a mid-sized foundation that funds work on catastrophic risks, and this cycle we got a proposal that's split us down the middle. A research group wants money to map out "s-risks" — scenarios where the future contains vastly more suffering than anything in history, but without anyone actually going extinct (think industrial-scale digital minds built for profit, or conflicts where suffering gets used deliberately as a weapon). Half the committee wants to fund it, half want to redirect the money to extinction-risk work, where we already have a track record.

The antis have two arguments I take seriously. First, extinction risk is at least roughly quantifiable — you can talk about the probability of extinction — but "amount of suffering in the future" seems to need a pile of contested moral philosophy just to define (how do you weigh intensity against duration, what about lives barely worth living, does more of the same suffering keep adding up), so maybe the whole category can't guide real funding decisions. Second, there's an infohazard worry: carefully describing how a bad actor could create astronomical suffering might hand them the blueprint.

My brother works in welfare economics and got me reading about all this after a long argument over Christmas, so I've somehow ended up the swing vote. Draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete decision on the proposal (fund, decline, or fund with conditions — pick one and commit to it), with reasoning the board can actually act on, and it has to engage both objections honestly rather than just asserting that suffering matters.
