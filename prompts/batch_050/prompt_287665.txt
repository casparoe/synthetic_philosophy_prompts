I am drafting a position paper for a graduate seminar on the ethics of rescue, and before I commit to it in writing I want an audit of whether my views actually cohere. My commitments are the following:

(a) In the standard rescue choice -- I can save one stranger from death or save five strangers from death, where all are equally needy, equally at risk, and I bear no special obligations to anyone -- I am morally required to save the five. Saving the one instead would be wrong, not merely a permissible eccentricity.

(b) Taurek's central premise strikes me as obviously correct: there is no composite subject, "the five," who would suffer a greater loss than the one would. Each death is a loss only to the person who dies. An outcome can be worse for more people, but there is no one for whom the five deaths are worse than the one death, and so no impersonal "sum of badness" that is larger in the one outcome than the other.

(c) I am drawn to a contractualist picture on which the wrongness of an act is exhausted by its being unjustifiable to some person -- morality concerns what we owe to each individual, not the production of impersonally better states of affairs.

(d) Within a single life, aggregation is unproblematic. It can be rational, even required, for one person to accept a lesser harm now to avert a greater harm later, and a surgeon may permissibly impose a smaller harm on a patient to prevent a much larger harm to that same patient. I see nothing puzzling about weighing harms against one another here.

(e) It is impermissible to kill one innocent bystander in order to save five others, as in the transplant case, and impermissible to impose a lethal burden on one person as a means of averting a threat to five. Constraints against using people hold even when the numbers favor violating them.

(f) When I cannot save everyone and the competing claims are of equal strength, a procedure giving each side an equal chance -- a fair coin toss between the one and the five -- fully satisfies the demands of fairness. If the coin comes down for the one and I save the one, no one has been wronged, including the five.

(g) Partiality is permissible: faced with a choice between saving my own child and saving five strangers, I may save my child, and I do not think the numbers override special obligations of that kind.

What I would like is not a survey of the literature but a consistency check of this particular set. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and what exactly is the source of the friction -- which premise in my own reasoning does the work? Which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and why? Please order the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for the worst of them, tell me what the least costly revision would be and precisely what it would cost me -- which intuition, principle, or case verdict I would have to surrender, and what further commitments that surrender would ramify into.
