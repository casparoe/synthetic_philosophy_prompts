I'm a third-year PhD student working on philosophy of science (laws and modality mostly) and next semester I'm TAing an upper-level metaphysics seminar where the dispositions unit always goes badly. The students read the standard stuff on finks and masks and come away thinking the debate is just "conditional analysis had a bug, we patched it," which flattens everything. So I want to make them a decision tree handout that forces them to actually locate themselves: starting from something like "a glass can be fragile even if it never breaks," each node should be a question they can answer without knowing where it leads, and each leaf a position. I want the tree built from the logical space first — does the disposition reduce to a categorical basis plus laws, does the analysis need to survive finking and masking or can those be handled some other way, are powers fundamental or grounded — and only then the named positions placed in it, not a list of names with a tree pasted on.

Constraints on what has to show up: the leaves must at least include the simple conditional analysis, Lewis-style reformed conditional analysis (the one that builds the fink-cases into the conditional), the intrinsic-categorical-basis picture, dispositional essentialism, and a primitivist powers view; if your tree generates a coherent combination of answers that nobody defends, keep the leaf anyway and say in a sentence whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. The diagnostic cases at the nodes should be concrete — the styrofoam-packed glass and the antidote case for masks, the live/dead wire and Martin-style electro-fink for finks, and at least one reverse-cycle or mimicking case if you think those earn a node — and phrased so a student answers by reacting to the case, not by recognizing a label. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely separate (I suspect the reformed conditional analysis and the intrinsic-basis view will be trouble, but tell me if I'm wrong) rather than pretending the tree resolves them.

Finally, since I'll be handing this to students as if it were authoritative, be honest about its weak points: tell me which node you think is most likely to mis-sort someone whose considered view is actually fine, and what kind of case or argument would make you restructure the tree — e.g. would a convincing example of an extrinsic disposition, or a case where the categorical basis itself is dispositional, break the ordering you chose?
