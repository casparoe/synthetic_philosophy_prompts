In my philosophy of the social sciences course this term, we spent three weeks on Popper's The Poverty of Historicism, and I keep going back and forth on where the debate he started should actually go next. The core of his argument, as our seminar reconstructed it, runs roughly as follows: the course of history is strongly influenced by the growth of human knowledge; we cannot rationally predict the future growth of our knowledge, since to predict a discovery in advance would be to make it now; therefore we cannot predict the long-term course of history; and therefore there can be no laws of historical development of the kind historicists claim.

I now have to choose a topic for my final research paper (around 6,000 words, which I may also use as a writing sample for graduate applications), and I want to work on whichever open question in this area matters most. Below are the candidates I have identified. If you think I have missed one that outranks most of these, you may add it, but please don't pad the list.

1. The scope question. Does Popper's argument rule out laws of history as such, or only unconditional prophecies? Conditional, law-like generalizations of the form "when a state's fiscal capacity collapses while competition among its elites intensifies, political instability follows" seem untouched by the knowledge-growth argument, since they forecast no novel ideas. If so, the argument's real target shrinks considerably.

2. The self-application problem. Popper pairs his anti-historicism with a defense of piecemeal social engineering: small interventions, learning from error. But if social outcomes depend on unpredictable future knowledge, why should the feedback loops of piecemeal reform be more reliable than historicist prophecy? Can a principled line be drawn between the social knowledge Popper permits and the kind he forbids, or is the boundary ad hoc?

3. The reflexivity question. Predictions about society can alter society — what Popper himself called the Oedipus effect, where a prediction helps bring about or prevent what it predicts. Is this a deep obstacle to laws of history, or merely a problem for publicized forecasts that a theory of conditional tendencies escapes?

4. The empirical pressure. Recent quantitative work on long-run history — attempts to model state formation and recurring cycles of instability across centuries from large datasets — claims to find statistical regularities. Does any of this genuinely bear on Popper, or do the two sides talk past each other because his argument concerns knowledge-dependent events while the regularities concern slow structural variables?

5. The meta-question. The debate rarely specifies what would count as a law of history — exceptionless, ceteris paribus, probabilistic? — and hence never says what would settle it. Is clarifying this upstream of everything else on the list?

What I want from you is a defended ranking, not a survey. For each question: which of the others depend on its answer; how tractable it is for someone with one term's training and a strict word limit; how much settling it would actually move the wider debate; and whether you would deprioritize it because its answer changes little downstream. State the criteria you are using, flag where they conflict (a question may be important but intractable, or tractable but downstream), and tell me what considerations would change your ordering. Finally, if you think any of my five is overrated or rests on a misreading of Popper's argument, say so directly and explain why.
