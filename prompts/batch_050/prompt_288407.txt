I sit on the advisory council of a truth and reconciliation commission in a country (which I will leave unnamed) that is emerging from roughly three decades of authoritarian rule marked by disappearances, censorship, and periodic massacres. The commission's chair, a clinical psychologist by training, defends our mandate with an analogy she treats as nearly self-evident: a society after atrocity is in the position of a patient after trauma. Just as an individual who buries a traumatic experience does not escape it but re-enacts it — in nightmares, compulsions, and destructive relationships — a nation that refuses to confront its past is doomed to repeat it in cycles of violence, denial, and authoritarian relapse. On her view, public testimony is the societal analogue of the talking cure, memorials and revised school textbooks are part of the treatment plan, and the many citizens who would rather let sleeping dogs lie are, in effect, counselling repression, which any therapist will tell you merely postpones and intensifies the symptoms. She uses the analogy to justify an ambitious programme: victim testimony broadcast on national television, state-funded memorials, and mandatory curriculum reform, all to be pursued even against considerable public resistance, on the grounds that patients rarely enjoy their treatment either.

A senior judge on the council pushes back. Societies, she says, do not have psyches; there is no single subject who was traumatised, no couch on which the nation lies, and no therapist with the standing to declare the treatment complete. Worse, the analogy seems rigged to deliver its conclusion in advance: it casts dissenters as symptoms, treats forgetting as pathological by definition, and quietly assumes that truth-telling is curative rather than — as some of our hearings have suggested — sometimes destabilising. I find myself genuinely torn. The therapy picture does capture something: unacknowledged grievances really do seem to resurface, and official silence really does seem to fester. But I cannot tell whether the analogy is doing real argumentative work or merely decorating a conclusion the chair holds on other grounds.

Could you audit the analogy for us? Please set out the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the patient, the trauma, the repressed material, the symptoms, the therapist, and the cure — and then identify where the correspondence breaks. I would like you to separate the disanalogies that matter for the chair's intended use (justifying compulsory public reckoning over the objections of citizens who prefer amnesia) from those that are merely pedantic. Please say what the analogy smuggles in that the target domain may lack — for instance, a unified remembering subject, a consensus on what collective health would even consist in, and the assumption that forgetting is always a failure rather than sometimes an achievement — and whether the chair's policy conclusions survive once these are laid on the table. If the therapy frame fails, could the same programme be defended on independent grounds (justice to victims, deterrence, institutional reform, duties to future generations), and would those grounds recommend the same design — for example, would they prioritise broadcast testimony and memorials, or prosecutions and compensation instead? Finally, if there is a better analogy for what a body like ours actually does, please offer it and show what it would license. Please write your answer in British English.
