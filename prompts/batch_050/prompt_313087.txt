So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine (long-form, not news), and I've been drafting a piece on the doomsday argument that takes a weird angle. My editor is intrigued but wants it stress-tested before she'll commit, and my usual philosopher friends are all on holiday or sick of me. Below is the current draft. Read it like a referee who wants the piece to fail, then give me the comments I ask for at the end.

DRAFT — working title "The Crater or the Retirement Home"

Here's the argument in it's bar-stool form. A bit over a hundred billion people have ever lived; eight-ish billion are alive right now; roughly 130 million more arrive every year. If our species gets the kind of future the optimists sketch — millennia ahead, billions alive at any given moment, maybe more — then the overwhelming majority of all humans haven't been born yet. Which makes you and me strangely early. A random draw from the pool of everyone-who-ever-lives shouldn't land in the first sliver of the pool. Since it did, we should lower our estimate of how big the pool is. That's the whole trick. No asteroids, no physics: just your birth certificate doing statistical work.

The philosophy here is fifty years deep and it's all about the machinery: what pool am I supposedly drawn from, whether I'm allowed to treat myself as a random sample, whether "I exist early" is evidence at all or a tautology wearing a trench coat. I have nothing to add to that fight and I don't want to re-litigate it, because I think the debate has been staring at the math while the world moved underneath it.

Here's the world moving. More than half of humanity now lives in countries where fertility is below replacement. Most demographers' middle-of-the-road projections have global population peaking this century and then shrinking, some of them shrinking fast. South Korea is the extreme case but not a weird one — it's the leading edge. Fertility might recover, of course; nothing here is a law of nature. But take the projection seriously for one minute and run the doomsday machinery on *that* future.

Suppose our species' actual trajectory is a bulge followed by a long, thin tail: two or three crowded centuries in which most humans who will ever live get born, then a dwindling population that hangs on for ages. The arithmetic of the tail is punishing. A stabilized population of a million or two, even if it lasted a hundred thousand years, adds roughly one modern decade's worth of births to the total. Which means that in the decline scenario, nearly everyone who ever lives is a bulge person. The bulge is where the people are. And a bulge person is exactly what I observe myself to be. The thing the doomsday argument found so suspicious — my early position — turns out to be the most predictable thing about me, given the demography. The typicality it demands comes free.

So the same observation that was supposed to foreshadow catastrophe now slots neatly into the decline scenario. And notice what the arguement was really doing all along: it never told us when the future ends, only that our position in the sequence should look unremarkable once you know the shape of the curve. The scary reading required the giant-future hypothesis to still be on the table, so that rejecting it could feel like bad news. The fertility data has spent decades quietly taking that hypothesis off the table. Which suggests a genuinely unsettling inversion: the doom the argument was pointing at may have already begun. It just doesn't look like a crater. It looks like a retirement home.

Things I already know I have to be careful about. One: "decline continues" is an assumption, not destiny. If fertility recovers and the species re-expands into a vast future, the original argument returns with its original bite — in fact it can be read as predicting that sustained recoveries don't happen, which is either deep or glib, I can't tell. Two: nothing here touches the ordinary ways things could go wrong; a thin-tailed future can still be cut short, and "doom by attrition over millennia" is arguably the argument winning on a technicality. Three: every number in this piece is approximate on purpose, but if any of them are off by enough to matter I need to know.

END OF DRAFT

Now the actual request. Don't rewrite the piece for me — I want comments, not a co-author. Specifically:

1. Mark every place where the reasoning is wrong, circular, or too slick, and say why in a sentence or two. I'm most nervous about the bulge paragraph, where I claim my early position becomes "the most predictable thing about me." I have a nagging feeling I've smuggled something in there — maybe something about which hypothesis I'm conditioning on, or maybe I've just picked a side in the self-sampling fight I claimed to skip — but I can't see it clearly myself.

2. Be honest: have I just reinvented a standard reply to the doomsday argument and dressed it up as a new one? Like, is this whole piece an elaborate way of saying "we have other evidence besides birth rank," which everyone already conceded? I'd rather hear it from you than from a philosopher on twitter.

3. Give me the two or three objections a smart hostile reader would raise, phrased the way they'd actually raise them, and for each one tell me whether I can preempt it in the text or whether I have to just concede it.

4. The "retirement home instead of a crater" line is the bit my editor loves, which makes me suspicious of it. Is it earned by the argument, or is it rhetorically cheating — i.e., am I quietly redefining "doom" so I get a good ending? If the demographic wind-down counts as the doom the argument predicted, does the argument get credit, or does it get retired?

5. Anything a fact-checker would flag, especially the loose demography. I kept things approximate deliberately, but flag anything that's wrong in a way that changes the point, as opposed to wrong in a way a footnote fixes.

And one last thing, off the record: is this piece actually interesting, or is it a TED talk in prose? Be blunt. I've got thick skin and a deadline.
