So I've been mulling over the standard line that historians can't escape the moral and conceptual standards of their own time, and I've landed on a stronger version of it that I actually think is true: not only do historians inevitably judge the past from their present standpoint, they should, and any attempt at fully "present-neutral" history is either dishonest or produces something worse. Basically the past doesn't get a veto over the questions we ask it.

But here's a wrinkle I want you to build into your answer. Imagine two historians, both rigorous, both working from the exact same archive on, say, a 17th century witchcraft prosecution. One writes in 1890 and reads the trial records as evidence of mass superstition slowly dying out; the other writes in 2020 and reads the same records as evidence of how legal institutions manufacture guilt in marginalized people. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the 2020 historian's framework would be completely unintelligible to everyone in the archive itself -- judges, accusers, accused, all of them. My view says that's fine, maybe even a feature: the archive doesn't get to set the terms. What I want from you is: what would count against this position?

Some things I'd want the answer to do. Distinguish defeaters that would actually sink the view from ones that would just be awkward for it -- e.g., does it matter if it turns out the view can't explain why some old historiography still reads as fair-minded and some doesn't, or is that a merely uncomfortable consequence? Tell me what defenders of this view themselves would concede as counting against it, not just what a strawman critic would say. Consider whether the view collapses into something its defenders would reject, like "all historical interpretation is just present politics with footnotes," and whether there's a principled way to stop that slide or whether the slide is fine. Also: does the thought experiment above actually stress-test the view, or did I rig it?

Be honest about how confident you are in each part, and flag the single point where you're least sure of yourself -- not a pro forma hedge, the actual weakest link.
