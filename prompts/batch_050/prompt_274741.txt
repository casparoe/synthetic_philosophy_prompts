Last weekend, a close friend and I fell into an argument that began over coffee on Saturday morning and was still going, by text message, on Sunday night. It concerned photography, and specifically whether we see through pictures.

My position was the one associated with Kendall Walton's transparency thesis: when I look at a photograph of my grandmother as a young woman, I am in a perfectly literal sense seeing her — indirectly, and decades after the fact, but no more indirectly than I see a ship through a telescope or my own face in a mirror. A photograph preserves a causal chain running from the light that left her face to the image in my hands, and that chain, I argued, is what seeing consists in. My friend denies this outright. On her view, photographs are representations like any other — more easily produced and harder to fake than paintings, but not different in kind. She kept returning to two thoughts: that I cannot see what no longer exists, and that the photograph, however faithful, confronts me with an image of my grandmother rather than with my grandmother.

We tested our views against the standard cases — mirrors, periscopes, live video feeds, the security guard watching a monitor in the basement, ultrasound images, the Mars rover's cameras — and disagreed about nearly all of them, which at least suggests the dispute is systematic rather than a quirk. Then one of us, I no longer remember which, invented the case that ended the argument without settling it. Imagine a company offering what it calls restoration. You send it the remains of a family archive destroyed in a fire, plus whatever else the family possesses: letters, descriptions, surviving photographs, genetic material. From all this, the company's system generates images that are pixel-for-pixel indistinguishable from the lost originals — but inferred, not transmitted. No light from my grandmother's face has any causal connection to them. My friend pronounced the case easy: the restored images are well-informed fictions. I found I could not rest there. If a generated image happens to match exactly what the lost photograph showed, does it really show nothing? Does the difference matter to what the grandchildren will feel when they look at it? Should it?

I would like you to write a short story, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, that takes this dispute seriously. Some constraints. First, the philosophical question must do real work in the plot: it should matter practically to the characters — for grief, evidence, inheritance, trust, or something else of comparable weight — and at least one character should change their conduct because of the view they come to hold. Second, both positions must receive their strongest possible statement in action rather than in dialogue; I explicitly do not want a scene of two people debating aesthetics over dinner, as we have already lived that scene and it resolved nothing. Third, the story must end without a verdict. No character should deliver the winning argument, and a thoughtful reader should close the story still genuinely uncertain which of us was right — and uncertain whether the question is even the kind that has an answer.
