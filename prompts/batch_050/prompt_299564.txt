So I write fantasy novels, three books into a series, and my readers are in a knife fight on the subreddit about which facts about my heroine Anselm are REALLY her and which stuff I could retcon in book four without anyone being able to say "that's not Anselm anymore." It got me thinking about this bit from Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism:

"For Aristotle it was essential in men to be rational, accidental to be two-legged. But there is an important difference between this attitude and the doctrine of meaning. From the latter point of view it may indeed be conceded (if only for the sake of argument) that rationality is involved in the meaning of the word 'man' while two-leggedness is not; but two-leggedness may at the same time be viewed as involved in the meaning of 'biped' while rationality is not. Thus from the point of view of the doctrine of meaning it makes no sense to say of the actual individual, who is at once a man and a biped, that his rationality is essential and his two-leggedness accidental or vice versa."

Ok here's my question. Quine is talking about actual individuals, and Anselm isn't actual. Every single thing true of her is true because I typed it. So maybe fictional people are the exception to his point? Test this for me. Rate each of these from 0 to 10 on how essential it is to Anselm, where 10 means changing it means she's a different character now, 0 means pure accident, easy retcon:

1. Anselm is the blacksmith's seventh daughter.
2. Anselm killed the Thorn King's herald at the end of book two.
3. Anselm has a burn scar across her left palm.
4. Anselm is left-handed (never stated in the text, but she's drawn that way on the covers).
5. Anselm doesn't trust priests (comes up once, in passing, in book one).
6. Anselm would never abandon a companion (never stated anywhere, readers just insist on it).
7. Her name is Anselm.
8. In my first draft she was a man called Anselmo. Cut before publication, but I still half think of it as true.

Then tell me whether you think the fiction case actually escapes Quine's point, or just hides the relativization somewhere else (my head, the published text, reader expectations?), and give the passage itself a score out of 10 for how well it transfers to made-up people.

Lead with the eight ratings, then ONE paragraph of reasoning, 220 words max for the paragraph. The quote doesn't count toward the limit.
