So I'm in a grad seminar on economic methodology and we just spent two weeks on Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on positive economics, the one where he argues you shouldn't judge a model by how realistic its assumptions are, only by whether it predicts well -- the famous "as if" idea where billiard players play as if they'd solved the physics equations and leaves arrange themselves on the tree as if each one was maximizing its sunlight, plus the really spicy bit where Friedman says the more significant a theory is, the more wildly unrealistic its assumptions will tend to be. Our seminar group chat has been roasting this position all week (apparently it gets nicknamed the F-twist), and for the final session everyone has to bring some piece of writing that engages with it. My contribution is going to be a parody, and I want help.

Write me a mock referee report, Reviewer 2 style, for a fictional economics paper, where the referee is a total true believer in the 1953 methodology. The paper under review made the fatal error of building a model with reasonably realistic assumptions -- firms face search costs, households don't know every price in the economy, prices take a while to adjust, that sort of thing. The referee recommends rejection on the grounds that descriptively accurate assumptions are the mark of an insignificant theory, and demands revisions: the partially-informed households should instead optimize as if they'd solved a full general equilibrium system, the sluggish prices should adjust instantly as if an auctioneer cleared every market, maybe a comment suggesting the authors test the model on data from an economy it wasn't designed to describe since that's a more impressive test. The humor has to come from the referee applying the essay's actual logic correctly and relentlessly -- as-if reasoning, predictions over descriptive realism, unrealistic assumptions as a badge of theoretical power -- pushed until the report goes unhinged, e.g. complaining that the empirics are too accurate to count as evidence, or praising a rival paper whose predictions failed but whose assumptions were beautifully false. Someone who knows the philosophy-of-econ literature should recognize that the referee understands Friedman perfectly; the joke is that he's right about what the essay says, not that he's stupid.

About 500-600 words, formatted as an actual referee report: recommendation line up top, numbered major and minor comments, deadpan all the way through, no winking. Don't have the referee cite the essay by name in every comment -- one throwaway line near the end demanding the authors engage with a certain 1953 methodological classic is plenty. Bonus points if one of the minor comments is about a typo that doesn't exist.
