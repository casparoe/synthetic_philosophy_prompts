Using his treatment of the Euler conjecture as your example, explain what he means by this and how it differs from both naive empiricism about math and the Euclidean/deductivist picture he attacks."

Here's the answer I need graded, from a student who's usually solid:

"Lakatos reconstructs the history of Euler's conjecture V - E + F = 2 as a classroom dialogue to show that mathematical knowledge doesn't grow the way textbooks pretend. Instead of stating definitions and then proving theorems once and for all (the deductivist style he criticizes), real mathematics advances by a dialectic of proofs and refutations. Cauchy's proof of the conjecture is met by counterexamples like the hollow cube or the picture-frame solid, and rather than simply abandoning the conjecture, mathematicians analyze the proof to find the 'hidden lemma' where the counterexample sneaks in, then make that lemma explicit as a condition of an improved theorem. Lakatos distinguishes strategies for handling refutations: monster-barring restricts the theorem to a safe domain where the counterexamples can't arise, while exception-barring redefines the term 'polyhedron' so the offending object no longer counts as one. His point in calling mathematics quasi-empirical is that formal theories work like Euclidean ones: truth is injected at the top in the axioms and flows downward to the theorems, except that in math the axioms themselves are conjectural and can be revised when counterexamples accumulate. So mathematics is fallible and grows by criticism, much like natural science, even though it isn't tested against experiments."
