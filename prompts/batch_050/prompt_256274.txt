I am a retired chemist — my career was in polymer kinetics, so I come to philosophy late and with a laboratory cast of mind — and since retiring I have been attending a philosophy of biology reading group at the university in town. Next month I am to lead the session on the definition of life, and the organizer has asked each participant to circulate a short statement of their own position in advance so the others can probe it. I have drafted mine, but before I send it around I would like an audit of whether the commitments actually hang together, because I have learned the hard way that what feels like common sense to a chemist does not always survive an afternoon with philosophers.

Here is my position as I currently hold it. (a) The best criterion available is something in the family of the NASA working definition: a living thing is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution. (b) Viruses are not alive; I feel fairly confident of this, and it was the consensus of the biologists in our group. (c) Sterile individuals — a mule, a worker bee — are straightforwardly alive. (d) Dormant bacterial spores and dry seeds are alive even while metabolically inert, in some cases for centuries. (e) Life on Earth arose from non-living chemistry by a gradual process; there was no single moment or sharp threshold at which matter first became alive. (f) "Alive" is a binary predicate — a system either is or is not alive — and the entire point of a definition is to draw that line cleanly; borderline cases strike me as a defect of a definition, not a feature of the world. The one test case I have thought through myself is fire: it grows, consumes fuel, and multiplies, and I take it to be excluded by the heredity requirement in (a). But I am far less sure that the rest of my commitments survive the same scrutiny.

What I would like is a consistency check, not a lecture on the philosophy of biology generally. Tell me which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious; and for each, say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me. I am especially interested in any tension my formulation hides from me — the kind the reading group will find in the first ten minutes. Finally, if you had to identify the single revision that would clean up the most of the position, which would it be? I have no allegiance to any one of these over the others, so do not spare whichever commitment turns out to be the problem.
