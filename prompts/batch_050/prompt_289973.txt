So I run the quality-assurance side of a small regional forensic lab -- we do fingerprints, shoeprints, a bit of firearms comparison, the usual pattern-matching disciplines -- and last night I got into a two-hour argument with my brother-in-law, who's a philosophy grad student, about whether the analysts' judgments are really "penetrated" by context. His claim, which I think is basically right but which he stated way too strongly, was that an examiner who knows the suspect confessed, or who sees the victim's photo before working the case, literally perceives the latent print differently, not just that they get biased when they write up the conclusion. I told him it doesn't matter where the bias sits as long as you control the information flow, and he said you can't control what you can't locate. It annoyed me because he's kind of got a point.

Act as a philosopher of mind who also has real familiarity with the contextual-bias literature in forensic pattern matching, and think this through carefully before you settle anything. I want you to turn the cognitive-penetration-of-perception debate into a procedure my lab can actually follow for deciding, for each kind of case we get, which information the examiner is allowed to see and when -- a proper sequence: which questions we ask about a piece of case information, in what order, what counts as a reason to shield the examiner from it entirely versus just document it, and how the answer should differ depending on whether the bias works at the level of seeing the print, of judging how much detail is "enough," or of writing the report. The procedure has to handle the counterarguments too: some context genuinely helps (known exemplars, case priority, quality triage), blind procedures have costs, and my examiners will revolt if they feel accused of bad faith, so include the points where the procedure knowingly trades accuracy for morale or throughput and mark them as trade-offs rather than pretending they're forced. Also say where the science genuinely doesn't settle whether the effect is perceptual or post-perceptual and what a cautious lab should do under that uncertainty.

Give me your answer as exactly eight numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding remarks: items 1 through 5 the steps of the procedure in order, item 6 where the procedure deliberately hands the call back to a human manager, item 7 a worked application to a high-profile homicide where the suspect confessed before the print work started, and item 8 a worked application to a routine burglary where it should come out the other way.
