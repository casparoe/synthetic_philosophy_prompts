Here's the position I've landed on after reading around the old Friedman 1953 essay on positive economics and some of the idealization literature. I call my version Predictive Immunity, and it has two parts:

(1) For any model with a clearly specified domain of application, the falsity of its assumptions is never *by itself* evidence against the model. Only predictive failure inside the domain counts. Perfectly rational agents, infinite populations, frictionless planes, fully mixed gene pools -- none of that matters if the model delivers where it's supposed to deliver.

(2) A licensing clause that I added myself to handle the obvious junk cases: an unrealistic assumption is legit only if the model's in-domain predictions are approximately insensitive to small realistic perturbations of that assumption. So epicycles and overfit curves fail the clause (tiny tweaks wreck the fit), while stuff like the ideal gas law or Hardy-Weinberg passes, since nudging the assumptions barely moves the predictions.

I honestly think (1)+(2) together survive every standard counterexample people throw at Friedman, and that critics mostly just attack (1) alone, which is a strawman of my version.
