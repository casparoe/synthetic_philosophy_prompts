ok so the fitting-attitude crowd says x is valuable iff there's sufficient reason to favor or desire x, and then the evil demon walks in: he'll torture everyone unless you admire him. there's clearly a reason to admire him (you'll prevent the torture), but he clearly isn't admirable. wrong kind of reasons. the solutions i've collected so far:

1. the object-given / state-given split (Parfit): the threat is a state-given reason — a reason to want to have the attitude, or to get yourself into it by whatever means — not an object-given one, and the analysis only ever meant object-given reasons.
2. Hieronymi's settling-a-question line: a reason to believe or admire must be a consideration that bears on settling the question of whether the demon is admirable; the threat doesn't bear on that, so it isn't a reason of the relevant sort at all.
3. Rabinowicz & Rønnow-Rasmussen: the reason the demon gives you supports the state of affairs of your admiring him, not him. your admiring him really might be valuable (it prevents torture), and that value is analyzable in fitting-attitude terms without making the demon valuable. value of the attitude vs value of the object.
4. the content restriction (Danielsson & Olson, roughly): a reason of the right kind is one whose content is about the attitude's object; reasons that are about the attitude itself, or its effects on bystanders, are wrong-kind by construction.
5. the circularity-shrug: just take "reason of the right kind" as primitive and say the analysis was only ever hooking value up to fittingness, never reducing it. on this reading D'Arms & Jacobson's point that the same problem arises for the funny and the shameful becomes a feature, since fittingness is doing the same work everywhere.
