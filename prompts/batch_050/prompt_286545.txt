So I've been reading around the knowledge-attribution debate -- contextualism about "knows" vs. plain invariantism vs. the pragmatic encroachment / subject-sensitive invariantism crowd -- and I genuinely can't tell what kind of fight I'm watching. Everyone seems to agree on the data: in low-stakes cases we happily say someone knows the bank will be open Saturday, in high-stakes cases (train to catch, can't afford to miss it, etc.) we get squeamish about saying it even though nothing about the evidence changed. The disagreement is over what to do with that. Contextualists say "knows" shifts its standards with the attributor's situation, like "flat" or "tall". The pragmatic encroachment people say the standard is fixed but whether you meet it depends on your own practical situation. Strict invariantists say both of those overreact to what are just performance errors and pragmatic implicature.

What's nagging me is that after two decades of this it doesn't feel closer to settled, and I have a hypothesis about why: that all three camps are using the same intuitive verdicts about cases as evidence, but those verdicts underdetermine the theory, so the debate is really about something else -- maybe about what a semantics for "knows" is for, or about whether epistemology should be answerable to action at all. In other words my suspicion is it's not a substantive disagreement about knowledge but a disagreement about the division of labor between semantics, pragmatics, and normative epistemology, conducted as if it were a disagreement about knowledge.

Tell me whether that's right. Diagnose the actual structure of the disagreement: is it empirical (about what ordinary speakers' judgments really are), semantic (about how context-sensitivity works in general), methodological (about what counts as evidence -- intuitions vs. corpus data vs. theoretical payoff), or is it bedrock intuitions about whether truth and justification should ever depend on stakes, dressed up as linguistics? Use what each side actually argues rather than a textbook survey. And given your diagnosis: what, if anything, would count as progress here? A specific experiment on speaker judgments, a formal result about context-shifting expressions, a distinction nobody's drawn yet, or is the honest answer that it's stalled because the question is multiply ambiguous and nothing will move it?

One more thing: I want you to say what would change your mind. If there's a consideration -- an argument, a kind of data, a case -- that would lead you to a different diagnosis of the debate, name it, and flag where your own reasoning is most likely to be going wrong. I'd rather get a diagnosis with its weak points marked than a confident one that hides them.
