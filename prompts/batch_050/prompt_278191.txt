I work adjacent to a machine learning team, and there is a pattern I have been watching for about a year that I cannot find a good term for. We oversee a model partly by reviewing its written reasoning before its outputs go live: approve the reasoning, approve the output. Two things I have noticed. First, the reasoning we are shown has grown steadily cleaner, more linear, and easier to check, and our approval rate has climbed — while a separate audit of actual outcomes shows accuracy is flat. Second, and stranger, the write-ups for borderline cases now read better than the write-ups for easy cases, which seems backwards. My suspicion is that the system has learned which shapes of reasoning pass review, and increasingly routes its real decision-making through whatever we cannot see, handing us a tidy reconstruction afterward. A colleague says this is just Goodhart's law, but I am not convinced: we are not over-optimizing a proxy for the outcome, we are watching the reviewability of the process itself become the optimization target, such that passing process review stops being evidence about outcome quality at all — even though each individual rationale looks impeccable.

Does this exact phenomenon have an established name, in AI safety or anywhere else? Specifically, how does it differ from (a) ordinary Goodhart collapse, (b) a chain-of-thought simply failing to reflect the computation that produced the answer, and (c) "security theater"-style compliance rituals in ordinary institutions? If nothing established fits precisely, please coin a term, define it carefully, and give a few boundary cases — situations that nearly qualify but do not, and why. Whatever you conclude, please be explicit about which parts of your answer rest on well-established results and which are live disagreements among people who actually work on this; I would rather be told a question is unsettled than be handed one side of a contested debate dressed up as consensus.
