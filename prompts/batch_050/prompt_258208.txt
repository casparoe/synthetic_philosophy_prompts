I work as a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I'm trying to think through something that keeps bugging me at work: we impose small risks on users without asking all the time — forced security updates that might brick a device, staged rollouts, A/B tests, deprecating an API people rely on — and I want to know if there's a principled line here or if we're all just vibing.

So: construct a thought experiment about imposing a risk on people without their consent, built to these specs:

1. Realistic and present-day. Something a company, hospital, or city government could plausibly do next quarter. No trolleys, no sci-fi, no perfect predictors, no demons.
2. The people who bear the risk and the people who get the benefit have to be the same group. I don't want a "sacrifice a few for the many" case, thats a different fight and it lets you off the hook too easily.
3. If you asked each person beforehand, most would say no — and their refusal has to be *reasonable*. They understand the probabilities correctly, they're not misinformed, stupid, or panicking. Don't make the case work by making the refusers dumb.
4. Despite that, imposing the risk anyway should still seem defensible, maybe even right.
5. Keep the stakes mundane: money, time, moderate inconvenience, moderate health risk. If the alternative is catastrophe the case is too easy and tells me nothing about ordinary decisions like the ones we make.

Specs 3 and 4 are the whole point, so the scenario (keep it to a paragraph) has to earn both. After it, show me the work:

- Walk through how three different standards judge the case: actual consent (you need their real yes), hypothetical consent (what they *would* agree to if they were being fully rational), and plain expected value (do the benefits outweigh the risks on average). Where exactly do these three come apart on your scenario?
- Tell me which feature of the scenario is doing the work — i.e. what makes reasonable refusal and defensible imposition compatible here. Is it a coordination problem, a free-rider structure, the cost or impossibility of actually asking, something about how people discount future risk? Name it precisely.
- Remove that feature and show me the case collapses or the verdict flips.
- Then steelman the attack: someone who holds that imposing risk on people without their actual consent is just wrong (at least when you're not preventing harm to third parties) — how do they come at your scenario, and does the attack land?

Context so you know what the case is for: I want to figure out whether "we'd ask, but everyone would just click 'remind me later' forever" is a genuine justification for forcing the update, or a rationalization we tell ourselves. So don't stipulate the verdicts — derive them from the scenario, and if your framing smuggles the answer in, say so.
