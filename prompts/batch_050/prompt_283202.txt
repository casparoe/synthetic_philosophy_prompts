Last night I got into a long argument with a friend (a software engineer, no philosophy background, very confident) about whether the large language model she'd been using to plan a trip believed anything. Her line was that it doesn't believe anything at all, that it just produces statistically likely text, and that saying it "knows" her hotel's check-in time is a category error. I pushed back, she called me gullible, and the argument ran long enough that afterward I sat down and wrote out my actual commitments, because I wanted to see whether the position I'd been defending off the cuff is even coherent. I'd like an audit before I send it to her.

Here is where I landed. (a) I accept Dennett's intentional stance: whether a system has beliefs is settled by whether interpreting it as a rational believer with goals yields good, compact, reliable predictions of its behavior, full stop -- not by anything about its internal architecture. (b) On that basis, current LLMs literally have beliefs, not merely "as-if" beliefs, and it is not a loose metaphor to say the model believed my friend's flight was on Tuesday. (c) A thermostat, by contrast, does not believe the room is at twenty degrees; attributing that belief to it is anthropomorphic projection, and I'd want to say this even if someone insists the intentional stance "works" on thermostats. (d) My dog literally believes that someone is at the door when she barks at footsteps -- same status as (b), not a courtesy attribution. (e) When an LLM asserts one thing in the morning and the opposite in the afternoon, it simply holds inconsistent beliefs, exactly as a muddled person does; the inconsistency is a fact about the system's beliefs, not a reason to deny it has any. (f) Finally, I want to be a realist about all of this: there is a fact of the matter about what a system believes, and when we attribute beliefs correctly we are latching onto something genuinely there in the world, not just projecting a convenient story.

Please audit this set rather than referee my dispute with my friend. Organize the response in four sections, in this order. First, "The tensions, ranked": identify which of my commitments actually pull against each other and which only appear to, with the genuine tensions ordered from most to least severe, and for each one name exactly which pair of commitments is in conflict and why the usual motivations for each can't be jointly satisfied (roughly 400 words). Second, "Apparent tensions that dissolve": pick the pairing that looks most damning on the surface but survives scrutiny, and show why (roughly 150 words). Third, "The cheapest fix": tell me the single revision that restores the most consistency for the least philosophical cost, and state plainly what that revision would force me to concede to my friend (roughly 200 words). Fourth, "The verdict": three or four sentences on whether the position I was actually defending at dinner is salvageable, and in what form.
