Hey, I'm a TA grading an undergrad population ethics exam and could use a second pair of eyes on one answer. The exam question was:

"Infinite populations create serious difficulties for total utilitarianism. Explain at least two distinct problems that arise when we try to apply total utilitarian aggregation to outcomes containing infinitely many people, and assess whether any standard modification of utilitarianism (for example average utilitarianism, critical-level utilitarianism, or discounting by time or location) resolves these problems."

Here's the student's answer:

"Total utilitarianism says we should rank outcomes by the sum of welfare they contain. When populations are infinite, this runs into trouble in at least two ways. First, the sum of welfare may simply diverge: if an eternally expanding universe keeps producing happy people, the total is infinite, and since infinity plus any finite amount is still infinity, total utilitarianism cannot distinguish an outcome where those people are very happy from one where they're barely happy, or even say that adding more happy people improves things. The ordering collapses and almost everything ties.

Second, there's a conflict with Pareto. In infinite cases you can have one outcome that is strictly better for every single person than another outcome, while both still have infinite total welfare, so total utilitarianism counts them as equally good even though one Pareto-dominates the other. This is what motivates the various impossibility results showing that no aggregation rule extending utilitarianism to infinite populations can satisfy all of the basic axioms like Pareto, anonymity, and separability.

As for fixes: average utilitarianism solves the divergence problem, because even when the total is infinite the average welfare stays finite, so we can just compare averages and get a complete ordering back. Critical-level utilitarianism also helps, since subtracting a constant critical level from each person's welfare turns infinite sums into finite ones. Discounting by time or distance could work in practice but seems morally arbitrary, since the location of a person in space-time shouldn't matter morally. So my verdict is that average utilitarianism is the best response."
