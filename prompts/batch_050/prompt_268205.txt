I am a philosophy professor preparing a colloquium paper defending what I call constraint-aware adaptationism. The claim is not that every trait is optimal, that selection acts alone, or that a plausible adaptive story establishes its own truth. It is that adaptationist explanation remains a legitimate and often indispensable research strategy when an adaptation hypothesis is formulated as a testable claim about the causal contribution of selection in a specified ancestral population. On my view, such a hypothesis must identify the trait’s target of selection, the relevant ancestral environment, the alternative trajectories available to the lineage, and the developmental or architectural constraints that limited those alternatives. I also distinguish three roles a trait component can play: selected effect, correlated by-product, and enabling constraint. I argue that Gould and Lewontin’s spandrels critique corrects naive adaptationism but does not justify treating adaptation hypotheses as generally inferior to constraint or drift hypotheses.

The audience will be a philosophy of biology department with several evolutionary developmental biologists and one population geneticist in attendance. The philosophers are broadly sympathetic to pluralism; the developmental biologists are skeptical of trait-by-trait selection stories and care about developmental bias, pleiotropy, modularity, and historical contingency; the population geneticist will be especially alert to drift, linkage, and the difference between selection on a trait and selection at a locus. None will be satisfied by merely restating that adaptationism is a useful heuristic.

Prepare my question-period briefing. Give me the ten objections I am most likely to receive from this particular audience, ordered by expected damage rather than by philosophical fame. For each: identify the likely questioner; phrase the objection in that person’s idiom; state the strongest version of the concern in two or three sentences; give me a response of no more than ninety seconds; indicate what I should concede; and identify where my response remains weakest. Include objections about the individuation of traits, mistaking maintenance for origin, retrofitting selection after a by-product has become necessary, developmental constraints masquerading as adaptations, and the evidential asymmetry between adaptive and non-adaptive explanations. Also include the methodological objection that my distinction among selected effect, by-product, and enabling constraint merely redescribes the problem rather than solving it.

Conclude with the question I should most hope nobody asks, the honest answer I would have to give, and one revision to the paper that would reduce its force without abandoning the central thesis.
