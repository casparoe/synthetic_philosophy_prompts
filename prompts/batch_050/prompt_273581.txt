Eight months ago I was diagnosed with a slowly degenerating inner-ear condition. My audiologist showed me a series of measurements taken weeks apart: each test registered a small decline, yet at no point could I have told you, from the inside, that my hearing on a given Tuesday was worse than it had been the previous Friday. Since then, the question of luminosity — whether being in a phenomenal state always puts one in a position to know that one is in it — has stopped feeling like a technicality and started feeling like a description of my life.

Oddly, my own case has not converted me. I find myself wanting to defend luminosity for at least some phenomenal states: when I am in pain, it seems to me, I cannot merely seem to myself to be in pain, and I distrust arguments that conclude otherwise from premises about discrimination thresholds. But I want this conviction stress-tested rather than confirmed.

So: what would count against the position? I am familiar with Williamson's margin-for-error argument and its sorites structure, so please go beyond it. Which further considerations — empirical findings about introspective error, cases of gradual phenomenal change, results about the logic of knowledge, commitments elsewhere in philosophy of mind — would have to hold for luminosity to fail even for pain? Which of these would a committed defender of luminosity themselves concede as genuine threats, and which would they reasonably absorb by qualifying the thesis (restricting it to occurrent states, say, or to being in a position to know rather than to actual knowledge)? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate where the fate of luminosity bears on the KK principle, and say whether the position can be insulated by qualification without being trivialized.
