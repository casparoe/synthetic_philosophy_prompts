I teach philosophy and I'm finally putting together a graduate seminar on rationality after years of promising my department I'd do it. For most of my career I've defended a version of the view that epistemic akrasia is always irrational: if you believe that p, you can't rationally also believe that your evidence (overall, on balance) fails to support p. I like the strong version -- not the weak "it's usually a bad sign" thing, but the claim that this combination of attitudes is structurally incoherent, full stop, the way believing p and not-p is. My students keep pushing back and honestly some of their cases are better than I'd like, so I want to stress-test my own position before I have to defend it against twenty grad students who smell blood.

So: what would actually count against the view? I don't want generic anti-conciliationist talking points I already assign. I want a map of what the position risks. Which kinds of cases does it have the hardest time describing -- things like agents who've been told (truly or falsely) that they've taken a pill that scrambles their reasoning about certain topics, or cases where the higher-order belief and the first-order belief seem to draw on the very same evidence so it feels like double counting? Are there cases where the akratic combination looks not just excusable but rationally required, and would I have to bite those bullets or can I redescribe them? Also: does the view stand or fall with particular positions on peer disagreement, and does it collapse into some rival (like a total evidence view) in a way that would make my "distinctive" thesis trivial? I'm also worried about self-referential weirdness -- what does my view say about believing that your own belief that akrasia is irrational is unsupported?

Rank the potential defeaters from genuinely fatal to merely embarassing. For each, say whether a committed defender of my view (me, ideally) would concede it counts against the position or would dismiss it as changing the subject -- I want an honest sense of what I'm actually committed to conceding. And if the honest verdict is that my view has been so heavily qualified over the years ("only when the higher-order belief is rational," "only for ideally coherent agents," etc.) that it no longer risks anything, say that too, and whether that's a fatal flaw or just what a mature principle looks like.
