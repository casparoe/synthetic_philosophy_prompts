I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and right now I'm working on a long piece about what philosophers call the inefficacy objection -- the worry that individual consumer choices don't matter for collective harms because any one person's contribution is a rounding error. Skipping a flight, not buying meat, biking to work: the emissions and the suffering happen at scales where one more or one less person changes nothing visible. My editor wants me to actually take a position in the piece rather than do the usual both-sides thing, so I need help thinking through one specific claim, and I want your real credence on it with the reasoning decomposed, not a survey of the literature.

Here's the claim, stated as precisely as I can manage: for a typical consumer in a rich country, permanently buying one less chicken per week reduces, in expectation, the total number of chickens raised and killed -- i.e., the expected effect is genuinely nonzero, not just morally symbolic. The best objection I know is the threshold one: grocery stores and distributors order in bulk, so almost always your abstention tips no decision and does nothing, but once in a while your non-purchase is the straw that breaks an ordering threshold and prevents a whole batch of chickens from being produced. Defenders say this washes out in expectation -- the rare big wins exactly balance the frequent zeroes, so expected impact tracks average impact. Skeptics say supply chains are too coarse-grained and buffered for the math to be that clean, or that expected-value reasoning is the wrong frame entirely and something like complicity or difference-making is what actually matters.

So: give me your credence that the claim is true, decomposed into the sub-questions it depends on -- something like: the probability that food supply chains transmit marginal demand signals at all (I gather economists talk about demand elasticity here, though I'm not sure I'm using the term right), the probability that the threshold mechanics really do wash out in expectation given that they respond, the probability that expected-value reasoning is even the right frame versus one of the alternative moral theories, and any other terms you think belong, each with a number and one line of justification, then the combination. Then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what empirical evidence would move it most. And one last thing: if your credence comes out high, tell me whether the same style of reasoning rescues voting in large elections too -- if it doesn't, where exactly do the two cases come apart? That's the part of the piece I'm dreading writing, because I suspect the answer is "they dont come apart" and I'm not sure I'm ready for that conclusion.
