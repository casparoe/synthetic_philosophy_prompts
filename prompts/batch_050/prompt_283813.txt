For our classical Chinese philosophy reading group I've been assigned the role of summarizer, and I'd like to push back on how last week's session ended before I write it up. We had just finished Mencius alongside Xunzi's essay arguing that human nature is bad, and our organizer closed with a framing she clearly found satisfying. Mencius, she said, treats moral development as gardening: people are born with four incipient sprouts — a seedling of compassion, a seedling of shame and dislike, a seedling of deference, and a seedling of approval and disapproval — and, much as water tends downhill, these grow into full virtues given nourishment and protection from weeds. Xunzi, she said, treats it as carpentry: our raw impulses are warped timber that must be steamed and clamped against a straightening frame, clay the potter forces into shape, and virtue is the finished product rather than anything the material was ever tending toward. Since both thinkers agree that ordinary people can become fully good through the right practices and environment, she concluded that their famous dispute is really a choice between two motivational metaphors — a difference in teaching style rather than in substance — and that picking between them comes down to temperament.

Something about this conclusion bothers me, but I can't yet articulate what, so I want the framing itself audited. Treat the gardening-versus-carpentry picture as an argument for her conclusion and tell me how far it holds. Concretely: give me the correspondence tables it implies — on the gardening side, what plays the role of the soil, the water, the weeds, the gardener, and the plant's own built-in direction of growth; on the carpentry side, what plays the role of the wood's warp, the straightening frame, the carpenter, and the finished tool. Then identify where each half of the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that genuinely bear on her conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Some things I suspect matter, though I may be wrong: whether the gardener metaphor can accommodate that Mencius's sprouts supposedly grow toward morality on their own rather than toward whatever the gardener happens to prefer; who counts as the gardener in a society where everyone is also a plant; where the carpenter's idea of straight is supposed to come from, and whether Xunzi can answer that without quietly smuggling in something sprout-like; and whether calling the two pictures pedagogically interchangeable ignores commitments on either side that aren't metaphors at all — say, Mencius's thought experiment about anyone's spontaneous alarm at seeing a child about to fall into a well, which reads less like an image and more like a claim that could turn out to be false.

End with a verdict: does her conclusion — a difference in temperament and teaching style, not in substance — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If the framing was doing the argumentative work, say so plainly and name which commitments have to be given up to keep it. And if you think a better analogy exists for what actually divides the two thinkers, propose it and show that it earns its keep better than gardening and carpentry do.
