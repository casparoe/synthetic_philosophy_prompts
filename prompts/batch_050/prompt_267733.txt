I'm in the second term of a political philosophy course, and we've just finished the unit on global justice. During a seminar on whether compatriots have special claims on us, I realized that the institution I've taken for granted my whole life -- the modern nation-state's allocation of its resources -- is a strange thing to think about in light of that debate, and I'd like help working out what it philosophically presupposes. Consider the actual pattern, which I take to be common knowledge: my country's government maintains an expensive welfare state for people within its territory, funds emergency consular services, and in a crisis -- a war breaking out abroad, a pandemic, a natural disaster -- will spend considerable sums evacuating its own citizens from danger while making no comparable effort for the equally endangered non-citizens standing next to them. At the same time, its official documents are full of universalist language: human rights, the equal moral worth of all persons, duties to humanity. And its budget for foreign aid is well under one percent of national income, even though a dollar spent abroad would often relieve far more suffering than a dollar spent at home. So the same institution asserts, in its rhetoric, that everyone matters equally, and acts, in its spending and its emergency priorities, as if some people matter vastly more.

Two details of the pattern make it harder to pin down than simple nationalist partiality would suggest. First, the state does not restrict its domestic benefits to citizens: permanent residents, and often even temporary workers, are covered by the healthcare system and unemployment insurance, and tourists receive emergency care -- yet none of these people, as I understand it, have the same claim on consular evacuation that a citizen who has lived abroad for thirty years retains. So the boundary the state polices shifts depending on which benefit is at stake. Second, when our seminar discussed why any of this might be justified, three different stories came up that all seemed to fit the practice: that co-membership in a nation generates special obligations the way friendship or family does, that those who contribute to and are coerced by shared institutions acquire claims on each other that outsiders lack, and that the whole system is really just a cosmopolitan division of moral labor, where assigning each person to a particular state's care is the best available way to get everyone's interests looked after. My problem is that I can't tell whether the actual practice -- with its shifting boundaries between citizens, residents, and foreigners -- is committed to one of these stories, fits all three equally well, or is internally inconsistent in a way no single story could rationalize.

So what I want is a reconstruction of the implicit political theory of this practice. What conception of special obligations, if any, does a state have to hold for the pattern I've described to make sense -- and which rival conceptions does the practice quietly rule out, for instance by its treatment of long-settled citizens abroad compared to permanent residents at home? Where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, such that a committed cosmopolitan who accepted the division-of-labor story could defend every element of it as it stands? And finally, take the strongest reconstruction and stress-test it: which element of the practice would have to change first if the state came to believe that compatriot claims are merely instrumental rather than morally basic -- the aid budget, consular evacuation, the citizen-resident distinction, or nothing at all?
