I'm a PhD student in political theory and I keep thinking about an argument I witnessed at a workshop last week, between two postdocs from the behavioral econ group. It was about nudging and it never got resolved, and I'd like a verdict on who was right at which point.

Setup: the university cafeteria moved the vegetarian dishes to the front of the line and the meat dishes to the back, and sales of vegetarian meals went up a lot. Anna said this is a paradigm case of legitimate nudging. Ben said it's manipulation and the fact that it's food policy doesn't change that.

Here's roughly how it went, as best I remember:

(1) Ben's opening move: the nudge works precisely *because* people don't notice it or don't bother to resist it — it piggybacks on laziness and status quo bias rather than giving anyone a reason. Any intervention whose mechanism of action bypasses the chooser's deliberation is disrespectful of their agency, even if the outcome is good and even if opting out is technically free. Freedom of choice isn't just about whether the alternatives are still available; it's about which faculty of yours the intervention addresses.

(2) Anna replied that this proves way too much. *Everything* in the cafeteria's layout influences choice through non-deliberative channels — shelf height, lighting, where the till is. There is no neutral arrangement. If any non-neutral arrangement that works partly through System 1 counts as manipulation, then serving food in a room at all is manipulation, so Ben's criterion can't be the right one. The real question is just whether the chooser can easily do otherwise, and here they can — walk to the back of the line.

(3) Ben conceded there's no neutral baseline, but said that's a point about inevitability, not about permission. The fact that *some* arrangement is unavoidable doesn't mean *every* arrangement is equally fine: the difference is intent plus mechanism. Arranging things in order to steer people via a bias, when the same people would not be moved by the *reasons* you have, is different from arranging things for logistics and incidentally influencing choices. He also said Anna's "just walk to the back" reply is disingenuous, because the whole design relies on most people not doing that — the policy's business plan, so to speak, is that the exit option won't be used.

(4) Anna then tried a different tack: transparency. She said the cafeteria could put up a sign saying "we've ordered the dishes this way to encourage vegetarian eating, which we think is healthier and better for the climate." There's decent evidence disclosed defaults and disclosed nudges often still work. If the nudge keeps working after full disclosure, she said, then the chooser is effectively endorsing it — they now know they're being steered and go along anyway — so the bypassing worry dissolves. Manipulation requires concealment or deception, and there's neither here.

(5) Ben's last move: that's too fast, and the evidence cuts both ways. A nudge that survives disclosure doesn't work *through* the disclosure; it works through the same old bias, and the disclosure just fails to neutralize it. If I tell you "I'm now going to exploit your status quo bias" and you do nothing, your inaction isn't endorsement, it's exactly the bias doing its job — calling it consent is equivocating on two senses of "going along." And he added that Anna's own health-and-climate justification shows the steering isn't answerable to what the chooser actually values at all: it's answerable to what the cafeteria thinks is good for them, which makes it paternalism wearing a transparency fig leaf. Anna said she could live with "paternalism" as a label as long as the means are choice-preserving, and then we all had to go to the next session.

Please go through moves (1)–(5) and say who's right at each step and why — including whether Anna's "no neutral baseline" move actually answers Ben's mechanism point or just changes the subject, and whether the disclosure move in (4) does the work she needs it to. Also: is there anything both of them are missing, e.g. a distinction or a further question that would have moved the argument forward instead of sideways? I'd especially like to know whether Ben's intent-plus-mechanism criterion can be stated without smuggling in conclusions, since that's the bit I keep going back and forth on myself.
