I am an economist advising a coastal city's newly formed intergenerational infrastructure commission: a dozen civil engineers, hydrologists, and urban planners, all quantitatively fluent, none trained in welfare economics. The commission must adopt a social discount rate for projects with horizons up to two centuries, and their first case is a sea wall whose principal benefit materializes around year 150. In a single two-hour session I need to move them from "discounting is just compound interest" to a position on each component of the Ramsey decomposition -- the pure rate of time preference, the growth term, and the risk term -- that they can defend in public. Plan the teaching rather than delivering it. Specify the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why: in particular, I suspect the growth term (future generations will be richer, so a marginal unit of benefit is worth less to them) must be fully absorbed before pure time preference is raised, because otherwise every subsequent argument about valuing the future less gets silently re-read as an argument about diminishing marginal utility -- confirm or correct this sequencing. Identify the one carrying example that isolates pure time preference -- presumably a scenario with zero expected growth and zero catastrophe risk, so that the only live question is whether a benefit's timing alone affects its weight -- and state what that example must deliberately lack to stay clean. Map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will arise: that market interest rates reveal the correct social rate; that any positive pure time preference simply means valuing future people less, full stop; that observed individual impatience and preference reversals license social discounting of the same shape; and that extinction hazard is a component of pure time preference rather than a separate term. For each, give the point in the sequence where I should head it off and the contrast or question that does the work. Conclude with a check for understanding -- I am considering presenting a proposed rate, having them compute what it implies about the year-150 benefit, and asking each member to state which component of the decomposition produces that implication and whether they would defend it -- and say what I should explicitly decline to cover in two hours (formal models of time-inconsistent choice, equity weighting, declining-rate schedules) while saying that I am declining it.
