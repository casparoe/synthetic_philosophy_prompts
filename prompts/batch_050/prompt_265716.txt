The phenomenon of pain asymbolia — in which patients, typically following damage to insular or cingulate regions or after cingulotomy, report that they still feel pain and can often describe its location, intensity, and quality, yet sincerely deny that it hurts, bothers them, or motivates them to do anything about it — is usually treated as a puzzle for theories of what pain is. But it also sits upstream of a cluster of larger disputes: whether the badness of pain is constituted by its phenomenal character or by some downstream attitude (aversion, care, dislike); whether the dissociation undermines phenomenalist and hedonist theories of well-being by counterexample or is instead better explained as a report artifact or a deficit of attention; what it implies for the welfare of creatures, such as fish or insects, that plausibly have nociceptive processing without the evaluative machinery that asymbolia patients have lost; what it implies for the welfare of artificial systems, whose pain-analogous signals might be functional all the way down; and what it implies for suffering-focused ethics, which needs to say what exactly should be minimized if badness is not intrinsic to the sensation itself.

Suppose I am on the steering committee of a small research funder with enough money for roughly three person-years of work in this area, spread across philosophy and the cognitive science of pain. Which open questions should we treat as load-bearing, and in what order should they be attacked? Give me a defended prioritization rather than a survey: for each candidate question, say what depends on its answer (so that I can see which questions are upstream of which), whether it is tractable with current tools — conceptual analysis, the existing clinical literature, psychophysics, animal-welfare science — and how neglected it is relative to its importance. Pay particular attention to one thing the committee keeps getting wrong in internal discussion: members conflate the question of what the asymbolia patients' experience is actually like with the question of what their reports show, and I'd like the agenda to state explicitly whether these can be investigated independently or whether the second is hostage to the first. Finally, identify at least one question in this vicinity that attracts a lot of attention but that you think the committee should decline to fund — either because its answer wouldn't change anything downstream, or because the disagreement around it is merely verbal — and explain what evidence, if it emerged in the next few years, would most change your ranking. No bibliography or reading list, please; the deliverable is the agenda itself and the reasoning behind it.
