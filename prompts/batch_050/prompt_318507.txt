So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm starting a big feature on insect (and other small-animal) sentience. Not the "do bees feel pain, look at this cool study" angle -- that's been done to death. I want the piece to be about the *structure* of the problem, specifically the thing people in this space call the scale argument: there are something like 10^18 insects alive at any time, so even a 0.1% chance that each one has a morally relevant inner life, multiplied across that population, could swamp everything else we worry about. Chickens, pigs, lab mice -- rounding errors by comparison, on that logic.

Here's what I need help with. I've got maybe 6,000 words and I can only properly develop three, maaaybe four, of the open questions in this area. Which ones should they be, and in what order? Some candidates I've been circling:

- Whether the probability-of-sentience estimates people throw around (often just gut feelings dressed up as percentages) can be put on any kind of principled footing, or whether the whole scale argument collapses if they can't.
- Whether "expected moral weight" is even the right way to aggregate, given that tiny probabilities times huge numbers is exactly where expected-value reasoning tends to go haywire.
- The neuron-count and brain-architecture proxies -- whether they're measuring anything connected to experience or just measuring what mammals happen to have.
- Whether welfare *range* matters as much as probability: even if insects are sentient, can their experiences be bad enough, or intense enough, to compete with a suffering mammal?
- The practical question of what anyone would even *do* differently -- insect farming, pesticide use, habitat stuff -- and whether there are interventions that are robust to being wrong about most of the above.

For the ranking, tell me the criteria you're using (I'm guessing some mix of: how much downstream stuff depends on it, how tractable it is right now, how much it would actually change a reader's behaviour or a funder's decisions). Also tell me which of my candidates you'd cut entirely and why, and flag any open question I'm missing that's more important than my weakest one. And be honest about which questions are basically philosophy-in-a-lab-coat versus which ones entomologists could genuinely settle in the next decade -- I need to know what I can actually report on rather than just gesture at.
