I'm in a seminar on theories of well-being, and this week we read a view one of the readings called tranquilism: roughly, nothing is ever positively good for a person, and what matters for well-being is solely the absence of suffering, pain, and unfulfilled craving -- a perfectly content, desire-free mind is at the ceiling of value, and no pleasure could raise it further. I found the view attractive at first, since it captures why relieving misery seems so much more urgent than adding pleasures to already-fine lives. But a classmate pressed an argument in discussion that she says shows the view proves too much, and after three days I still can't locate the flaw, if there is one. Here it is, as I've reconstructed it. (1) A state of affairs is good for a person only insofar as it removes or prevents suffering or frustrated desire in them; there are no positive goods, only the absence of bads. (2) So two states in which a person has zero suffering and zero craving are equally good for that person, no matter what else they contain -- a lifetime of serene, satisfied contentment and a state of feeling absolutely nothing are on a par, since both contain none of the only thing that matters. (3) But there are two ways for a person's future to contain no suffering: they can live on contentedly, or they can cease to exist painlessly, since a nonexistent person plainly suffers nothing and craves nothing. (4) By (2), then, a happy person's continued contented life and their painless immediate death are equally good for them. (5) Worse, continued life always carries some risk of future suffering while nonexistence carries none, so for anyone, death is if anything the safer and therefore better of the two options. (6) Hence tranquilism entails that the best thing a benevolent agent could do for everyone who currently exists is to painlessly extinguish them, and that we do our friends no favor by keeping them alive. (7) That conclusion is monstrous, so (1) is false and tranquilism is refuted.

My problem is that I can't tell whether the argument is invalid or simply valid with a premise I should surrender, and my classmate refuses to say which she thinks it is. If I had to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than accept (6) -- the thought that well-being bottoms out at undisturbed contentment, and that piling pleasures on top adds nothing, still seems to me like a real insight I'd hate to lose. But I also see that (2) through (5) each looked individually innocent when she stated them. So: which step should I reject? If the argument is invalid, name the exact inference that fails, explain why it looked like it followed, and say what distinction I'd need to draw to block it. If it's valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up, what giving it up costs a tranquilist in particular, and whether there's a way of keeping the spirit of (1) while denying that a painless extinction would be a favor to anyone.
