I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, and this term I'm organizing a reading group on the problem of biological individuality — the question of what makes something one organism rather than many, or a mere part of one. A colleague in my department studies corals and keeps needling me about siphonophores, and after a few months of meetings I think we've genuinely exhausted the standard criteria, which is exactly my problem: I want one more, and I want it to be genuinely different. Here are the accounts we have already covered, so please don't repeat or repackage any of them: (1) the genetic homogeneity criterion — an organism is the set of cells sharing a genotype, descended from a single zygote; (2) the metabolic or physiological integration criterion — an organism is a maximally integrated, self-maintaining metabolic whole whose parts exchange matter and energy so thoroughly that they cannot function when pried apart; (3) the immunological boundary criterion — whatever the immune system consistently tolerates and defends against disruption counts as part of the self; (4) the evolutionary-unit criterion — an organism is whatever can function as a unit of selection, typically via a single-cell reproductive bottleneck, germline sequestration, and internal policing mechanisms that suppress conflict among lower-level entities; and (5) autopoiesis — an organism is a self-producing network of processes that continuously generates and maintains its own boundary.

What I'd like from you is a sixth criterion for organismhood that is not a rewording, a special case, or a mere conjunction of the five above — something that individuates organisms on a different basis altogether. It may help to know that I'm not asking for the correct criterion, just a defensibly novel one, but please don't gesture at something so vague (like "an organism is whatever feels unified") that it draws no line at all. Then stress-test your proposal against four borderline cases: the Portuguese man o' war, a colony of genetically identical, functionally specialized zooids that cannot survive independently; a honeybee colony, whose members are genetically distinct but reproductively and behaviorally integrated; the human holobiont, a host plus its gut microbiome, where the microbes are metabolically entangled with the host but have their own evolutionary interests; and a large clonal aspen stand, thousands of stems connected by one root system. Tell me, for each case, what verdict your criterion delivers and whether that verdict is a selling point or an embarrassment. Finally, be honest about whether your criterion actually survives this exercise or quietly collapses into one of the five — if it's really the evolutionary-unit criterion wearing a hat, I'd rather you say so. Please write your answer in American English.
