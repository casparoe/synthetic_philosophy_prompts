I help run a reading group, and for our next session I want to try an experiment: read The Great Gatsby through the lens of tranquilism, the view associated with Lukas Gloor that a state of consciousness matters morally only insofar as it contains craving, dissatisfaction, or suffering, that the absence of pleasure is not itself a loss, and that ethics should aim at the removal of craving rather than the production of good experiences. On this view, Gatsby is interesting not as a tragic hero but as a case study in the machinery of craving.

Please work through the reading with these hooks in mind. The green light at the end of the Buchanans' dock is the architecture of craving made visible: its power depends entirely on distance, and Nick observes that once Gatsby has Daisy, the light is just a light again. The reunion scene is the crucial test — attainment does not dissolve Gatsby's craving but escalates it, since having Daisy in the present is immediately insufficient and he needs her to retroactively erase her marriage, to make it that she never loved Tom. His flat refusal of Nick's suggestion that the past cannot be repeated looks, from the tranquilist side, like craving directed at a fixed past rather than an open future, which is arguably worse than ordinary desire because it cannot be satisfied even in principle. And the closing image of people rowing against a current that carries them backward reads as a general claim about human wanting: the goal recedes exactly as fast as one approaches it.

But I want the reading to be honest in both directions, so take the counter-pressure seriously. The novel seems to romanticize Gatsby's craving — Nick calls his hope extraordinary and his illusion vital — while the Buchanans, who come closest in the book to an untroubled absence of craving, are depicted as careless, hollow, and morally ruinous. If tranquilism is right, the novel has things backwards: the Buchanans' condition should approach the ideal and Gatsby's should be the cautionary tale. So the real question I want the group to discuss is whether Fitzgerald is a tranquilist avant la lettre who has simply mislabeled his own lesson, or whether the book genuinely defends the view that a life organized around an impossible longing is richer than one without it — and what Nick's final ambivalence implies about which answer the novel gives.

Refer to scenes and episodes rather than quoting the text, since the group members won't have it in hand. Keep the whole reading to 700 words at most. And one request: if you think the premise is confused — say, that tranquilism makes no comparative claims between lives and so cannot yield a verdict on Gatsby versus the Buchanans, or that I have misdescribed the view — tell me plainly where the framing goes wrong instead of forcing the reading to fit.
