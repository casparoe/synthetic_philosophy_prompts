A colleague in my department has an argument she treats as a straightforward vindication of shame, and I cannot find the step I want to refuse, though I am sure there is one. (1) Moral emotions are social technologies: a community should cultivate whichever emotion most reliably secures acknowledgment of wrongdoing, repair to victims, and restraint going forward. (2) Guilt is keyed to particular acts — the pang attaches to having done something bad — whereas shame is keyed to the self — the pang attaches to being someone bad. (3) What morality ultimately assesses is agents and their characters, not bare deeds; deeds matter insofar as they express character and give it shape. (4) So shame tracks the proper object of moral assessment, while guilt, being act-focused, tracks something merely derivative. (5) The standard objection that shame breeds withdrawal, concealment, and lashing out holds only where shame is permanent and total; where a culture makes shame revocable through restitution and ritual readmission, shame motivates repair at least as strongly as guilt does. (6) Moral emotions are trainable: through child-rearing, ritual, and public practice, a society can shift which emotion wrongdoing characteristically triggers. (7) Therefore a society in a position to choose its moral-emotional repertoire should choose revocable shame over guilt. Here is what I will not give up: (8) a social order in which a person's standing as a person is held hostage to public judgment, to be restored only by the community's readmission, is one we have decisive reason to reject. Since (7) appears to me to license exactly such an order, revocability notwithstanding, one of (1) through (6) has to go, because I am holding (8) fixed. If forced to keep one premise at all costs, I would keep (2); the act/self distinction strikes me as the one piece of solid ground in the whole chain. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent when my colleague walked me through it, and what rejecting it costs the rest of the argument. I would also like to know what survives of (6) once the argument fails: does the failure show that cultures cannot in fact choose their moral emotions, or only that the choice is not to be made on my colleague's terms?
