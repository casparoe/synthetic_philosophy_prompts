I'm drafting a short paper on rational belief for a grad seminar and want someone to check my commitments before I commit to them in print. Here's where I've landed:

1. Lockeanism: rational outright belief is just credence above some threshold, say 0.95. High confidence is sufficient for belief, nothing else needed.
2. Because of the lottery, rational belief is not closed under conjunction. You can rationally believe of each ticket that it will lose without rationally believing that they'll all lose.
3. But rational belief is still closed under single-premise entailment: if you rationally believe p, and p obviously implies q, you rationally believe q.
4. In the preface case, the careful historian rationally believes each individual claim in her book and also rationally believes, on inductive grounds, that the book contains at least one error. Both at once, and that's perfectly fine.
5. Whatever you rationally believe, you may flat-out assert, and you may use it as a premise in practical deliberation -- belief is a license to treat the proposition as true when acting.
6. Stakes make no difference to what's rational to believe; only your evidence does.

Which of these are genuinely in tension and which only look like it? For each real tension, say what has to give, rank them by severity, and tell me what the cheapest overall fix is. I've gone back and forth on whether (1) and (3) conflict but I suspect the real problem is somewhere else.
