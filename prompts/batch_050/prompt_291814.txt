Hey, so I'm planning a session for my undergrad seminar on ethics and existential risk (about 22 students, 75 minutes) and I want something interactive on the unilateralist's curse. When I explain it in lecture — if lots of agents each have a noisy private estimate of whether some irreversible action is worth taking, and any one of them can just go ahead and do it, then the action gets taken more often as the group gets bigger and better informed, even when doing it is actually a bad idea — they nod along politely but I can tell it doesn't land. It sounds like a trick of the math to them, not a real feature of the world. I want them to feel it.

Design me a game I can actually run in class. Rough shape I have in mind: each student privately gets some noisy information about whether pressing a metaphorical big red button is good or bad for the group's point total, anyone can press at any moment, and it only takes one presser for it to happen to everyone. Small but real stakes — points toward participation, or chocolates I've actually brought, whatever works. I'd like the structure to let me demonstrate that the disaster rate goes UP as I add more well-informed decision makers, which presumably means several rounds with different sized groups? Work out the actual numbers for me — signal distributions, payoffs, timing — so the effect reliably shows up within my 75 minutes, including at least one round where the button is genuinely bad and gets pressed anyway by a well-meaning student with a high signal. Cards, envelopes, dice, whatever materials you need, but keep it low-prep.

Then give me a debrief script: map specific moments from the game onto the structure of the curse, connect it to real cases (geoengineering, publishing dual-use research, sending signals to space), and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students conferring before deciding, someone figuring out that personally abstaining is pointless, that kind of thing — and how to handle each. Also be honest about which feature of the curse the game can't reproduce. My guess is the genuine irreversibility and the fact that in real cases the estimates aren't independent draws, but tell me if you think it's something else. One more thing: don't have the activity name the curse up front — I'd rather they live through it and then get the label in the debrief. Oh, and these are philosophy students, not econ students, so nothing that requires them to compute expected values on the fly.
