You are a philosopher with expertise in aesthetics and the philosophy of humour, and you have a good working knowledge of the three main families of humour theories: superiority theories (humour as an expression of triumph or felt superiority over someone), incongruity theories (humour as the perception of a mismatch between what is expected and what occurs), and benign violation theory (humour as the simultaneous appraisal of something as a violation and as benign). Think carefully before settling on your design; begin by briefly brainstorming two or three candidate scenarios, note why the rejected ones fail, and then develop the winner in full.

Construct a single comic scenario -- one joke, pratfall, cartoon, or short incident, described in at most 150 words -- engineered so that the three theories diverge as follows: incongruity theory and benign violation theory both predict that the scenario is funny, while superiority theory predicts that it is not. Then construct a second scenario, again at most 150 words, where incongruity theory predicts humour but benign violation theory predicts none (i.e., a case of incongruity without any violation, benign or otherwise), and say what superiority theory predicts here and why.

Constraints on both scenarios: no puns or wordplay (linguistic ambiguity lets each theory claim the case too cheaply); nothing involving real named people or groups; the humour must not depend on taboo, shock, or cruelty (which would hand superiority and violation readings an easy foothold); and the scenarios must be realistic enough to be used as stimuli in an actual psychology experiment with undergraduate participants.

After presenting each scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification: give the verdict each theory delivers and derive that verdict from the theory's own commitments rather than simply asserting it. Then, for each scenario, identify the single load-bearing design feature that forces the divergence, and explain what happens if that feature is removed -- specifically, whether removing it collapses the case back into one all three theories handle identically. Finally, for whichever theory comes out worst overall, present the strongest counterattack its defender could mount against your construction (for instance, by reclassifying an element of the scenario so the theory's prediction changes), and assess whether that counterattack succeeds.

Take definite positions throughout: commit to specific verdicts for each theory on each scenario, and commit to a verdict on whether the counterattack succeeds, giving your reasons. Do not hedge by presenting multiple readings and leaving the choice to me; where genuine ambiguity exists, resolve it explicitly and say why your resolution is the right one.
