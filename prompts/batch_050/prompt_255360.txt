Please give me your credence that the transition to agriculture was, on net, bad for the people who experienced it — more precisely, that a typical member of a farming population in the first two or three millennia after local adoption had a lower quality of life than a typical member of the immediately preceding foraging population in the same region — stated as a number between 0 and 1, with the reasoning decomposed rather than defended in general terms. My own is about 0.6. Break the question into at least the following: (i) the empirical question of whether the "original affluent society" picture of forager life — ample leisure, adequate nutrition, low disease burden — survives modern ethnographic criticism of small-sample work on extant foragers; (ii) the aggregation question of whether the comparison should be per-capita at a fixed population size or in terms of total welfare, given that farming ultimately supported far larger populations, and how much the verdict depends on contested premises in population ethics; and (iii) the lock-in question of whether "bad on net" is even the right category, given that each step of intensification may have been individually rational while population growth progressively closed off reversion to foraging. Assign a rough probability or weight to each component, show how they combine into your final number, and identify which one is doing the most work — that is, which, if resolved the other way, would push your credence across 0.5. Finally, if your number differs from mine, tell me in which of these three components the disagreement most likely lives.
