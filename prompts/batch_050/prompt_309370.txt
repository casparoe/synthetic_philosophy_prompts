I've been TAing a moral philosophy seminar and we've spent three weeks on the question of why promises bind, and honestly the state of the literature feels stuck. Roughly, the camps seem to be: the expectation/assurance people (Scanlon-ish), who say promising wrongs are a special case of the general wrong of letting someone down after you've gotten them to rely on you; the conventional-practice people (Hume, Rawls-ish), who say the obligation comes from participating in a valuable social practice whose rules you've invoked; and the normative-power or consent people, who say promising is the exercise of a standing power to create obligations at will, like a little sovereign act. Each side has a stock objection to the others that everyone already knows: the expectation view supposedly can't handle promises the promisee doesn't care about or learn of, the practice view has the problem of people bound by conventions they never signed up for (and promises made by isolated castaways), and the normative-power view gets accused of just relabelling the mystery -- of course obligations bind if you have a power to bind yourself, but why think we have that power rather than just saying we do? Nobody in my seminar changed their mind even once in three weeks, and the papers we read mostly talk past each other.

So here's what I want: a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled, not another verdict on which view is right. My own hypothesis, which you should feel free to demolish, is that the three camps aren't actually disagreeing about promises at all -- they're disagreeing about what a moral explanation has to do. The expectation people think you explain an obligation by reducing it to a more general harm; the practice people think you explain it by locating it in a social structure; the normative-power people think some obligations are basic and don't need reducing, so they experience the other two as missing the phenomenon while they experience him as mystifying it. If that's right, the stock objections land as "your theory doesn't do the thing my theory thinks a theory should do," which is why nobody ever moves. Tell me whether that diagnosis holds up, whether the disagreement is substantive or about standards of explanation (or something else -- maybe different intuitions about the hard cases are doing the real work), and what, concretely, would count as progress: a new case nobody's theory handles, a distinction between kinds of promising, a result from some adjacent field, or is the impasse just what bedrock disagreement looks like.
