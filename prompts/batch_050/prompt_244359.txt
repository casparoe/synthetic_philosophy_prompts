I'm in an introductory philosophy of religion course, and we just read Plato's Euthyphro (in Jowett's translation). A friend from my debate society, who hasn't read the dialogue, summarized its central argument for me as follows: 'Socrates refutes divine command theory with a dilemma. Either the gods love what is pious because it is pious, or it is pious because they love it. The first horn means morality exists independently of the gods, as a standard above them; the second means anything could be holy if the gods willed it, which is absurd. So Socrates proves that right and wrong cannot depend on any god's will, and religious ethics collapses. Incidentally, this is the same argument he uses earlier when he points out that the gods quarrel.'

I suspect this is too neat, but I'm not confident about where. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful reader of the dialogue would accept, which subtly misrepresent it, and which are just wrong? In particular: does Socrates' argument around 9e–11b — the point about being god-beloved being something that happens to the pious rather than what makes it pious — actually establish that a moral standard exists independently of the gods, or does it show something narrower about how piety can and cannot be defined? Does it matter that the dilemma targets a proposed definition of piety rather than every possible claim that morality is grounded in divine will? Is the quarreling-gods passage earlier in the dialogue really the same argument, or does it do different work? And does anything in the dialogue license the conclusion that religious ethics collapses, or is my friend generalizing past what the text supports? I'd like to be able to correct him precisely, citing Stephanus numbers rather than impressions.
