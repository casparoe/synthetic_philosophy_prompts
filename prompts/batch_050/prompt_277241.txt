A colleague of mine teaches a seminar on rational choice and has long used the familiar self-torturer setup: a device with a dial of 1,000 settings, each setting imperceptibly more painful than the one before, the increments always below the threshold of noticeable difference, and the top settings agonizing; a subject is offered money for each notch turned. Over the years she has told me her verdicts on a series of variants, and I would like to know what she will say about a new one.

Her verdicts so far:

1. One notch only, $10,000, the device removed afterward: take the deal.
2. All 1,000 notches offered as a single irreversible package for $10 million, it being known that the endpoint is agony: refuse.
3. Stepwise, $10,000 per notch, increments imperceptible, free to stop at any point: refuse to begin. Her reason, stated roughly, is that she would have no signal telling her when to stop.
4. Stepwise as in (3), except that each increment is clearly perceptible though small: begin, and stop at whatever point the next increment is no longer worth $10,000. She denies that this procedure lands her anywhere bad.
5. Stepwise as in (3), with imperceptible increments, but with a binding pre-commitment that locks the dial at notch 150, a mild persistent ache she judges well worth $1.5 million: accept, and turn all 150.

The new case: stepwise, $10,000 per notch, each increment clearly perceptible, stopping physically free at all times — but her cumulative earnings are displayed on a large screen in front of her, and she knows from a previous trial with a similar setup that when the running total is visible she does not stop at her planned point; she continues until the pain is severe, and afterward she rates the final outcome as worse than never having begun.

Please answer the following, in American English:

First, predict her verdict on the new case, and identify which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog, saying on which dimension the analogy runs.

Second, set out the two or three decision rules that best fit her five verdicts. Show that each candidate rule reproduces all five verdicts, and state what each predicts about the new case; at least two of the rules should diverge on it. Note explicitly any initially plausible rule that her track record rules out.

Third, give your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with reasons, and say what her predicted verdict on the new case follows from.

Fourth, assess the coherence of her pattern. In particular: is refusing (3) while accepting (1) defensible, given that the first step of (3) simply is (1)? Does her pattern treat each choice as a separate transaction or treat sequences as the unit of evaluation, and what does that imply about where the original puzzle's pressure comes from — intransitive preferences, the separability of choices, or the use of imperceptible comparisons?

Finally, describe one further variant of the setup, not among the six above, whose verdict would most cleanly discriminate between the two leading candidate rules, and say what each rule would predict for it.
