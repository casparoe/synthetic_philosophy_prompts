I host a podcast on the philosophy of economics, and my next guest is a decision theorist who defends the rationality of ambiguity aversion in Ellsberg-style choice problems — roughly, the view that it can be rational to evaluate a prospect by its worst-case expected utility across a set of candidate probability distributions, rather than by a single prior. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the paradox, pressing instead on the places where the defense is hardest to sustain. Include at least one question on each of the following: (a) the symmetry challenge — any prior over the unknown urn's composition that treats red and black alike still yields probability one-half for each color, so why can't an ordinary Bayesian simply model ambiguity as second-order uncertainty and declare the two urns equivalent; (b) the charge that abandoning the sure-thing principle exposes the agent to dynamic inconsistency — for instance, reversing choices as information about the urn's composition gradually arrives — and whether her reply commits her to holding that rational plans need not be time-consistent; (c) the extension problem — outside the lab, probabilities are almost never given, so what does the view imply for real choices like buying insurance or pricing climate policy, and does it risk licensing paralysis whenever some worst case is conceivable. For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give (for example, if she says worst-case evaluation captures a legitimate desire to hedge, ask what exactly is being hedged when only one ball will ever be drawn).
