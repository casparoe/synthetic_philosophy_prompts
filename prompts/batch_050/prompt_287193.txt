I edit fiction submissions for a small literary magazine — about twenty stories a week, of which several involve worlds whose moral fabric differs from ours: societies where cruelty counts as kindness, where betrayal is a duty, where the narrative voice treats something monstrous as unremarkable. Increasingly our first readers flag these as "broken" or as evidence that the author is a monster, and I need a consistent way to triage them before I spend an hour reading.

Please write a decision procedure my first readers and I can actually run on a flagged story, grounded in what philosophers of fiction call imaginative resistance — the familiar fact that readers who happily imagine talking animals, time travel, and impossible physics balk when a fiction asks them to imagine that cruelty is right. The procedure should include:

- An ordered set of diagnostic questions that distinguish at least three diagnoses: genuine imaginative resistance (the story asks us to treat a moral deviance as true-in-fiction and we cannot), mere implausibility (the author failed to earn or set up the deviance), and mere offense (readers dislike the content but could imagine it fine). For each, give a concrete test a reader can run on the page — for instance, whether substituting an equally deviant non-moral proposition into the same passage still produces the balk.
- For each diagnosis, what it implies editorially: which is fixable by the author in revision, which might be a feature worth publishing over reader objections, and which is grounds for rejection.
- The points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to an editor, stated honestly — including the contested question of whether resistance reveals a defect in the fiction or a limit in the reader.

Then run the procedure on three short sample passages, which you should invent yourself: (i) one where the diagnosis is fixable authorial failure, (ii) one where the procedure says the story is fine and the first reader's objection should be set aside — an answer I will not like, since my readers are the ones complaining — and (iii) one where the procedure must hand the decision off.

The whole thing must be runnable by a first reader with no philosophy background in roughly fifteen minutes per story, and it should say explicitly what it cannot do.
