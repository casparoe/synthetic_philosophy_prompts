I am a retired field entomologist who has recently begun studying moral philosophy, and I am preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher defending a view she calls calibrated scale-sensitive sentientism. Her position is that we should neither dismiss insect welfare because the probability of insect sentience is low nor treat every insect as if it were certainly sentient. Instead, we should estimate each taxon’s probability of having valenced experiences, multiply that probability by the likely intensity and duration of the experiences and by the number of individuals affected, and include the result in policy decisions.

Her view has four less familiar commitments. First, she assigns probabilities using clusters of markers—flexible behavioral trade-offs, state-dependent learning, motivational persistence, self-directed maintenance, and neural integration—rather than treating any one marker as decisive. Second, she insists that uncertainty is correlated across related species: if an apparent marker turns out not to track sentience in one lineage, this should usually alter our credence in similar lineages rather than being treated as an independent failure. Third, she distinguishes consciousness, valenced experience, and morally significant agency, but argues that only valence directly contributes to welfare. Fourth, she proposes an uncertainty budget: if more than half of an intervention’s expected moral value comes from organisms whose probability of sentience is below one percent, the intervention may be recommended as exploratory but not as morally mandatory. She nevertheless denies that this creates an absolute threshold, since sufficiently strong evidence could raise a presently low credence.

Draft nine interview questions for a one-hour conversation. They should progress from the foundations of the view to its most difficult consequences, and each question should contain a likely reply followed by a targeted follow-up. Avoid biographical questions, requests for reading recommendations, and the unhelpfully broad question of whether insects can feel pain. At minimum, the sequence should:

1. Challenge whether her marker cluster is calibrated by anything independent of intuitions formed from mammals, and press the response that convergent evolution provides independent confirmation.

2. Ask how she can assign numerical credences to sentience when we lack direct access to any nonhuman experience, followed by a question about what would count as being wrong in a systematic rather than merely taxon-specific way.

3. Press the scale problem: if even a very small probability attaches to enough trillions of insects, their expected welfare may swamp the certain welfare of a much smaller number of vertebrates. Include a follow-up for the likely response that expected value is not the same as certainty or urgency.

4. Examine whether her rule against treating species’ probabilities as independent weakens the scale argument, especially when billions of individuals belong to the same species but billions belong to distinct lineages.

5. Test the distinction between sentience and valence by asking whether an organism could have non-valenced experiences that matter morally, and what practical difference her answer makes.

6. Ask whether vast numbers of individually mild aversive states can aggregate against a smaller number of severe states, and require her to confront both ordinary compensation and the possibility that some harms are not fungible.

7. Interrogate the uncertainty budget. In particular, ask why a probability of sentience below one percent can support research but not obligation, and follow up on whether this introduces an arbitrary cutoff under another name.

8. Apply the theory to a concrete agricultural case in which replacing a broad-spectrum insecticide with a more selective method reduces insect deaths but raises food prices and increases pesticide use elsewhere. The question should bring out the difference between reducing expected insect suffering and unintentionally shifting suffering across taxa.

9. End by asking what empirical discovery would most substantially lower her credence in insect sentience, then ask why that discovery has not already lowered it.

The questions should be intellectually firm but civil, and should anticipate the replies most natural to her stated position rather than attributing to her views she has not defended.
