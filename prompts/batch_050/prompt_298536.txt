Construct a thought experiment for use in a political economy seminar, designed to show that "the socialist calculation debate" is really two distinct debates that are usually run together: Mises's argument that rational economic calculation is impossible without market prices for the means of production, and Hayek's argument that even with prices, the knowledge required for central planning cannot be concentrated in a single authority because it is dispersed, local, and often tacit. I want a scenario in which the two arguments come apart: the economy in your case must clearly escape Mises's objection yet still fail, or at least visibly strain, under Hayek's.

The scenario must meet the following specifications. First, it must be realistic and contemporary: an economy or large organization recognizable from the present day, with no science-fictional devices — no superintelligent planners, no brain scans, no perfect demand forecasting. Second, it must contain genuine market-generated money prices for everything relevant, including capital goods and labor, so that monetary calculation in Mises's sense is formally possible. Third, the economically crucial knowledge in the scenario — what to produce, how, when, and for whom — must be of the kind Hayek emphasized: tied to particular circumstances of time and place, held by many dispersed actors, and at least partly impossible to articulate in a form a planning board could use. Fourth, the scenario itself should be no longer than a single paragraph.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it works. State the verdict each position delivers and explain why: what Mises's calculation argument says about the case and why it does not bite; what Hayek's knowledge argument says and why it does; and what Lange's proposed mechanism — a planning board adjusting prices by trial and error in response to shortages and surpluses — would predict, given that prices in your case already exist and clear. Then identify the single feature of the scenario that does the work of separating the two arguments, and explain what happens to each of the three verdicts if that feature is removed. Conclude by stating the strongest objection a defender of Lange would raise against the case — for instance, that trial-and-error adjustment does not require the planner to possess the tacit knowledge at all — and assess whether that objection defeats the scenario or merely relocates the difficulty.
