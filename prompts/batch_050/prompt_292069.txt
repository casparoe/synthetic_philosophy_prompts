ok so I've been in enough threads about this that I want to just write down my actual policy-ethics commitments and find out if they're coherent. Roughly:

(1) If a change makes some people better off and nobody worse off, by their own lights, it's basically always worth doing — this is my main test for whether a policy is defensible.
(2) I'm a subjectivist about "better off": it means satisfying the preferences people actually have, not the preferences they'd have if they were better informed or raised differently. Correcting for that is paternalism and I don't trust it.
(3) I don't accept ex ante justifications on their own: if a policy predictably leaves some people worse off than they'd otherwise have been, pointing at the favorable odds everyone faced beforehand doesn't fix that.
(4) I'm fine with mandatory childhood vaccination programs, even though a small number of kids will be harmed by side effects who would have been fine otherwise.
(5) Nearly all real moral progress has come from Pareto-improving bargains and trades, not from anyone overriding anyone.

Which of these actually hang together? I suspect (3) and (4) are fighting, but I'm not sure I'm framing the problem right, and I have a nagging feeling (4) causes more damage than that. Give me the tensions in order of severity, for each one say what would have to give, and flag any apparent tension that dissolves if you're careful about it. Max 500 words.
