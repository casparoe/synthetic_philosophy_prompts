So I just got back from the pub where I had a massive, slightly shouty argument with a mate about national myths of heroic defeat -- Masada, Kosovo 1389, Dunkirk, the Confederate Lost Cause. It started over something stupid (a plaque, of all things) and ended with me defending a position I'm now not sure I believe, so I want it properly stress-tested.

The position, roughly: societies don't get to choose *whether* they have a myth of noble defeat, only *which one*. Defeats that matter to a group get mythologised no matter what historians do, because the myth is doing work that accurate history can't do -- it makes sacrifice mean something, it binds strangers together, it consoles. And the implication I defended: debunking these myths with careful historical correction is at best pointless and at worst harmful, because the mythic demand doesn't go away, it just regenerates in a cruder and often nastier form. So the real choice is never myth vs truth, it's a better myth vs a worse one, and the honest task is curating myths rather than correcting them.

What would count against this? I want a proper map of where it's exposed, not just my friend's pub-level objections ("so truth doesn't matter then??"). Specifically:

- Which real cases would be the strongest counterexamples? E.g. are there places where a defeat-myth genuinely *was* dismantled by historical work and nothing equally potent grew back (I was trying to think about Germany post-1945 and the stab-in-the-back legend, and whether the Lost Cause has actually deflated in parts of the American South, but I couldn't remember the details well enough)? What would a case have to look like to be decisive rather than just ambiguous?
- Which assumptions in my position are doing the heavy lifting and could just be false? E.g. the claim that the social function of these myths *can't* be performed by anything else, or that mythic thinking is supply-driven rather than something elites deliberately manufacture for their own ends.
- What does the position actually *predict* that its rivals don't? If a debunked myth dies quietly, my friend says his view predicts that and mine scrambles; if it mutates and comes back uglier, I say mine predicts that and his scrambles. Is there any observation that would settle this, or are we both armchairing?
- Am I guilty of having insulated the thing? It occurred to me walking home that I was treating every successful correction as "the myth regenerated invisibly" and every failed correction as "see, you can't kill myths", which does smell unfalsifiable. If so, how much does that matter for a view about how societies work, as opposed to a physics claim?
- What would a defender of my position concede as a genuine defeater -- as opposed to what only a critic would count? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and if the honest conclusion is that my position is mostly vibes wearing a theory's clothes, say so.
