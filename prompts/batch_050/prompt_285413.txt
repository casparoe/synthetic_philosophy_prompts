I got into an argument with a friend recently about whether adding more people to a decision-making body actually makes its decisions better. She kept invoking something like the wisdom of crowds -- if each person is even slightly better than chance, majority rule gets arbitrarily reliable as the group grows. I had a vague memory that this result (the Condorcet jury theorem, I think) leans on assumptions that can quietly fail, but I couldn't produce a concrete counterexample on the spot and it bugged me.

So: construct a thought experiment for me, to these specs. Build a realistic scenario -- a hiring committee, a medical review panel, a parole board, something like that, no sci-fi prediction machines -- with a small odd-numbered group (5 or 7 members) voting by majority on a yes/no question that has a genuine right answer. The scenario must satisfy ALL of the following at once: (1) every single member is individually better than a coin flip at getting the right answer; (2) members vote independently, in the relevant sense -- nobody sees or is influenced by anyone else's vote, no shared evidence that would correlate their mistakes beyond what the setup makes explicit; (3) nobody is corrupt, lazy, strategic, or irrational; and yet (4) the majority verdict is LESS reliable than just picking one member at random and going with their judgment. Small numbers, please -- I want to be able to verify the arithmetic by hand, so actually show the computation of both the group's accuracy and the average individual accuracy, don't just assert it.

After the scenario, do the checks: state precisely which standard assumption of the jury theorem your case violates, and explain why the other assumptions being intact isn't enough to save the conclusion. Then explain why the spec can't be met if I additionally require every member to have identical competence -- i.e., show me what makes the impossibility work in that case, at least informally. Also tell me what happens to your scenario as I scale it up (same structure, more members): does majority rule eventually overtake the average member, or does the gap persist, and why? Finally, the epistemic hygiene part: be clear about what's mathematically settled here versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff -- for instance, I'd guess the probability theory is airtight but whether real committees even approximately satisfy the independence condition, and whether results like this have any normative upshot for how we should actually design institutions, are live disputes. Don't present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
