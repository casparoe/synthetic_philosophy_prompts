I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I recently picked up Thomas Szasz's famous argument that mental illness is a myth -- roughly, that "illness" properly applies to lesions and dysfunctions of the body, so calling unwanted thoughts, feelings, or behaviors illnesses is a category error that dresses moral and social judgments up in medical language. What got me thinking was an episode I half-remembered and then read up on: in December 1973, the American Psychiatric Association's Board of Trustees voted to remove homosexuality from the DSM-II, and a 1974 referendum of the full membership upheld the decision, with a replacement diagnosis for people distressed about their orientation. The decision followed years of activist pressure, including protests at APA conventions, alongside internal scientific argument. As an engineer, the thing that jumps out at me is that a condition went from being a disease to not being a disease by a vote, after lobbying -- the way you'd deprecate a class in an API or reclassify a category of bug tickets, not the way smallpox stops being a disease.

Analyze this episode through Szasz's lens, in both directions. First, what does Szasz's framework predict and explain here? It seems tailor-made for his view: nothing about the "patients" changed between 1972 and 1974, no lesion was discovered or ruled out, and the classification changed in response to social and political pressure -- which looks like a confession that the diagnosis was a value judgment all along. But second, steelman the strongest anti-Szasz reading of the same episode: maybe it shows psychiatry working exactly as it should, correcting a classification that never met the criteria for disorder (no inherent distress or dysfunction) once the evidence was examined, the way any science corrects errors -- and the activists were just the mechanism that forced the re-examination. On that reading, the vote refutes Szasz rather than confirming him, because it shows psychiatry can distinguish genuine disorders from mere social deviance.

Then do the harder work: ask whether Szasz's position can actually draw the line the episode seems to demand. If the homosexuality case shows that some diagnoses are pure social control, does anything in his argument stop that conclusion from sweeping up schizophrenia, depression, and everything else -- and if nothing stops it, is that a feature or a reductio? Finally, tell me what the episode adds to Szasz that his abstract argument doesn't supply on its own: does watching a profession vote a disease out of existence, under pressure, teach us something about what a diagnosis *is* that no amount of conceptual analysis of the word "illness" could? Where the historical facts matter, feel free to flag where the record is contested rather than papering over it.
