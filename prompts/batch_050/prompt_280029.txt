Two people from my philosophy of biology reading group got into a real argument last week about "survival of the fittest" and I've been going back and forth on who won. Here's roughly how it went.

Marisol opened with the classic charge: the phrase is a tautology. If the fittest are just defined as the ones who survive and reproduce, then "the fittest survive" is true by definition, and natural selection can't explain anything any more than "bachelors are unmarried" does.

Dev said the tautology objection only works against a straw version. The standard fix is that fitness isn't actual survival -- it's a propensity, a disposition to survive and reproduce in a given environment. Two organisms can differ in fitness even if the less fit one gets lucky and the fitter one gets struck by lightning, just like a fragile glass is fragile even when it's never dropped. So no circularity: fitness is defined by what an organism is disposed to do, not by what it actually does.

Marisol had two comebacks. First: if fitness is a propensity measurable only in terms of expected reproductive output, then it's still defined through survival and reproduction numbers, so the circle just moves back one step -- you've relabeled survival as "expected survival" and called it progress. Second, and she said this was the stronger point: a propensity you can only ever detect through the very outcomes it's supposed to explain explains nothing. Saying the survivors had higher fitness is like saying the glass broke because of its breakability. It *sounds* causal but does no work.

Dev pushed back hard on the second point. He said that objection proves way too much: it would knock out fragility, charge, and every other dispositional concept in science, and nobody thinks those are explanatorily empty. And fitness isn't only detectable through outcomes -- you can estimate it independently from traits. Faster antelopes get eaten less; that's a prediction you can make before counting survivors, not a redescription after.

Marisol's last move: the trait-based estimates are just proxies, and they can misfire. An antelope with every "right" trait can die in a freak flood, and there's no principled way to specify which environmental conditions count as "normal" for the propensity to manifest. Worse, since fitness has to be relativized to an environment, and environments change constantly, "fitness" ends up tracking whatever happens to correlate with survival in the moment -- so it predicts trait frequencies rather than explaining them, which is what she thought the original objection was really getting at all along.

Dev's final answer was that this last point conflates fitness being *defined* mathematically (as expected contribution to future generations) with fitness having a *causal basis* in traits -- and that you can hold the first is non-tautological while the second does the explaining, and nothing in her argument touches that distinction.

So: can you go through this round by round and tell me who's right about what? Specifically -- does the propensity interpretation dissolve the tautology objection or just relocate it, does the "only observable through its effects" objection prove too much like Dev says, does the environment-relativity point land, and is Dev's final distinction between the mathematical definition and the causal basis actually as clean as he thinks? If both are missing something, I'd like to know what.
