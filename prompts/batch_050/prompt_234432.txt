I am trying to find out whether a pattern I keep noticing in discussions of fair machine learning has an established name, or whether it needs one. I will describe two instances concretely, since the abstract statement seems to lose something.

First instance: lending. A bank deploys a default-prediction model. At launch, auditors check the standard desiderata and the model does about as well as anyone can manage: its risk scores are calibrated within each demographic group, and its error rates are roughly balanced across groups given the existing difference in underlying repayment rates. The model is left in place. Because its decisions determine who receives loans, and repayment history feeds back into the credit data the model trains on, the underlying repayment rates of the groups drift further apart over several years. At the next audit, the bank finds that the package of criteria it originally satisfied is no longer jointly achievable at all: whichever criterion it restores, another one breaks. Nothing nefarious happened; the drift was a foreseeable mechanical consequence of deployment.

Second instance: content moderation. A platform's classifier is audited at deployment and passes a similar battery of group-level checks. But its decisions shape which users stay, leave, or change their posting behavior, so the populations and base rates the classifier sees gradually shift. After a few years, the platform faces the same situation as the bank: criteria that were simultaneously satisfiable at launch no longer are, at any error rate.

I know the static version of this territory: when base rates differ across groups, several natural fairness criteria cannot all hold at once, and this is a proven result, not a practical difficulty. What I am asking about is different in structure. There, the incompatibility is present from the start. Here, it is absent at deployment and is *manufactured by the system's own operation over time* — the impossibility arrives later, as an emergent property of the feedback between decisions and data. My questions: Does this temporal, self-induced version of fairness-unsatisfiability have an established name in the literature? What are the nearest named concepts — feedback-loop effects, concept drift, long-term impact studies, the static impossibility results themselves — and how exactly does the phenomenon I describe differ from each? If nothing fits cleanly, I would like a coined term with a precise definition, plus boundary cases: what would count as an instance, and what nearby phenomena (e.g., drift that is exogenous rather than self-caused) would fall outside it. A bare label will not satisfy me; the carving is what I need.

Please indicate how confident you are in your answer, and single out the specific point on which you are least certain.
