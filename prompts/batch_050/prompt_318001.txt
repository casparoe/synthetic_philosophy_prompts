ok so a little context: we adopted a dog in the spring, right around when my daughter turned three, and ive spent the whole summer watching the two of them learn things in completely different ways. the dog is pure trial and error, whatever earns a treat gets repeated forever. my daughter will stand at the bottom of the slide and you can literally watch her rehearse the whole disaster in her head before deciding against it, and half her tricks are things she obviously imported from us, words, counting, her grandma's method for getting shoes on the right feet. meanwhile the spider in the kitchen window hasnt tried a single new thing in its entire life and is doing great.

this got me reading about Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of kinds of minds — Darwinian creatures (everything pre-tested by natural selection, the experiments happened to its ancestors and the failures died), Skinnerian creatures (trial and error within one lifetime, keep whatever pays off), Popperian creatures (run simulations in your head first so your bad ideas can die instead of you), and Gregorian creatures (named after the psychologist Richard Gregory — minds that import their tools from culture: language, numbers, other peoples solutions).

i want a poem about this, in four short movements, one per kind of creature, and i want the form of each movement to behave the way that kind of mind behaves:

1. Darwinian: an old, tight form executed perfectly — a triolet or something like it — where the repeated lines come back word for word, nothing improvised, everything already tested. the spider's stanza.
2. Skinnerian: take one line from movement 1 and work it over by trial and error — show a couple of failed variants (struck through or bracketed) and let the version that earns the treat survive to close the movement. the dog's stanza.
3. Popperian: the speaker tries lines out and rejects them before committing — drafts left visible, crossed-out alternatives kept in, so the reader watches the bad lines die instead of the poem. my daughter at the bottom of the slide.
4. Gregorian: a patchwork visibly built out of borrowed stuff — cliches, half-remembered lines, little phrases my daughter says that she clearly picked up from us — held together with stolen tools.

around 30-36 lines total. it should be a real poem and not a diagram: someone whos never heard of Dennett should just read it as a poem about a spider, a dog, and a kid, but someone who knows the taxonomy should be able to tell which movement is which without being told, and to feel why the four have to come in that exact order.
