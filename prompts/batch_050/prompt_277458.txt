I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character comes to possess something like perfect impunity — the details needn't concern you — and in preparing him I have been reading Glaucon's speech in Book II of Plato's Republic (Jowett translation). Glaucon tells the story of the shepherd Gyges, who finds a ring that makes him invisible, and then says:

"If now there should be two such rings, and the just man should put on one and the unjust the other, no one could be found, it would seem, of such adamantine temper as to persevere in justice and endure to refrain his hands from the possessions of others and not touch them, though he might with impunity take what he wished even from the marketplace, and enter into houses and lie with whom he pleased, and slay and loose from bonds whomsoever he would, and in all other things conduct himself among mankind as the equal of a god. And in so acting he would do no differently from the other man, but both would pursue the same course. And yet this is a great proof, one might argue, that no one is just of his own will but only from constraint, in the belief that justice is not his personal good, inasmuch as every man, when he supposes himself to have the power to do wrong, does wrong."

Here is where I stumble, and I should be grateful for careful help. Glaucon begins with what looks to me like a prediction — a claim about how everyone would in fact behave once detection is impossible — and ends with what looks like a very different claim: that justice is not a good for the person who has it, but a burden borne under compulsion. I do not see how the second follows from the first. A novelist might write a character who stays honest with the ring on, and nothing in the passage seems to tell us whether Glaucon would call that character impossible, or merely rare, or whether his argument even permits him to distinguish the two. Could you reconstruct the argument's structure for me — what exactly the conclusion is, what premises do the work, and what the thought experiment is supposed to show that mere observation of ordinary human conduct could not? In particular: is Glaucon reasoning from what people desire to what is valuable, and if so, is that inference one he is entitled to, or one he is exposing as the vulgar view so that Socrates can answer it? I ask because my whole book turns on which of those readings is right, and I should not like to dramatize a misunderstanding.
