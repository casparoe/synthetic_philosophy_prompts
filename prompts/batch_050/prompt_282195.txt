My mother died last month at seventy-four, and I am giving the main eulogy at her memorial next Saturday. For thirty-one years she was a palliative care nurse, the one families requested by name, and for just as long she was a devoted reader of Emmanuel Levinas -- she worked through Totality and Infinity in her forties with a dictionary and a pencil, and the margins of her copy are a second memoir. She was not religious, and she left written instructions for this service, as characteristic as anything she ever did. Two of them are easy: no talk of her being in a better place, no assurances that she is watching over us. The third is the one I am writing to you about. She asked that no one, as she put it, sum her up. No sentence beginning "above all, she was." No inventory of virtues, no reduction of her to a lesson the rest of us can carry away like a party favor. She believed, following Levinas, that a person is never a set of describable qualities -- that the face of the other makes its claim on you before and beyond anything you could say about them, and that to eulogize someone by packaging them into a coherent character is a polite form of violence, a way of finally getting them under control now that they can no longer interrupt you.

Please write the eulogy, roughly five minutes at a measured pace. The room will be mixed: my father and siblings, grandchildren, a dozen nurses she trained, former patients' families, and two or three of her philosopher friends who will notice immediately if the speech gets Levinas wrong or merely name-drops him. The difficulty I want you to solve is structural, not decorative: a eulogy that takes her conviction seriously cannot do most of what eulogies conventionally do. It cannot praise her by cataloguing her goodness, cannot claim that I or anyone fully knew her, and cannot recruit her into teaching the audience one final tidy thing. But it also cannot be a cold or withholding speech, because her whole view was that we are claimed by others, obligated before we choose to be, and a eulogy that refused warmth in the name of respecting her alterity would betray the thing itself. I would like the speech to enact her philosophy rather than lecture about it -- to speak, somehow, about what her presence demanded of the people near her, and about the particular way she answered the demands of dying strangers, without ever converting her into a theme. If you use anecdotes, and I hope you will, choose ones that let her remain slightly out of reach rather than ones that pin her down. She disliked eulogies where the speaker makes the deceased into evidence for the speaker's own sensitivity, so please keep me in the background as much as the form allows.

One more request: end without consolation-by-summation. The family and the nurses need comfort, and I want them to leave with something solid, but the comfort has to come from the structure of her view itself -- from what it means that she spent a life answering faces, ours included -- rather than from any claim about where she is now or what she would have wanted us to feel. If you think a short closing line addressed to her directly, rather than about her, could carry that weight without sentimentality, I would welcome the attempt; she always insisted the second person is the only grammatical mood ethics has.
