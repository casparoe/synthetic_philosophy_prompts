So I keep running into this thing in "for want of a nail" discussions (r/history, ACX comments, etc) and I want to know if it has a name. You're an expert on philosophy of history and counterfactual reasoning -- please actually brainstorm adjacent concepts before answering, don't just grab the first label that comes to mind. The pattern: people lump all "small cause, big effect" stories together, but I see two very different animals. Type A: the small cause truly flips the trajectory -- if the coin lands differently, the whole region's history for the next century is genuinely different. Type B: the small cause changes *who* or *when* but not *what* -- shoot the archduke or don't, and some other spark sets off the great war a few months later; remove the charismatic leader and a different one with similar policies fills the slot. People even argue about famous cases by tacitly reclassifying them from A to B ("that storm didn't matter, the empire was declining anyway"). The butterfly effect is sort of nearby but it's about sensitive dependence in deterministic systems, not about which of these two kinds our actual world is. And the contingency-vs-inevitability debate is about particular events, not this distinction itself. So: does the A/B distinction -- call it history-as-amplifier vs history-as-shock-absorber -- have an established name or literature, and if not, can you coin and define it? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, covering: the nearest existing concepts and why each doesn't quite fit, your proposed definition, the trickiest boundary cases (like something being absorbed at one scale and amplified at another), and how you'd test which type a given case is. No intro or conclusion.
