Last week I was at a dinner party where someone confidently asserted that the world is becoming less violent, and a philosopher at the table shot back that this is only true if you rig the bookkeeping first. The argument went in circles for an hour because, as far as I could tell, everyone was quietly counting different things: one person meant battlefield deaths, another meant murder, a third meant something broader like exploitation and preventable suffering. I came away suspecting that much of the disagreement is about the choice of yardstick rather than about the world itself, and I'd like help thinking this through more carefully.

Consider the following six candidate conceptions of violence, each of which could serve as the basis for a long-run verdict on whether the world has grown less violent over, say, the past eight centuries: (1) the per-capita rate of death by interpersonal violence, i.e., homicide; (2) the absolute number of deaths from war and organized conflict per decade; (3) the share of all deaths, from any cause, that are attributable to violence; (4) the prevalence of socially and legally accepted violence, such as whether slavery, torture, and corporal punishment are lawful and considered legitimate; (5) self-reported victimization, i.e., what proportion of people alive in a given era experience assault, domestic abuse, or chronic fear of attack; and (6) so-called structural violence, meaning preventable death and suffering produced by social arrangements rather than by any identifiable aggressor, as when institutions knowingly let people starve. Sort these six into tiers by how much weight each deserves, taken on its own, in adjudicating the long-run decline-of-violence claim. For each one, give me a sentence or two on what it captures well, what it systematically misses, and why it lands where it does; in particular, attend to the per-capita-versus-absolute distinction and to the question of who gets counted as a victim.

Finally, two small additions. First, I have devised a metric of my own, which I call the conditional exposure share: the fraction of all person-years lived in a given century during which a person faced an annual risk of violent death above one in two hundred. Tell me which tier this lands in and why. Second, end your answer with a single number between 0 and 1 representing how much of the disagreement over whether the world is becoming less violent you take to be merely definitional -- disagreement that would evaporate once the parties fixed a common metric -- as opposed to genuinely empirical, meaning it would persist even among parties who had agreed on exactly what to count. Justify the number briefly.
