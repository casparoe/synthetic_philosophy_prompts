After a long argument with a friend about what egalitarians ought to equalise, I want a rigorous classification instrument for a political philosophy seminar. My friend maintained that only people’s welfare can matter fundamentally and that correcting their preferences is paternalistic; I argued that preferences formed under deprivation or exclusion may be unreliable evidence of what justice requires. We repeatedly failed to agree on where particular compromise views belonged.

Write a decision tree that takes a student from the initial claim that avoidable interpersonal inequalities stand in need of justification to a named position on the currency of egalitarian justice. Build the tree from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of famous authors. Each branch should be labelled with a diagnostic question that someone could answer without already knowing the standard terminology. Use concrete consequences, not labels, to make the divisions.

Throughout the tree, use the following fictional test case. The town of Northmoor introduces an annual independence grant intended to place every adult on an equal footing. Ada uses hers to compensate for a mobility impairment, although inaccessible public buildings still prevent her from participating fully. Bruno demands a much larger grant because his privileged upbringing has left him with expensive tastes. Celia reports herself perfectly content because years of exclusion have taught her not to expect education, travel, or public office. Dara receives the same money and opportunities as everyone else but is systematically treated as a social inferior. Edmund has an unusually wide range of valuable options but does not especially enjoy the one he chooses.

The tree must include diagnostic questions concerning: outcome versus genuine opportunity; actual preferences versus preferences corrected for adaptation, ignorance, or manipulation; compensation for disability versus responsibility for expensive tastes; the difference between possessing resources and being able to convert them into valuable activity; whether social standing is merely instrumental to some other good; and whether justice requires strict equality or only an adequate minimum in the relevant currency.

There should be between eight and ten leaves. They must locate, at minimum, subjective welfare equality, equality of opportunity for welfare, resource egalitarianism, capability egalitarianism, relational egalitarianism, sufficientarianism, and at least one coherent pluralist or lexical hybrid. Add unnamed leaves where a combination of answers is logically available but lacks a familiar label, explaining briefly whether the gap seems to result from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Standard surnames may be attached to positions where helpful, but the classification must not depend on recognising them.

For every leaf, provide: the sequence of answers leading to it; its verdict on each Northmoor resident; and one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment. End by flagging any positions that your questions cannot reliably separate, and state what additional case or distinction would be needed to separate them. The finished tree should help students discover which conception of equality they actually accept, rather than allowing them to choose whichever label sounds most attractive.
