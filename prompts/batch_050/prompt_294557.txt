In September 2015, a photograph of a drowned Syrian toddler, Alan Kurdi, lying face-down on a Turkish beach circulated worldwide and produced, within days, a measurable shift in European public opinion, a surge in donations to refugee charities, and concrete policy responses, including Canada's accelerated resettlement program and Germany's suspension of Dublin Regulation returns for Syrians. Within months, the momentum reversed: the New Year's Eve assaults in Cologne and the November Paris attacks hardened publics, borders closed, and the image itself became an object of kitsch reproduction and, later, of counternarrative propaganda.

Analyze this episode through the lens of Levinas's account of the face of the Other and ethics as first philosophy, treating the analysis as running in both directions. First, the mapping: who occupies the position of the self and who the Other in this episode, what Levinas's claim that responsibility precedes choice and consent would say about what European publics and governments faced, and where his insistence that the face resists representation -- that to grasp the Other as an image, a type, or a theme is already to betray the ethical relation -- leaves the photograph. Is a photograph an encounter with a face, or precisely the thematization Levinas warns against, or something his framework lacks the resources to classify? Second, apply his own bridge from ethics to politics: the arrival of the third party, which forces the comparison of incomparable Others and justifies institutions, law, and calculation. Does the episode vindicate this move -- the one face commanding a response that had to become policy for millions -- or expose it as unstable, since the same logic of comparison that justified opening borders later justified closing them? Third, run the analysis in reverse: is the rapid evaporation of the moral response, and its replacement by image fatigue and backlash, an anomaly for Levinas (suggesting he never anticipated mediated, mass-reproduced faces), a confirmation (the image was consumed as representation and so the ethical demand was never truly encountered), or evidence that his account describes a phenomenology of obligation with no predictive content at all? Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and conclude with a verdict on which side of the analysis -- Levinas illuminating 2015, or 2015 exposing a gap in Levinas -- is more instructive.
