I've just finished TA-ing a graduate epistemology seminar in which we spent four weeks on the norm of assertion debate, and I've come away with the nagging feeling that the participants have been talking past one another for roughly twenty-five years. The broad shape of the dispute is familiar: one camp holds that knowledge is the norm of assertion — that one shouldn't assert a proposition unless one knows it — while rival camps propose truth, justified belief, reasonable belief, or certainty instead. Each side trades in what look like the same kinds of data: lottery cases (it seems somehow wrong to flat-out assert that one's ticket will lose, even though that's overwhelmingly likely), Moore-paradoxical conjunctions, unlucky cases where a speaker asserts something true and reasonably believed but doesn't know it, and the observation that the standard conversational challenge to an assertion is a request for the speaker's epistemic credentials. The defenders of the knowledge norm claim these data uniquely favour their view; their opponents produce counter-cases — blameless assertions of falsehoods, or assertions that would be absurd to retract upon challenge — and claim those data favour theirs. Nobody appears to have been moved an inch.

My hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and not a conviction, is that the debate is stalled because it is secretly three different debates wearing one hat: a debate about the epistemic standard for epistemically proper assertion (a question about what we're entitled to say), a debate about the constitutive rules of the speech act itself (a question about what makes something an assertion at all, analogous to asking what makes something a move in chess), and a debate about the norms of conversational criticism (a question about which challenges are licensed and which responses count as adequate to them). If that's right, then a single set of cases cannot adjudicate all three at once, and the apparent intractability would be an artefact of the parties measuring their theories against different explananda while citing the same intuitions. But I'm not confident of this: perhaps the disagreement is genuinely substantive, and the camps differ over something deeper — over whether there even is a unified speech act answering to the name 'assertion', or over what kind of thing a constitutive norm could be, or over whether intuitions about propriety and intuitions about blamelessness are being illicitly conflated (an assertion can be improper without the speaker being blameworthy, and some parties seem to slide between the two).

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Please diagnose the debate using what the main positions actually say and the cases they actually lean on, rather than surveying the positions for me. Specifically, I'd like to know whether the impasse is best explained as verbal, as a clash of bedrock intuitions about the same cases, or as the sides answering different questions under a single heading; whether the appeal to constitutive norms is doing genuine work or is a red herring; and — given your diagnosis — what would count as progress: a distinction (and if so, which), an empirical result from the experimental work on how ordinary speakers actually treat assertion and retraction, a formal analogy with games or promising, or an argument that no resolution should be expected because the category itself fails to carve nature at a joint. If you think my three-debates hypothesis is wrong, I'd rather hear that with reasons than have it politely accommodated.
