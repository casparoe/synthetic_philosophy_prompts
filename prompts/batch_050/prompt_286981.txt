Had a long argument with a friend last night about Popper and whether “laws of history” are even possible, and I’m still annoyed because his summary sounded superficially convincing. It went roughly like this:

Popper’s target in The Poverty of Historicism isn’t prediction as such, but unconditional historical prophecy: the idea that society must move through discoverable stages towards an inevitable destination. His central argument is that history depends partly on the growth of knowledge, future knowledge cannot be predicted before it is discovered, and therefore the future course of history cannot be scientifically predicted. From that it supposedly follows that there can be no laws of history at all, because the social sciences can explain events after the fact using the logic of situations but cannot establish lawlike regularities. Trends such as democratisation, declining violence, or technological acceleration are merely retrospective patterns rather than laws. Since large-scale predictions are impossible, Popper rejects social engineering across the board and thinks reform should rely on the invisible hand, trial and error, and piecemeal tinkering rather than on general social theories.

I challenged the jump from “we can’t predict future knowledge” to “there are no laws of history”, and my friend said I was missing that Popper is only attacking historicism, not every conceivable historical regularity. But when I pushed him, he basically admitted that his version also rules out weaker claims, such as institutional arrangements making some outcomes more likely, conditional generalisations about revolutions, or stable tendencies operating only under specified conditions. That seemed too strong to me, and I’m not sure whether Popper is being represented accurately or through the strongest possible reading of him.

Can you audit my friend’s summary, sentence by sentence if useful? Separate what is fair, what is a defensible but debatable gloss, and what is probably just wrong. In particular:

- Is the unpredictability-of-future-knowledge argument really about all historical prediction, or only a narrower class of unconditional forecasts?
- Does Popper’s argument rule out laws of history, or only laws that entail a determinate historical trajectory?
- How does the distinction between laws and trends actually work here? Could a genuine law generate different trends in different contexts, or a trend arise from a changing conjunction of laws and initial conditions?
- Does rejecting historicist laws commit Popper to rejecting conditional, ceteris-paribus, or institution-dependent social regularities?
- Is “social engineering across the board” the right description of what Popper opposes, given his distinction between utopian and piecemeal approaches?
- What exactly does explanation by “the logic of the situation” amount to, and does that itself presuppose some kind of lawlike social theory?
- If Popper’s argument succeeds, what remains possible: explaining revolutions retrospectively, warning about unintended consequences, designing institutions robust to several futures, or making bounded forecasts? Which of these would count as a law of history in an interesting sense?

My suspicion is that the summary is directionally right about inevitability and teleology but turns a targeted argument against unconditional historical prophecy into a much stronger thesis that no lawlike knowledge of society is possible. Tell me whether that suspicion is right. Please answer in British English, and don’t turn it into a bibliography or reading list.
