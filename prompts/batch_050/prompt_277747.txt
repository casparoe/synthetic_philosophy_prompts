Last weekend I had a long and unresolved argument with a close friend, and I would like your help both in deciding what to actually do and in understanding what the argument was really about.

I serve on the board of a small volunteer-run community organization of about 200 members. Two months ago our treasurer — a volunteer of many years, widely liked — came to me and confessed that over the past year he had taken roughly $4,000 from the organization's accounts, partly to cover personal debts. He came forward before anyone suspected anything, he has already repaid about half, and he is plainly devastated. The board must now decide between two responses. The first is quiet restitution: he resigns as treasurer, repays the balance on a schedule, the board tightens its financial controls, and the membership is told only that he has stepped down. The second is public acknowledgment: at the annual meeting he stands up, states what he did, and asks the membership to let him remain in the organization in a non-financial role while he completes repayment.

I described the case to my friend in the abstract, without names, and she argued heatedly for the second option. Her view, as I understood it, is that communities go wrong when they convert wrongdoing into a private administrative matter: the wrong here is not merely a missing $4,000 but a breach of the trust of 200 people, and it can only be repaired in front of those people. She added that a community in which public disgrace is a live possibility is one in which people take their obligations seriously, and that our instinct to spare him the ordeal is squeamishness dressed up as kindness. I argued for the first option: he has already acknowledged what he did and is making it right, which is the hardest part and was entirely voluntary; forcing him to display himself before the membership shifts the response from what he did to who he is, and risks crushing a person who is already doing everything one could ask of him. Her reply was that my framing is itself the problem — that modern life systematically dissolves the social experiences that give wrongdoing its weight, and that this is a loss, not progress.

Please advise me on what the board should do, but do not stop at a verdict. I want the structure of the disagreement laid out: whether my friend and I actually differ about facts (what each response does to the wrongdoer, to the victims, and to the community's future conduct), about values, or about something else entirely; what the strongest version of each position is, stated well enough that she would recognize hers; whether her empirical-sounding claim that a community benefits from making wrongdoing publicly weighty is the kind of claim that evidence could settle, and if so what that evidence would look like; and whether a group like ours can even deliberately choose this aspect of its own character, or whether such things resist being chosen. Throughout, separate carefully what is genuinely well-established about how people respond to being confronted with a bad action as opposed to being marked as a bad person, and about what practices of public censure do to the communities that use them, from what remains honestly contested among people who have thought seriously about these questions. Do not present either of our positions as the obvious consensus view.
