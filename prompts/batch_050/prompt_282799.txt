In his 1908 Cologne lecture Space and Time, Hermann Minkowski introduced the world-line picture of special relativity and wrote:

"The whole world appears to be resolved in such world-lines, and I may just anticipate, that according to my opinion the physical laws would find their most perfect expression as mutual relations among these world-lines."

Rietdijk (1966) and Putnam (1967) turned this picture into an argument against becoming: since simultaneity is frame-relative, an event still future for me here-now can already be present — and hence, on pain of making reality observer-dependent, real — for a suitably moving observer coexisting with me now; so the future must be as determinate as the past. The replies have been just as constant for sixty years: simultaneity in special relativity is a coordinate convention from which no ontology can be extracted, or, following Howard Stein, becoming survives if it is relativized — each here-now has a determinate past light cone and an open future. Both camps still cite the same Lorentz transformations, agree on every experimental consequence, and nobody has moved.

My hypothesis is that the impasse turns on how one reads sentences like Minkowski's. One side treats the four-dimensional representation as the finished physics and reads its ontology straight off the diagram; the other hears the hedge — "according to my opinion," "most perfect expression" — and takes him to be stating a methodological preference about the form physical laws should take, which no geometrical fact could upgrade into a verdict on temporal becoming. But I may be wrong: perhaps a genuinely formal question is hiding here, namely whether any coherent becoming relation can be defined on Minkowski spacetime at all, and settling that would be a theorem, not an intuition pump.
