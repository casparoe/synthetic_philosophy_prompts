I have no formal training in philosophy; what follows comes from a few years of reading and thinking on my own, so forgive any nonstandard terminology. In 1963 Edmund Gettier published a very short paper showing, by way of two small stories, that a belief can be true and justified without amounting to knowledge. The standard examples are familiar: the stopped clock you happen to glance at during the one minute of the day when it shows the right time, the sheep-shaped rock you mistake for a sheep in a field that does, out of sight, contain a sheep. Since then, as I understand the history, epistemologists have proposed a long series of repaired definitions of knowledge -- rule out inferences that pass through false premises, rule out "defeaters," require the right causal connection between fact and belief, require that the belief track the truth across nearby possible situations -- and each repair has been buried under a purpose-built counterexample within a few years. Meanwhile a minority position holds that the entire project was misconceived: knowledge cannot be decomposed into simpler ingredients and should be treated as basic.

What I want is not another proposed fourth condition, and not a survey of the ones already on offer. I want a diagnosis of why this debate has run for sixty years without converging, given that the participants share the cases, share the verdicts on the original cases, and share their methods of argument. My own suspicion -- offered as a hypothesis, not a conviction -- is that the disagreement is not really about knowledge at all but about what a successful analysis of a concept is permitted to look like. Every condition that survives more than a few rounds seems to smuggle knowledge back in: a "defeater" turns out to be characterized in terms of what a well-placed subject would know, and "tracking the truth" gets spelled out in terms of what the subject would believe in counterfactual circumstances. So perhaps the two camps are really divided over whether that kind of circularity is fatal to an analysis or is simply what conceptual analysis always delivers.

Is that the right diagnosis? Or is the true structure of the impasse something else: a verbal dispute masquerading as a substantive one, divergent intuitions about cases that neither side can argue the other out of, a hidden empirical question about how ordinary speakers actually deploy the word "knows," or a concept that simply has no clean boundaries, so that the demand for necessary and sufficient conditions was itself the error? And depending on your answer: what, if anything, would count as progress -- a new species of case, an impossibility result, evidence about ordinary usage, a change of question? If your diagnosis implies that some particular hard case (fake barn country, say) is where the real disagreement lives, say so and explain why. And if the honest conclusion is that nothing will settle this, I would like that argued with reasons rather than shrugged at.
