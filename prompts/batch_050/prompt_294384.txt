I coordinate comfort audits for a hospice network whose funder has adopted a crudely hedonist metric: more pleasure, less displeasure. The staff keep colliding with the heterogeneity problem: what, if anything, makes morphine euphoria, relief when nausea stops, the taste of ice cream, prayerful absorption, sexual arousal, lucid dreaming, and merely not being afraid all instances of one thing we can record on the same form? Design a bedside procedure a nurse can run in under ten minutes, without philosophy training and without pretending false precision, to decide which episodes count as pleasure, how to compare them across kind, intensity, duration, and stability, when to discount pleasure as deluded, alienated, addictive, coerced, or informationally hollow, and where the form must read clinical judgment rather than compute an answer. Preserve the distinctions from the felt-quality, attitudinal, desire-based, and family-resemblance answers only insofar as they change what a nurse writes down. Apply the procedure to opioid euphoria after dose escalation, to a patient who reports joy because she thinks the chaplain is her deceased brother, and to a dying patient whose only request is to be left alone and who reports contentment when undisturbed. Include at least one outcome the family will hate, state what the procedure cannot settle, and answer in American English.
