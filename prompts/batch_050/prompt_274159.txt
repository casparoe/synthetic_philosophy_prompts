I help run a university philosophy society whose members are bright but have no physics and only second-hand exposure to quantum mechanics -- they know Schrödinger's cat from popular culture and not much else. For next term's series on thought experiments, I have a seventy-five-minute session on quantum suicide and quantum immortality. I want a plan for how to teach it, not the explanation itself.

Two things make this session harder than it looks, and the plan should reckon with both. First, the topic bundles together two ideas that are usually run together: the quantum suicide setup as a proposed way of distinguishing between interpretations of quantum mechanics (the claim that an experimenter who survives repeated trials has first-person evidence for many-worlds over collapse), and quantum immortality as the further extrapolation (that because some branch always persists, one never experiences one's own death). I want the arc to build the first properly and then let the second emerge as an extension of it, with the seams visible, rather than presenting one mushy idea.

Plan the session along these lines:

The order of ideas. What must be in place before the setup makes sense at all -- I assume the branching picture introduced as one live interpretation among others, the distinction between what an outcome looks like third-person and what it could be like first-person, and some grip on self-locating uncertainty -- and why that order and not another. Which of these can be assumed from earlier sessions and which I must rebuild from scratch.

The carrying example. I expect to build the whole session around the iterated quantum-triggered device: a measurement with two outcomes, repeated, where one outcome kills the subject and the other leaves them fully intact to run the next trial. Say what features the example must have for the argument to get going (genuinely binary outcomes, a genuinely quantum trigger, no middle states in the stipulation), why repetition rather than a single trial is what does the work, and why Schrödinger's cat, the example they already half-know, is the wrong vehicle here.

The misconception map. List the misunderstandings this audience will predictably form, in the order they will form them, and mark the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I anticipate at least: that the thought experiment is meant to prove many-worlds outright; that immortality in this sense means invulnerability rather than the existence of a surviving branch; that this is a practical recommendation or an experiment anyone should want to run; and the conflation with ordinary risky behavior where most of you, in every branch, simply takes the bullet. I suspect there are one or two I haven't anticipated; include them.

The honest objection. At some point the plan must make room for the strongest deflationary response -- that real death is gradual and branchy rather than instantaneous, so the clean binary stipulation never obtains outside the thought experiment -- and say where in the session it belongs: early enough that it doesn't ambush the whole construction, late enough that students have felt the pull of the argument first.

What to refuse to explain. Specify what I should openly bracket at this level -- presumably the Born rule, the measure problem, and the question of whether many-worlds is actually true -- and how to say so without making the session feel evasive.

The check for understanding. End with one question I can pose that distinguishes students who understood the structure from students who merely found it spooky. My candidate is: a participant concludes, "so if many-worlds is true, fearing death is irrational." Tell me what a good answer to that looks like in terms of the machinery we've built, or propose a better closing question and justify the swap.
