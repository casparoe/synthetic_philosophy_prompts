I am an applied statistician; for the past decade my working vocabulary has been structural causal models: directed acyclic graphs, interventions via do-operators, identifiability, mediation analysis with natural direct and indirect effects, and the occasional argument about colliders. Three months ago I joined a research group evaluating psychotherapies, and my new colleagues keep reassuring me -- or warning me -- that talk therapy "works at the level of meaning, not mechanism", as though this settled something. This has pushed me into the philosophy of mind literature, where I have been reading about the causal exclusion argument associated with Jaegwon Kim: roughly, that if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and mental properties are not identical to physical properties, then there is no causal work left for the mental to do, on pain of systematic overdetermination.

Please restate this argument as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in. I want the full apparatus translated: the physical causal closure premise, the supervenience or realisation relation between mental and physical properties, the exclusion principle itself, and the threatened dilemma between epiphenomenalism, reduction, and overdetermination. Tell me what each of these becomes when expressed in terms of variables, structural equations, interventions, and (if it helps) the distinction between causes of an effect's probability distribution and causes individuated at different grains of description. I suspect several standard moves -- interventionist accounts of causation, the idea that mental and physical descriptions are the same system at different levels of abstraction, causal drainage arguments -- have crisp counterparts in my framework, and I want to know which ones.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me the residue report. What, if anything, in the exclusion argument has no expression in the language of causal models? Candidates that worry me: the notion that one cause "excludes" another rather than merely mediating it; the distinction between a property and the variable that carries it; the very idea of a level of reality as opposed to a choice of variable granularity. For each residue item, tell me whether it indicates something my framework cannot say -- a genuine gap I should feel -- or whether it is an artefact of metaphysical vocabulary that dissolves once you insist that causation is a relation between variables in a model rather than between properties in the world. I would rather be told that my toolkit is missing something than be flattered that the philosophers were confused.
