I have just taken over our department's upper-level seminar on medieval moral philosophy, and the final exam needs a new essay question on Abelard. Write one question on his thesis that sin consists in consent rather than in the deed, answerable in 75 minutes, that forces candidates to do more than summarize the position. I would suggest building it around the two hardest consequences of the view: first, that someone who fully consents to a wrongful deed but is prevented from carrying it out is as guilty as someone who performs it; second, Abelard's apparent willingness to say that those who persecuted Christ and the martyrs did not sin insofar as they sincerely believed they acted rightly — and would indeed have sinned had they acted against their own consciences. The question should press candidates on whether Abelard can hold both that acting against conscience is always sinful and that moral error is still possible, or whether the position collapses into a subjectivism on which no sincere agent ever does wrong; you might let candidates draw on his own test cases, such as the servant who kills his master in self-defense or the mother who accidentally smothers the child she is trying to warm. Provide (i) the question as it would appear on the paper, (ii) a model answer, and (iii) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, with a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker answers mishandle each point. In the model answer, flag where the interpretation of Abelard is genuinely contested, and state what consideration — textual or philosophical — would lead you to a different verdict.
