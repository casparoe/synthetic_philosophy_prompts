Is there a name for this? I'm a novelist, and I keep running into a strange structure whenever I try to think clearly about the identity of my characters across drafts. I've been rewriting the same novel for nine years, and the protagonist of the current draft shares a name, a childhood, and roughly half her scenes with the protagonist of the first draft, but almost nothing of her temperament. When friends ask whether she's "the same character" as the woman I started with, I find I can argue every possible answer into the ground. Yes, she's the same: there's an unbroken chain of revisions, each one small, connecting them. No, she's different: no reader of the first draft would recognize her. She's both: same in the respects that matter for the filing system, different in the respects that matter for the reading. She's neither: maybe "same character" was never a well-formed predicate to begin with, the way "same wave" isn't. What strikes me is that this isn't ordinary indecision between two options. Each of the four answers fails on its own terms, in a different way, and the failure of the third and fourth feels like a discovery about the question rather than a shrug.

I mentioned this at a dinner party and someone who'd studied philosophy said it reminded her of an ancient Indian thinker — possibly Buddhist, possibly a logician — who apparently treated certain questions as defective in exactly this fourfold way, rejecting an assertion, its denial, their conjunction, and even the rejection of all three. But she couldn't remember whether this was a method for dissolving bad questions or a claim about the world, and I may be garbling it entirely. So: does this pattern — the systematic collapse of an assertion, its negation, "both," and "neither," where the point is that the question itself presupposes something false — have an established name and literature? I'd like more than a label. I want to know how the described structure differs from (a) plain aporia or undecidability, where we simply can't tell which of two answers is right, (b) familiar puzzle cases like the Ship of Theseus, where the standard view is that the question is merely vague or conventional rather than ill-formed, and (c) the position that the correct answer really is "both," full stop. And if the Indian tradition I half-remember genuinely does this, does it apply the fourfold rejection to anything like my case — the persistence of persons or selves over time — and would it say my character problem is a real instance of the pattern or just a superficial lookalike?
