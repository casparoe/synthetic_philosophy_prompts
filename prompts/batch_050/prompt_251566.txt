I'm a second-year graduate student in a philosophy PhD program, applying for a one-semester internal fellowship that would buy me out of teaching so I can finish a dissertation chapter. My dissertation is on attitudes toward death across philosophical traditions, and the chapter I'm proposing is on the Zhuangzi — specifically on a tension I can't get out of my head and that I think the standard readings skate past. On one hand, the Inner Chapters are full of passages that treat death with remarkable equanimity or even cheer: Zhuangzi drumming on a tub after his wife dies, the skull on the roadside that declines to return to the anxieties of the living, the friends who greet illness and death as just further episodes in the great transformation. On the other hand, the same text is practically obsessed with survival strategy — the useless tree that lives out its years precisely because it is good for nothing, the advice to wander in the realm where nothing can harm you, the repeated warnings about the dangers of displaying talent and getting caught up in politics. So the text seems to say both that death is no great loss, perhaps even a release, and that a great deal of practical wisdom consists in not dying. Every reading I know resolves this too quickly, either by saying the survival advice is ironic, or by saying the equanimity passages are hyperbole aimed at consoling the bereaved, or by declaring the text too layered and composite to have a view. I think there's a better resolution available — something like the claim that the text distinguishes death as an event within the process of transformation from premature death as a failure of artful living, so that the fear it wants to eliminate is not aversion to dying per se — but I haven't worked out whether that distinction is stable or whether it collapses under pressure from specific passages.

Write the fellowship proposal (roughly two pages) for this project. It should contain: a precise statement of the question, sharpened so that it admits of a determinate answer rather than just calling for a survey of the passages; an explanation of why the quick resolutions sketched above are unsatisfying, framed so a fellowship committee of non-specialists can see what's philosophically at stake and not just what's philologically unresolved; a proposed method, including how I plan to handle the problem of doing interpretive work on a layered, multi-author text without begging the question by assigning each voice to a different stratum, and a rough month-by-month plan for a single semester; a description of what a successful result would look like, including what the deliverable chapter would argue; and an honest account of what would count as the project failing — for instance, what I would conclude if the best evidence ends up favoring the view that the text simply contains two irreconcilable voices, and whether that would itself be a publishable result or a dead end. Please don't include a bibliography or cite specific scholars or translations by name; I'll supply the scholarly apparatus myself, and the committee cares more about the question and the plan than the name-dropping.
