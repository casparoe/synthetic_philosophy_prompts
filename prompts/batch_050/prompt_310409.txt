You’re a professional moral philosopher with a specialty in filial ethics and the theory of personal relationships. Think this through carefully rather than giving me a survey of familiar views; I want you to pressure-test a position, not just summarize the literature.

The position I’m considering is a modified version of Jane English’s friendship model of filial obligation. Roughly:

Adult children do not owe their parents care, contact, financial help, gratitude, or deference simply because they are biologically related or because the parents made sacrifices while raising them. Those sacrifices are not debts that can later be called in. What grown children owe depends instead on the current quality and history of the relationship, understood through the norms of friendship: affection, loyalty, mutual concern, willingness to help within reason, and a special responsiveness created by having shared one another’s lives. But unlike ordinary friendship, the parent-child relationship usually has a much longer shared history and often leaves the parent vulnerable in distinctive ways later in life. So the relevant duties can sometimes be stronger than those between ordinary friends, even though biology itself does no independent moral work.

Call this the continuity-adjusted friendship model. Its main claim is that past parental sacrifice matters only indirectly: it can help explain the relationship’s history, generate expectations, or reveal what kind of care the child received, but it does not create a repayable debt. Likewise, estrangement matters, but not as an automatic moral eraser. A child might have good reasons for ending a relationship while still owing some limited, non-relational assistance to a severely dependent parent -- just as one might owe rescue or basic aid to a vulnerable person even if one no longer likes them.

What would count against this position? I want a map of its real exposure, not just a list of objections. Please distinguish:

1. Defeaters that would be genuinely fatal -- for example, showing that the view cannot explain some central filial obligation without smuggling in biology, gratitude, or debt after all.
2. Defeaters that would force major revision but not abandonment.
3. Cases that are merely awkward or emotionally counterintuitive.
4. Objections the model’s own defender should concede as serious, as opposed to objections that rely on assumptions the defender would reject from the start.

In particular, test it against cases like these: loving but financially comfortable parents; decent but emotionally distant parents; parents who sacrificed enormously but were controlling; abusive parents whose abuse stopped decades ago; estranged parents with dementia; parents who deliberately had a child partly to secure future elder care; siblings with very unequal capacities to help; and a parent who explicitly frames every childhood expense as something the child now has to repay. Feel free to replace any of these with a better case if one exposes the theory more cleanly.

I’m especially interested in four pressure points. First, does the model collapse into a generic theory of special obligations, where parent-child duties are just one instance of duties arising from any close relationship? If so, is that a flaw or the point? Second, can it explain duties to a parent with advanced dementia, when friendship-like reciprocity and shared agency are mostly gone, without quietly switching to a duty-of-rescue account? Third, does it unfairly let adult children dissolve obligations by unilaterally deciding that the relationship is no longer friendship-like? Fourth, does the debt model it rejects contain a kernel the friendship model needs -- not literal repayment, maybe, but some asymmetrical obligation created by having received unchosen care during one’s total dependence?

Please also say what the theory predicts that its rivals do not. For example, what would we expect to see in the moral structure of filial duties if this model were right, and what would count as evidence that biological kinship or parental sacrifice has independent moral force? Where possible, distinguish an argument that would merely make the view less attractive from one that would show it had misidentified the source of the obligation.

End with a verdict: is the continuity-adjusted friendship model genuinely vulnerable to evidence and counterexamples, or has it been qualified enough that almost any hard case can be absorbed? If the latter, is that flexibility reasonable philosophical sensitivity or a sign that the theory is becoming unfalsifiable? I’d rather you be blunt than balanced for balance’s sake.
