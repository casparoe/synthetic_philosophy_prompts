so i'm trying to write something on the value of information and I realized I don't even know what to ask of a theory of it. everyone cites Good's theorem -- free information never has negative expected value for a Bayesian who'll make a decision after learning it -- and then everyone else rattles off cases where information plainly hurts: the genetic test result you can't unlearn that makes you uninsurable, the player in a game who does worse once her information becomes common knowledge, the dieter who's better off not knowing exactly how good the cake tastes, the trader who'd profit from news if nobody could trade on it but can't once everyone can. so before i read another paper, write me the desiderata: what does an adequate theory of when information is worth acquiring actually have to deliver?

the list should cover at least:

- the cases it has to get right, with the case behind each -- Good's theorem itself as a datum to explain rather than just assume, information with negative value in strategic settings, temptation and self-control cases where ignorance is a commitment device, cases where learning changes the thing you're learning about (acting on the prediction defeats the prediction), and boundedly rational agents for whom processing the information is itself costly
- the distinctions it can't collapse -- expected vs realized value of information, ex ante vs interim vs ex post, value to the decision-maker vs value in the world, instrumental vs intrinsic/curiosity value, and my information being better vs everyone knowing that it's better
- the formal results it can't contradict -- Good's theorem in its home domain at minimum, plus whatever you think the no-trade and common-prior results imply for any account that says information is just always good

for each requirement say why it's a requirement and not just one camp's taste, and flag which ones you'd call genuinely non-negotiable. the part I most want: flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable. e.g. can one theory treat Good's theorem as a real theorem and also explain why rational agents sometimes pay to stay ignorant, without quietly redefining the agent or the decision problem between the two claims? if the answer is no, the list itself is the interesting result.

then briefly -- like a paragraph each, no table -- score the obvious contenders against the list: the naive "expected value of information is non-negative, full stop" reading, the refined decision-theoretic version (non-negative relative to a fixed decision problem with costless processing and no strategic spillovers), and whatever game-theoretic account handles the common-knowledge cases. keep the scoring short; the spec is the deliverable, not the verdicts.
