I work at a company where every deploy needs a manual sign-off from one particular team, a step that's been in the process since before anyone still here was hired. I wrote up a proposal to delete it — it's slow, noone can articulate what it actually catches, and we have no record of an incident it plausibly prevented — and a collegue replied in the thread with just: Chesterton's fence. Which everyone treated as checkmate.

What precise claims could that one-liner be making? Give me a menu of readings, from the trivial (find out why something exists before you remove it) up through the strong ones — there's always a reason; the reason is discoverable by thinking hard in advance; sheer survival is evidence of function so removal is on average harmful; the burden of proof is permanently asymmetric — and for each say whether it's a platitude, substantive but defensible, or just false. (Is there really always a reason, given drift, cargo-culting, and fences built for problems that never applied to you?)

Then the part I actually need: which reading does my colleague's move require for it to be a rebuttal rather than a request for homework I've already done? And does the principle weigh more for evolved institutions than for a process someone designed in 2016? Finally, it seems to quietly assume removal is hard to reverse — if bringing the sign-off back is one revert away, does the fence argument lose its force, or does some weaker version survive anyway?
