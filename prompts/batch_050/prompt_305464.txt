Seven weeks ago my aunt died, and under her will I have become the managing trustee of a small family foundation with an endowment of about four million dollars and an annual grant budget of roughly two hundred thousand. At our first meeting, the three trustees deadlocked immediately. One proposed dividing the year's budget into equal small grants so that no qualified applicant would leave empty-handed. Another argued for concentrating the money on very few recipients chosen by strict criteria. The third, my cousin, who had apparently been reading famine ethics, proposed that we have a moral obligation to spend the endowment down to nearly nothing within a few years, since people are suffering now. At that point someone invoked Garrett Hardin's lifeboat essay, and the meeting dissolved. I have since reread the essay, "Lifeboat Ethics: the Case Against Helping the Poor" (Psychology Today, September 1974), and the passage that stopped me was not the famous line about complete justice and complete catastrophe but this one:

"Some say they feel guilty about their good luck. My reply is simple: 'Get out and yield your place to others.' This may solve the problem of the guilt-ridden person's conscience, but it does not change the ethics of the lifeboat. The needy person to whom the guilt-ridden person yields his place will not himself feel guilty about his good luck. If he did, he would not climb aboard. The net result of conscience-stricken people giving up their unjustly held seats is the elimination of that sort of conscience from the lifeboat."

For our next meeting I want to settle criteria before we look at a single proposal, so please write the desiderata that a defensible doctrine of limited beneficence must meet — a doctrine stated generally enough that it governs both a foundation like ours and a state deciding on aid, since Hardin's passage operates at both levels. Requirements:

- Between eight and twelve numbered desiderata, each stated as a test a policy could pass or fail, followed by the concrete failure mode that motivates it (the kind of policy, real or imaginable, that the requirement exists to exclude).
- For each, mark whether it is non-negotiable or the preference of a particular camp, and name the camp (Hardinian triage, Singer-style rescue obligations, harm-based accounts, cost-effectiveness-first philanthropy, and any others you need). Do not score the camps against the list; I will do that with my co-trustees.
- The list must include at least one desideratum generated by the passage above. I read Hardin as making a selection argument — a policy that runs on guilt will, if complied with, breed the guilt out of the population that complies — and I want to know whether "a doctrine must not systematically erode or select against the motivations it depends on for its own compliance" is a genuine desideratum or a trick. Interpret the passage first: is it an empirical prediction, a reductio of guilt-based ethics, or a covert normative claim smuggled in by the phrase "unjustly held seats"? Consider whether it proves too much (would it condemn every sacrificial norm that has ever thinned its own adherents?) or too little.
- Include at least one desideratum on the safety-factor problem, since it is our live dispute: what, if anything, justifies retaining a margin — endowment principal, national reserves — when spending it would relieve present suffering? Say whether "preserve capacity against future shocks" is a real requirement or self-serving drift, and what would distinguish the two.
- Include at least one desideratum on Hardin's admission problem — the question of which ten to let in and what to say to the ninety — i.e., whether an acceptable doctrine must supply an administrable selection procedure, not merely a criterion of rightness, and what it may permissibly say to those it excludes.
- Identify which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidate doctrines, and say whether any subset of your list is jointly unsatisfiable; if so, name the minimal revision that restores consistency.

Roughly 1,200–1,500 words, not counting the passage. The trustees who will read this include one who thinks Singer settled the matter in 1972 and one who thinks Hardin did in 1974; the list has to be usable by people who disagree about the answer but can agree about what an answer must do.
