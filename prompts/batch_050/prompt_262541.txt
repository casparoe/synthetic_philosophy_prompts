I am drafting a historical novel set at a seventeenth-century court, and my antagonist, a first minister, justifies a purge with a speech my editor finds disturbingly strong. I need to know where it breaks, because if it does not break, neither does my plot. The minister reasons as follows, drawing, he says, on Pascal's melancholy observation that justice and force must be joined because neither suffices alone, and that since justice could not be made to prevail, men have instead arranged for the powerful to be declared just.

(1) Every legal order we know was founded by conquest, usurpation, or a settlement among victors — never by appeal to a justice that existed before the order itself.
(2) A norm constrains conduct only insofar as some power enforces it; unenforced justice is a wish, not a law.
(3) Therefore what a society calls justice is simply whatever its prevailing power successfully labels just.
(4) One cannot coherently appeal to a standard against the very thing that constitutes it, so appeals to justice against an established power are confused.
(5) Hence the honest man discards justice-talk entirely and speaks only of interests and force.

The minister believes this, but I cannot allow him to be right: the same reasoning would vindicate every durable tyranny in history and make my heroine's resistance a conceptual confusion rather than a crime. Somewhere between Pascal's observation and (5) something slipped, yet I cannot name it. If I must choose, I would rather surrender (2) than (1), but I want to know what that purchase costs.

Please respond with these sections, in this order: Diagnosis (name the step that fails, one paragraph); Why It Looked Sound (one paragraph); The Cost of Each Exit (roughly 200 words, weighing what the minister must give up depending on which premise he surrenders); A Better Speech (roughly 100 words — the strongest version of the argument he could deliver without the flaw).
