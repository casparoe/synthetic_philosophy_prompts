Here's a thought experiment I've been chewing on. Imagine a company builds exact cognitive duplicates of people -- same memories, same reasoning habits, same track record of being right and wrong, same evidence. You and your duplicate are both shown the same data about, say, whether some policy will work, and you walk out with credence 0.8 and it walks out with credence 0.4. Now the standard conciliationist move says you should meet in the middle at 0.6, and the steadfast crowd says you can sometimes hold your ground. But here's what bugs me: with a normal peer, the steadfast person can always say "maybe they made a mistake I didn't make, or maybe I have some insight they lack." With a perfect duplicate none of that is available -- the only difference between you is the output. So either conciliationism is obviously right in the duplicate case (and then the steadfast view needs to explain what extra ingredient real-world disagreement has that licenses standing firm, and whether that ingredient actually does the work), or you have to bite the bullet and say you can rationally stay at 0.8 against someone who is, by stipulation, exactly as reliable as you are, which sounds insane to me.

Write an essay that works through this: does the duplicate case show that conciliationism is fundamentally about there being no epistemically relevant asymmetry between you and the other person, or does it show that real peer disagreement always involves asymmetries the duplicate case strips away, so it's actually a poor guide to the normal debate? Also consider whether self-disagreement over time (you yesterday vs. you today, same evidence, different conclusions) is more like the duplicate case than like disagreement with another person, and what that implies. Reach an actual verdict and defend it -- don't just survey the options.

Max 700 words, and please write it in British English.
