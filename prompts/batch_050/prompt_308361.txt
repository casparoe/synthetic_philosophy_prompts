Here's a practice I've been puzzling over, and I want to know what philosophy is smuggled inside it. In the UK you're automatically enrolled into a workplace pension when you start a job, unless you actively opt out. Organ donation in England went opt-out a few years ago. Lots of consumer law works the same way: sign up to something on your doorstep or online and you get 14 days to cancel, no questions asked -- but walk into a shop and buy the exact same thing and there's no cooling-off at all. The official line is always that this fully respects your freedom, because nobody's forcing you: the default is reversible, the cooling-off period just gives you room.

But something strange is going on underneath. If my not bothering to enrol in the pension under an opt-in system counted as good evidence that I didn't want to save, there'd be no reason to switch to opt-out. So the whole practice seems to presuppose that the same person, facing the same options, reveals something different -- something more genuinely theirs, or better for them -- under one choice architecture than under another. What theory of preferences or autonomy does that actually presuppose? It can't be revealed preference. But then where do the 'real' preferences come from, the ones the better-designed architecture supposedly tracks? Is there a coherent answer that doesn't collapse into 'the architect knows what's good for you', i.e. plain old paternalism with extra steps? And please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with -- is it maybe philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license exactly the same defaults?

There's also what looks like an inconsistency: the same state that auto-enrols me into a pension because it doesn't trust my choices happily sells me lottery tickets and lets bookies advertise during the football. Can one implicit theory cover both, or is the practice just incoherent?

And what would have to change if we swapped out the presupposed view? If welfare just is preference-satisfaction, full stop, should we scrap the defaults? If we went frankly welfarist instead, should we just mandate pension contributions and drop the pretence that consent is doing anything?

One wrinkle I've been chewing on: suppose a pension app sets each person's default contribution rate individually, inferred from their spending data and what the algorithm predicts they really want. If the implicit theory behind the current practice is right, does anything go wrong there? It feels obviously different, but I can't tell whether that feeling tracks a real presupposition -- maybe the current practice quietly depends on defaults being uniform and crude.

Answer in British English please.
