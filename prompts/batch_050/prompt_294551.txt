I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past year reading philosophy in earnest, and I have been working my way through Wilfrid Sellars's essay Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind. I will confess at the outset that I find his prose considerably harder than anything I encountered in forty years of reading physics, so I have been leaning on a guidebook to twentieth-century philosophy of mind for orientation. Its chapter on Sellars summarizes his attack on the Myth of the Given as follows: 'Sellars's target is the idea that the mind is confronted with items — sensations, sense-data, immediate experiences — whose character we know simply by having them, independently of any concepts we happen to have acquired. Since nothing can play this role, Sellars concludes that all awareness is in fact inferential, a product of judgment rather than receptivity: there is no non-inferential knowledge, and the empiricist project of grounding knowledge in experience collapses, leaving us with a web of beliefs rather than contact with a world. Sense-datum theories are thereby refuted.'

My difficulty is this. In the opening section of the essay, Sellars himself remarks, in effect, that no philosopher who has attacked givenness has meant to deny the plain difference between inferring that something is the case and seeing it to be the case, and that if being 'given' meant no more than being observed, the existence of data would be as uncontroversial as the existence of philosophical puzzles. That opening seems to sit awkwardly with the guidebook's claim that Sellars denies non-inferential knowledge altogether and reduces all awareness to inference. I suspect the summary overreaches, but I cannot yet locate precisely where, or say whether the fault lies in the summary or in my own grasp of the text.

Could you audit the summary for me? Which parts would Sellars accept as a fair statement of his target; which parts conflate things he is careful to keep apart — I have in mind, for instance, the difference between saying that perceptual knowledge presupposes conceptual abilities and saying that it is inferred; and which parts misstate his conclusion? In particular, does anything he argues actually support the guidebook's closing claims that empiricism collapses and that sense-datum theories stand refuted, and how should the opening passage I have described be reconciled with whatever survives your audit? Please answer in continuous prose, as one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
