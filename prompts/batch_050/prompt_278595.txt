Last weekend a friend and I got into a genuinely heated argument that has been nagging at me since. She defended the view (which she attributes to a small corner of the effective-altruism-adjacent literature, associated with the name Lukas Gloor) sometimes called tranquilism: roughly, that nothing is positively good in itself, that experiences matter only insofar as they are free of craving, disturbance, and suffering, and that a state of complete undisturbed contentment is therefore every bit as perfect as the most intense bliss -- because both are equally unbothered. I kept pressing the obvious-feeling objections: that this makes a lifetime of meditation equal to a lifetime of love and adventure, that it can't explain why anyone should get out of bed, that it seems to imply there's nothing lost when a happy person never exists. She had patient answers to all of it, and I left the argument annoyed that I couldn't land a clean blow.

So I'd like you to channel my irritation productively: write me a satirical commencement address, about 600 to 700 words, delivered by a fictional graduating senior who has spent four years as the campus's most committed tranquilist and has somehow been invited to give the graduation speech. The address should be perfectly deadpan and internally consistent -- the speaker never slips into implying that excitement, joy, or achievement are positively good (that would be the one heresy), but instead earnestly congratulates the class on the cessation of their various cravings, praises the university for four years of relieved anxieties, and offers the graduates the tranquilist's version of go-out-and-change-the-world advice. The humor should come from the actual structure of the view, exaggerated but not invented: the collapse of bliss and calm contentment into equivalence, the awkwardness of motivational language when nothing is worth wanting, the recasting of ambition as a pathology the college has helped cure, the strange etiquette of consoling rather than congratulating.

Crucially, I want the satire to be fair enough that my friend would wince and then concede the hits. The address should quietly dramatize the real pressure points -- whether motivation without craving is coherent, whether a view on which perfect equanimity needs no improvement can celebrate anything, whether the asymmetry between relieving suffering and creating happiness can survive contact with a graduation ceremony -- without ever stating them as objections or letting the speaker notice the tension. A reader who knows the negative-utilitarianism literature should recognize that the author understands exactly what is being satirized; a reader who doesn't should still find it funny and come away having learned what tranquilism claims.
