Draft the joint statement that a defender of Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist argument and a defender of Don Marquis's future-like-ours argument could both sign after a two-year adversarial collaboration conducted in good faith. Neither side has converted; the point of the document is to make their remaining disagreement exact rather than to declare a winner.

Structure the statement as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: propositions about the ethics of abortion and about the logic of each argument that both parties will put their names to. These should be substantive, not vacuous -- for instance, claims about what the violinist case does and does not show, about whether the wrongness of killing ordinary adults needs a unified explanation, about whether personhood at conception would settle the moral question, and about which common objections to each argument (the contraception objection to Marquis, the responsibility-or-tacit-consent objection to Thomson) each side concedes are serious rather than easily dismissed.

Second, a disagreements table with two columns, each row stating one residual disagreement in wording neutral enough that both parties accept it, and then each side's position phrased as that side itself would insist on being phrased. At minimum the table should cover: whether the right to life includes a right to the continued use of another person's body; whether what primarily makes killing wrong is the deprivation of the victim's future or something else; and whether the two arguments are even answering the same question or have been talking past each other.

Third, a cruxes section. For each crux -- a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side -- give a concrete case or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance. To keep this honest, include at least two stipulated test cases that go beyond the standard literature: (a) a variant of the violinist case in which unplugging does not kill the violinist but erases his future -- he survives with no memory, no projects, and no prospect of developing any; and (b) an ectogenesis scenario in which any fetus can be transferred to an artificial womb at low cost and risk, so that abortion never requires ending the pregnancy inside the woman's body. For each case, state what Thomson's framework and Marquis's framework each imply, whether either side regards the implication as a reductio of its own view or as a bullet it will bite, and what modification, if any, each side would consider if the case cut against it.

Conclude with a short section on which of the disagreements the collaboration now judges to be partly verbal -- disagreements about what a right is, or about what the ordinary wrongness of killing consists in, that dissolve or shrink once terms are fixed -- and which remain genuinely substantive, with a one-paragraph explanation of why the substantive ones resist resolution by either side's standard moves.
