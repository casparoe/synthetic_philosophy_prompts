I run the data team at a regional health network, and we're merging patient records from two clinics that recently combined. The merge has surfaced a problem I need to design a policy around, and I'd like your advice.

Here's the situation. For some patients, the two source systems contradict each other: one clinic's record says a patient is allergic to penicillin, the other's says the patient has no known drug allergies. Both records come from systems we have independent reason to trust. Our prescription-support software is a rule-based engine that chains inferences from whatever is in the unified record. One of my engineers warns that this is dangerous in a specific way: she says that in the standard logical framework our engine is built on, once you accept both a claim and its negation, the inference rules let you derive literally any conclusion whatsoever — so a single contradictory allergy entry could, in principle, be used to justify recommending every drug, or none, for any patient. Her proposal is that the moment a contradiction is detected in a file, the engine should treat the entire file as unusable and refuse to draw any inference from it at all, even about unrelated matters like the patient's blood type or appointment history.

A second engineer thinks this is overkill. He proposes we simply record both truth values for the contested claim, flag it, and let the engine keep reasoning normally from everything else, while blocking only those inferences that pass through the contradiction. When I asked him whether his proposal amounts to saying that the allergy claim is both true and false at once, he shrugged and said maybe it is, for our purposes — the record genuinely asserts both, and pretending otherwise is what causes trouble.

I'm torn. The first proposal seems to throw away vast amounts of reliable information because of one localized defect; the second seems to rest on something I can't quite get my head around — treating a contradiction as if it could actually hold. I also worry that the second proposal merely hides the problem: if the engine "blocks inferences that pass through the contradiction," someone has to decide which inferences those are, and that seems like the whole difficulty restated rather than solved.

Please advise me on how to design the reasoning policy. In particular: Is the first engineer right that a single contradiction logically entitles every conclusion, and if so, is quarantining the whole file the right practical response? Does the second engineer's proposal really commit us to the possibility of true contradictions, or can it be motivated in a more conservative way — and does that distinction matter for how the system should behave? And how should we decide which inferences are contaminated by a contradiction and which are not?

After your advice, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "recommended_policy" (a one-sentence statement of the policy you endorse), "verdict_on_explosion_claim" (one sentence on whether one contradiction really licenses arbitrary conclusions), "true_contradictions_committed" (a boolean for whether your recommendation requires accepting that contradictions can be true, plus a one-sentence justification in "commitment_rationale"), "contamination_rule" (one sentence stating how to identify which inferences a contradiction taints), and "biggest_residual_risk" (one sentence on the most serious danger your recommendation leaves open).
