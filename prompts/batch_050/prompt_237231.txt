Having spent my career in experimental physics and only lately turned to philosophy, I have arrived at the following position: the independence axiom of expected utility theory is a genuine requirement of rationality, and the preferences people reliably exhibit in Allais-type choices, however psychologically robust, are mistakes. I would like to know what would count against this view, with the candidate defeaters ordered from fatal to merely awkward. Several things interest me specifically. First, would it matter if the pattern behind the Allais preferences -- the disproportionate attraction of certainty -- turned out to express a defensible attitude toward outcomes rather than a framing artifact, and how could that be shown? Second, is the standard defense, that the Allais chooser would repudiate his own preferences once the choice is decomposed into stages, or that he is exposed to exploitation by a sequence of trades, itself empirically vulnerable, and if so, how? Third, could a rival account that permits departures from linearity in probabilities be shown to be preferable on grounds that expected utility theory itself respects, rather than merely more accommodating of the data? Finally, has the axiomatic position been rendered unfalsifiable by the standing policy of classifying every violation as irrationality rather than as counterevidence -- and if so, is that a defect in a normative theory, or simply the correct posture for one? I am accustomed to theories that risk something in the face of recalcitrant results; I want to know what, if anything, this one risks, and which of these threats its own defenders would concede as genuine.
