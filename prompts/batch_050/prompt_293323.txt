I coordinate selections for a mid-sized grant program. Three times a year a panel of nine reviewers each submits a ranked list of 10–15 proposals, and we have to mash those into a single funding ordering by the end of a two-hour meeting. Every few cycles someone rediscovers Arrow's impossibility theorem and declares the whole exercise incoherent, and every few cycles someone else proposes a shiny new tallying method as if the theorem didn't exist. I'm tired of both camps.

What I want is a reusable procedure, derived from what Arrow's theorem actually says, that I can run at the start of each cycle to pick an aggregation rule for that cycle's slate and defend the choice afterwards. Concretely:

1. A diagnostic stage first: the questions to ask about this cycle's situation before touching any rule. Do the proposals plausibly lie on some natural spectrum, or are they genuinely incomparable? How much do I trust reviewers not to rank strategically? Do we need a full ordering of everything or just a defensible top-k (we only fund the top 4, and I have a feeling needing less than a full ranking softens some of the impossibility bite)? Can we credibly elicit intensity of preference, or does the theorem's ordinal restriction genuinely bind us? For each question, say what the answers rule in and out.

2. The core of the procedure, structured around the theorem's real content: since any rule must violate at least one of Arrow's conditions, each cycle is an explicit choice of which condition we're sacrificing and what that sacrifice costs here. Walk me through at least: dropping independence of irrelevant alternatives (Borda-style scoring and its clone-candidate / irrelevant-alternative shenanigans), restricting the domain (when single-peakedness plausibly holds and plain majority rule becomes safe), tolerating intransitivity at the top (Condorcet cycles and tournament-style fixes), relaxing non-dictatorship in a controlled way (a chair with tiebreak power; also, one reviewer having a veto — is a veto actually dictatorship in Arrow's sense or something weaker?), and exiting the theorem's scope entirely by going cardinal, including what fresh problems that buys you on the manipulability front.

3. For each branch: the failure mode to warn the panel about in advance, so when it bites mid-meeting nobody treats it as a scandal or a reason to improvise a new method on the spot.

4. Then run the whole procedure on three cycles: (a) twelve proposals that are all variations of "how much of the budget goes to X vs Y" — I suspect single-peakedness holds here; (b) fifteen incomparable proposals (a poetry anthology, a water sensor network, a math workshop...) where two reviewers are known to bury front-runners they dislike; (c) a cycle where our funder mandates that one specific reviewer's rankings count double. Include at least one case where your procedure recommends something I'll dislike, and say so.

5. End with a seperate section on what the procedure can't settle: which of our recurring fights are values questions wearing a technical costume (like whether "the panel's aggregate preference" is even the right thing to compute), and where Arrow gets invoked as an excuse to avoid deciding something uncomfortable.

Keep it usable by someone who's read the Wikipedia article on Arrow exactly once, not a social choice theorist. And where translating the theorem into a checklist loses something important, flag the loss rather than papering over it.
