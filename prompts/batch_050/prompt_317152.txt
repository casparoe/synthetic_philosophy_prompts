I work on the safety team at a mid-sized AI lab, and I've been in a long-running argument with a colleague that I keep losing, so I'm hoping someone can help me see whether I'm actually right or just confused. Here's the situation. When we decide whether a model is ready to ship, the sign-off process is entirely behavioural: the model gets probed with thousands of adversarial prompts, we measure whether it refuses the things it should refuse, answers honestly on our honesty test-suite, doesn't scheme in the simulated environments we built for that, and so on. If the outputs look good across all of that, the model passes. What nobody does, at any point, is look at how the model got to those outputs -- the reasoning traces, the internal steps, whatever you'd call the process. My colleague says this is fine: the outputs are the thing users are exposed to, so the outputs are the thing to check. I keep feeling there's something off, partly because we already know from training that models can land on correct final answers through garbage reasoning (we've had maths models that got reinforced for right answers produced by totally broken intermediate steps), and it seems weird to me that we'd treat passing behaviour as settling the matter when we know that can happen.

So here's my actual question, which I think is really a philosophy question wearing a lab coat. What does our release practice presuppose about the relationship between a system's outputs and its internal processes? Does it only make sense if you assume good behaviour is strong evidence for a good underlying process -- or is it actually the opposite, presupposing that the process is simply inscrutable and therefore irrelevant even if it matters? Are those the same commitment or two different ones, and does it matter which one the practice is really built on? It also strikes me that the same company does the exact opposite elsewhere: when we interview engineers, we absolutely care how they got their answer, not just whether it's right, and a candidate who reaches the right answer by confused means gets rejected. So either the release process and the hiring process presuppose inconsistent views about when outcomes suffice as evidence about processes, or there's a principled difference between the two cases that nobody has articulated to me.

Please tell me: what theory is implicit in each practice, whether the combination is coherent, what would have to change in our release pipeline if we took the process-based view seriously (and whether that change is even feasible given that we can't currently read a model's mind, only its visible reasoning), and where you think the practice is just underdetermined -- i.e. compatible with several views and not really committed to any of them. Please write your answer in American English.
