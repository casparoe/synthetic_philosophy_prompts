I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in metaphysics. The students have completed a first course in formal logic but have not yet encountered the problem of future contingents; we meet twice a week for 75 minutes, with about sixteen students. I want an activity, spanning two consecutive meetings, that makes them feel the force of the fatalist argument before they meet it in print: if it was already true yesterday that there would be a sea battle today, then nothing anyone did in the interim could have made a difference — and, with equal vividness, the pull of the response that a proposition about a genuinely open future is neither true nor false, along with the costs of saying so. Neither Aristotle's name nor the phrase "sea battle" should be uttered until the debrief.

Concretely, the design should have the students do three things, in whatever order you judge most effective. First, commit themselves — in writing, sealed, with a verdict procedure fixed in advance — to claims about events that will not be settled until the second meeting, so that during the intervening week their own sentences are sitting there being, or failing to be, true. Second, in the second meeting, be subjected to a staged version of the fatalist argument aimed at them personally rather than at an abstract admiral, so that someone in the room has to explain why their deliberation during the week was not idle. Third, working in small groups, devise a scheme for scoring one another's forecasts that is required to admit "indeterminate" as an answer, and thereby run into the problem themselves: after the event, the actual outcome is the only evidence anyone has, so there seems to be no fact of the matter about whether the forecaster who said "indeterminate" was right. The scoring schemes should fail in instructively different ways, and the activity should keep two arguments visibly distinct that students reliably conflate — the logical argument from bivalence and the causal or epistemic arguments from determinism and foreknowledge.

Please supply: complete rules and materials, assuming nothing more exotic than envelopes, cards, and a whiteboard; a minute-by-minute timeline for both meetings; a payoff scheme that uses no real money; a debrief script that maps specific moments of the activity onto the standard moves in the literature (rejecting bivalence, the Ockhamist distinction between hard and soft facts about the past, tense-logic treatments, supervaluationism) without lecturing; a candid list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, students treating the exercise as one about reliable prediction rather than about truth, or quietly coordinating so that outcomes match predictions — and how to handle each; and finally a frank statement of which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect the asymmetry between the fixity of the past and the alleged openness of the future will resist staging, and I would rather you tell me plainly what I must supply in discussion than pretend the game covers it.
