I am a journalist covering science and ideas for a national newspaper, and last year I wrote a long piece about what must be the strangest public art commission in the country. Some years ago the council of the northern port city where I grew up finally removed the bronze statue of Sir Cuthbert Mallow — shipowner, MP, benefactor of the docks, and, as a decade-long campaign established to most people's satisfaction, a man whose fortune rested on the lethal conditions in his yards. The statue was not sold or warehoused. It was melted down, and a young sculptor was commissioned to cast a new work from the same metal. The result — a life-size figure of a woman mending a ship's sail, simply called Mending — is unveiled in the square next month, and because I reported the project from the foundry floor I have been asked to speak for five minutes at the ceremony.

The speech is proving harder than the article. The square will hold a few hundred people: the sculptor and the foundry crew; councillors who fought over the decision for years; campaigners on both sides of the removal, some of them still raw; a party of schoolchildren; and, in the front row, Mallow's great-great-granddaughter, who opposed the removal and has conducted herself throughout with a dignity I want to honour rather than punish.

Here is the difficulty I cannot write my way around. Reporting the piece, I kept circling a question I never resolved: what, precisely, did we destroy, and what is about to be unveiled? A statue and the bronze that constitutes it do not seem to be the same thing. You can destroy the statue while the bronze survives — we did exactly that, in a furnace at 1,100 degrees — but you cannot destroy the bronze while the statue survives, short of destroying the metal itself. That asymmetry is, as I understand it, why philosophers tend to say the statue and the lump are two things sharing one shape, each with its own conditions for surviving change. There is also a respectable minority view that the statue simply is the bronze, temporarily statue-shaped, in which case nothing was destroyed at all, only rearranged — but that view, whatever its philosophical merits, would make the campaigners' victory hollow and the granddaughter's grief a category error, and I am not prepared to tell either of them that from a lectern.

So the speech must do its work between those positions. It cannot tell the crowd that Mallow's statue lives on in the new figure: on the weight of the arguments, something genuinely ended in the furnace, and the front row is owed honesty about what it was. It cannot say that nothing was lost but metal, since that denies that statues are the sort of thing that can be destroyed at all — an insult to everyone who fought, on either side, precisely because statues are that sort of thing. And the consolation honestly on offer — that the bronze itself persists — is partial in a way the speech must not conceal: the foundry manager told me that a few per cent of the charge is lost to slag and oxidation in every pour, and fresh tin went in to bring the alloy back to specification, so even the metal is not perfectly continuous with what stood in the square for a century.

Please write the speech: about 650–700 words, in plain language throughout — no philosophical vocabulary, no philosophers' names — with the metaphysics carried entirely by the foundry, the furnace, and the two figures. Give each side of the old argument one sentence it can hold on to without feeling managed. One wry moment is welcome; easy uplift at the close is not. End with whatever the speech has actually established the occasion to be.
