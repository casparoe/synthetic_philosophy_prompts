You are a philosopher of science who has written on abduction and scientific methodology; think carefully and distinguish levels of claim before answering.

A friend of mine defends scientific realism by repeating that inference to the best explanation is how all good reasoning works, and so the best explanation of a theory's predictive success is that the theory is approximately true. I have noticed that the phrase "best explanation" does a lot of silent work here, and I suspect different people mean very different things by it. Please lay out, as an explicit menu, the distinct precise theses that talk of inferring to the *best* explanation could be expressing. In particular, I want you to separate at least: (a) a merely descriptive claim about how scientists and ordinary people in fact reason; (b) a normative rule of method licensing a conclusion as probable because it explains the evidence well; (c) the claim that explanatory virtues — simplicity, unification, scope — are themselves evidence of truth, as opposed to pragmatic or aesthetic features; and (d) the disambiguation between inferring to the *likeliest* explanation and inferring to the *loveliest* one, i.e., between the explanation we already have most reason to think true and the one that would, if true, explain the most. For each reading, say whether it is close to a platitude, substantive but defensible, or genuinely contentious, and briefly indicate the main pressure on it — for instance the worry that the best available explanation may only be the best of a bad lot, or the problem of assigning prior probabilities to hypotheses no one has yet formulated. Finally, tell me which of these readings, if any, is actually strong enough to do the work my friend needs, and whether that reading is one he is entitled to assume without begging the question against the anti-realist.
