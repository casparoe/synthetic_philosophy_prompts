A close friend of mine has been making an argument she picked up from Douglas Hofstadter's writing: that a person's self — the distinctive patterns of thought, humor, and reaction that make them who they are — gets imperfectly copied into the minds of people who know them well, so that when someone dies, fragments of them genuinely persist, distributed across other brains. She insists this is literal, not metaphorical.

Her verdicts on the cases I've put to her so far: a widow who has so internalized her late husband's outlook that she can reliably predict what he would say about anything — yes, a real fragment of him survives in her. An identical twin whose co-twin dies — yes, and a large one, because nobody carries more of the pattern. A biographer who spent thirty years immersed in a long-dead novelist's letters and drafts — a faint but genuine presence. Millions of casual viewers of a television celebrity — essentially nothing, since knowing someone's public mannerisms isn't running their patterns. And a dead person's own diary, however detailed — nothing at all, because ink on paper doesn't think; the pattern has to be actively running in something.

The new case she hasn't ruled on: a chatbot trained on the complete messages, emails, and recorded conversations of a dead man, which reproduces his style of thought well enough that his family finds conversations with it unsettling, and which thousands of strangers now talk to daily. Does her view commit her to saying he persists in it — perhaps more fully than in any single human — or does it commit her to denying that?

Please answer as a philosopher of mind would: think carefully, and start by brainstorming at least two or three distinct rules that each fit all five of her existing verdicts, stating what each rule predicts about the chatbot and why at least two of them diverge on it. Then give me your best prediction of her actual verdict, identify which earlier case is the closest analog, and describe one further hypothetical case that would most cleanly tell the remaining candidate rules apart.
