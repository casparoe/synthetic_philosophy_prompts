I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, and in preparing a dissertation chapter on the morality of emergency powers I reconstructed an argument against deontological constraints that I now cannot locate the flaw in, though I am fairly confident there is one. I first encountered a compressed version of it in an unnamed colleague's conference paper, which presented it as a decisive objection; the version below is my own regimentation, and I would like to know which step to reject and at what cost.

(1) A genuine deontological constraint is a prohibition whose normative force does not depend on the consequences of complying with it: that is what distinguishes a constraint from a rule of thumb that consequentialists can also endorse.

(2) The prohibition on intentionally killing an innocent person is the paradigmatic deontological constraint; any view that permits killing one innocent person whenever doing so produces sufficiently good consequences has abandoned constraints altogether.

(3) Therefore the constraint forbids killing one innocent person even when doing so is the only way to prevent five other innocent persons from being intentionally killed.

(4) Nothing in the rationale for (3) is sensitive to the number five. Whatever consideration makes it impermissible to weigh one killing against five killings — that the victim is an inviolable person rather than a counter in a calculus, that the agent may not treat anyone as a means — applies equally when the alternative is five thousand killings, or five million.

(5) Therefore the constraint forbids killing one innocent person even when refraining guarantees vastly more intentional killings of the very kind the constraint exists to prevent.

(6) But a constraint whose justification rests on the inviolability of persons, and which systematically requires agents to permit more persons to be violated in precisely the prohibited manner, is a constraint that requires outcomes worse by its own lights.

(7) Any normative principle that systematically requires outcomes worse by its own lights is incoherent, or at minimum self-effacing in a way no foundational principle can afford to be.

(8) Therefore deontological constraints are incoherent as such; the residual insight that killing innocents is ordinarily wrong is best captured as a strong but defeasible presumption within an ultimately consequentialist framework, perhaps with thresholds set by intuition.

My sense is that the trouble lies somewhere between (4) and (7), and that some slide involving the standpoint from which outcomes are evaluated is doing the work, but I cannot make this precise. If forced to choose, I would rather retain the constraint on intentional killing than accept (8), since my dissertation's treatment of emergency powers depends on there being things a state may not do to its citizens even under severe pressure; but I do not want to retain it by an ad hoc stipulation about numbers. Please identify the step or steps you reject, explain why each looked compelling, and state plainly what a deontologist must concede — about thresholds, about agent-relativity, about the relation between wrongness and badness — in order to reject them. If, on your analysis, the argument is sound and (8) follows, I would rather hear that than a rescue.
