I serve on the acquisitions committee of a small municipal art foundation, and two colleagues, Leila and Marcus, have deadlocked over our next commission in a way that has turned into an argument about Hume’s Of the Standard of Taste. The finalists are a severe, dissonant metal installation that several curators call profound and a warm, narrative mural that local residents overwhelmingly prefer. Leila says Hume gives us a way out of mere majority rule: beauty is not simply in the object, but neither is every preference equal, because critics with trained sensitivity, extensive comparison, disciplined practice, and freedom from prejudice form the standard by which the rest of us can be corrected. On that basis, she says, the curators’ admiration should outweigh the residents’ enthusiasm. Marcus replies that this misreads Hume as an elitist: if the joint verdict of qualified judges is the standard, then it cannot be overturned by a few self-appointed experts, and if we instead say the real judges are whoever avoids prejudice, we have only pushed the question back, since deciding what counts as prejudice already requires knowing what good taste is. Leila answers that this is like denying that experienced wine tasters can be better than novices because novices cannot define expertise; training can make responses more discriminating without making beauty a measurable physical property. Marcus says the analogy fails because wine faults can be chemically explained, while aesthetic disagreements often track class, education, race, and period, so the appeal to ideal judges may launder the prejudices of one social group into objectivity. Leila concedes that actual critics are corruptible, but insists Hume is describing an ideal standard, not deferring to whatever fashionable critics happen to say, so response-dependence is not relativism. Marcus then says that an ideal no actual human satisfies may be normatively empty, and that if two fully informed, unprejudiced communities still diverge, Hume leaves us with no fact of the matter.

Please adjudicate the dispute as it was actually made, point by point. Who is right about whether Hume is an objectivist, a relativist, or something more complicated? Is the joint verdict of qualified judges evidence of aesthetic value, constitutive of it, or both? Is the charge of circularity against Leila decisive, or can practice and comparison identify better judges without already assuming the answer? And for the committee, which commission choice does the strongest reading of Hume favor, if either: the curator-backed installation, the publicly loved mural, postponement for broader viewing, or a decision procedure rather than a verdict on beauty itself?
