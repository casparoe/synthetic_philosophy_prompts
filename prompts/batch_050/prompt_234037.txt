At a party last weekend two of my friends got into a surprisingly heated argument that started from a nature documentary about the sphex wasp. For context: the wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the entrance of its burrow, leaves it there, pops inside to check the burrow is clear, then comes back out and drags the cricket in. If you nudge the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, it comes out, drags the cricket back to the entrance, and does the whole inspection routine again -- and it will do this over and over, never once just grabbing the cricket and pulling it straight in.

Priya's position was basically: this settles it, the wasp has no free will, it's a wind-up toy. The experiment proves the "decision" to inspect was never a decision at all, since it fires regardless of whether it's needed.

Marcus said that's too fast. Predictable, rigid behavior isn't proof of no inner life -- maybe the wasp just really likes inspecting the burrow. And if you define free will out of existence whenever behavior is mechanical, you've assumed what you were supposed to prove, since a deterministic universe would make everything mechanical in that sense.

Priya came back with what she said was her real point: it's not the rigidity, it's the failure to revise. The wasp can't notice its loop is pointless. Freedom isn't doing something different each time, it's being *able* to step back and ask "is this working?" -- and the wasp demonstrably can't.

Marcus then pointed out that humans fail this test constantly. People rehearse the same argument with their spouse for the 40th time, check a locked door five times, stay in jobs they hate for a decade. If the revision test is the criterion, most human behavior fails it most of the time, so either humans are mostly sphexish too or the criterion is wrong.

Priya said the difference is humans can revise when the failure is made vivid enough, the wasp can't even in priniciple. Marcus asked how she could possibly know the "even in principle" part about the wasp, and also: a creature with a fancier program -- one that *does* revise when prompted -- is still just running a program. So hasn't her flexibility test just relocated the problem instead of solving it? At that point someone put on music and they dropped it, but they were both still annoyed.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I especially want to know whether Marcus's last move is a genuine counter or just a subject change, and what both of them might be missing.
