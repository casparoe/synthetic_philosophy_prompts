ok so here's something i've been chewing on. Everyone knows the Aumann-style agreement result: if two agents share a common prior (i.e. before getting any private evidence they'd assign the same probabilities to everything), if it's common knowledge that both are rational Bayesian updaters, and if their actual posteriors are common knowledge, then they can't agree to disagree — the posteriors have to coincide. Fine. What I want is a thought experiment engineered specifically against that theorem, in a non-lazy way.

Construct a scenario in which two people (1) genuinely share a common prior — stipulate this, don't cheat by smuggling in different priors — (2) both update on their evidence in a basically Bayesian way, (3) talk to each other honestly and repeatedly, and yet (4) end up in a stable, mutually known disagreement that is rational on both sides. Constraints: the setting has to be realistic — two people doing an ordinary job (actuaries, radiologists, wine buyers, poker regulars, whatever you like), no perfect predictors, no sci-fi mind-reading, nothing that couldn't plausibly happen next year. Keep the scenario itself short, like one solid paragraph.

One warning to keep you honest: it's known that if the theorem's conditions really do hold, then even just swapping credence numbers back and forth forces the two sides to converge. So your case can't merely rely on slow or lazy communication — it has to genuinely break one of the theorem's assumptions while keeping the others intact.

After the scenario, give me the engineering report:

- Identify exactly which assumption of the agreement theorem your case violates, and show that the other assumptions genuinely hold in the story. This is the part where most attempts cheat, so be careful and specific.
- Explain why violating that one assumption is enough to block the convergence result — where does the standard line of argument break down in your case?
- Now patch the violated condition. (E.g., if the culprit is that the two can only exchange compressed summaries rather than their full evidence, imagine they're forced to hand over everything raw.) Does the disagreement collapse? If yes, say what that implies about how much of real-world disagreement the common prior framework can even in principle explain, and how much it has to blame on something else.
- Finally, steelman the attack: how would someone who thinks persistent disagreement always traces back to different priors, or to one side being at least a little irrational, try to reclassify your case as one of those? Does their move work?

Be upfront about how confident you are in each part of the analysis, and single out the specific claim you're least sure of and why.
