So a bit of backstory first: I'm a retired organic chemist (spent 35 years doing synthesis, so forgive me if I misuse the jargon), and since retiring I've gotten really into philosophy, especially anything about group decision-making. I also volunteer on the grants commitee of a small regional science foundation, which is where this comes from. We award a handful of small grants each year, and our written rule is that a project gets funded iff a majority of the seven of us judges it strong on each of three criteria: scientific originality, feasibility within two years, and benefit to the local community. This cycle we hit a genuine snag with one application, and I promised the board I'd write a memo on it. That's where I need your help.

Here's the vote pattern that caused the mess. Call the members M1 through M7. M1 said yes on all three criteria. M2 said yes on originality and feasibility but no on community benefit. M3 said yes on originality and benefit, no on feasibility. M4 said yes on feasibility and benefit, no on originality. M5 said yes only on originality. M6 said yes only on feasibility. M7 said yes only on benefit. So each criterion gets a 4-to-3 yes majority, but only ONE of the seven of us actually thinks the project clears all three bars. When the chair tallied criterion-by-criterion and announced the project qualified, half the room erupted, because 6 out of 7 members would have voted against funding it if asked directly. Now the board is split between people who say the rule is the rule and people who say the outcome is obviously absurd. My instinct as a chemist is that this smells like those voting paradoxes I've been reading about, but I can't tell who's right.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in social choice theory who also has practical experience advising real committees. Think carefully and start by briefly brainstorming at least four procedures we could adopt going forward (e.g. aggregating on the criteria, aggregating on final verdicts, requiring supermajorities, discussing before voting, switching to numerical scores), and only then draft the actual memo. The memo should recommend one specific procedure for future cycles AND say what we should do about this particular application, since we can't just leave it hanging. It needs a clear bottom line the board can vote on, an explanation of why the paradox arose in the first place, and an honest, non-strawman treatment of the strongest argument for the side you recommend against. One thing I keep wondering about: does the right answer depend on how much we defer to each other's expertise? Like, I know nothing about whether a marine biology project is feasible in two years, so my "no" on feasibility for that application was basically a guess. Keep it around 700 words, written so that non-philosophers (retired engineers, a lawyer, one accountant) can follow it.
