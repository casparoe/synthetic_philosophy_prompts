So I got kinda obsessed with the sunk cost fallacy after binging a bunch of personal finance podcasts, and I tried to turn the basic idea into an actual argument with steps, and now it seems to prove something insane and I can't find where it slipped. Here's what I have:

(1) A rational decision should only weigh the costs and benefits that are still ahead of you, since those are the only ones your choice can actually change.
(2) Whatever time, money, and effort you've already poured into something is gone no matter what you pick, so none of it is ahead of you.
(3) Therefore how much you've already invested in something should make zero difference to whether you stick with it.
(4) So there's no rational difference between quitting a degree in week one and quitting it with one exam left — in both cases you just ask "is finishing worth it from here?"
(5) Same logic: walking out of a 20-year marriage should be, rationally, exactly as easy as bailing on a third date, since the 20 years are gone either way.
(6) Both of those are absurd. The guy one exam from graduating is obviously in a different spot than the guy in week one, and anyone who treats two decades of marriage like a bad tinder date is a sociopath.
(7) So (1) is false, and rational decisions SHOULD take unrecoverable costs into account.

But (7) is basically saying the fallacy isn't a fallacy, and I'm pretty sure the econ people are right that sitting through a terrible movie just cause you paid $15 for the ticket is dumb. So something's wrong with my steps, not with the movies. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Fair warning: I'd really like to keep (1) if at all possible, it's the cleanest thing I beleive about decision making and I use it constantly. If keeping it means biting some bullet, tell me exactly which bullet.
