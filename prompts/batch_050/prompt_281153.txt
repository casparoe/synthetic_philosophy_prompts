Ok so I need help with a class I teach. It's an intro metaphysics course at a small liberal arts college, second-year undergrads mostly, about 14 students, and I have a 75-minute session coming up on material constitution -- the statue and the clay, that whole cluster of puzzles. Usually when I just present it, half the room immediately says "obviously the statue and the clay are the same thing, this is a pseudo-problem" and the other half says "obviously they're different, one can survive being squashed and the other can't," and then everyone just talks past each other for an hour and nobody understands why the other side is so confident.

Act as a professional philosopher who also has years of experience designing active-learning philosophy classes. Think carefully about this, and before writing your final answer, brainstorm 3-4 different possible activity designs (a sentence or two each) and briefly say why you're picking the one you pick. Then give me the full activity.

What I want is a structured activity where the students have to *administer* identity over time, not just opine about it. My rough idea, which you should feel free to scrap or heavily modify: some sort of "registry office" simulation where groups of students act as clerks responsible for issuing object ID numbers, keeping ledgers, charging storage fees or insurance premiums per registered object, and approving requests (reshaping, repainting, squashing, rebuilding). The point being that once you're forced to keep books, the "it's obviously one thing" crowd has to say what happens to the ledger entry when the statue is flattened, and the "it's obviously two things" crowd has to explain why their ledger now charges double insurance on two objects that share every atom and fit in one shelf spot. I want the paradox to bite through the bookkeeping rules, not through anything I say.

Specific requirements:
- Concrete rules and setup I can actually run: materials (index cards? tokens? a shared spreadsheet?), the exact decisions clerks have to make, and the staged events I throw at them (a squashing, a statue melted down and re-formed into a new statue from the same lump, a gradual part-replacement case, whatever you think works best -- pick maybe 3-4 events, not ten).
- Some small real stakes so the bookkeeping decisions feel like they matter -- could be points, could be a budget of candy, whatever, but it shouldn't take real money or take long to explain.
- No lecture beforehand and no naming the debate. Students should not hear "material constitution," "coincident objects," or "persistence conditions" until the debrief.
- A timeline for the 75 minutes.
- A debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the actual positions in the debate (constitution vs. identity, coincident objects, the lump-favoring views, and the "counting is relative" deflationary move), so I can show each group which position their ledger rules committed them to and what it costs them.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it -- e.g., groups that immediately coordinate on a clever hack to avoid the problem, or students who treat it as a puzzle with one right answer.
- An honest note about what the activity can't capture. I'm particularly worried that the modal intuition (the statue *could* have survived a slow flattening, or whatever) is exactly the thing a ledger game might flatten away -- say whether your design can get any grip on that or whether I have to supply it in the debrief.

Also one constraint: the room has one whiteboard and no projector that day, so nothing that requires slides or a shared screen.
