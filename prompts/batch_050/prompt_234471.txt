I am preparing a graduate seminar on the ethics of artificial speech, and I find that my own thinking keeps snagging on an early step of the analysis, so I should like a careful conceptual explanation rather than a survey of positions. The passage at issue is from Augustine's De Mendacio (On Lying), chapter 3:

"For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

Augustine's point here is that lying is constituted by a mismatch between what the speaker holds in mind and what the speaker produces in words, not by the actual truth-value of the proposition uttered: one may speak truly and still lie, and speak falsely without lying. What I want explained is why this criterion, which most subsequent definitions of lying inherit in some form, becomes so difficult to apply to the outputs of contemporary large language models — and what the live options are for repairing the difficulty. In particular, please address the following, in an integrated way rather than as a checklist. First, identify precisely which ingredient of the Augustinian criterion does the problematic work: is it the requirement of a mental state with propositional content, the requirement of a deliberate divergence between that state and the utterance, or something else, and why is it not enough simply to note that the model has internal representations? Second, explain why the standard escape routes each seem to concede something important: treating confident next-token regularities as a functional surrogate for belief (which appears to let in too much, counting hallucinations as lies); relocating the wrongness of lying from the inner mismatch to the public act of assertion or commitment (which appears to let in too little, since a model may systematically produce misleading outputs it could not in any sense assert); and abandoning 'lying' in favour of 'deception' or 'misleadingness' (which seems to surrender the intuition, which Augustine captures, that a speaker can be culpable even when the audience is not in fact taken in, and even when what is said is true). Third, say whether Augustine's own resource — his category of the speaker who is "erring and rash" but does not lie — suggests a principled resting place for AI outputs, or whether that category collapses once there is no believer behind the utterance at all. Throughout, I want the gut-level conceptual mechanics made vivid: where exactly the analogy with human lying breaks, and which load-bearing assumption in Augustine's framework fails first.
