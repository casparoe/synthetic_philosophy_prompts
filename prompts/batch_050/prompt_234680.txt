I retired three years ago after four decades as a sensory chemist for a large food company, where much of my work involved training and calibrating taste panels -- the same people tasting the same products month after month, with my job being to determine when a report of a flavor change reflected the product and when it reflected the taster. Since retiring I have been working my way through the philosophy of mind, and I recently read Dennett's essay "Quining Qualia," whose central exhibit -- two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn -- describes almost exactly the predicament I managed professionally. Chase insists the coffee now tastes worse while suspecting the taste itself is unchanged; Sanborn suspects the taste has drifted gradually but can never hold his present sensation up against his past one to check. Sitting with the case over several evenings, I assembled the following argument, and it disturbs me because it appears to prove something no one in my old profession could possibly accept.

(1) A subject who notices that a familiar taste now seems different cannot, by introspection alone, determine whether the qualitative character of the experience has changed or whether only her reactive attitudes toward it -- her standards, comparisons, memories, likings -- have changed. (2) Nor can she settle the matter by consulting memory, since any recollection of how the coffee used to taste is itself a present experience whose fidelity cannot be checked against the past experience it purports to record. (3) Therefore there is no procedure, even in principle, by which the subject can discriminate between change-in-qualia and change-in-response. (4) Where no procedure, even in principle, can discriminate between two hypotheses about a matter, there is no fact of the matter as to which hypothesis is true. (5) Hence there is no fact of the matter about whether the qualitative character of one's own experiences has changed or remained constant over time. (6) But my entire career, and indeed ordinary life, presupposes the opposite: that a panelist's reports can track genuine changes in her experience, that a patient's claim that the pain is worse today means something different from the claim that her tolerance is lower, that the wine expert who detects a note she missed last year has learned something about the wine rather than merely suffered a silent scrambling of her qualia. (7) So something in the argument must give, and since (6) is non-negotiable, the trouble lies upstream.

My own suspicion, formed by years of watching panel data drift in ways no instrument could adjudicate, is that the mischief occurs somewhere between (3) and (5), but I have not been able to state the objection in a form that does not simply beg the question in favor of the tasting-room common sense I started with. What I would like from you: identify which step you would reject; explain why that step looked acceptable on its own terms; and price out what rejecting it costs -- in particular, whether abandoning it forces one toward the view that first-person reports of experience are incorrigible, or toward some verificationism about the mental that seems no less extreme than the conclusion I am trying to avoid. For what it is worth, the claims I would most like to preserve are (6) and the intuition behind (1): I genuinely cannot see, either from the armchair or from thirty years of calibration records, how a taster could know from the inside which sort of change she has undergone. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender, and what a defender of qualia -- someone who believes Dennett's target was misconceived from the outset -- would diagnose differently.
