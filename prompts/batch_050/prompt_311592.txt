In the philosophy reading group I run, we've spent the last month on conditionals, and our two most stubborn members have reached an impasse I'd like to see formalized. Dana holds that indicative and counterfactual conditionals are genuinely different connectives: the difference in truth conditions between, say, the indicative "If Oswald did not kill Kennedy, someone else did" and the counterfactual "If Oswald had not killed Kennedy, someone else would have" is semantic, and any adequate logic needs two distinct conditionals with two distinct analyses. Marcus holds that there is only one "if": the apparent contrast in such pairs comes from what the antecedent's mood signals about how the speaker is relating it to the actual course of events -- roughly, whether the antecedent is being entertained as an open epistemic possibility or supposed against a background where its falsity is already settled -- and a single semantics plus pragmatics and context does all the work. They agree they keep talking past each other and have asked me to help them draft the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between them would produce, rather than another round of dueling examples.

Please draft that document. It should include, in this order: (1) a section titled "Agreed Claims," around 200 words, giving roughly six numbered claims both would sign -- about the minimal pairs themselves, about what any theory must explain (including why tense and aspect seem to track the felt difference), and about which simple proposals (for instance, treating both as the material conditional, or treating both as strict implication) both sides reject and why; (2) a section titled "Residual Disagreements," around 200 words, stating each disagreement in a form its own side would accept, with wording neutral enough that neither statement presupposes its side's solution; (3) a section titled "Cruxes and Test Cases," around 250 words, identifying the two or three sub-questions where movement would force one side to concede ground, and for each crux a constructed case -- ideally a novel minimal pair, not the Oswald one -- that both agree bears on it, with Dana's predicted verdict and Marcus's predicted verdict recorded side by side, including at least one case where their accounts deliver the same verdict and one where they diverge; (4) a short closing section titled "Verbal or Genuine," around 100 words, saying which piece of their dispute, if any, dissolves once each side's terms are precisified, and which piece survives any amount of terminological hygiene.
