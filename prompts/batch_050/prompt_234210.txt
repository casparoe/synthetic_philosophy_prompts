Yesterday I had a long argument with a friend about whether falsificationism can really distinguish mature science from pseudoscience. She defended what she calls the collapse argument: (1) A theory is falsifiable only if there is some possible observation report whose acceptance would logically compel its rejection. (2) A theoretical claim never by itself entails a concrete observable prediction; the prediction follows only from the theory together with auxiliary hypotheses, initial conditions, and assumptions about instruments and measurement. (3) When the prediction fails, logic establishes only that at least one member of that whole conjunction is false, not that the theoretical claim is false. (4) Scientists can therefore preserve any favored theory by revising an auxiliary, redescribing the initial conditions, or questioning the measurement. (5) If falsificationists reply that the entire theoretical package is falsified, then they face a dilemma: either the package contains every relevant background assumption, in which case no actual test is ever cleanly interpretable, or it does not, in which case any conflict can be blamed on something outside the package. (6) Consequently, falsificationism either excludes mature sciences such as physics and astronomy from science, or admits astrology and homeopathy whenever their defenders specify some testable package and then revise it after failure.

I cannot accept the conclusion, but I also do not want to rescue falsificationism by making an empirical theory unfalsifiable whenever its defenders are sufficiently imaginative.
