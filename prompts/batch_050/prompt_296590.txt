I hold a view in population ethics that I have not seen defended in exactly this form, so let me state it precisely before asking you to stress-test it. The view has three parts:

1. Lexical band: no amount of pleasure, however intense or widely distributed, can compensate for even a single instance of suffering above a certain intensity threshold (call it agony-level) that is not redeemed by the sufferer's own eventual retrospective endorsement of having lived through it.
2. Weak band: below that threshold, suffering and pleasure trade off against each other, but at asymmetric rates — a unit of mild suffering outweighs a unit of mild pleasure of matching phenomenal intensity.
3. Existence clause: bringing a new person into existence can be bad (if that life contains suffering) but is never good in itself, however happy the person would be; their happiness only counts once they exist.

Call it tiered negative utilitarianism. I arrived at it by trying to keep what is compelling in both Benatar-style asymmetry arguments and lexical negative utilitarianism while avoiding the world-destruction conclusion: since the lexical band attaches only to unredeemed agony, a world of ordinary lives with ordinary pains need not be worse than nothing, and the view mandates the prevention of agony rather than the end of sentient life.

What would count against this view? Specifically:

- Which cases does it handle worse than any of its parents (classical negative utilitarianism, weak negative utilitarianism, total utilitarianism plus an asymmetry clause)? Look hard at the seams between the three parts: the boundary of the agony threshold, and the cases where the endorsement condition does the real work — torture survivors who later affirm their lives, or people with advanced dementia who suffer intensely and will never be in a position to endorse anything.
- Which intuitions must I concede as costs, and which alleged defeaters can I reasonably dismiss as question-begging? For example, the pinprick objection was designed to be absorbed by the threshold; does it actually die there, or does it reappear in a sharper form at the threshold's edge?
- Does the view genuinely escape the world-destruction conclusion, or does it smuggle it back in? Consider whether any defensible probability estimate makes creating a new person a risk of unredeemed agony that no future endorsement reliably cancels.
- Is the three-part structure stable, or does pressure on any one part collapse the whole into a parent view or into incoherence? For instance, does the endorsement condition quietly import a preference-satisfaction theory that conflicts with the hedonistic framing of the first two bands?
- Are there results elsewhere in the literature — on the logic of lexical orderings, or the impossibility theorems in population axiology — that this composite trips over even though each parent survives them?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of this view would themselves concede as genuine rather than dismiss. If your honest assessment is that the view is well insulated, say so — and say whether that insulation is a virtue or a sign that it has been qualified into emptiness.
