A metaphysics committee is preparing a practical manual for interpreting modal claims in law, history, and ordinary reasoning. It must choose between two deliberately simplified frameworks. Evaluate the frameworks as defined here rather than attempting to reconstruct any historical philosopher’s exact position.

Framework C, counterpartism: No concrete individual exists in more than one possible world. A claim that an individual could have had some property is true just in case an appropriate counterpart of that individual has the property at some possible world. Which similarities matter—origins, psychology, social role, material constitution, plans, or something else—depends partly on the conversational context. An individual may have several counterparts in the same possible world.

Framework T, transworld identity: The very same concrete individual can exist at multiple possible worlds. For the purposes of this comparison, assume that an individual’s fundamental kind and broad origins are essential to it, while its memories, character, career, social position, and later psychological development are contingent. One individual cannot have two distinct simultaneous successors within a single world.

Assess how each framework handles these cases:

1. Maya was equally suited to medicine and architecture and chose architecture. Consider whether Maya could have become a physician.
2. In a nearby world, Maya is conceived from the same egg but a genetically different sperm and consequently has somewhat different traits. Consider whether that child could have been Maya.
3. In another world, a child with different biological origins receives Maya’s name, grows up in Maya’s family, occupies Maya’s later social position, and develops a very similar personality. Consider whether Maya could have lived that life.
4. Scientists in another world synthesize a person whose present physical and psychological state exactly duplicates Maya’s current state, after which the duplicate pursues a life Maya might have pursued. Consider whether Maya could have had that future.
5. A world contains two equally similar people produced through an instantaneous duplication of Maya, after which they lead different lives. Consider whether Maya could have become the left successor, the right successor, both, or neither.
6. A ceremonial ship is built in another world from entirely different materials but according to the same blueprint and for the same civic function. Consider whether the actual ship could have been made from those materials.

Provide the following:

1. A case-by-case table giving each framework’s verdict and a confidence score from 0 to 10.
2. A score from 0 to 10 for each framework on five dimensions: fit with ordinary modal language, ability to explain contextual variation, resistance to arbitrary similarity judgments, treatment of origins and essential properties, and coherence in duplication cases.
3. A final ranking using these weights: 30 percent ordinary-language fit, 20 percent contextual variation, 20 percent resistance to arbitrariness, 20 percent origins and essential properties, and 10 percent duplication cases. Show the resulting weighted scores.
4. A brief explanation of which case most damages each framework and whether the damaging result reveals a genuine metaphysical problem or merely an unpopular verdict.
5. One modification to each framework that would most improve its overall score, followed by a final judgment as to whether the committee should adopt C, T, or a hybrid as its default framework.
