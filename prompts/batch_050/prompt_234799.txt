Next month I am presenting a paper in the jurisprudence colloquium of a law faculty, and I want to be ready for the discussion period. The paper defends the claim that a corporation can be morally blameworthy in its own right -- that blaming the company is not merely a loose way of blaming whichever executives or employees physically carried out the act. My argument is built on Thomas Hobbes's account of artificial persons in Chapter XVI of Leviathan (the chapter on persons, authors, and things personated), and in particular on this passage:

"When the actor doth anything against the law of nature by command of the author, if he be obliged by former covenant to obey him, not he, but the author breaketh the law of nature: for though the action be against the law of nature, yet it is not his; but, contrarily, to refuse to do it is against the law of nature that forbiddeth breach of covenant."

My reading is that Hobbes detaches wrongdoing from performance: the one who carries out a deed need not be the one who owns it, because blame tracks authorship rather than bodily movement. I then argue in three steps. First, a corporation's charter, board resolutions, delegations of authority, and internal decision procedures are authorization devices of exactly Hobbes's kind, institutionalized and writ large. Second, through these devices the members constitute an artificial author that owns acts no single human being authored, so the corporate person can itself break the law of nature. Third, it follows that role occupants may sometimes be blameless where the corporation is at fault, and the corporation at fault where every individual acted permissibly within their role.

The audience will be about fifteen people: mostly criminal-law theorists of a retributivist bent, who think blame requires mens rea and regard corporate liability as either a category mistake or a deterrence instrument dressed in moral vocabulary; several corporate-law scholars committed to the nexus-of-contracts picture, who will deny there is any "it" capable of authorizing anything; two philosophers who work on group agency and will press whether an authorization structure amounts to genuine agency without something like reasons-responsiveness; and one historian of early modern philosophy who has actually read Chapter XVI closely.

Prepare my question-period prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience -- not from a generic ethics class -- ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased in the voice of the person who would raise it; for each, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a reply of about two minutes, with a note on where that reply is soft. I also want a separate assessment of where my reading of the passage is most vulnerable as interpretation: Hobbes's own scheme seems to make the artificial person the actor who is personated by representatives, with authorship flowing from natural persons -- so does the text support the corporation as author, or does it instead point blame back toward shareholders and members? Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I have twenty minutes of questions, not a second paper, so keep it concrete.
