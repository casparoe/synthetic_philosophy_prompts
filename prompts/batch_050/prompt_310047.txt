In my philosophy of science reading group, we stumbled into pragmatism through an argument about models. One person said the ideal-gas law is “true enough” for engineers despite idealizing away intermolecular forces; another replied with the slogan “truth is what works,” and then used the same slogan to claim that even a comforting conspiracy theory could be “true for” someone if it organizes their social life and eases their anxiety. I suspect a single piece of shorthand is being stretched across very different claims. In How to Make Our Ideas Clear, C. S. Peirce writes: “The essence of belief is the establishment of a habit, and different beliefs are distinguished by the different modes of action to which they give rise.” Taking that passage as a constraint, unpack the slogan into a menu of precise theses: for each one, say whether it is a claim about meaning, justification, the nature of truth, or a proposal to stop talking about truth; specify whose “working” is at issue (an individual’s satisfaction, a community’s continued inquiry, predictive success, coordination of action, long-run convergence, and so on); and classify the reading as trivial, substantive and defensible, or likely false.

Please do not merely sort the readings into “Peircean” and “Jamesian” labels; where a reading fits Peirce’s emphasis on habits of action and the indefinitely continued community of inquiry, or James’s emphasis on concrete experiential consequences and satisfaction, explain the fit in terms of the reading itself. Then test the menu against three cases: the ideal-gas law used in engineering, a self-fulfilling prophecy whose acceptance helps make it come about, and the comforting conspiracy theory that stabilizes a believer’s life while systematically misdescribing others. In each case, distinguish the success of the representation from the success of the practice built around it. End by identifying which readings, if any, would actually support my acquaintance’s inference from “this belief works for him” to “it is true for him,” and whether Peirce’s sentence about belief and habit gives that inference any support or instead blocks it.
