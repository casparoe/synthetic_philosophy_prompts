I am a retired experimental physicist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been making my way through the history of philosophy with a reading group of other latecomers. At a statistics seminar I recently attended out of old habit, a speaker presented an observational result and hinted at a causal interpretation, whereupon someone in the audience shut the discussion down with the familiar slogan "correlation is not causation," as though that settled the matter. It struck me afterwards that in forty years of science I must have heard and uttered that sentence hundreds of times without ever being able to say precisely what it asserts.

The difficulty sharpened when our reading group reached Section VII of Hume's An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, where I came upon this passage:

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

I would like you to do two things. First, lay out as a list the distinct precise claims that "correlation is not causation" could be making, running from the near-trivial (for instance, that a correlation coefficient alone never logically entails a causal claim) through the substantive (for instance, that no purely observational regularity, however extensive, can by itself distinguish genuine causation from confounding) to the radical (for instance, that there is nothing more to causation than regular succession, so that the slogan gestures at a distinction that does not exist). For each reading, give a verdict on whether it is true, and state whether it would actually justify what the audience member did, namely treating the speaker's causal interpretation as ruled out rather than merely under-evidenced.

Second, bring the Hume passage to bear on your list. Hume reports that we never observe anything in the operation of causes beyond one thing following another. If he is right about that, which of your readings of the slogan survive? Does the slogan presuppose a notion of causal connexion that goes beyond constant conjunction, and if so, is the slogan in tension with Hume, or does Hume's observation actually supply the slogan's best defence? I would also welcome a remark on whether the answer changes if one reads Hume as denying real causal connexions in the objects themselves, as opposed to denying only that we can observe them, since our group could not agree on which of those he meant.

Please indicate, for each claim you make, how confident you are in it, and single out the one point in your answer that you are least sure of. I intend to bring your list to the reading group, so clarity about the structure matters more to me than a definitive resolution.
