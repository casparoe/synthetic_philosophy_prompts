In a seminar on Buddhist philosophy, I defended a comparatively modest version of the no-self doctrine. On my view, anatta denies that there is an enduring, independently existing subject who owns, controls, or remains identical behind changing bodily and psychological processes. It does not deny that persons exist conventionally, that experiences are unified at a time, or that causal and psychological continuity can support memory, responsibility, and concern for one’s future. I call this the ownership-denial interpretation: what is absent is an inner proprietor, not an organized stream of embodied experience. This lets the Buddhist position distinguish anatta from nihilism and handle ordinary claims about personal identity pragmatically, while treating persistent selfhood as a useful construction built out of continuity, appropriation, and social practice.

What would count against this position? Please map its exposure rather than simply rehearsing familiar objections to no-self. Consider especially what I call the asymmetry of retrospective appropriation: although my body, memories, intentions, and character traits all change, I can apparently remember some experiences only from the first-person perspective, and I cannot similarly re-present your past experiences as having been mine. Would that phenomenon establish an enduring subject, require only a momentary minimal subject, or admit a fully process-based explanation? Also examine possible trouble cases involving memory transplantation, gradual psychological discontinuity, dissociation, meditation-induced alterations in self-experience, and moral responsibility where the later person barely resembles the earlier one. Which cases would genuinely falsify ownership-denial, which would merely make its conventional-person machinery look ad hoc, and which are compatible with it once the view is stated carefully?

Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward consequences, and separate what a committed Buddhist reductionist would concede from what only an opponent would regard as damaging. I am particularly interested in whether the position risks collapsing into either bundle nihilism or a thinly disguised minimal-self theory, and whether appeals to convention and causal continuity quietly smuggle back the very identity they claim to explain. If the view can accommodate every apparent counterexample by redescribing it, assess whether that flexibility is evidence of strength or evidence that it has become unfalsifiable. Rank the strongest challenges from fatal to manageable, and identify what philosophical or empirical discovery would most forcefully undermine the position.
