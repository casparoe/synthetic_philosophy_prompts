I spent most of last year shadowing pharmacists, lab technicians, and one structural engineer for a book about everyday expertise, and I kept noticing a pattern I can't find a word for. When the consequences of an error went up, the people I watched didn't simply become less confident in what they believed. They did something subtler: they revoked the belief's permission to participate in their reasoning. A technician would run her morning dosage calculations off yesterday's calibration figures without a second thought; the moment she learned a misdose that day would actually hurt someone, she stopped letting those figures into her computations at all and recalibrated from scratch — while insisting, if you asked her directly, that she was quite sure the old figures were right. When the emergency passed, the same numbers, with no new evidence behind them, quietly regained their old role. The engineer was happy to design to the standard load tables until a journalist asked whether the bridge could ever fall; after that he refused to "build anything on those tables" until he had rederived the safety margins himself, even though he never stopped saying he trusted them. In both cases it was as if the belief itself survived intact but its license to appear in inferences had been temporarily suspended.

I know this is somewhere in the neighborhood of the debate about whether what you know depends on what's at stake — the bank cases and so on. But as far as I understand that debate, it concerns the conditions for knowledge itself, and my pattern doesn't look like that: the technician never denies knowing the figures are right, and her confidence doesn't visibly drop. Nor does it look like ordinary caution, since caution would presumably leave some residue once the danger passes, and this leaves none. So: does this phenomenon — suspending a belief's inferential role while continuing to hold it, in response to stakes rather than to evidence — have an established name? If it sits between existing notions, I'd like to know exactly where: how it relates to stakes-sensitive accounts of knowledge, to the old distinction between believing something and merely accepting it for a purpose, and to simply having lower credence while believing outright. And if nobody has named this exact thing, please propose a name with a definition sharp enough to settle the boundary cases — for instance, whether an actor "putting a worry out of his mind" before going onstage is the same phenomenon or a different one, and whether the pattern can occur with things you retrieve from memory as well as with explicit premises.
