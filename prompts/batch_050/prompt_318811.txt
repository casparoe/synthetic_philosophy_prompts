A colleague who teaches a philosophy of science survey course gave me the following summary of the pessimistic meta-induction for a handout, and I'd like to know how far to trust it before I use it with students:

"The pessimistic meta-induction is Larry Laudan's argument against scientific realism. Scientific realism is the view that our best scientific theories are literally true and that the entities they postulate -- electrons, genes, tectonic plates -- exist. Laudan pointed out that the history of science is a graveyard of once-successful theories that we now regard as false: the caloric theory of heat, the phlogiston theory of combustion, the crystalline spheres, Fresnel's ether theory of light, and so on. These theories made novel, accurate predictions, so empirical success evidently does not require truth. By induction, our current successful theories will likewise turn out to be false, so realism about them is irrational. Realists have tried to reply with the 'no-miracles' argument -- that the success of our theories would be a miracle if they were not at least approximately true -- but Laudan showed this is just a circular appeal to the same track record the meta-induction undermines. Structural realism, which claims theories get the mathematical structure right even when they get the entities wrong, is sometimes offered as a fallback, but Laudan's argument shows that even the structure of past theories has been discarded, so this retreat fails as well. The lesson of the episode is that induction from the historical record refutes realism, just as induction from the record of swans once refuted the claim that all swans are white."

I'm fairly confident the broad strokes are right, but a few things nag at me. In particular, I seem to remember the no-miracles argument being the realist's primary motivation rather than a reply concocted against Laudan, and I have a vague sense that the "induction" in the meta-induction isn't supposed to work the way the swan analogy suggests. Could you go through the summary and tell me: (a) which parts accurately state Laudan's argument and the realism it targets, (b) which parts overstate or distort either the argument or its target -- including whether realism really commits to the literal truth of our best theories, whether the no-miracles argument is well-described as circular, and whether the historical record supports the claim that structure is discarded as readily as entities, and (c) whether the final analogy to the swan induction is a fair characterization of the argument's logic or a misrepresentation of it.
