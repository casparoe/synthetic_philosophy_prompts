I hold, and plan to defend in print, the following position: teleosemantics is the only viable naturalistic theory of mental content. The rough idea is that a representation means what it does because that is what it, or the mechanism producing it, was selected to do — selected by evolution, or by learning within a lifetime — and that this history of selection is precisely what allows a state to misrepresent, to be about something that isn't there. My further claim is the exclusivity: every competitor either cannot make sense of misrepresentation at all (pure causal or informational theories, which fire away whether or not the represented thing is present), or covertly presupposes the very content it was meant to explain (functional or conceptual-role theories that individuate roles in semantic terms). So teleosemantics isn't merely one option among several; it's the only game in town that plays by naturalistic rules.

Treat this position as the object of scrutiny, not as the conclusion to be reached. What would have to be true for it to be false? I want a map of its exposure, and I want it organized by severity rather than by topic. Specifically:

First, which objections, if they succeeded, would be decisive — not awkward, but fatal? Candidates I'd like assessed include the indeterminacy worry (selection pressures are described at too coarse a grain to fix whether the frog's snap mechanism represents flies, small dark moving things, or nutritious specks, and every attempt to fine-grain the description imports the content it was supposed to deliver); the swampman-style objection (a molecule-for-molecule duplicate with no history seems to have thoughts, which suggests history can't be constitutive of content); and the charge that selectionist content never gets past simple perceptual indicators to the contents of beliefs and other concept-involving states, so the theory collapses into a learning-based use theory exactly where the hard cases live. For each, say what the world, or the argument, would have to look like for the objection to go through, and whether that's a live possibility or a merely logical one.

Second, which objections would merely be awkward — points where the theory must pay a price (counterintuitive verdicts, revisionary consequences for error ascription) but survives the payment? I'd like the price stated plainly.

Third, is the exclusivity claim itself exposed in ways the positive theory is not? Could teleosemantics be true while my claim that rivals necessarily fail turns out false — say, because some informational theory patches the misrepresentation problem without smuggling, or because the naturalistic demand I impose on rivals is one teleosemantics itself only partly meets?

Fourth, has the position been insulated? Defenders have recourse to qualifications — ideal or normal conditions for function performance, liberal construals of what counts as selection, appeals to consumer systems that use representations rather than produce them. Do these qualifications leave the theory risking anything, or have they made it flexible enough to absorb any counterexample at the cost of saying nothing determinate? I'd like an honest verdict on whether the position, as I'd need to state it to survive, still has content.

Throughout, distinguish what the theory's own sophisticated defenders concede as genuine problems from what only its critics press, and separate what is settled in this literature from what remains genuinely contested — I don't want a contested resolution presented as received wisdom. My own prior is that the indeterminacy worry is the most dangerous of the three and the swampman intuition the least; if your assessment ranks them differently, say so and say why.
