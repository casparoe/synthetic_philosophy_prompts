So I rewatched Memento last weekend with some people from my philosophy reading group -- we do a "film plus paper" thing where we pair a movie with something we've read, and this time it was Clark and Chalmers on the extended mind. Halfway through, one of my friends said the movie is basically just a feature-length illustration of the thesis: Leonard can't form new episodic memories, so he builds a memory system out of Polaroids, notes, the wall chart, and the tattoos, and if we're willing to call Otto's notebook part of his mind, consistency seems to demand we call Leonard's tattoos part of his. But the more we talked, the less clean that got, and I want a careful reading that works through it.

Here's what I want pushed on. First, does Leonard's system actually satisfy the usual "glue and trust" conditions -- constant availability, automatic endorsement, fluency of access, and prior conscious endorsement of what's stored? He clearly endorses his tattoos, but he also writes things in states he later can't inhabit, and there are scenes where he reads his own handwriting with total trust and scenes where the whole point is that trust is misplaced. Second, the manipulation angle: Teddy and others exploit the system, and Leonard himself -- in the ending, if I'm reading it right -- deliberately seeds his future self with misleading information, framing someone for a crime. Biological memory can be self-deceived, but this seems like a different structure, one agent in time deliberately corrupting the records of his own extended mind. Does the parity principle survive a case where the external store can be edited by strangers and by your own past self in ways biological memory (allegedly) can't?

Third, and this is the part I find most interesting: the film seems to invert the usual intuition about which substrate is more reliable. Leonard's internal flashbacks are confabulated (the whole Sammy Jankis situation suggests his biological memory is a mess of projection and repetition), while his notes are at least sometimes accurate. So maybe Memento isn't just an illustration of active externalism but an argument that the parity standard is met more easily by the external store than the internal one -- which would be a weird and strong result. I'd like a reading that takes the film seriously as a piece of philosophy, says where it supports and where it genuinely pressures the extended mind thesis, and is honest about where the movie resists the reading (e.g. is Leonard even a single continuing agent in the way the thesis needs?). Also, at the end: what does the film contribute to the debate that the papers themselves don't -- is there something about *living* an extended mind that only narrative can show?
