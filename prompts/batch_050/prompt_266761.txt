ok so in my political theory reading group we just did the chapter where Michael Walzer, in Spheres of Justice (1983), defends a state's right to control immigration by comparing political communities to neighborhood clubs: clubs get to decide who joins, existing members don't owe admission to outsiders, and a club that had to take anyone who applied would stop being a community with a shared life at all. half the room treated this as basically settling the open borders question -- states are like clubs, clubs may exclude, done.

i want you to act as a political philosopher who works on immigration ethics and actually audit this analogy rather than just accept or dunk on it. think it through carefully; maybe start by brainstorming possible mappings before you commit to the important ones. then:

1. give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the club's charter, the members, the dues, the clubhouse, the membership vote, the expelled member -- and flag where you're forced to strain to find any analogue at all
2. tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion (right to exclude would-be immigrants) versus which are just true-but-irrelevant differences. some candidates i've been chewing on: people are born into states but choose clubs, leaving a state is enormously costly in a way quitting a club isn't, clubs exist inside a state that enforces their rules and property so the analogy seems to presuppose the very thing it's justifying, and clubs don't rule over non-members the way border enforcement rules over excluded people. which of these cut deep and which are cosmetic?
3. does the exclusionary conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the club picture doing all the argumentative work? and is there a better analogy in the vicinity -- condominium associations, families, universities, churches -- that does the job more honestly?

one more thing: please be careful to separate what's genuinely settled in this literature from what's live and contested. e.g. if some objection to the club analogy is widely seen as decisive and another one splits people, say so -- don't present a contested liberal critique as consensus, and don't present the analogy as more discredited than it actually is if people still defend versions of it. i want to know where the fault lines actually are.
