I'm a second-year philosophy student taking a course on philosophy of mind, and I've volunteered to run a one-hour revision tutorial for first-years on the distinction between original and derived intentionality. The complication is that these students have only ever encountered the topic through Searle's Chinese Room, which they all found persuasive, so they're arriving with the conviction that anything that merely manipulates symbols according to rules couldn't possibly mean anything by them, full stop. My own view is closer to Dennett's — I think the two-bitser thought experiment (the soft-drink machine designed for American quarters that, transplanted somewhere else, ends up sorting a different local coin just as reliably) shows that what something is "about" depends on selection history and function rather than on some intrinsic mark of the mental — but I don't want to lecture them into agreement. I want them to genuinely grasp that there is a real problem here about what confers content, and that the intuitive answer has costs.

Please help me design the explanatory arc of the session rather than write it for me. Specifically, I'd like: the order in which the component ideas need to be introduced and what each one depends on (for instance, whether the derived intentionality of words and pictures has to be in place before the original/derived contrast can even be stated, and where the Chinese Room's conclusion secretly leans on premises the students haven't noticed they accepted); the single example I should build the whole session around and what properties it must have and lack to carry the weight — I'm torn between the two-bitser and something like a thermostat or a book, and I'd like a principled way to choose; the misconceptions these students will predictably bring or form mid-session, in the order they're likely to surface (e.g., that Dennett is denying that our thoughts are about anything, or that "function" just means whatever an interpreter finds it useful to say, which seems to make content observer-relative in a way that horrifies them), and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than answered on the fly; and a check for understanding I can run at the end that would show they've internalized the dialectic — say, a novel case they have to classify and argue about — rather than just having been talked out of Searle. Also tell me honestly what I should leave out at this level (selection history versus current dispositions, teleosemantics proper, the later Dennett on interpretationism) and say so to them, rather than letting half-explained versions leak in.
