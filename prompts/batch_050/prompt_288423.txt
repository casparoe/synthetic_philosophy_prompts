I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a long feature about a dispute in contemporary metaphysics that I believe lay readers can be made to care about: whether a statue and the lump of clay constituting it are one object or two. Rather than write a he-said-she-said survey, I want to structure the piece around a constructed exercise. Imagine that two camps — on one side, defenders of the constitution view, who hold that the statue and the lump are two distinct objects sharing all the same matter and location, differing in their persistence conditions and modal properties; on the other side, four-dimensionalists, who hold that there is one object here, persisting through time by having temporal parts, and that the statue's and the lump's apparently different fates are handled by appeal to which temporal stretches we are picking out — were brought together for a month and required to produce a joint statement of what they agree on and exactly where they still differ. Draft that document.

The document should contain, in this order:

First, a numbered list of agreed claims: the facts of the central cases both sides accept (the lump survives being squashed into a ball while the statue does not; the two coincide exactly for some interval; intuition and ordinary language pull in both directions at various points), plus any methodological commitments both camps can sign — for instance, that an adequate theory must not multiply objects beyond what the cases force, or must not convict ordinary speakers of massive error without an error theory.

Second, a statement of the remaining disagreements, each formulated in wording that both parties would accept as a fair rendering of their own position — neutral enough that neither side would object that its view has been caricatured. Alongside each disagreement, note explicitly whether it is a substantive dispute or one that dissolves once the parties fix how they are counting objects and what they mean by "is," and be prepared to downgrade disagreements accordingly.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one camp to revise or abandon its position. Candidates you might consider include whether modal and sortal differences between coincident things require a difference in the things themselves (the so-called grounding problem for the constitution theorist), whether talk of temporal parts is a genuine ontological posit or a redescription that changes the subject, and which theory better handles adjacent cases — gradual replacement of the clay's parts, a statue made from two lumps fused at the midpoint, fission cases — without ad hoc exceptions. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts or is committed to saying about that case, so that the document makes clear what would count as one camp being wrong.

Fourth, a short final section in which each camp states what it takes to be the strongest objection to its own view and what it concedes, if anything, that its view costs.

Constraints: the document must stand on its own as a piece of philosophical drafting. Do not cite, quote, or name particular philosophers or publications — write it as the two parties' own joint text, in the register such a text would have. Aim for formulations precise enough that a neutral referee could use the document to tell, given some new argument, which camp it damages and how much.
