Write a poem about the backward induction paradox in the centipede game, intended to be read aloud at the opening session of a graduate seminar on rational choice. The game, for the poem's purposes: two players take turns; on each turn a player may either take the pot and end the game (keeping somewhat more than half, the remainder going to the other player) or pass, whereupon the pot grows and the turn moves to the other player; after a fixed, commonly known number of rounds, the game ends and the pot is divided. Backward induction begins at the final round, where the last mover has no reason to pass, and then unspools backward through every earlier round — if the player who would move last will certainly take, the player before her has no reason to pass either, and so on — until it concludes that the first player should take immediately, even though both players would have walked away far richer had the passing continued to the end.

The formal constraint is the heart of the commission: the poem's structure must enact the reasoning it describes. Write twenty-one lines in six stanzas of six, five, four, three, two, and one lines, so that the poem visibly shortens as it proceeds, the way the game's future shrinks as its endpoint approaches. The final single line should state the conclusion of the backward induction — the move that, once it is rational at the end, becomes rational at every earlier point — and it should be composed so that, on reaching it, the reader feels compelled to reread the opening stanza differently. In other words, the poem should make the reader perform the induction: the last line's logic should reach back through the poem and retrospectively discredit the patience and trust of the earlier, longer stanzas.

Keep both forces in the poem alive: the cold correctness of the unravelling argument and the genuine loss it certifies — the mutually better outcome that rationality, on this picture, makes unreachable. Do not resolve the tension by declaring either the logic or the longing for cooperation simply mistaken; the seminar will spend the term on precisely that question. The poem may be unrhymed or loosely rhymed, but avoid any recurring refrain, since the point is that nothing in the game returns. A reader who knows the game theory should be able to identify, in the final line, exactly the step at which reasoning from the end does its corrosive work; a reader who does not should still find a good poem about trust collapsing under the weight of what each person knows the other knows.
