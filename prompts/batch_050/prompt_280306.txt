I am a journalist who covers science and ideas and often has to explain political breakdown in the vocabulary of causal inference. I am comfortable with causal graphs, counterfactuals, treatment effects, mediators, moderators, feedback loops, regime shifts, latent variables, identification problems, and model averaging, but I am not trained in historiography.

Please restate three broad interpretations of the French Revolution—class-struggle, ideological, and contingency-centered—in that vocabulary as faithfully as possible. Use a single setup so they can be compared: distinguish slow-moving stock variables from fast triggers, causal mechanisms from forecasting correlates, and claims about necessity from claims about probability. Then translate the familiar image of revolutions consuming or eliminating their own leading factions. Does it describe a treatment effect of revolutionary institutions, a selection process generated by strategic survival, an endogenous feedback loop, or several different processes that historians too often collapse?

After the translation, provide a residue report in three parts: what survives cleanly, what survives only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in causal-inference vocabulary. Pay particular attention to class interests that may be latent or internally conflicted, ideology as something actors both use and are constituted by, and contingency that is not merely residual variance. In each case, say whether the failure reveals a limitation of causal vocabulary or a vagueness in the historiographical claim.

Finally, state whether the translated interpretations yield genuine disagreements about counterfactuals, causal mechanisms, or probability distributions—or whether some of the dispute reduces to differences over levels of explanation and standards of historical narration. Please do not turn the answer into a historiographical survey or a list of scholars. I want the conceptual translation and, above all, an honest account of its failure modes.
