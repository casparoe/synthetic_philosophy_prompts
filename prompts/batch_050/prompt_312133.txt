I'm part of a reading group working through the political sections of Aquinas's Summa Theologiae, and we spent last session on the question of sedition (II-II, q.42). One member of the group, who has libertarian sympathies and clearly enjoyed this text more than the rest of us, built the following argument out of it, and I've been unable to show her where it fails, even though the conclusion seems to license near-permanent revolution. In the second article Aquinas writes that "a tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler," and he adds that those who defend the common good and withstand a seditious party are not themselves seditious, any more than a man is quarrelsome for defending himself. He also says, in the same article, that sedition is by its genus a mortal sin, and that there is no sedition in disturbing a regime of the tyrannical kind -- the sedition belongs, if anywhere, to the tyrant. Here is her argument, as we've reconstructed it:

(1) A government directed to the ruler's private good rather than the common good is unjust, and disturbing it is not sedition, since sedition is an offense against the unity of law and common good, which such a regime lacks.
(2) One who disturbs an unjust government in defense of the common good is therefore in the moral position of someone resisting sedition, not committing it -- the tyrant is the seditious party.
(3) Resisting a seditious party in defense of the common good is not merely permissible but an act of justice, on pain of saying that self-defense is wrongdoing.
(4) Hence forcible action to remove a tyrant -- up to and including killing him, since lesser means have failed in paradigmatic cases -- is an act of justice open to whoever is positioned to perform it.
(5) Every actual government directs some of its policy to the private advantage of rulers rather than the common good; perfect orientation to the common good is a limiting case, not a political reality.
(6) Therefore every actual government is tyrannical in proportion to its failures, and private citizens who judge their regime corrupt are justified in resisting it by force, and indeed count as defenders of the common good when they do.
(7) But this cannot be Aquinas's position: he classifies sedition as a mortal sin by its very genus, and he plainly does not think any aggrieved subject may take up arms on his own authority.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a summary of scholarship. Which premise or step should we give up? If I had to choose, I'd rather keep (1) through (3), which seem very close to the text, and give up something later -- but I want to know what rejecting that later step actually costs, and in particular whether the flaw is a slide in the move from "not sedition" to "permitted," a slide from imperfect government to tyranny proper, or a suppressed premise about who may do the disturbing. Please also say what happens to (7): does Aquinas have the resources to hold that resistance to tyranny can be blameless while private violence against rulers remains forbidden, or are those two claims in genuine tension in his system?
