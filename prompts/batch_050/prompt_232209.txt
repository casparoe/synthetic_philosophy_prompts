Write the spring bulletin of the Wexford County Ornithological Society, a fictional amateur birding club, 500–650 words, deadpan and entirely in-universe. The society, having sent its executive board to a continuing-education course on confirmation theory, has adopted Nicod's criterion together with the equivalence condition and restructured its research program accordingly: since a non-black non-raven confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black just as surely as a black raven does, all fieldwork has moved indoors, where members catalogue confirmatory instances — a green apple, a red bicycle, the treasurer's yellow raincoat — at a fraction of the cost of raven expeditions. The humor must come from getting the philosophy right without ever stating it as philosophy: the budget committee reports that raven-based confirmation has been defunded as redundant; the sampling-methods subcommittee warns that an observation only counts if the object was selected before its color was checked, provoking a bitter dispute over whether last month's haul, drawn from the clubhouse lost-and-found, is admissible evidence; one embittered member submits a photograph of an actual black raven and is informed that the observation is welcome but adds nothing the raincoat did not already provide; and a skeptical letter-writer who objects that each indoor instance confirms the hypothesis only negligibly is answered by the secretary's triumphant arithmetic showing that the society logged 4,200 instances this quarter. A reader trained in formal epistemology should recognize every move and find the satire fair; a reader without that training should still find the society absurd. No authorial winking, no glossary, no one explaining the joke.
