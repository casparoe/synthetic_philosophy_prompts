So I just got out of a genuinely annoying three-hour argument with my friend about New Zealand. She's obsessed with the fact that a few years back they did a "wellbeing budget" -- instead of just targeting GDP growth like everyone else, the government explicitly said it would allocate money based on wellbeing priorities, stuff like mental health, child poverty, loneliness. I said this was basically PR, because the whole thing leans on the same kind of life-satisfaction survey measures that drive the World Happiness Report rankings, and those measures don't measure happiness, they measure how rich and secure people feel when you ask them to rate their life on a ladder from 0 to 10. She says I'm being a philosophy-bro and that this is just what governing for happiness looks like in practice.

So: analyze the wellbeing-budget episode through the lens of the measurement debate. Not the politics of it, the measurement of it. Run it in both directions:

First direction: take the standard criticism -- that self-reported life evaluation tracks income, stability, and how people think their life is *supposed* to be going, rather than their day-to-day felt experience, and that a person with a objectively decent but emotionally flat life will report high on it. What does that criticism predict a wellbeing-budget-style government will actually end up doing? Which of its spending priorities does the criticism explain well (mental health funding seems like it should be the hard case for the critic, or maybe its the easy case?) and which does it fail to explain?

Second direction: suppose the criticism is right and the metric is really measuring life evaluation rather than happiness. Does the episode still make sense as policy? Is targeting life evaluation maybe the *right* thing for a state to do even if its the wrong thing to call happiness? And does the episode reveal something the armchair version of the debate misses -- like, is there a difference between "should an individual chase happiness" and "should a state chase a happiness ranking" that the whole argument I had was glossing over?

End with a verdict: was I right that its PR, was she right that this is what governing for happiness looks like, or is the honest answer that the measurement debate can't actually adjudicate this case? If you think it can't, say what extra fact about the world *would* settle it.
