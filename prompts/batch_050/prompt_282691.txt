You are a moral philosopher specializing in axiology and suffering-focused ethics. I run a reading group on theories of wellbeing, and I want a decision tree for our handout that sorts participants into positions on the value of pleasure and suffering.

The tree should start from a shared commitment — roughly, that what ultimately matters is the character of conscious experience — and lead, through six to eight branching questions, to these leaves: classical hedonism, negative utilitarianism, preference-based views, a pluralist position on which suffering and joy both count but asymmetrically, and tranquilism. Since tranquilism is the least familiar leaf, use this working definition: experiences are good only insofar as they are free of craving and suffering; pleasure has no independent positive value (it matters only as the relief of a craving, the way scratching matters only given the itch), so a perfectly desireless state is not neutral or lacking but entirely good, however it feels.

Each node should be a concrete case on which the views genuinely come apart — phrased so someone can answer it without knowing any labels — for instance: whether a blissful but desireless meditator lives better than an ecstatic thrill-seeker; whether satisfying a craving you wish you didn't have makes the moment better; whether an empty world is worse than one filled with untroubled contentment; whether some quantity of mild pleasures could ever outweigh a single hour of agony.

Before writing the tree, briefly map the logical space and note any coherent-looking combination of answers that no named position occupies, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why the seat is empty. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate — I expect tranquilism and lexical negative utilitarianism to be candidates — and say what further question might pry them apart.
