I'm writing an essay for a political theory module and I keep running into the same picture: a constitution is like Ulysses tied to the mast. The people, in a calm moment, foresee that some future majority will get swept up in the heat of things -- a war scare, a moral panic, a charismatic demagogue -- so they lock certain things away (free speech, due process, elections on a fixed timetable) and hand the key to judges, who get cast as the sailors with wax in there ears. My lecturer leans on this really hard to defend strong judicial review: if constitutional entrenchment is just rational self-binding, then courts striking down democratically enacted laws isn't undemocratic at all, it's the people honouring their own considered judgement against their own momentary weakness.

Something about it feels off to me but I can't quite put my finger on it. Can you audit the analogy? Concretely, I'd like:

- the mapping made explicit (a table is fine): what plays the role of Ulysses, the ropes, the sirens' song, the sailors, the wax, and the standing order to ignore him no matter how much he begs;
- where the mapping breaks down -- in particular, does it matter that Ulysses binds only himself, whereas a founding generation binds everyone who comes after, including people who weren't asked? And that his later self is literally the same person, temporarily crazed, whereas a future majority might just have different values rather than impaired judgement? Calling your opponents' politics a 'passion' feels like it begs the whole question;
- the sailors bit seems dodgy to me -- judges aren't outside the polity the way the crew are outside Ulysses' head, so who stops the sailors untying him early, or quietly rowing him onto the rocks themselves?;
- and a point a friend made: Ulysses wants to *hear* the song and survive it -- the binding is what makes the indulgence safe. Is there any constitutional analogue of that, or does the analogy smuggle in the idea that democratic 'passions' are fine so long as they're rendered harmless?

Then the question I actually care about: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the defence of judicial review survive, and if not, what does the real work instead? If the mast-and-ropes picture is beyond repair, is there a better everyday model of precommitment to replace it with? And does the picture do any better where it's also used in psychiatry -- advance directives, where someone with bipolar disorder signs away their right to refuse treatment during a future manic episode? There at least it genuinly is the same person tying themselves down, so if the analogy fails even there I'd like to understand why.
