Last weekend I got into a long, stubborn argument with a friend about the Judy Benjamin problem (van Fraassen's soldier scenario), and it ended without a winner. Her position: purely conditional information — a message whose whole content is of the form "if you're in A, then such-and-such probability" — can never, by itself, justify changing your credence in A. Conditional testimony, she insisted, is evidentially inert with respect to its own antecedent. My suspicion is that this principle only looks plausible because the standard scenario hides an assumption about *why that particular message was sent*, and that the principle collapses the moment the speaker's choice of antecedent is itself correlated with the truth. Rather than continue the argument by email, I want to settle it pedagogically.

I teach an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology, and I'd like one question for the final exam (90 minutes, 40 marks) built around the variant described below. Please deliver three things: (i) the exam question itself, (ii) a model answer of roughly 700 words, and (iii) a marking scheme.

Constraints on the exam question:

- It must be fully self-contained: describe the scenario from scratch (a soldier parachuted into a field divided into quadrants R1, R2, B1, B2, where R-quadrants are Red territory and B-quadrants Blue, with a uniform prior of 1/4 each) without ever using the name "Judy Benjamin" or citing van Fraassen. Students who have merely memorized the standard verdict — that her credence in Red stays at 1/2 — should get no shortcut from pattern-matching.
- Part 1: Headquarters radios: "If you are in Red territory, the probability that you are in R2 is 3/4." Ask for her posterior over all four quadrants under the update policy that holds the Red-versus-Blue odds fixed, and have students state explicitly what extra constraint that policy imposes beyond ordinary conditionalization.
- Part 2: Ask students to instead set up the minimum-relative-entropy update under the constraint P(R2 | Red) = 3/4, carry out the minimization (a numerical one-parameter search is acceptable), and report whether P(Red) moves off 1/2 and in which direction. (The answer: it falls, to roughly 0.47; anything in 0.46–0.48 with correct setup should earn full credit.) Then, in prose: why does the entropy-minimizer do this, and does that result count against min-cross-entropy updating, or against my friend's inertia principle? Students must defend one of these verdicts, not gesture at both.
- Part 3, the part that settles my argument: now add a messaging protocol. HQ receives a radar fix that correctly identifies her sector with reliability 3/4, and its standing policy is to send a 3/4-conditional refining whichever sector the radar indicates. She receives the Red-antecedent message. Students must compute her posterior over the four quadrants, cleanly separating the evidential contribution of *which antecedent HQ chose* from the contribution of the conditional content; state which premise of the inertia principle fails here; and specify exactly what assumption about the message-selection process would be needed to make the principle hold.

The model answer must likewise reach a defended verdict on the philosophical upshot — whether the inertia principle is false in general or merely inapplicable once selection effects are in play — and should address whether the order of the two updates in Part 3 (selection evidence first, then content, or vice versa) can matter, since strong students will raise this and weak students will be silently confused by it.

The marking scheme should identify five must-hit moves with marks summing to 40, each accompanied by a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it. Please make sure the typical-error notes cover at least these: treating the 3/4 in the message as an unconditional probability; applying Jeffrey conditioning on the wrong partition; in Part 3, either ignoring the selection effect entirely (and so answering that P(Red) is unchanged) or double-counting the radar evidence; and dismissing min-cross-entropy as obviously mistaken without identifying the substantive constraint that the inertia principle adds beyond Bayes. The scheme should make precise what separates an A answer from a B answer — specifically, that a B answer can have every computation right while missing the diagnosis in Part 3.
