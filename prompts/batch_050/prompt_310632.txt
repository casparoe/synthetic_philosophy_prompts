I’m a retired physical chemist who has recently got hooked on philosophy, and I heard a podcast give this summary of the Born-rule problem for Everettian quantum mechanics: Everett removes collapse and makes the theory deterministic, but then there is no fact about which outcome will happen to me, only successors in different branches. So probabilities cannot be genuine chances. Decision theory is supposed to show that a rational agent must still bet using squared amplitudes, thereby recovering the Born rule. But branches are not lottery tickets, and one low-amplitude branch is just as real as one high-amplitude branch, so calling the former “less probable” seems to sneak in a rule the ontology does not explain.

How fair is that? Please separate what a careful Everettian would accept from what conflates branch existence, branch weight, self-locating uncertainty, and subjective probability. Also, does the decision-theoretic move really recover the Born rule without adding assumptions, and does the final objection show that it fails? If my question rests on a muddled premise, tell me plainly rather than politely forcing an answer out of it.
