Analyze Robert Moses's low parkway bridges on Long Island as the canonical case for the claim that artifacts can have politics, and then pass judgment on whether the case -- and the claim -- survives scrutiny. Here is the setup as I want you to treat it. On the standard telling, drawn from Robert Caro's biography and made famous by Langdon Winner's 1980 essay, Moses had some two hundred overpasses built over the parkways to Jones Beach with unusually low clearances, too low for buses, so that poor and black New Yorkers who could not afford cars were kept from the beaches without any law needing to say so. The politics, in this story, is not imposed on the bridge from outside; it is concreted into it, and it outlasts the intentions of its designer by decades. Take that telling as your starting point and reconstruct the strongest version of the argument that this artifact has a politics: what exactly is the mechanism by which the bridge does political work, and how does it differ from a law, a fence, or a fare, if at all?

Then deal honestly with the complication: historians have since argued that the bridge story is substantially wrong -- that the clearances reflected ordinary engineering practice of the era, that rail access to Jones Beach existed, that Moses's exclusionary aims were pursued by other means and the bridges were at most incidental. I do not need you to adjudicate the archival dispute; take the challenge as given and answer the interesting question. Winner's essay in fact contains two claims that the retelling usually runs together: that some artifacts are deliberately or effectively designed to settle political questions, and that certain technologies are inherently political in the sense that adopting them requires, or is strongly compatible only with, particular forms of social organization (his example being large-scale technical systems rather than bridges). Say which of these two claims the bridge case was actually evidence for, and what happens to each if the case is apocryphal. Consider, too, the fallback position that the intent never mattered: that whatever Moses wanted, the infrastructure in fact sorted populations by race and class for half a century, and that an artifact's politics lives in its durable effects rather than its designer's head. Decide whether that fallback saves the thesis or quietly concedes it -- whether 'politics without a politician' is still politics in any sense that does work.

Reach a verdict. I do not want a menu of options. Tell me whether, in the end, 'artifacts have politics' is a thesis about design intentions, about effects, about organizational requirements, or about none of these; whether the Moses bridge case, treated as a cautionary example of its own overreach, strengthens or weakens the thesis; and state the single sharpest formulation of the claim that you think survives your analysis, along with the one concession you think its defenders must make to critics and refuse to retreat from.
