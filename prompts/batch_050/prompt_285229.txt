Next month I'm giving a talk at a small business-ethics symposium in Frankfurt, and I want to be ready for the Q&A. My thesis, roughly: there is no general "right to work" in the sense people usually mean, but there IS a right to leisure, and it's the morally stronger of the two. States and employers have positive duties to protect rest (enforceable caps, real vacations, a right to disconnect), while work is at most a means people need fair access to -- nobody has a duty to work, and working a lot is not a virtue. I plan to argue that the dignity-of-work intuition is basically leftover Protestant work ethic rather than a freestanding moral claim, and that karoshi shows where the work-as-duty picture ends up when nobody pushes back on it.

The audience is awkwardly split in thirds. One third German labor lawyers and union-adjacent folks -- 35-hour week, co-determination, deeply attached to the idea that employment is how people get social integration and standing, so they may agree with my policy conclusions while hating my premise. One third Japanese occupational-health and labor law people who've dealt with karoshi and overwork-suicide litigation -- they know the empirical horror stories better than I do and I can't lecture them. One third American business-school/management types with a fairly Protestant-work-ethic outlook who genuinely find their meaning in work and think leisure rights mostly protect freeloaders.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense of the view. The objections I'm most likely to get, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way that particular third of the room would actually phrase it (in their vocabulary, not generic philosophy-speak), with a note on which ones have real force versus which are just predictable. For each: what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. I especially want the objections where one group can use MY OWN material against me -- e.g. the Germans pointing out that my leisure rights only work on the back of their labor institutions, or the Japanese folks saying the problem was never the valorization of work but power imbalance, so my thesis misdiagnoses their cases. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, so I can prepare an answer instead of praying.
