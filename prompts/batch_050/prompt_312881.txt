Over the past few weeks I have been keeping notes on when I am willing to call a process predictable or random, and I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. Here they are:

1. An ordinary coin flip: unpredictable, even though I accept that the outcome is fixed by the physics of the toss.
2. An eclipse a century from now: predictable, despite being governed by the same Newtonian mechanics as the coin.
3. Weather more than about two weeks out: unpredictable, and I do not think better instruments will change that, since the atmosphere's sensitivity to initial conditions puts a ceiling on forecast skill regardless of how much data we gather.
4. A double pendulum whose initial state I measure to high precision: predictable over the short term, then not. Predictability here seems to come with a horizon rather than being a yes-or-no property.
5. A pseudorandom number generator whose seed I know: predictable. The same generator with the seed hidden from me: unpredictable. So predictability looks observer-relative.
6. A roulette wheel: I call this random, full stop, and I notice I do not add the qualifier "but really deterministic underneath" the way I do for the coin.
7. A quantum measurement, say spin along an axis that has not been measured: unpredictable in what feels like a stronger sense than the coin, since no amount of additional knowledge fixes the outcome.
8. A lottery machine whose ball trajectories are chaotic but whose outcome statistics are perfectly stable over thousands of draws: I would say each individual draw is unpredictable, yet the machine as a whole is highly predictable, because its distribution is.

The pair I cannot reconcile is 1 and 6. The coin and the roulette wheel are physically alike: both deterministic, both sensitive to initial conditions, both practically unknowable to me in advance. Yet I describe one as unpredictable-but-deterministic and the other simply as random. Meanwhile, case 5 suggests predictability is a relation to what some observer happens to know, while cases 3 and 7 seem to treat it as a property of the world itself.

Is there a single account of predictability that delivers all eight verdicts — for instance, one that treats predictability as a three-place relation between a system, an observer's information and computational resources, and a timescale? If so, state it precisely and say which clause each case motivates. If no such account exists, tell me which verdict you would overturn and why. Then apply the resulting account to two cases I have not listed: a chaotic system that an observer can simulate exactly, but only at the same speed at which the system itself unfolds; and Laplace's demon, for whom nothing in a deterministic universe is unpredictable. Should I conclude that "unpredictable" says nothing about the universe and everything about us, or does that conclusion overreach?
