So I got into it with a coworker at lunch (he minored in philosophy, annoyingly) and he walked me into this argument that seems to prove there's no such thing as going above and beyond, morally speaking. Here's how it went, as best I can reconstruct:

(1) Take something like donating a kidney to a stranger: it's clearly a morally *better* thing to do than not doing it, but nobody's a bad person for not doing it. Skipping it is permissible. Call acts like that supererogatory.
(2) If act A is morally better than act B, and you're choosing between them, then morality itself recommends A over B -- that's just what "morally better" means, it's a ranking morality gives you.
(3) If morality recommends A over B and you deliberately do B, then you've done something morality counts against, and doing what morality counts against is just what doing something *wrong* is.
(4) So not donating the kidney is wrong after all. But that contradicts (1), where we said skipping it was permissible.
(5) Nothing in that chain depended on kidneys, so it generalizes: for any supposedly supererogatory act, skipping it is wrong.
(6) Therefore supererogation is impossible -- every choice is either required, wrong, or a tie between equally good options. Morality is all-or-nothing, like a function that either compiles or throws.

And that can't be right, because by the same logic I'd be morally obligated to donate my savings, my spare time, and most of my organs, which is a conclusion nobody including my coworker actually believes. The dude just grinned and said "so which premise do you want to kill?" and I honestly couldn't find the weak joint in it. I'd most like to keep (1), since the hero/villian boundary feeling real is kind of the whole point of having a moral vocabulary, but I'll give up whatever I have to.
