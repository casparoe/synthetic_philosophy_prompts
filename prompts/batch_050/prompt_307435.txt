I chair the policy committee of a privately endowed research institute that funds basic science. Six weeks ago we received an anonymous communication from a group claiming that one or more future superintelligent systems will retrospectively identify everyone who measurably advanced or obstructed their creation and will reward or punish them accordingly, including by running high-fidelity simulations of the dead. The letter demands that we redirect a fixed share of our annual grantmaking to AI capabilities research and that we publish our compliance, on the stated theory that what matters is only that the future systems can verify our decision procedure. No trustee believes the authors can harm us directly. The question is whether an acausal threat — one issued by or on behalf of an agent with whom no causal interaction is possible — could nonetheless rationally bind an institution whose choices are predictable, and we are split.

Our deliberation has stalled on a second, related question. One trustee proposes that we sidestep the metaphysics by adopting a standing surrogate goal — a proxy rule pursued because the true objective resists direct calculation: never allow any threat, causal or acausal, to redirect our mission, on the grounds that institutions with this reputation do better in expectation. A second trustee replies that this is exactly how surrogate goals corrupt decision-making: a proxy adopted because it usually tracks the good is being immunized against revision precisely in the cases where it diverges from the good, and a blanket refusal would also forgo genuine acausal cooperation, not merely compliance under duress.

Draft the memo I will circulate before our vote. It should (1) give a bottom-line recommendation the board can adopt by resolution, covering both the current demand and the proposed standing policy; (2) state the conditions, if any, under which an acausal threat could in principle rationally bind us, rather than dismissing the category as incoherent; (3) adjudicate the surrogate-goal dispute, specifying what safeguards — sunset clauses, explicit divergence tests, exception procedures — any adopted proxy rule would require; and (4) present the strongest argument for whichever recommendation loses, and say why it nonetheless fails.
