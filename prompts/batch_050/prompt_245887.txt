For the final meeting of my university seminar on Rawls and religion in public life, our instructor has asked each of us to contribute a piece of writing that dramatizes one idea from the course rather than summarizing it. I would like to write about the overlapping consensus, and I want the piece to be a villanelle: nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, governed by two refrains.

The form is meant to do philosophical work. In an overlapping consensus, citizens who hold incompatible comprehensive doctrines affirm the same political conception of justice, each from within their own doctrine and for their own reasons. I want the two refrains to be that shared conception: the same words, repeated identically every time they return, while each surrounding stanza speaks in a different voice and supplies different grounds for endorsing them. Because our seminar has centered on religion, at least two of the voices should be recognizably religious — say, one drawing on scriptural authority and one on natural law — alongside secular voices such as a Kantian and a utilitarian. The refrains themselves should read as plausible political principles (fair cooperation among free and equal citizens, or something of that sort) without quoting Rawls, and each stanza's route to them should be recognizably its own doctrine's route.

What the poem should leave unsettled is Rawls's own worry about stability: whether identical words affirmed on such different grounds amount to genuine agreement or merely to a fortunate convergence that could dissolve when circumstances shift — a modus vivendi rather than a consensus. Please end the poem so that this question is posed but not answered, and after the poem add a brief prose note explaining how your formal choices track the distinction between the two, since I may be asked to defend them in class.
