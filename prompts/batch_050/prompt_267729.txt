I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind with a minor focus in cognitive science, and this semester I'm TA-ing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on agency and free will (about 14 students, one 75-minute session). We recently read about the sphex wasp's famous digger-wasp routine -- the way it can be trapped in an endless loop by a simple intervention, moving its cricket back to the burrow's threshold again and again without ever registering that the situation has changed -- and my students nodded along but clearly regarded sphexishness as something that happens to insects, not to them. When I mentioned this to my advisor, she challenged me to show the class that the same mechanical rigidity is uncomfortably available to humans: we run cached routines, fail to notice when a cue that once mattered has changed, and mistake repetition for reconsideration. I'd like to design an in-class activity for next week that makes the students feel their own sphexishness before the concept is ever named.

What I want is a runnable activity, not a lecture. Participants should have to learn and repeat some structured routine or game under time pressure, and the rules or environment should be quietly rigged so that a cue they were trained to check stops mattering partway through -- so that at least some of them keep "resetting" or re-verifying long after doing so has become pointless, without noticing. I'd rather the activity not involve any biology or wasps on its face, so the reveal lands harder. Include concrete materials, rules, and a way to log who notices the changed cue and when, plus a debrief that maps what happened onto the sphex loop, then turns to the harder question the seminar is actually about: is the difference between the wasp and us that we can break the loop, or merely that we usually do? Be honest, too, about where the analogy strains -- the wasp plausibly lacks the capacity to notice, whereas my students have it and failed to exercise it, and I want the debrief to reckon with whether that difference makes the activity more or less damning for a certain picture of human freedom.

Please format the response as a numbered list of exactly five items -- (1) setup and materials, (2) rules of the activity as participants hear them, (3) a running timeline with the moment the cue stops mattering marked, (4) the debrief plan, and (5) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it -- with no introduction or conclusion; each item can run a short paragraph if needed.
