So I was at the pub with two friends from my reading group (we're working through some classics on probability for a philosophy of science module) and they got into a proper argument that I couldn't settle. Marcus has been reading John Venn's Logic of Chance (the 1888 third edition, he found it free online) and he quoted this bit from the preface, where Venn complains about how the subject gets taught: "When they have searched for illustrations drawn from the practical business of life, they have very generally, but unfortunately, hit upon just the sort of instances which, as I shall endeavour to show hereafter, are among the very worst that could be chosen for the purpose." Marcus's take: Venn is the godfather of the frequency interpretation, and what he's really saying here is that probability only properly applies to repeatable setups -- dice, cards, long series of events -- and that once you try to apply it to one-off real life cases (this witness is telling the truth, this surgery will work, it'll rain tomorrow) you've left solid ground. So, Marcus says, when the weather app says 70% chance of rain tomorrow, that's at best loose talk unless you cash it out as a claim about a big class of similar days.

Priya pushed back hard. She said Marcus is reading way too much into a grumpy preface. Her reading: Venn is just complaining that textbook writers pick artificial examples and then bungle the practical ones (he goes on a tangent about writers on the credibility of witnesses that Marcus skipped over). Nothing in the passage says single-case probabilities are meaningless. And she pointed out that in the very same preface Venn calls probability "a branch of the general science of evidence which happens to make much use of mathematics" -- which she says sounds way more like probability as reasonable degree of belief than like some austere frequencies-only doctrine, and actually cuts against Marcus. Marcus came back with: "science of evidence" doesn't mean subjective belief, Venn elsewhere insists you always need a series, and anyway the rain forecast only makes sense as a reference-class claim. Priya's final move was that if Venn were a strict frequentist he'd love insurance tables and mortality stats, which are practical AND series-based, so the "very worst" instances must be something narrower -- cases where each event is sui generis, like judging a particular witness -- and that means the passage doesn't settle the frequentist vs subjectivist fight at all. At that point the pub closed.

Can you adjudicate? Specifically: (a) who has the better reading of what Venn is actually complaining about in that passage, (b) does the passage support Marcus's claim that one-off probabilities are illegitimate unless reduced to frequencies, and (c) does the "science of evidence" line genuinely help Priya's subjectivist side, or is she overreading it the way she accuses Marcus of overreading his bit? I'd like a point-by-point verdict rather than a both-sides fudge, but also -- please say how confident you are in each part of the verdict, and flag the single point where you're least sure of your own judgement and why.
