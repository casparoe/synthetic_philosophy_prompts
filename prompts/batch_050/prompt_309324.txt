Write a dialogue between two equally capable researchers deciding how to spend a fixed one-year modeling budget for an early-warning system that must flag regime shifts in several incomparable domains: fisheries collapse, hospital demand, grid failures, and online misinformation cascades. The historical data are patchy, interventions are rare, labels arrive late, and the deployment environment is nonstationary. One character, Mira, argues that the no-free-lunch lesson is decisive: across all possible task orderings, every learner is exactly as good as every other, so the project should abandon the dream of a general inductive engine and instead build an explicit registry of admissible worlds, assumption-indexed models, worst-case guarantees, refusal criteria, and cheap probes that test which assumptions are locally paying rent. The other character, Soren, accepts the theorem but denies its practical moral: real deployed tasks are drawn from a tiny, structured corner of the logically possible space, where low-complexity mechanisms, approximate conservation laws, causal invariants, smoothness pockets, and repeated human institutions make cross-domain transfer rational; the right response is not uniform skepticism but a hierarchical meta-inductive strategy that prices assumptions, updates their credibility from audit trails, and keeps a generalist model as a disciplined prior rather than an oracle.

Have them argue over the actual allocation, not slogans: how much budget goes to a shared foundation model versus domain-specific simulators, causal-invariance screens, adversarial permutation tests, reserve capacity for distribution shift, and human elicitation of forbidden interventions. Each must answer the other's strongest point: Mira must explain why structured optimism is not just an unstated uniformity prior smuggled back in; Soren must explain why meta-induction escapes the same regress that no-free-lunch creates at one level up. Let them concede where forced, distinguish average-case, minimax, and anytime-valid criteria, and end with a concrete decision rule for when the system should predict, abstain, gather data, or demand an experiment. Avoid citations, named authorities, and verdicts by authorial fiat; make the disagreement turn on which uncertainties are measurable before deployment and which are only payable after failure.
