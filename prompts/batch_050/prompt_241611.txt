hey, so i'm a phd student in philosophy (mostly i work on testimony in the anglophone literature, but i've been branching out) and ive been trying to get my head around Mīmāṃsā lately, specifically the svataḥ prāmāṇya doctrine — the idea that the validity of a cognition is intrinsic to it, generated right along with the cognition itself instead of needing some external certification from another pramāṇa.

here's what i can't get past. if every cognition is valid simply in virtue of arising, then error seems to become impossible. take the classic fight between the Mīmāṃsakas and the buddhist logicians over whether scripture is a pramāṇa at all. the Mīmāṃsaka has the cognition that the veda is a genuine source of knowledge, the buddhist has the cognition that it isn't. on svataḥ prāmāṇya, aren't both of those cognitions automatically valid? doesn't the whole view collapse into a relativism where nobody can ever just be *wrong* about anything?

and it's even weirder when you combine it with the apauruṣeyatva stuff. i get the motivation for saying the veda has no author — if there's no author, human or divine, there's nobody who could've been mistaken or lying, so the usual worries about testimony don't get off the ground. but then authorlessness plus intrinsic validity seems to make the veda valid for two independent reasons that dont even need each other, which makes the whole system feel sort of overdetermined and redundant.

so my actual question: given that intrinsic validity makes all cognitions equally valid, how could the Mīmāṃsakas justify arguing against their opponents at all? seems like consistency demands they just shrug and say the buddhist view is pramāṇa-certified too. is there some move where they restrict svataḥ prāmāṇya to vedic testimony only, or is the theory just internally inconsistent in the way it looks to me?
