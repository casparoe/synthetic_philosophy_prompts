So I'm prepping my seminar on personal identity and one of my students sent me this passage from a popular philosophy of mind book she's been reading (she won't tell me which one, probably embarrassed). It runs:

'Locke's theory of personal identity is the memory theory: you are identical with whoever you remember being. If you genuinely can't remember doing something, then it wasn't you who did it, full stop — which means you can't justly be punished for it, and Locke happily accepts this. Reid demolished the theory with a single case: the brave officer. As a boy he was flogged at school; as a young officer he remembers the flogging; as an old general he remembers his officer's bravery but has forgotten the flogging. So memory contradicts itself — the general both is and isn't the boy — and the theory collapses. Reid's conclusion: memory is merely evidence of identity, not what identity consists in, and real personal identity is just the persistence of an indivisible immaterial soul.'

My instinct is that this is a competent but not fully honest gloss, and I want a careful audit before I respond to her. I know the relevant chapter of Locke's Essay (the identity and diversity one) and Reid's essay on memory in the Intellectual Powers decently well, but I keep going back and forth on a few things. My own suspicions: Locke talks about consciousness rather than memory narrowly, and I'm not sure that's an innocent swap; I also think Locke's actual position on punishment is more hedged than 'happily accepts' suggests, something about human courts versus divine judgment; and 'demolished' seems strong given how much ink neo-Lockeans have spilled keeping the view alive. But maybe I'm being charitable to Locke and the book is right that Reid's case is fatal on its own terms.

Here's the structure I want, roughly in this order:

**What's fair** (~150 words) — the parts of the gloss a specialist would nod along to, even if compressed.

**What's off** (~250 words) — the specific misrepresentations, from the consciousness/memory conflation to anything else you catch, with an explanation of why each one matters rather than just being pedantic.

**The brave officer, done properly** (~200 words) — lay out the argument as Reid actually gives it, including exactly where the contradiction is supposed to arise (I think transitivity is doing the work, but spell it out), and check whether the book's compressed version preserves or distorts it.

**Does the refutation stick** (~200 words) — what a defender of Locke can say back, whether the objection targets Locke himself or a straw simplification of him, and whether 'memory is merely evidence' is actually Reid's considered view or the book rounding him off.

**Bottom line** (~75 words) — one paragraph I can basically quote back to my student: is the book's summary fair, unfair, or fair-but-misleading, and should she trust its conclusion?
