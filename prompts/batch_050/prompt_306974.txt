I TA an undergrad moral philosophy seminar — about 18 students, one 75-minute session — and we're on Williams's integrity objection, specifically the one thought too many business with the guy who has to run a moral calc before saving his drowning wife. The students all nod along, but I suspect they read it as just another gotcha against consequentialism, which misses it. As a longtime LW reader, the interesting claim to me is that deliberation itself can be a moral cost: it's not that the husband computes the wrong answer, it's that he computed at all, and the extra thought is a mark against his marriage, not his philosophy. Design an in-class activity (staged choice, role-play, whatever) that makes students feel this before anyone names it — something where acting on the deliberative norms they'd normally endorse (be able to give your reasons! check for bias!) is itself what goes wrong. Give me rules, roles, whatever small stakes or time pressure it needs, and a debrief mapping specific moments onto Williams. Bonus if it creates real tension with the epistemic-hygiene norms a lot of them have absorbed from the same blogs I read, since that tension is kindof the live version of the objection. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which part of Williams's point can the activity not actually reproduce, so the facilitator has to supply it verbally?
