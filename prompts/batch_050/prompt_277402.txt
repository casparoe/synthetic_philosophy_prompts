Give me your credence in the following proposition: for a healthy adult who dies at 70 but who would otherwise have lived roughly fifteen more good years, their death is bad for them -- where "death" means the state of being dead, not the process of dying, and "bad for them" is meant in the ordinary welfare sense.

Rather than a bare number, decompose the credence into the sub-questions it rests on, assigning each a rough probability with one line of justification:

(i) Can something be bad for a person without ever being experienced by them? This is the divide between deprivationism and the Epicurean thought that where we are, death is not, and where death is, we are not.

(ii) Conditional on deprivationism, can a harm befall a person at a time when they do not exist? Give your probability distribution over the timing options -- the badness is located after death, before it (as impending loss), at the moment of transition, or at no time at all -- and say whether the core proposition survives each answer.

(iii) Can Lucretius's symmetry argument be answered? That is: is postmortem nonexistence relevantly unlike the eons before your birth, for instance because you could not have been born much earlier without being a different person, whereas you could have died later while remaining yourself?

(iv) Is the counterfactual deprivationism needs -- the life you would have lived -- determinate enough to ground the comparison, given that had you survived, everything downstream of your survival would have gone differently?

Then combine these into a single credence for the original proposition, identify which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and tell me what argument or consideration would move that term most.
