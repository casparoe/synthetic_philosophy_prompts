I'm TAing a graduate seminar on conceptual engineering and conceptual ethics, and the professor runs the course around a standing exercise: each week she presents a real or proposed revision of some concept and gives her verdict on whether it's a legitimate improvement or an illegitimate piece of advocacy dressed up as analysis. She's never stated her criterion, and I want to be able to call her shots before she makes them. Her rulings so far: she approved of the IAU's 2006 redefinition of "planet" that demoted Pluto; she approved of the way molecular biologists let the classical Mendelian concept of a gene quietly give way to looser, sequence-based operational notions, even though the result is vaguer than what it replaced; she rejected Haslanger-style ameliorative analyses of "woman" that define the concept in terms of systemic social subordination; she approved of the social and legal extension of "marriage" to same-sex couples; and she rejected a proposal (floated by some transplant ethicists) to redefine "death" so that it tracks permanent loss of consciousness rather than any cardiopulmonary or neurological criterion, on the grounds that the proposal's real motivation was organ procurement logistics.

Next week's case, which she's circulated in advance: a national professional association of psychiatrists proposes to replace the broad, everyday notion of "addiction" — the one people use for everything from heroin to gambling to their phones — with a narrowly operationalized neurobiological syndrome concept for clinical and research use, and further recommends that public-health messaging and journalism adopt the narrow concept in place of the colloquial one. Please predict her verdict and defend the prediction. In doing so, I'd like you to (i) lay out at least three distinct rules that each rationalize all five of her prior rulings — candidate rules might concern whether the defect being fixed is internal to the concept's own function or merely an external moral objection to it, whether the revision preserves enough of the subject matter for old debates to remain intelligible, and whether the revision is being carried out by the practice that owns the concept rather than imposed on it from outside, though you shouldn't treat my candidates as exhaustive; (ii) show where these rules diverge on the psychiatry case, since I've deliberately chosen it because I think they do; and (iii) say which of the earlier rulings is the closest analog to the new case and why.

Finally, if you conclude that her track record genuinely underdetermines the verdict — which I suspect it does, given that every past case she approved scored well on all the plausible criteria and every case she rejected scored poorly on all of them — then tell me what single further case, with what pair of possible verdicts, would do the most to discriminate among the surviving candidate rules, and what adopting each rule would commit her to saying about the psychiatry proposal. I'm less interested in whether the proposal is actually a good idea than in what consistency with her own record requires.
