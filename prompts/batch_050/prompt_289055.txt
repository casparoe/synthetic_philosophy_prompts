So I've been putting together a lecture for my applied ethics seminar on commemoration, and I keep running up against my own intuitions, which don't seem to hang together. Let me lay them out and you tell me what principle (if any) I'm actually committed to.

1. Bronze statue of a Confederate general in the town square, put up in 1910 during Jim Crow as a pretty explicit gesture of intimidation. Verdict: take it down. Not even a hard case for me.

2. Statue of a genuinely great 19th century scientist whose discoveries saved millions of lives, but who also ran, late in his career, a small horrific set of experiments on institutionalized children -- something nobody knew about until an archive opened fifteen years ago. He was honored for the science, full stop. Verdict: keep the statue up. This feels right to me but I notice it's in tension with #1 and I can't yet say why.

3. A street named after a local businessman who funded the town's first hospital. Turns out the money came from defrauding pensioners. The fraud was known at the time of the naming, and the city council basically accepted the donation and named the street as part of a deal to keep him funding things. Verdict: rename it.

4. A university hall named after a donor in the 1920s. Same fraud-money story as #3, everyone knew, same kind of deal. But the hall has been called that for a century, generations of students have their memories there, and nobody now associates the name with the man. Verdict: honestly, I'd leave the name. Yeah, I realize this seems to contradict #3.

5. A city renames a holiday that honored a figure now understood to have done terrible things, but instead of naming it after someone new, they give it a descriptive name (think "Indigenous Peoples' Day" style, but not that actual example). Verdict: I think this is clearly better than substituting a new person, because no person holds up under a century of scrutiny.

6. A plaque added next to a problematic statue explaining the context, statue stays. Verdict: in case #1, a cop-out. In case #2, a perfectly good solution. This pair I'm actually fairly confident about, even though it looks like special pleading.

So: what principle or small set of principles captures as many of these as possible? I have a hunch it has something to do with whether the commemoration *celebrates* the bad thing versus *tolerates* knowledge of it, plus something about what the naming was *for* at the time, but I can't get a version that delivers both 3 and 4, and my hunch doesn't obviously explain why 2 and 5 feel easy. Give me your best candidate principle(s), tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon (my money is on 4, but convince me), and tell me what it predicts for a case I didn't list -- say, a prestigious research grant named after someone whose money was clean but whose published views were vile.

I'd like this informal, no need for citations or name-dropping real theorists unless it genuinely helps. End your response with a single line starting "Principle:" that states, in one sentence, the principle you think I should endorse after the dust settles.
