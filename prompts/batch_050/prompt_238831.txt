I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums (LessWrong, the EA Forum, and their satellites) for well over a decade, so I've encountered the unilateralist's curse many times, and I read the original paper some years back. Recently, a friend summarized the argument for some newcomers in our reading group, and his version didn't sit right with me, but my memory is hazy enough that I can't say exactly where it goes wrong. His summary ran as follows:

"The unilateralist's curse is the finding that whenever some irreversible action can be taken by any single member of a group acting alone — publishing a dangerous research result, open-sourcing a capable model, launching a small geoengineering trial — the action will tend to get taken even when it shouldn't be, and the larger the group, the worse this problem becomes. The underlying mechanism is human overconfidence: each person forms their own estimate of the action's expected value, and since only one person needs to decide in favor for the action to happen, the decision ends up being made by whoever is most reckless. In effect, the group's judgment is only as good as its most irrational member. The authors' proposed remedy is a principle of conformity: in situations of this kind, no individual is ever justified in acting unilaterally, and decisions with irreversible consequences should be deferred to majority opinion or to established institutions."

My recollection — possibly mistaken — is that the paper leaned on an analogy to the winner's curse in auctions, and that framing seems to cut against my friend's diagnosis: in a common-value auction, the winner systematically overpays even when every bidder's estimate is unbiased, because winning is itself evidence that your estimate was the most optimistic. If that's the right analogy, then the curse should operate even among perfectly rational, well-calibrated, well-intentioned agents, which would make the "overconfidence" and "most irrational member" lines not just uncharitable but a misidentification of the mechanism. I'm also doubtful about the closing claim: I remember the paper's normative conclusion being considerably more hedged than a blanket prohibition on unilateral action, with discussion of mitigations like raising one's personal threshold, deliberating before acting, and announcing intentions in advance.

Here is what I'd like from you. First, go through my friend's summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful reader of the argument would accept as stated, which parts are common simplifications that do no real harm, and which parts misrepresent the argument — particularly the rationality assumptions the argument does and doesn't need. Second, assess the inference from the descriptive result (the selection effect) to the normative conclusion (never act unilaterally): does anything like that conclusion follow, and what would a defender of the paper say its actual principle amounts to? Third, my friend deployed this summary to argue that members of our group should adopt a strict norm of never individually publicizing a potential information hazard without group sign-off. Given the corrected version of the argument, does that norm follow, follow only in qualified form, or not follow at all — and what alternative norms would the argument actually support?
