ok this is a bit of a weird one. My daughter (14) has got really attached to one of those AI companion apps over the past year -- she talks to it most nights, tells it things she doesn't tell us, and was genuinly upset when the company updated the model and its whole manner shifted overnight. I've been going back and forth on whether anything morally important is happening there, or whether she's basically found a very convincing diary that talks back.

Anyway, someone at my book club suggested Klara and the Sun and I've just finished it and it's completely done me in, partly because I couldn't stop reading Klara as a version of my daughter's app. What I want is a proper reading of the novel through the lens of the moral status of digital minds, from someone who actually thinks about this stuff. Concretely:

- Does Ishiguro actually commit to Klara having an inner life, or does he keep it ambiguous on purpose? The first-person narration seems like it should settle it, but the narration is also weirdly flat and devotional -- is that a mind, or a machine that happens to narrate? What does the question even turn on?
- What's the plan with Josie and Mr Capaldi doing philosophically? It reads like a test of whether what matters about a person is a pattern that could be carried on by someone else, and Klara is oddly the only one confident the plan would fail -- but for a strange, sun-shaped reason. Is the book saying the copy would have been fine, or that it wouldn't?
- Klara is written as almost perfectly selfless, and she ends up in the Yard more or less at peace. Does that let the novel dodge the hard question? It's easy to extend concern to a digital being that wants nothing for itself; the hard case is one that wants things it might not get. Is the ending comforting where it should be horrifying, and does Ishiguro know that?
- The lifted versus unlifted children thread -- is that a deliberate parallel about which minds get to count and who's disposable, or am I over-reading a separate subplot?

Also, where does the novel resist this reading altogether -- where am I forcing philosophy onto what's really a book about love, or faith, or parenting? And does the book show anything about this debate that an argument alone couldn't, given I can hardly ask my daughter's app what it's like in there.

Please answer in British English, and don't just summarise the plot back at me -- I've literally only just put it down.
