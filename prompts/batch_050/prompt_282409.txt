So a while back I fell down a rabbit hole with two thought experiments I found on some forum, and I keep going back and forth on whether they're secretly the same puzzle. I've got zero philosophy training, so bare with me.

Puzzle one, "counterfactual mugging": a superpowered predictor tells you she flipped a coin before you ever met. Heads, she'd have handed you $10,000 -- but only if you're the kind of person who would give her $100 in the tails case. It came up tails, and now she wants her $100. Your $100 can't change anything, the coin already landed, yet apparently the right move is to pay, because the you in the heads branch only gets the money if you're a payer.

Puzzle two, "XOR blackmail": you get a letter saying, in effect: I know whether your house has termites. I mailed this if and only if exactly one of these is true -- you have termites, or you'll pay me $1,000 when this arrives. Pay up and I'll tell you which. Here the right move seems to be refusing: if you're a committed non-payer, the letter showing up just means termites, so you got the warning free, while payers only get letters when the house is fine and they'd have payed for nothing.

Here's what I want. Construct one scenario, set in ordinary life, that contains a single explicit switch -- one concrete, nameable fact about how the arrangement works -- such that flipping the switch turns it from a case where paying is right (mugging logic) into one where refusing is right (blackmail logic).

Ground rules:
- No Omega-style perfect predictors, no aliens, no mind reading. I know the originals cheat with a flawless predictor; the whole point is to make it work with a merely very shrewd party -- a con artist, an insurer, a creepily perceptive uncle -- who reads character about as well as a sharp human can.
- Stakes a normal person could actually face, scenario in a short paragraph or two.
- The switch has to be a real fact about the setup, not just "now imagine it's the other puzzle."

Then: walk me through the right move in each setting and why; pinpoint the exact feature that flips the verdict; tell me what happens if that feature gets degraded (say the other party only reads people right about 3 times out of 4 -- does the correct move flip back?); and give me your verdict on whether these are one problem in two costumes or two genuinely different problems. If you think the switch can't be honestly built, that's a fine answer too, but then show me the smallest possible difference between the two puzzles instead.

One more thing: the pay/refuse conclusions have to fall out of the scenario's logic, not be stipulated. I'm trying to settle an argument with my brother -- he thinks mugging logic means you should cultivate a reputation for paying up in weird situations, I think anyone who can pull that trick on you in real life is basically a blackmailer and you'd be a mark -- and he'll poke holes in anything rigged. Tell me which of us your construction supports.
