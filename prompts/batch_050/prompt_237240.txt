I am a high school senior, and for my psychology elective we were recently given a magazine article on the philosophy of psychiatric diagnosis. One passage presents the view of the philosopher Ian Hacking, and I would like to know how far I can trust it. Here is how the article presents his position:

"In the 1990s, Ian Hacking introduced what he called the looping effect of human kinds. His point was that classifying people is not like classifying rocks or beetles. When geologists label a mineral, the mineral does not read the geology journals; but when psychiatrists define a condition and people learn that the description fits them, they begin to understand themselves in its terms and to act accordingly. Their altered behavior then gives clinicians new material to study, so the official picture of the condition shifts in response. Classification and classified keep reshaping one another, which is why psychiatric categories have histories in a way that chemical elements do not. Hacking's best-known illustration is the epidemic of multiple personality disorder, which swelled as the diagnosis became famous. His conclusion is that conditions of this sort are social constructions: real in their effects, but produced by the classifying practice rather than discovered in nature, and so lacking the stable, mind-independent reality of a disease like tuberculosis. The practical moral he draws is that we should be deeply wary of psychiatric classification, since the labels generate the very phenomena they claim merely to describe."

The article then turns to my generation, arguing that teenagers absorbing diagnostic vocabulary from social media is the looping effect in action, and that this shows modern diagnostic manuals are not simply imperfect but are actively manufacturing the disorders they catalogue.

The final moves strike me as suspiciously fast, although I am inclined to accept the core idea that being labeled changes the people labeled. Could you audit the summary for me? Specifically:

1. Which parts would a careful reader of Hacking accept as accurate, and which parts conflate his view with a stronger or simpler claim he does not actually make?
2. Is the "self-fulfilling label" picture in the second and third sentences an adequate account of the looping mechanism, or does it leave out part of how the loop is supposed to run?
3. Is the step from "these categories have histories and are shaped by classifying practices" to "these conditions lack the genuine reality of tuberculosis" one that Hacking himself endorses, or a slide the article makes on his behalf?
4. Does the article's final conclusion about diagnostic manuals follow from the accurate parts of the summary? And is the social media example a legitimate extension of Hacking's idea, or a different phenomenon wearing his vocabulary?

To be clear about what I am asking: I do not want a verdict on whether psychiatric diagnoses are in fact real. I want to know whether this article has represented Hacking fairly, and what, if anything, remains of its conclusion once any misrepresentations are corrected.
