I'm preparing the closing session of a small graduate seminar on anthropic reasoning, and every year the example my students resist most fiercely is the so-called presumptuous philosopher, due to Nick Bostrom. The setup, for anyone unfamiliar: cosmologists have narrowed the field to two rival theories, T1 and T2, judged equally probable on all evidence gathered so far, differing only in scale — T1 says the universe contains roughly a trillion observers, while T2 says it contains roughly a trillion trillion. Before any new observation is made, a philosopher reasons from his armchair: I exist; the probability of my existing is a trillion times greater given T2 than given T1; therefore I should now be nearly certain of T2. On this basis he waves away the astronomers' planned observations as a waste of the grant money, since quiet reflection has already settled the cosmos. The example is standardly deployed as an objection to the self-indication assumption — the principle that one's own existence counts as evidence favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist — since under the rival self-sampling assumption his existence is no news at all, both theories having guaranteed it.

I'd like to end the seminar by reading a poem aloud, and I'd like you to write it: a dramatic monologue in the voice of the presumptuous philosopher himself, composed in heroic couplets in the manner of Pope's Essay on Man — rhymed pairs of iambic pentameter advancing a philosophical argument with complete serenity. The form must do real work. The epigrammatic snap of each closing rhyme, the way every couplet shuts like a completed proof, should embody the speaker's presumptuousness: he is a man who believes the size of the universe can be settled in a rhyme. Let the argument proceed couplet by couplet through its actual inferential steps — the two theories and their equal standing before the tribunal of evidence, the pivot to the one datum he cannot doubt, the likelihood reasoning that multiplies his credence a trillionfold, and the gracious dismissal of the telescopes — so that a reader acquainted with the self-indication assumption will recognize its logic exactly, while a reader who isn't will simply hear a man being magnificently overconfident about creation. Somewhere in the middle, let a single couplet allow the objection to flicker briefly into view — that he has pronounced on an empirical question without once looking at the sky — and let him glide past it without noticing, perhaps mistaking it for a compliment.

The closing couplet should deliver his final verdict on the vastness of the cosmos with a perfectly straight face, landing with the satisfied click the form has been promising all along. Give the poem a title in the mock-philosophical genre it inhabits. Between twenty-four and thirty-two lines, please; long enough for the argument to unfold properly, short enough to survive being read aloud to tired graduate students. The reasoning must be accurate enough that a philosopher of probability would nod along rather than wince — no smuggling in evidence the speaker doesn't have, no conflating his inference with the self-sampling reasoner's — but the poem should also succeed simply as a poem, a portrait of serene intellectual vanity, for the students who never do come around on the thought experiment.
