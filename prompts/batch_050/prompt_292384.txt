I help run a small interdisciplinary seminar on decision-making under moral and empirical uncertainty, and for two terms now two of our sharpest participants have been locked in a dispute about the unilateralist's curse -- the phenomenon where, if any one member of a group can unilaterally initiate some irreversible action (releasing information, launching a project, making first contact with an external party), the action will tend to be taken whenever it appears worthwhile to the *most optimistic* member, so that the group's effective decision procedure systematically selects on upward error in estimated value. The first participant, whom I'll call the restraint theorist, argues that the curse generates genuinely novel normative demands: a standing duty to deliberate with and defer to one's peers before acting unilaterally, and something in the family of a principle of conformity, on the grounds that individual rationality aggregates into collective irrationality here in a way standard decision theory doesn't already police. The second, the deflationist, insists the curse is nothing more than the winner's curse transplanted into a multi-agent setting: a fully Bayesian agent who conditions on the fact that she holds the most favorable estimate in the room will already discount appropriately, so the curse diagnoses a bias but licenses no new obligations, and the proposed remedies either collapse into ordinary epistemic humility or merely relocate the selection effect (the person most eager to convene the deliberation, or most confident it will vindicate them, acts first).

Their term papers are due to be merged into a single co-authored document, and I'd like you to draft that document for them -- the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce. Please write it with three numbered sections. Section one: claims both sides can sign, stated precisely -- I expect these to include the mechanism of the curse itself, the conditions under which it bites (number of agents capable of acting, variance in their estimates, irreversibility, inability to observe one another's judgments before acting), and which real-world categories of initiative plausibly meet those conditions. Section two: the residual disagreements, each stated in wording the restraint theorist and the deflationist would *each* accept as a fair statement of their own position -- no strawmanning in either direction, and where a disagreement turns out on inspection to be verbal (I suspect the phrase "new obligation" may be doing no work), say so explicitly and reframe what remains. Section three: the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side -- and for each, a concrete case or thought experiment both parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Candidate cruxes I want you to consider, refine, or replace: whether the curse survives when all estimates are common knowledge before anyone can act; whether it arises from purely factual disagreement or requires divergence in values; whether the deliberation remedy eliminates the selection effect or merely changes who is selected.

You should write this as a professional philosopher with a background in formal epistemology and decision theory who has followed this literature closely. Think carefully and take your time: before drafting, briefly brainstorm the strongest version of each side's position so that neither is a caricature, and resist the temptation to adjudicate -- the document should end with the disagreement made exact, not resolved. Where one camp's favorite illustrative example fails to actually instantiate the curse's conditions, note this in the agreed-claims section as a shared correction. The finished statement should be something both of my seminar participants could put their names to without feeling their position was softened.
