I've been keeping a list of decisions I'd make when I genuinely have no probabilities to work with, and I want to know what principle, if any, sits underneath my verdicts. Here are the cases:

- My bank offers two products and flatly refuses to give odds (the regime has changed, historical data is useless, etc). One returns 3–4% for sure, the other either −60% or +150%. Verdict: take the safe one, and anyone who gambles here is being reckless.
- Same setup, but now the second product returns either 2.5% or 5%. Verdict: take it. Refusing on worst-case grounds feels timid, since the worst case costs you almost nothing.
- A friend turns down a safe civil service job to open a bakery because, she says, she'd forgive herself for failing but never for not trying. Verdict: thats a perfectly good reason, not a bias.
- My cousin agonizes for weeks over every decision because he's terrified he'll regret whichever way he goes. Verdict: he's broken something. Regret-avoidance as a life strategy is a recipe for paralysis, and he should just pick and stop looking back. (Yes, I notice this sits awkwardly next to the bakery case. That's part of why I'm asking.)
- Picking a dishwasher: model A is a gamble, either junk or excellent; model B is a known mediocre-but-fine. Minimizing my maximum possible regret ranks A above B. Then I learn there's a model C that might be brilliant or might be garbage, and suddenly the same rule ranks B above A — even though nothing about A or B changed. Verdict: the A-versus-B ordering shouldn't flip because I heard about C, and any rule that does this is broken.
- At a dinner party someone ran the Rawls-style thought experiment where you design a society's basic rules without knowing what position you'll end up in. Protecting the worst-off felt obviously correct there. But if I lived by that rule day to day I'd never cross the street — the usual Harsanyi-ish objection — so it can't be my general rule either.

My tentative principle: use maximin when the worst case is ruinous and irreversible; otherwise minimize maximum regret; and if you have any real grip on probabilities at all, just maximize expected value. Test this against my cases. If it needs repair, repair it, but state the repaired version precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause. Then tell me what it should say about a case I haven't decided: a coastal city choosing whether to spend a huge sum on a flood barrier when the sea-level models disagree wildly and nobody trusts anyone's probabilities.

One thing I definately don't want: a tour of every decision rule in the literature ending with "it depends." Commit to a single principle, or the smallest set you can actually defend, and tell me flatly which of my verdicts I have to give up — including if the answer is that my bakery and cousin verdicts can't both stand.
