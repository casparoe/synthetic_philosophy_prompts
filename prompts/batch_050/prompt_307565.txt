Like many longtime readers of rationalist blogs, I first encountered superrationality through Hofstadter's Luring Lottery and the Platonia dilemma, and the idea has been argued over endlessly in the comments sections ever since. The standard objection is well worn: superrational reasoning works only when the players are genuinely symmetric, and real agents never are. I'd like to see that objection dramatized rather than rehearsed, so please write me a short story, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, on the following setup.

A woman is paid to have her brain scanned and instantiated as twenty identical emulations, each running in a separate sealed instance with no way to communicate. The emulations are offered a one-shot lottery modeled on the Luring Lottery: each may submit at most one entry consisting of a positive integer, the winner is whoever submits the smallest integer that no one else submitted, and the prize, paid to the winner's designated heirs, is a fixed sum divided by the winning number. The twist is that the twenty instances were not launched from the same snapshot. They were each snapshotted a few hours apart over the course of a week, so each copy knows the others are near-duplicates of itself, but not exact ones, and each knows exactly which small, mundane differences separate it from its siblings (one went to sleep before the scan, one had just read a particular email, and so on).

The story should turn on a specific failure mode that almost never gets dramatized: superrational reasoning can fail not only through defection but through correlation. If every copy reasons identically, they may all coordinate on the same choice, and in a smallest-unique-number lottery, perfect symmetry is the worst possible outcome. I want at least one copy to notice this and to reason explicitly about the regress that follows: any clever symmetry-breaking trick it devises is one the others will also devise, unless the trick depends on something that genuinely distinguishes this copy from the rest, and the only such things are the trivial divergences between the snapshots. Whether exploiting a trivial difference is still superrational, or has quietly become ordinary evidential reasoning, is exactly the kind of question I want the story to fight about.

A few constraints. First, dramatize the reasoning through scenes and dialogue between the copies (or between a copy and its memory of the original woman) rather than delivering an essay in dialogue's clothing. Second, have at least one character do a concrete expected-value calculation on page, so the stakes are quantitative rather than gestured at. Third, the ending should not resolve the philosophy: no triumphant vindication of symmetric reasoning, no smug lesson that game theory beats it, and no tacked-on moral. The last scene should leave the reader uncertain whether the winning move, whatever it was, was rational, lucky, or a category error. Fourth, the story should be legible to someone who has never heard of Hofstadter, so the lottery's mechanics and the idea of reasoning about one's own reasoning's outputs must be established inside the fiction, not name-dropped.
