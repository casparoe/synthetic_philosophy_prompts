Over the past year, my father — a retired civil engineer, and not a foolish man — has become convinced that the medical research establishment is systematically corrupt: that trial data are routinely fabricated, journals are bought, and regulators are captured. What began as skepticism about one particular drug has hardened into a general account of how that entire world works. This has recently stopped being an abstract irritation. My mother was diagnosed two months ago with a treatable cancer, and he is pressing her to refuse the recommended treatment in favor of an alternative regimen he has assembled from his own reading.

What unsettles me is not any single claim of his but the shape our disagreements have taken. Every study I cite, he absorbs as further evidence of the corruption — after all, who funded and published it? When I ask what could in principle change his mind, the conditions he names are ones no real institution could ever satisfy. And when I try to defend my own trust in the medical literature, I notice that I keep citing the outputs of the very institutions whose reliability is under dispute, which he points out is circular, and I have to concede the point. Increasingly I suspect the real difference between us is not that my beliefs are better supported but that we each take certain things as fixed without argument — he, that concentrated power always corrupts; I, that thousands of independent researchers spread across countries and decades could not sustain a coordinated fraud — and everything downstream follows from those commitments.

So I would like advice on three things. First, when two people disagree not over conclusions but over what counts as evidence in the first place, is genuine argument between them even possible, and if so, what form does it take? Second, is my position actually more rational than his, or merely more conventional? If everyone's reasoning ultimately rests on commitments they cannot justify without going in a circle, what distinguishes reasonable bedrock from unreasonable bedrock — or is the whole distinction just a matter of which side is winning? Third, practically: my mother's treatment decision cannot wait for either of us to resolve this. What does honest engagement look like when the dispute concerns the entire apparatus for establishing facts rather than any particular fact — and is there a way to contest a person's deepest background commitments without simply declaring them crazy, which I am certain would end the conversation and possibly my parents' willingness to consult me at all?
