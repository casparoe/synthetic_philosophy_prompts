I am a high school junior in a biology course that has moved from basic Mendelian genetics into behavioral genetics. I keep hearing heritability used as though it means how genetic a trait is, but that seems to produce strange conclusions. My tentative principle is this: heritability is the proportion of observed variation in a trait, within a specified population and range of environments, that is statistically attributable to genetic variation; it is not a measure of how genetically caused, important, or changeable the trait itself is. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of biology who is also an excellent statistics teacher. Think carefully, begin by brainstorming several possible formulations of the principle, and then develop the best one. No bibliography or citations are needed.

Test the principle against these hypothetical cases and my verdicts: (1) In a district where every child receives adequate nutrition and schooling, a valid study estimates the heritability of reading scores as 0.70. I think it is legitimate to say that 70 percent of the variation among students is associated with genetic differences, yet I also find myself wanting to say that 70 percent of a particular student’s reading ability comes from genes and 30 percent from her environment. (2) A tutoring program raises every student’s score by the same amount without changing the spread of scores. I think the heritability estimate could remain 0.70 even though an environmental intervention changed the outcome. (3) In one town, educational opportunities are nearly equal, while in another they differ dramatically; reading-score heritability is higher in the first town. I think neither estimate gives the trait’s heritability simpliciter. (4) Two towns each show high within-town heritability for reading scores, but the average difference between the towns is entirely due to lead exposure in one town. I think the within-town estimates do not support saying that the between-town gap is genetic. (5) Nearly everyone develops two eyes because of ordinary human development, but the rare variation in eye number is mostly due to accidents. I think eye number can have very low heritability while still being deeply biological and genetically caused. (6) A trait is highly heritable under all environments previously studied, yet a newly introduced diet changes it substantially. I think this does not contradict the earlier estimate. (7) Parents who transmit genetic variants associated with reading also tend to provide more books and tutoring, so genetic and environmental advantages are correlated. I suspect a high estimate may still describe variation under present conditions without cleanly separating genetic and environmental causal contributions.

What precise principle, or small set of principles, captures the verdicts worth preserving? Identify which case motivates each clause, and be explicit about which of my verdicts must be abandoned—especially the individual-level claim in case 1. If the answer requires distinguishing broad-sense from narrow-sense heritability, or variance description from causal explanation, include that distinction without relying on formulas. Finally, state what your repaired principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: a genetically homogeneous population in which all variation is environmental, and a highly effective gene-targeted intervention for a trait that was highly heritable before the intervention existed.
