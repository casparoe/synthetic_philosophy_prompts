I help run a monthly data-literacy evening at my workplace — about a dozen colleagues, none of us statisticians, roughly 75 minutes. On my own reading I got absorbed by the phenomenon where a pattern holds within every subgroup of a dataset and yet reverses when the groups are pooled (I believe the standard name is Simpson's paradox), and I want the group to feel it rather than hear about it. Design a game we can genuinely play in which the paradox emerges from the participants' own choices, not from numbers I hand out: ideally, the group should first make a real decision or recommendation on the basis of pooled statistics they generated themselves during play, and only afterwards, when we split the same data, discover that their own results point the opposite way. The confounding factor should arise from how people sort themselves during play — self-selection by ability or confidence, for instance — and not from anything I secretly rig in advance. Please include: the rules and materials; concrete payoff numbers or success probabilities chosen so the reversal appears with high probability under realistic play; a contingency plan for the evening if the reversal fails to show up; a short debrief that maps each phase of the game onto the statistical idea and also faces the uncomfortable point that splitting the data is not always the right move, since pooling is sometimes exactly what you should do; what will predictably go wrong the first time we run it; and which feature of the phenomenon a game like this cannot reproduce. Since I lack the training to check your mathematics, please state how confident you are that the activity will produce the intended effect, and single out the design element you are least sure of.
