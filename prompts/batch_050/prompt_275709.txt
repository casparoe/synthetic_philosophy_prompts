I co-teach a second-year undergraduate metaphysics course, and next term I have a single 80-minute seminar with 16 students on the necessity of origins — the thesis that each of us could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than we in fact did, the claim associated with Kripke's work on modality. The timing is deliberate but a little cruel: we have just spent three weeks on personal identity, and my students have been thoroughly trained to think that who you are is a matter of psychological continuity, memories, character, and at most your body — in short, qualitative and intrinsic facts about a person. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel, through their own judgments rather than my summary, that origin essentialism has real pull: that they themselves will refuse to call a qualitatively identical person "you" once the only difference is which gametes were involved, even after cheerfully accepting radical changes to every intrinsic feature. They should not hear the thesis stated, let alone anyone's name attached to it, until the debrief. No lecture up front.

Design the activity so that the students construct the problem themselves. I'd like a mechanic built around staged forced choices with private written commitments that later get exposed — vote first, discuss after — because I know this group and several of them will simply align with whoever speaks first. Concretely, give me: the sequence of vignettes they vote on, with the actual card text written out rather than a description of what the cards would say, arranged so that they first endorse surviving enormous intrinsic change and then balk at a change invisible in the resulting person; the rules and roles if you include a role-play element (a hospital or fertility-clinic scenario would work, but use it only if it earns its place); a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 80 minutes; and a way to surface the asymmetry between intrinsic change and origin change so that it registers as their discovery rather than my point. Then, once they have committed to origins mattering, I want the activity to turn on them and make them feel the price: the modal fragility (their own existence hanging on which of millions of near-identical microscopic alternatives occurred, though the world would have looked no different), the epistemic cost (identity not being scrutable from any observation of the resulting person), and, if you can manage it, the sorites pressure on the gametes themselves (how much can the originating material be altered before the result is no longer you?).

Include a debrief section that maps each phase of the activity onto the corresponding commitment or cost, names the thesis only at the end, and canvasses the main escape routes — denying that origins matter, treating identity as brute, treating it as conventional — with what each one costs, so I can close the session by showing them the live options rather than a settled answer. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the student who insists DNA settles everything (and a case that separates genetic similarity from gamete identity, since a clone shares the genome but is not the same person), the quiet-student problem, and the student who says this is just obvious biology and misses that the question is modal rather than empirical. End with an honest note on which feature of the thesis the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself afterwards — I suspect the distinction between what we could discover empirically and how we legitimately describe counterfactual situations is exactly the part a game can't stage.
