I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science, and over the summer I began running experiments on how people assign probabilities to everyday indicative conditionals — sentences like "If the train is late, Maya will miss her connection." The design is simple: participants read short vignettes, rate the probability of a conditional on a slider, and separately rate the probability of the consequent given the antecedent. In my grant proposal I wrote that these two quantities should coincide, citing what I took to be an established result in the philosophy of language: that the probability of "if A, then B" just is the conditional probability of B given A.

At our lab meeting last week, a philosophy postdoc told me I had Lewis backwards — that his triviality results are a problem for my design rather than its foundation. I went back to the secondary literature and came away more confused than before. Some authors call the identity "Adams's Thesis" or "the Equation" and treat it as an empirically well-confirmed generalization about how competent speakers use "if." Others say Lewis proved it cannot be correct if indicative conditionals have truth values, and that this is precisely why theorists like Adams deny that such conditionals are true or false at all, while Stalnaker and his followers insist they are propositions like any other. I genuinely cannot tell whether these camps are even disagreeing about the same equation, or whether one side is making a mathematical point and the other a psychological one.

So, to put my question plainly: given that Lewis proved the Equation, what exactly is left for philosophers to fight over? Is the residual dispute merely about the pragmatics of "if" — a matter of how we talk rather than what the word means? And for my experiment specifically: if the Equation is a theorem, is there any point in measuring both quantities, since they must come out equal for any rational subject anyway? I'd rather find out now than in my dissertation defense.
