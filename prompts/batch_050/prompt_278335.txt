I'm trying to get clear on what makes a generic statement true, and I'd like you to reverse-engineer the principle behind my gut verdicts on a set of cases. Here are the verdicts, stated as I actually have them.

"Ducks lay eggs" strikes me as plainly true, even though male ducks never lay eggs and most female ducks are not laying at any given moment. "Ducks are female" strikes me as false, even though roughly half of all ducks are female. "Lions have manes" is true, even though only adult male lions have them. "Canadians are right-handed" is true, because the great majority of Canadians are. "Books are paperbacks" is false, and this one bothers me, because I believe most books in circulation actually are paperbacks. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is true, and this bothers me even more, because only a small minority of mosquitoes carry malaria. "Sharks attack swimmers" seems acceptable, even though the overwhelming majority of sharks never attack anyone. "Ticks carry Lyme disease" is true, though again only a minority of ticks do. And "Pigeons carry disease" is the case I keep going back and forth on: people say it constantly, but as far as I know pigeons are not especially disease-ridden compared to other urban animals, so I cannot decide whether the generic is true-but-misleading or simply false.

My tentative principle was this: a generic of the form "Ks are F" is true when being F is characteristic of Ks, rather than merely common among them. But this seems to deliver the wrong verdict on the mosquito, shark, and tick cases, where the property is rare, and possibly the wrong verdict on the paperback case, where the property is common but the generic still feels false. So here is what I want from you. Test my tentative principle against all the cases, and then repair it or replace it. Give me the single principle, or small set of principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, and tell me which clause of your principle is motivated by which case. Be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to abandon, and say whether I should treat that as an acceptable cost or as a reason to prefer a messier account that saves every verdict. Tell me what your principle says about my unresolved pigeon case. Finally, tell me what it predicts for two cases I have not listed: "Sea turtles live long lives," where in fact the vast majority die as hatchlings, and "Violent criminals reoffend," where you should assume for the sake of argument that most do not.

Please write your answer in American English.
