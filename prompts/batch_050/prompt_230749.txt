I'm a second-year graduate student in philosophy of science, and over the past year I've been keeping an informal record of my office mate's verdicts on confirmation cases, because she has strikingly firm intuitions and I suspect she is running a rule she has never stated outright. The cases she's pronounced on so far: a particle physics collaboration predicts a previously unobserved resonance at a specific energy and then finds it -- she says this strongly confirms their model; an economist constructs a model with several free parameters to reproduce a well-known dataset and cites the quality of the fit as evidence for the model -- she says the fit confirms it barely at all; a medical researcher derives from her theory that an existing drug should help a condition no one had thought to test it on, and a subsequent trial succeeds -- she counts this as strong confirmation; a rival research program responds to a new experimental anomaly by bolting on an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis tailored to that very result -- she says that once the anomaly has been accommodated in this way it no longer counts in the program's favor at all. In each case I've recorded both her verdict and her off-the-cuff reasoning, but the reasoning shifts around enough that I can't tell what principle is doing the work.

The occasion for asking is a new case that came up in our reading group, in the vicinity of the standard example of general relativity's handling of the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, which was on the books decades before the theory. The new case is this: a team of glaciologists develops a model of ice-sheet dynamics to explain some recently observed surging behavior; the historical data they used in building and calibrating the model all postdates 2010. After publication, a historian of science points out that the model entails, with no adjustment whatsoever, a precise value for a puzzling measurement reported in an obscure expedition log from the 1950s -- a measurement the team had never heard of, could not have seen, and plainly did not build into the model. I asked my office mate whether this retrodictive success confirms the model, and she hesitated for the first time since I've known her.

So my questions: First, predict her verdict, and give your reasoning -- I want a prediction about her, not just the right answer. Second, reconstruct the candidate rules that are each consistent with all four of her prior verdicts (I can see at least three: something in the family of "only evidence that postdates the theory confirms it," something in the family of "evidence confirms only if it wasn't used in constructing the theory," and something in the family of strict conditionalization on which already-known evidence can't raise a theory's probability at all), and say what each candidate predicts about the glaciology case and where they diverge. Third, tell me which of her four prior verdicts is the closest analog to the new case and why. Fourth, if her track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, describe the single further hypothetical case -- as a verdict question I could put to her -- that would most efficiently tell the live candidate rules apart, and say which rules each possible answer to it would eliminate.
