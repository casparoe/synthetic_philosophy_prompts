Earlier this year I inherited a sum of money large enough that I've had to think seriously, for the first time, about how to direct it, and I've decided to split it between two kinds of intervention: programs that relieve or prevent severe suffering (factory farming, treatable painful illness) and programs that create or enable positive experiences (the arts, public spaces, education for its own sake). In working out the split I realized my decision depends on a position I have since learned is called tranquilism: roughly, the view that the only thing of intrinsic moral significance is the absence of suffering and of craving, so that pleasure and happiness matter only insofar as they relieve a discomfort or satisfy a desire, and a state of perfect, craving-free contentment is just as good as a state of bliss, provided both are free of suffering. If tranquilism is true, almost everything should go to the first category; if it is false, the second category deserves a real share.

My own credence that tranquilism is correct is about 0.15. I'd like your number instead, but more than the number I want the decomposition that produces it. Break the question into the sub-questions it actually depends on -- for instance: whether pleasure has intrinsic value beyond desire-satisfaction and relief; whether the felt asymmetry between the badness of suffering and the goodness of happiness reflects the world or merely the salience of pain to introspection; what can legitimately be inferred from experiences of deep, desireless contentment (in meditation, in dreamless sleep, in quiet absorption); and whether the value of a state must be comparable from within experience at all. Assign a rough probability to each, explain how they combine into your final figure, and identify which single sub-question your credence is most sensitive to -- i.e., the one where a reasonable person at 0.15 and you most likely diverge.
