I've been playing Undertale with my teenage nephew, and our argument afterwards surprised me. He had finished the genocide route and found the game's treatment of him there -- Sans confronting him in the Last Corridor, the world emptying out, the sense that the game itself has started to hate him -- genuinely affecting, and he said, half-joking, that he felt he'd done something wrong. I asked: wrong to whom? The monsters are sprites and dialogue trees; nobody suffers when you kill Toriel. He pushed back that the game seems designed to insist otherwise, or at least to make it feel otherwise. Neither of us could articulate what the game is actually claiming.

So I'd like a reading of Undertale through the debate over whether video-game characters can be objects of moral concern at all. On one side, there is the thought that moral standing requires the capacity for experiences -- suffering, preference-satisfaction, a point of view -- and no current NPC has any of that, so wrongdoing in games is at worst a vice of the player (a rehearsal of cruelty, a coarsening of one's dispositions) and never a wrong done to the character. On the other side, there are functionalist and simulationist intuitions the game itself seems to exploit: characters who remember your resets, plead, flee, and hold grudges start to look like they have something like interests, and Flowey -- who retains memories across resets and treats others as disposable precisely because of that -- reads almost like an argument about what moral oblivion does to a person.

What I want is a reading that takes both the game's text and the philosophy seriously: Which of the game's famous moments actually support the claim that the characters matter, and which only support the weaker claim that the player's character matters? Is the genocide route's punishing bleakness an aesthetic argument, a moral argument, or a conflation of the two -- and would the conflation be philosophically defensible? And where does the game resist the reading: is Undertale, in the end, making a point about moral status, or merely about empathy, habituation, and what it does to you to treat even fictitious suffering as entertainment? If the game can't quite decide, say so honestly rather than forcing a verdict.
