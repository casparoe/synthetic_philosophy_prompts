so i've been chewing on the unilateralist's curse -- the thing where if lots of people each have the power to do something that affects everyone, it tends to get done by whoever's most optimistic about it, even when most people would have judged it a bad idea. I want to know what principle my case judgements are actually tracking, because when I lay them out they don't obviously cohere. Here they are:

1. A virologist works out a much cheaper way to synthesize a dangerous pathogen. Journals won't touch it and her institution tells her to drop it, so she posts it on a preprint server, genuinely convinced the biodefense community needs to see it. I think she shouldn't have.

2. After years of stalled international talks, one mid-sized country starts injecting aerosols into the stratosphere to cool the planet, on the strength of its own climate models. Seems clearly wrong to me.

3. An engineer at a chemical plant leaks documents showing the company buried safety failures. Her union rep and most of her coworkers begged her not to -- they figured the plant would just get shut down, everyone fired, and the risk was overblown anyway. She did it anyway. I think she did the right thing.

4. A small team with access to a big transmitter beams a detailed message at a nearby star system without consulting anyone. Irreversible, affects the whole planet, decided by a handful of people -- structurally a lot like case 2. And yet I find I don't really object. This is the one that bugs me.

5. Four of five housemates vote against getting a cat. The fifth, sure everyone will come around once they meet it, brings one home anyway. Wrong, obviously -- but it feels wrong in *the same way* as 1 and 2, which is strange given the stakes are nothing.

6. A developer at an AI lab, convinced that keeping the model closed concentrates too much power, uploads the weights to the internet. This seems structurally identical to case 3 -- one person overriding the collective judgement because she thinks she sees the real stakes -- but my verdict flips: I don't think she should have.

So: is there one principle that delivers all six verdicts? My best guess is something about creating new irreversible risk versus exposing harm that's already there, but that struggles with 5 (no planetary risk from a cat) and maybe with 4. Alternatives I've considered: whether a genuine deliberative process existed and was bypassed; whether the dissenter's private evidence is the kind others could in principle check; whether the people urging caution have a conflict of interest (which might be what separates 3 from 6). Give me your best candidate principle, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save and which one you'd overturn if forced to simplify. Also tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed -- say, a security researcher who publishes an exploit publicly because the vendor has ignored it for a year and she thinks it's the only way to force a patch.
