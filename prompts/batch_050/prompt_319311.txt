At a pub night for my philosophy society, two people got into a real argument about Hume's essay "Of Suicide," specifically the bit where he defends suicide against the charge that it wrongs other people. Roughly, Hume says there that someone who leaves life does society no harm but merely stops doing it good, which if an injury at all is the lowest kind, and that since our obligations to society rest on reciprocal benefit, someone who withdraws from society altogether can't still be bound by them. (That's a paraphrase, not the exact wording.)

Marta said this argument proves way too much: if my duties to society really rest on ongoing reciprocal benefit, then withdrawing would dissolve not just the duty to contribute but duties not to defraud people or trash my promises, which is absurd, so Hume's premise about reciprocity must be wrong. Daniel said she's conflating ceasing to benefit someone with harming them, and the right analogy is emigration -- nobody thinks an emigrant wrongs their home country just by leaving, even though the country educated them. Marta pushed back that the emigration analogy fails because society invests in you before you can consent to anything, and debts already incurred can't be shed by walking away; also plenty of suicidal people are *currently receiving* society's benefits (hospital care, say), so on Hume's own reciprocity logic they'd still be bound. Daniel answered that if unasked-for benefits could saddle you with a binding obligation to stay alive, that conclusion is far worse than anything in Hume, and anyway Hume is only talking about the duty to promote society's good, not all duties whatsoever. Then Marta said fine, but that reading makes Hume a contractarian about obligation, whereas the "only ceases to do good, lowest kind of injury" line reads purely utilitarian, and the two readings come apart in cases -- e.g. someone whose death would save society money. Daniel said they're not rivals, they're complementary: reciprocity shows there's no obligation to stay, the no-harm point shows staying isn't owed even apart from obligation.

I stayed out of it and now I'm curious. Who's right about which specific move here, who's more right overall about what Hume's arguement actually commits him to, and is there something both of them are missing?
