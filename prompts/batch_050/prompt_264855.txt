So I mentor undergrads who are applying to grad programs and jobs, and I keep running into the same argument with a friend who does hiring at a biotech company. He insists meritocracy is simple: give the position to whoever would do it best, full stop. I keep saying that "deserves the job" and "would do the job best" are different claims that can pull apart, and he keeps asking me to give him one clean example where they actually do. Everything I come up with either turns on discrimination stuff he concedes immediately, or collapses into "well they didn't earn their talents anyway," which he thinks proves meritocracy wins by default.

So: construct a thought experiment where the meritocratic principle (the position goes to whoever would perform it best) and the desert principle (the position goes to whoever most deserves it) deliver different verdicts about the same concrete hiring decision. Constraints: it has to be a realistic scenario -- two candidates, one job, nothing sci-fi, no perfect information about the future. The meritocratic verdict has to be genuinely robust, not gerrymandered -- don't rig the case by defining "merit" in some obviously cooked way; the candidate the meritocrat picks should be the one any reasonable hiring committee would rank first on predicted performance. And the desert verdict can't just piggyback on the move "no one deserves their inborn abilities or their upbringing," because that move dissolves desert rather than locating it somewhere else; the candidate who deserves it more has to deserve it more in virtue of something they did. Bonus difficulty if the divergence survives on two different accounts of what the basis of desert is -- say, effort and contribution -- rather than depending on one controversial choice of desert-base.

After the scenario, walk through it: state what each principle says and why, identify exactly which feature of the case is doing the work of prying the two verdicts apart, and say what happens to the case if you remove that feature (does it collapse back into the boring both-pick-the-same-person case?). Then the hard part: give the best response my friend could make -- either that the desert claim in your scenario is actually disguised compassion or need, not desert, or that hiring was never the right venue for desert in the first place -- and say whether you think that response defeats the case or just relocates the disagreement.
