So I'm prepping a session for my philosophy-and-literature seminar (mixed undergrads, they've all read Frankenstein, or at least claim to) and I want to read Mary Shelley's novel through the special composition question -- van Inwagen's thing, i.e. under what conditions do a bunch of objects compose a further single object. I've been sketching the standard menu of answers for them: nihilism (nothing ever composes anything, there are only simples arranged various ways), universalism (any old bunch of things, however scattered, always composes a further thing), and van Inwagen's own answer (things compose something exactly when their activity constitutes a life, so basically only organisms). And it struck me that Frankenstein is weirdly perfect for this and I havent seen anyone lean on it.

Here's what I mean. Victor assembles the creature out of parts taken from charnel houses and dissecting rooms. Before the animation, what is on the slab? On universalism there was already a single object there the moment the parts were gathered -- maybe even before, scattered across different graves. On nihilism there never is a creature, only simples arranged creaturewise, which makes the whole second half of the book, told in the creature's own voice, literally about nothing. On van Inwagen's answer the creature comes into existence exactly at the moment of animation, when the life starts. Which answer does the novel itself seem to presuppose, and does it presuppose a consistent one? Victor clearly treats animation as the decisive event, but the horror of his project depends on the parts having belonged to people, and it's not obvious that wrongness is explicable unless the parts were already, in some sense, more than raw material.

Then there's the scene I most want the reading to grapple with: Victor destroys the half-finished female creature before animating her. What did he actually destroy? On universalism she already existed and he's killed someone; on the organicist answer she never existed and he's only wrecked an object; on nihilism there's no fact of the matter. Does the novel's moral framing of that scene -- the creature's rage, Victor's guilt, the way it triggers everything after -- commit to one of these, and would the scene read differently under each?

A few things I want from the reading: take both sides seriously, don't just use the book as a cute illustration of the metaphysics or use the metaphysics as a fancy vocabulary for plot summary. Say where the SCQ lens genuinely illuminates scenes (the animation, the creature's dawning self-conception while watching the De Laceys, the mate demand) and where it distorts what Shelley is doing -- the book is also obviously about abandonment and responsibility, and I want you to say honestly where the composition reading has to yield to that. End by telling me what would change your verdict: is there some feature of the text or some argument in the composition debate that, if it went differently than you've assumed, would flip your reading? I.e. where is this interpretation most likely to fall apart. No quotes or citations needed, I'll dig those up myself.
