I help run a small political philosophy reading group at my local library -- mostly retirees, a couple of grad students, one very opinionated accountant -- and in three weeks it's my turn to lead. We're doing what you might call the "late Mill problem": how can the author of On Liberty, the great patron saint of individual freedom, end his career writing the Chapters on Socialism (published after his death in 1879) and sounding genuinely sympathetic to socialist ideas, while also listing hard-nosed objections to actually existing socialist schemes? The group read the first chapter of the Chapters for homework and they're already splitting into camps: half say Mill basically stayed a free-market liberal who just felt bad about poverty, half say he became a socialist full stop and the liberty stuff was a young man's phase. I think both readings are wrong, and I want the session to show why, but I don't want to lecture them into submission.

So don't explain Mill's late views to me -- plan how I should explain them, in a ninety-minute discussion where I mostly ask questions. Tell me the order the pieces have to come in: my guess is they first need Mill's distinction (from the Principles) between laws of production and laws of distribution, i.e. that how wealth gets divided is a social choice not a fact of nature, before any of the socialism stuff makes sense -- but maybe something else has to go even earlier? Then I want one carrying example for the whole session: I'm tempted to use a worker-owned co-op competing against a regular wage-paying firm in the same town, since Mill pins a lot on cooperative production as the endpoint rather than state ownership. Does that example do the work, or does it smuggle in something that misleads? Also map the misconceptions for me and say where in the sequence to kill each one: I'm expecting "socialism means the government runs everything" (whereas Mill's objections in the Chapters are largely aimed at exactly that picture), "Mill's worries about incentives and who does the unpleasant work are just him chickening out," and "the stationary state chapter is a seperate topic from the socialism chapter." One of the grad students will definately try to turn it into a Marx session, so tell me where to let that in and where to shut it down.

Finally: give me one question I can pose in the last ten minutes whose answer would tell me the group actually got the point -- that Mill can hold competitive markets and worker cooperation in the same head without contradiction -- rather than just having picked a camp, plus what I should openly not try to settle in ninety minutes (I'm assuming the whole "was he *really* a socialist" labeling fight is a trap, but maybe you disagree).
