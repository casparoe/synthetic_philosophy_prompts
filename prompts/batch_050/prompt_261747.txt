I am preparing a seventy-five-minute undergraduate seminar on the timing of conscious experience for a mixed group of psychology, neuroscience, and philosophy students. The central case is the color phi phenomenon: when two differently colored dots appear briefly in different locations, observers often report seeing one dot move and change color partway along its path, even though the information needed to construct that trajectory becomes available only when the second dot appears. I want to use the case to teach the difference between a Stalinesque account, on which the brain delays and edits perceptual content before it becomes conscious, and an Orwellian account, on which an earlier conscious experience occurs but is immediately displaced by a revised memory or report. Please treat those labels as defined here rather than as an invitation to discuss their intellectual history.

Do not simply explain the phenomenon to me; design the teaching sequence. Specify what must be established first and why, how and when to introduce the animation, what ordinary assumptions about perception, memory, and verbal report must be separated before the philosophical problem becomes intelligible, and how to prevent the discussion from collapsing into the claim that the brain somehow sees the future. Include one counterfactual variation that sharpens the issue, such as what follows if the second dot never appears. Give a misconception map in the order students are likely to develop the misconceptions, indicating exactly where each should be headed off; likely candidates include treating a verbal report as transparent evidence of the experience, conflating neural processing time with experienced time, assuming that an Orwellian account is merely ordinary forgetfulness, assuming that a Stalinesque account requires a long conscious delay, and supposing that introspection alone can decide between the accounts. End with one understanding check that shows whether students grasp the underdetermination rather than merely remember the terminology. The seminar should not take a final position on which account is correct, and it should not require citations or a survey of experiments.

Present the answer as a timed teaching plan followed by a compact table with the columns Phase, Minutes, Conceptual dependency, Instructor move, Misconception addressed, and Success signal.
