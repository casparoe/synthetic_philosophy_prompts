So I'm in a philosophy of biology reading group and we have somehow spent three entire sessions on the propensity interpretation of fitness without getting anywhere. There are two of us who keep butting heads: I defend the idea that fitness is a real propensity (roughly, an organism's dispositional capacity to survive and reproduce in its environment, not just its actual reproductive output), and my friend thinks this is all a comforting fiction — she leans toward the line that fitness-talk is just a statistical redescription of what happened, that selection isn't a cause of anything, and that the old tautology objection ("the fittest survive, and the fittest are just whoever survives") was never really answered, only rebranded. Our group leader got tired of us and told us to do one of those adversarial collaboration things and produce a joint statement. Neither of us knows how to write it, so I'm outsourcing the draft.

Write the joint statement the two of us could both sign. Structure it as: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims — things like what fitness actually predicts in experimental evolution setups, why the naive version of the tautology objection fails, what drift is and how it differs from selection in outcome rather than in kind of process — but only include things both camps genuinely accept, no smuggling; (2) a disagreements table with each disagreement phrased in words that side would actually use about its own view (so my side gets stated as "fitness is a causal property of organisms," not as some strawman about magic vitality); (3) a cruxes section. I suspect our real cruxes are: whether selection explains or merely summarizes trait change in a population, and whether a propensity that can never be measured except through actual reproductive outcomes deserves to be called a propensity at all. For each crux, describe a case both of us would accept as bearing on it — feel free to invent one, like a population where the higher-fitness strain is driven extinct purely by sampling accidents across many replicate lines — and write down, in advance, what each of us predicts that case shows. If part of our fight turns out to be just about words, the statement should say so explicitly and identify which part.

One more thing: the statement should include at least one hypothetical experimental-evolution result that would count as evidence against the propensity view and one that would count against my friend's deflationary view — if you think one side's position is unfalsifiable in this way, the statement should say that out loud, since that's itself a finding. Keep the whole document under 1100 words.
