I am in Year 13 and preparing a talk for our school's philosophy society on Kavka's toxin puzzle: an eccentric billionaire offers you an enormous sum if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon, where drinking it will make you painfully ill for a day but do you no lasting harm. The money is paid once the intention is detected at midnight, whether or not you go on to drink; and since you know in advance that once the money is banked you will have no reason to drink, it seems you cannot rationally form the intention — even though forming it is clearly the rational thing to do. My talk argues that the puzzle is not really about billionaires, prizes, or intention-detecting devices, but about the bare gap between having reason to intend something and having reason to do it. To make that argument, I need a case built to order.

Please construct a variant of the toxin puzzle meeting all three of the following constraints:

1. No third party and no reward. The benefit of forming the intention must flow from the intention itself — from what having the intention does to or for the agent — rather than from anyone detecting, rewarding, threatening, or relying upon it.

2. A fully rational and fully informed agent. No self-deception, wishful thinking, induced forgetfulness, or deliberately cultivated irrationality may do any work; the agent knows at every stage exactly what they will and will not have reason to do later.

3. The temporal structure preserved. The benefit of intending must be secured at the time of intending, the cost must fall due later, and the agent must know in advance that when the cost comes due they will have no reason to pay it — and that nothing will undo the benefit if they decline to pay it.

After the scenario, please:

- show, constraint by constraint, that the case meets each of the three conditions rather than merely stipulating that it does;
- explain how the puzzle re-emerges: why it still seems that the agent both ought to form the intention and cannot rationally form it;
- identify which feature of Kavka's original set-up your case has discarded, say what this shows about where the real source of the puzzle lies, and whether reinstating that feature (adding an external offerer back into your case) changes anything;
- and give the strongest objection available to someone who insists that your mechanism only works upon an irrational agent — for instance, if the benefit is something like a placebo or confidence effect that a clear-headed person could not enjoy — together with your reply.

Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph, realistic rather than science-fictional: no mind-reading machines, no drugs that directly induce intentions, nothing a careful doctor or psychologist would dismiss out of hand.
