So here's the deal. I teach an early modern survey every other year and I've gotten tired of my own lecture on Montaigne's "Of Cannibals" (I use the old Cotton translation, it's fine). The lecture does the usual thing: half the class walks out thinking Montaigne is a straightforward cultural relativist, the other half notices he turns around and calls Europeans worse than the cannibals and wonders how a real relativist gets to say that. Both halves have a point, and honestly I think both readings are missing what the essay is actually doing.

Here's the passage that got me stuck on this. It's the very opening, before the cannibals even show up — Montaigne telling the story of King Pyrrhus eyeballing the Roman army:

When King Pyrrhus invaded Italy, having viewed and considered the order of the army the Romans sent out to meet him; "I know not," said he, "what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations) "these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it."—As much said the Greeks of that which Flaminius brought into their country; and Philip, beholding from an eminence the order and distribution of the Roman camp formed in his kingdom by Publius Sulpicius Galba, spake to the same effect. By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report.

What bugs me: the standard standoff assumes the essay's whole point is a verdict (either "everyone calls barbarian whatever isn't their custom, so suspend judgment" or "look, he does judge, so he's a secret universalist about cruelty"). But this opening isn't a verdict about anyone. It's a methodological promise — don't take things on trust from common report, judge by the eye of reason. So my half-baked idea is that the relativist/universalist fight is a fight about Montaigne's conclusions when the interesting question is about his procedure: what is "the eye of reason" actually supposed to be here? Is it a faculty that delivers cross-cultural moral verdicts (in which case which ones, and how does that square with the skepticism elsewhere)? Is it more like a debiasing routine — a discipline of noticing when your judgments are just inherited report — that could in principle be run by anyone from any culture without smuggling in a universal moral standard? Or is it just a rhetorical costume he's wearing for the Pyrrhus anecdote and abandons by the middle of the essay? I genuinely don't know, and I think the answer changes how you read the famous comparisons later on (the prisoner stuff, the valor stuff).
