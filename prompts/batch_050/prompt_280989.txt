At a rationality meetup last weekend two friends of mine got into a long unresolved argument about the death penalty and I want a referee. Background: Dan had been reading Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (the 1764 treatise) and brought him up. Rough transcript:

(1) Dan: Beccaria basically settled this 250 years ago, and his core argument is about deterrence -- lifelong penal servitude deters better than execution because it's a lasting spectacle rather than a brief one, and deterrence is the only thing that legitimates punishment in the first place. So the death penalty fails even on its proponents' own terms.

(2) Maya: you've misread him. Beccaria's deepest move is the social contract one: nobody entering society could have handed the state the right over their own life, so the state simply lacks the authority to execute anyone, full stop. That argument doesn't lean on deterrence at all, which is exactly why it survives even if executions turn out to deter marginally better than life imprisonment.

(3) Dan: the contract argument proves way too much. Nobody would "rationally consent" to life imprisonment either, so by that logic Beccaria abolishes punishment wholesale, and neither of us believes that. The deterrence argument is the only part that does real work, which means the actual crux is the modern deterrence literature, which is, to put it kindly, a mess.

(4) Maya: no, the contract argument handles that fine -- you can consent to limited, reversible losses of liberty as the price of security, but not to your own destruction, because then there's no you left to enjoy the security you bought. And we're both ignoring the innocent-execution problem, which is an epistemic argument, not a deterrence or a rights argument, and it's the one cost you can't compensate anyone for ex post.

Go through the four moves and say who's right at each step, with reasons. Specifically: on the exegetical question in (1) vs (2), what is Beccaria actually committed to, and does it even matter for policy who's right about him? Does Dan's reductio in (3) land against the contract reading, and does Maya's revocability fix in (4) actually hold up or is it ad hoc? And is the innocence point really orthogonal to the other two arguments, or does it interact with them in a way neither of them noticed?
