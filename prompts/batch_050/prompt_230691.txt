I'm teaching a course on aesthetics and authenticity, and I want to screen Orson Welles' F for Fake (1973) as the centerpiece. Read the film through the philosophy of forgery and the value of originals. The film is a puzzle for that literature: Welles devotes much of it to Elmyr de Hory, a forger whose fake Modiglianis hung in museums undetected, and to Clifford Irving, who wrote a book celebrating de Hory and was then exposed for faking Howard Hughes's autobiography — yet the film's most famous passage has Welles admiring Chartres cathedral as a great unsigned work, made by people who left no names, which seems to argue that authorship and provenance don't matter to value at all. Meanwhile the film itself cheats: Welles tells the audience that everything shown is true, and then reveals near the end that an extended story about Picasso was his own fabrication.

Here are the questions I want worked through. First: does the film take a position on the standard debate — roughly, whether a perfect fake loses aesthetic value once exposed (Goodman's worry that authenticity is itself an aesthetic property) or whether only the object's perceptible qualities matter, with our indignation at fakes being a confusion of aesthetics with snobbery or investment value? The de Hory material pushes one way, Chartres pushes another, and I want to know whether that's a contradiction, a deliberate tension, or a coherent third view. Second: how should we understand the film's own lie? When Welles confesses the Picasso story was invented, is the earlier footage retroactively devalued the way an exposed forgery is — that is, does the film perform on the audience the exact experiment the philosophers only describe? And does Welles, a lifelong stage magician who treats filmmaking as trickery, have a consistent answer to why his lie is charming while Irving's was criminal? Third: what does the film show that the philosophical literature tends to miss — I'm thinking of the role of the experts, dealers, and biographers who authenticate fakes and are made fools of, which suggests the value of originals may rest on institutions rather than on the object.

Please give me a prose reading of a few pages, faithful in both directions: don't flatten the film into an illustration, and don't bend the philosophy to fit it — where the film resists the reading or Welles is simply being evasive, say so. Then end with a table summarizing the reading, with columns: Film sequence; Philosophical claim or concept it dramatizes; Direction of support (for/against authenticity's aesthetic value, or ambiguous); Where the film undercuts or complicates the claim.
