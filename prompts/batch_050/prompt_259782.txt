I'm writing something for a philosophy of mind reading group and want a proper reading to push against, so bear with me if this is a bit in the weeds. Read A Clockwork Orange -- I'm mainly thinking of Kubrick's film, but bring in Burgess's novel where the two diverge in a way that matters (the ending especially) -- through Dennett's attack on qualia, specifically the Chase and Sanborn thought experiment from his essay Quining Qualia: the two coffee tasters, one who insists the coffee tastes exactly as it always did but that he's lost his taste for it, the other who says the taste itself has changed, and Dennett's claim that there's no fact of the matter about which of them is right, which is supposed to show that qualia understood as ineffable, intrinsic, directly apprehensible properties are a confused posit.

The Ludovico technique looks to me like this dramatised. After the conditioning, Alex can't so much as contemplate violence, or hear Beethoven's Ninth, without crippling nausea. On a Chase-style reading, the Ninth still sounds precisely the same to him -- the auditory phenomenology is untouched -- and what's changed is only his reactive attitudes and desires. On a Sanborn-style reading, the experience itself has altered; the music literally sounds different now. The film never settles this, and arguably couldn't, since all we're ever given is behaviour, expression, and Alex's say-so -- and his say-so is exactly the sort of thing Dennett thinks can't bear the weight. Same again at the end, when the treatment is reversed after the scandal and Alex is restored to himself: reversal of what, exactly?

So, three things. First: is the film best read as Dennett's point made flesh -- that once all the behavioural and reactive facts are in, there simply is no further question about what happened to Alex's qualia? Second: or does the film quietly presuppose the realist picture it seems to illustrate the falsity of, since the horror of the treatment seems to depend on there being an answer to what it's like for Alex, and the chaplain's objection that the state has destroyed Alex's capacity for choice rather than made him good seems to assume something intrinsic survived (or was violated by) the conditioning? Third, where does the analogy break down: is the Ludovico technique really a clean Chase/Sanborn case, given that aversion conditioning rewires behavioural dispositions wholesale in a way a gradual drift in coffee preferences arguably doesn't?

A couple of housekeeping requests. Flag anywhere I've stretched the film or Dennett to make them fit. And please seperate what's fairly settled here -- e.g. that no amount of armchair reflection adjudicates Chase against Sanborn, which is close to common ground -- from what's genuinely contested, namely whether that undecidability shows qualia are an incoherent posit, which plenty of working philosophers of mind flatly deny. Don't present eliminativism about qualia as the consensus just because it's the lens I handed you.
