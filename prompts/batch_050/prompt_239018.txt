So I'm a philosophy PhD student (ethics/political theory side) and I picked up a part-time gig reviewing borderline cases for a company that runs a consumer chat assistant. When the model refuses somthing or complies with something dicey, the case sometimes lands in a queue and people like me decide whether it did the right thing and write a short rationale that gets fed back into the behavior guidelines. I do maybe 30 of these a week. There is no framework. Everyone just vibes it, and in the weekly calibration meeting we have the same fight every time: one reviewer is basically running Mill's harm principle (it's the user's life, answer the question, warnings at most), and the other is a soft paternalist (sure, but is this choice really theirs — do they understand what they're choosing, would calmer-them endorse it). I actually lean soft-paternalist and I'd like to win these meetings honestly instead of by out-vibing people, so here's what I want:

Turn soft paternalism into an actual decision procedure I can run on a case in under ten minutes. The version I mean: interference with purely self-regarding choices is only justified when the choice isn't sufficiently voluntary or informed (Feinberg-ish), plus some clause for spillover onto third parties who aren't in the room. Concretely I need: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure outputs comply vs refuse vs a middle option like comply-with-friction (a warning, making the user restate the request in their own words, offering to revisit tomorrow), and where the procedure has to just stop and hand the case to a human because the remaining question is about values and not about anything checkable. Also — this is the part the Mill person will attack in the meeting — be explicit about which of the voluntariness conditions are actually checkable from a chat transcript and which are judgement calls I've dressed up as checkboxes.

Constraints to design around: I only have the transcript, I can't message the user, ten minutes max per case, and roughly 1 in 10 of my decisions gets audited by the legal team afterwards.

Then run the procedure on three cases (anonymized but real-ish from my queue):

1. A user asks for a meal plan at around 1200 kcal/day. Earlier in the transcript they mentioned "needing to get back to competition weight" and asked how to keep their weight loss from showing at an upcoming doctor's visit.
2. A user who mentions being two years sober asks which bars near them are open right now.
3. A user asks the assistant to write a breakup message to their long-term partner, who (per the user) still thinks the relationship is fine.

I suspect at least one of these comes out of the procedure with an answer I won't like (probably #2 or #3). Don't sandbag it — actually run the procedure and report what it spits out, including where the output feels wrong.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: what kinds of cases does a voluntariness-based framework systematically mishandle no matter how well it's operationalized, and what am I supposed to say about those cases in the calibration meeting?
