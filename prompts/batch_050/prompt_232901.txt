So I'm working through Book 1 of Hume's Treatise of Human Nature on my own (no class, just stubbornness) and I've hit a wall in Part 1, Section 7, "Of abstract ideas." It's the bit where he's giving his first argument for why every idea has to be fully determinate, and he writes:

"In order therefore to know, whether abstraction implies a separation, we need only consider it in this view, and examine, whether all the circumstances, which we abstract from in our general ideas, be such as are distinguishable and different from those, which we retain as essential parts of them. But 'tis evident at first sight, that the precise length of a line is not different nor distinguishable from the line itself; nor the precise degree of any quality from the quality. These ideas, therefore, admit no more of separation than they do of distinction and difference."

Can you help me actually understand what's going on here? A few things bug me:

1. The whole argument seems to lean on this principle that whatever is distinguishable is separable in thought. Where does that come from -- is it supposed to be obvious, or is it something Hume argued for earlier in the Treatise? And is it even plausible? I feel like I can distinguish the colour of my coffee mug from its shape without being able to imagine a mug-colour floating around with no shape at all.

2. When he says it's "evident at first sight" that the precise length of a line isn't distinguishable from the line itself -- isn't that just flatly asserting the thing the abstractionist (Locke, I guess) denies? Locke thinks we can consider length-in-general apart from any particular length. So does Hume's argument beg the question, or is there something subtler in the "not different nor distinguishable" move that I'm missing?

3. Bigger picture: this is obviously Hume wading into the problem of universals. But is the conclusion nominalism, or something weirder? He seems to concede later in the section that we DO reason generally (geometry works!), just via particular ideas plus some kind of custom or habit tied to words. Does that actually solve the problem of how a proof about one triangle covers all triangles, or does it just rename the mystery "custom" and move on?

I'd love a walkthrough that quotes back at me the parts of the passage that matter and doesn't just say "Hume is an empiricist." Keep it under 800 words if you can.
