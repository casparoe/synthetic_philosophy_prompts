I sit on the boards of two small organizations and have helped run a couple more, and over the years I have noticed a pattern I cannot find a word for. A community choir, a tenants' union, and a small game studio I was involved with all started out with completely different purposes, and within about three years all three had converged on nearly the same furniture: a bank account requiring two signatories, a chair and a treasurer, written minutes, liability insurance, a part-time administrator, a social media presence, and an annual general meeting that mostly exists to re-elect the people running it. The striking thing is that this did not happen because these things served each group's goals particularly well -- for the tenants' union the formal structure arguably slowed down exactly the rapid response it was founded for. It happened because the outside world only has slots for certain shapes: the bank would not open an account for an unincorporated group, the venue required an insurance certificate, the grant required audited accounts, the platform required a named responsible person. One group I know was founded explicitly to avoid all bureaucracy and ended up with a constitution anyway, because the landlord would not sign a lease with anything else.

I initially thought this was an instance of what the AI risk literature calls instrumental convergence: the claim that agents with very different final goals will nonetheless pursue similar intermediate goals, like self-preservation and resource acquisition, because those are useful for almost anything. But the more I look at it, the less it fits. Instrumental convergence, as I understand it, is derived from the inside -- the agent reasons that certain subgoals serve its ends, whatever those ends are. What I am seeing seems to come from the outside: the organizations do not choose the shape because it is useful, they are pressed into it because every system they must deal with was built to interact with that shape and no other. The ends stay different; the means are not even freely chosen. It is also not quite the cynical observation that staff eventually run things for their own benefit, since in my cases the structure is genuinely unwanted by everyone involved.

So my question is threefold. First, does this externally forced convergence of organizational form have an established name, and is there a literature on it? Second, how exactly does it differ from instrumental convergence proper -- am I right that the direction of derivation (inside-out versus outside-in) is the essential difference, or is there a better way to carve it? Third, if there is no established name, please coin one, define it precisely, and give me some boundary cases: would a colony of different ant species evolving similar caste systems count, or only cases involving institutions? Please also say whether the distinction matters for the AI safety debate itself -- that is, whether we should expect advanced AI systems to converge on familiar subgoals for the internal reasons the orthogonality-based arguments give, or whether much of the convergence we will actually observe will be of my external kind, because any AI deployed in our world has to plug into banks, courts, and platforms built for a certain shape of actor. Write your answer as continuous prose, like a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
