I am a retired chemist who has taken up philosophy in earnest over the past two years, and I keep circling back to a puzzle about how societies handle atrocity. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in political philosophy and the theory of punishment, and to think carefully rather than give me the standard encyclopedia summary. Before you settle on your main answer, please brainstorm at least three distinct candidate presuppositions the practice might rest on, note them briefly, and then develop the one or two you find most defensible.

The practice I have in mind is the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission's amnesty arrangement, as I understand it. Rather than prosecuting the perpetrators of apartheid-era political violence, the Commission offered individualized amnesty: a perpetrator could apply for amnesty for specific acts, and would receive it on condition that the acts had a political objective and that he made full disclosure of what he had done. Amnesty was granted act by act, person by person, not in blanket form. Meanwhile, victims testified publicly about what they had suffered, and the Commission treated the establishment of a truthful public record as itself a central achievement, alongside -- and in some sense in place of -- criminal punishment. Argentina, by contrast, eventually moved toward prosecuting its junta-era offenders, and Rwanda used community-based proceedings that mixed confession, reduced sentences, and reintegration.

Here is what I want reconstructed. First, what does the conditional structure of the South African scheme -- truth traded for impunity -- presuppose about the relationship between truth and punishment as responses to wrongdoing? Does the scheme only make sense if a truthful public account is a genuine substitute for retribution, a kind of partial payment of the same debt, or can a strictly retributivist about desert also endorse the trade on purely instrumental grounds without conceding anything about what punishment is for? I am genuinely unsure whether the practice presupposes a theory of justice or merely a prudential compromise, and I would like you to distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with.

Second, the individualized, application-by-application character of the amnesty seems to me to presuppose something about the nature of responsibility -- namely, that guilt for systemic violence can be cleanly decomposed into the acts of individual agents. Is that right? If apartheid's wrongs were in part structural and collective, does the Commission's design commit it to an atomistic picture of responsibility that sits awkwardly with its own findings about a system of oppression?

Third, please say whether the South African and Argentine approaches presuppose genuinely incompatible views of what a society owes victims, or whether they could be licensed by the same underlying theory applied under different empirical circumstances. If a single theory could license both, state that theory precisely enough that I can see where the empirical premises do the work.

Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- where several rival theories would license it equally well -- please say so plainly rather than forcing a single reading.
