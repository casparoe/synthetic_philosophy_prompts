I've been trying to work out, from cases, when it is and isn't wrong to bring a person into existence, and I want to know what principle my judgments actually add up to.

A couple conceives a child knowing it will inherit a disease that causes decades of severe pain: clearly wrong. The same couple conceives knowing only that the child will endure one week of intense pain within an otherwise wonderful life: fine, I think. A couple conceives an ordinary child with an ordinary mix of joy and hardship: fine. A couple decides not to have children at all: no one is wronged, not even slightly, since there's no one there to be deprived. A scientist creates a person whose life is unrelenting misery except for one moment of happiness: monstrous. A scientist creates a person whose life is blissful throughout and who then painlessly ceases to exist: admirable, maybe, but skipping it wrongs no one.

So far there's a pattern: the suffering of people who will exist counts against creating them, but the forgone happiness of people who never exist doesn't count against not creating them. The trouble is one more case. A couple conceives knowing the child will live a genuinely wonderful life and then die slowly and painfully at the end. I keep wanting to call this worse than the one-week case even if the overall balance of good over bad is much larger — which suggests I don't actually think the total balance settles it. Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts, or must I drop one? And what does the resulting principle say about the case I keep circling: conceiving a child whose life will be good for fifty years and then, due to foreseeable environmental collapse, grim but barely worth living for twenty more?
