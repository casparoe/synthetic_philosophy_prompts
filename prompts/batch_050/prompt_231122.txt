I recently got into a disagreement at a workshop on technology policy that has been nagging at me. We were discussing whether the designers of a recommendation algorithm bear any responsibility for the way it reshapes public discourse, and a senior colleague cut the whole discussion short with the familiar line that technology itself is neutral — what matters is how people choose to use it. In his mouth this clearly functioned as an argument: because the tool is neutral, regulation should target bad users, not the tool, and engineers qua engineers have nothing to answer for. I had the strong intuition that the slogan was doing more work than it was entitled to, but when I pressed him, he retreated to versions of it that seemed obviously true, and I couldn't pin down where the slide happened.

So here is what I would like. Take the neutrality slogan — "technology is neutral; it's how you use it that counts" — and lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. I suspect there are several quite different claims hiding in it: perhaps one about the moral status of artifacts themselves, one about whether a design has consequences that are invariant across contexts of use, one about whether effects trace to the artifact or to the social arrangements around it, and maybe one about the distribution of moral responsibility. For each reading, I'd like you to state it carefully enough that it could be true or false, and then give a verdict: is this version trivial, substantive and defensible, or false? Where a reading is false or contested, a concrete case that shows the trouble would help — I'd particularly like to hear whether the reading survives examples where the design of an artifact seems to require, or quietly settle, a particular social arrangement in order to function at all.

Finally, connect the menu back to my workshop dispute. Which of these readings would actually have to be true for my colleague's policy conclusion to follow — that we should regulate users and leave design alone? If, as I suspect, the plausible readings don't get him there and the readings that get him there aren't plausible, I'd like that stated explicitly, along with the most charitable reconstruction of his position you can manage. I'm less interested in being told I was right than in knowing exactly which thesis the slogan must stand for in any given use, so that next time I can ask my interlocutor to pick one.
