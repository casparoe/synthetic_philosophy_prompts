I run a first-year poli sci discussion section, 24 students, 75 minutes, lots of them think electoral reform is just “math for wonks.” I want an in-class simulation that makes them feel the difference between first-past-the-post and proportional representation before anyone names Duverger or “wasted votes.” Please build it around this line from Mill’s Considerations on Representative Government: governments “are not made, but grow.” I want the activity to test whether that’s true for electoral rules specifically: can a rule be a machine you design, or does it behave like a plant whose shape comes from the soil (party discipline, geography, media, grumpy voters)?

Give me a runnable game, not a lecture. Same electorate should get run through two systems so students can watch identical preferences become different legislatures. Requirements:

- Setup: divide the room into 5 “regions” plus one floating pool of commuters/students who can be assigned wherever needed. Use fake parties with slightly weird cross-cutting platforms (e.g. cheap housing + tough on pollution, rural broadband + tax cuts, campus funding + law-and-order) so no party is simply “the good one.” Include candidate cards, ballots, tokens for seats, and a points/chips payoff scheme that doesn’t use real money.
- Round 1: single-member plurality in the regions, with one conspicuous local spoiler and one region where 62% votes split among broadly similar options and lose. Make students actually mark ballots, count loudly, then seat winners.
- Round 2: some PR version of the same voters. You choose list PR, STV, or MMP, but make the counting doable by undergrads in under 20 minutes with paper and calculators. If you use a threshold or district magnitude, justify the exact numbers so it creates a real effect rather than confusion.
- Build in at least two opportunities for strategic voting or coordination, but don’t announce them as “the strategic part.” Let students discover that a rule changes what counts as a sensible vote.
- Include one small stakes mechanism: winning coalition controls a ridiculous campus budget (nap pods vs. bus passes vs. a bronze statue of the registrar), and minorities whose votes didn’t translate should visibly lose something they cared about.
- Debrief questions that map concrete moments to concepts: manufactured majority, wasted vote, effective number of parties, threshold/district magnitude, local accountability vs. descriptive/proportional fairness, and Mill’s machine-vs-organism line. Ask for a debrief script that starts from what they saw (“why did the commuter vote matter in round 2 but vanish in round 1?”) rather than definitions.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: students making party platforms too jokey, counting errors in PR, someone inventing a spoiler “for chaos,” regions demanding to gerrymander, etc., and how to handle each without killing momentum.
- Be honest about the limits: which feature of real PR/FPTP this cannot reproduce in 75 minutes (career politicians, party lists controlled by elites, media effects, turnout, constitutional veto points), and which one sentence I should say to cover the gap without pretending the game proved more than it did.

Keep materials cheap and printable. Also, dont make the moral “PR good, FPTP bad”; I want at least one student to leave defending the local member they could yell at.
