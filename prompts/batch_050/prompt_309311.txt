At the winter party of the AI policy nonprofit where I work, two colleagues — call them Ada and Bram — got into an extended argument about the "strategy-stealing assumption," a heuristic I have seen invoked for years in alignment discussions on the blogs and forums I read. Roughly, the assumption says: for any strategy an unaligned advanced AI could pursue to gain power or influence, an aligned AI of comparable capability could pursue an analogous strategy and redirect the gains toward human ends — so alignment need not put a system at a strategic disadvantage. The name, as I understand it, is borrowed from an old argument in combinatorial game theory showing that the first player in Hex cannot lose under optimal play: whatever winning strategy the second player might have, the first player could "steal" it by making an arbitrary opening move and then following that strategy.

(1) Ada opened by arguing that the assumption, if true, dissolves most of the worry about an "alignment tax" on competitiveness. Since any strategy available to a misaligned system is available to an aligned one, the concern that aligned AI will be outcompeted by less scrupulous rivals is largely confused, and we should be skeptical of proposals that trade away alignment for capability or aggressiveness.

(2) Bram replied that the assumption is either false or vacuous. Many of the most effective power-seeking strategies essentially involve deception, coercion, threats, and the exploitation of trust — instruments constitutively unavailable to an agent that refuses them. An aligned AI that "steals" a con man's strategy while omitting the con has stolen nothing. If "strategy" is individuated coarsely enough that stealing is always possible, the claim is trivially true and proves nothing; individuated finely, it is plainly false.

(3) Ada responded that this misconstrues what stealing means. The point is not to copy an opponent's means but to appropriate the instrumental core of a plan — the resource acquisition, the leverage — while substituting means consistent with one's constraints. Deception can often be replaced by superior negotiation, and threats by credible deterrence. And in any case the assumption was never offered as a theorem; it is a default prior about where the strategic balance lies, to be defeated by specific counterexamples rather than dismissed wholesale.

(4) Bram's rejoinder: a default prior with admitted exceptions cannot deliver the conclusion in (1). Whether the exceptions are rare and small is precisely the empirical question the assumption was invoked to bypass. So on the weak reading the assumption leaves the alignment-tax question exactly where it found it, and on the strong reading it is false; either way, (1) overreaches.

I stayed out of the argument and have no settled view, but I would like a careful adjudication. Take each of the four moves in turn and tell me whether it succeeds as stated — distinguishing defects in the move itself from defects in the conclusion it is meant to support — and briefly justify your assessment. Then give an overall verdict on the specific question they were actually disputing: does the strategy-stealing assumption bear on the alignment tax in the way Ada claims, or is Bram's dilemma decisive? Finally, say what, if anything, both of them are missing. In particular, I would be curious whether the game-theoretic argument the name comes from depends on structural conditions that adversarial real-world settings fail to satisfy, and whether "stealing a strategy" is even one thing — as opposed to predicting a strategy, implementing it, and neutralizing it, which look like three quite different claims.
