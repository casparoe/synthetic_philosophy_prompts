My grandmother is in mid-stage dementia and, honestly, she's pretty content most days — she likes her routines, the bird feeder outside her window, her music. But twenty years ago she signed a very detailed advance directive saying that if she ever ended up "like this" (her words), she wanted no antibiotics, no feeding tube, nothing beyond comfort care. My family is split, and I've been reading around the ethics literature on precedent autonomy and it's made me more radical, not less: I've come to think that the directive shouldn't bind her care at all, because authority over a life belongs to whoever is currently living it, and a directive can only speak for a person who would still endorse the values behind it. Once the person has lost the ability to even grasp those values, let alone endorse them, enforcing the directive is really just enforcing the preferences of a stranger (the past self) over the interests of the person actually in the bed. That's the position I want argued.

Please argue that advance directives lose their moral authority whenever the later self has undergone what you might call a collapse of the valuing perspective — not a mere change of preferences, but a loss of the capacity to understand or endorse the values the directive expresses.
