For the final week of my graduate seminar in formal epistemology, I want my students to see the central dispute about imprecise credences argued rather than summarized, and I intend to give them a model essay. Write an essay of roughly 1,000 words defending a definite verdict on the question whether rational credence may be imprecise — that is, whether an agent may permissibly decline to assign a single number to a proposition when her evidence is sparse, conflicting, or of uncertain provenance, holding instead a range of values.

Anchor the discussion in the following case, which I have used in class. A hospital committee must decide whether to adopt a new anticoagulant. One trial, run on patients closely resembling the hospital's population, found that 62 of 100 improved. A second, larger trial used a protocol the committee cannot fully reconstruct, and the hospital's statistician can determine only that the true pooled success rate lies somewhere between 0.55 and 0.80. A junior committee member proposes splitting the difference and treating the drug as if its success rate were the midpoint; her colleague insists that the honest state of belief is the whole interval and that collapsing it conceals genuine ignorance.

The essay should engage the two strongest objections to the colleague's position. First, dilation: conditioning an imprecise state on evidence that appears evidentially irrelevant to the drug's efficacy can widen the interval, apparently leaving the agent less confident for no good reason — explain why this is supposed to be an embarrassment. Second, action guidance: precise credences feed directly into expected-utility reasoning, whereas interval credences seem either to leave the committee's choice underdetermined or to smuggle precision back in through an arbitrary tie-breaking rule. You may also consider accuracy-based and betting arguments for precision, and Ellsberg-style intuitions on the other side.

Do not end on a balanced non-verdict. Conclude either that imprecise credence is a rationally legitimate response to impoverished evidence despite these objections, or that rationality ultimately demands a point value and the interval is at best a heuristic — and argue for whichever you choose. Write in continuous prose throughout: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables; the essay should read as a letter to a colleague would, in unbroken paragraphs.
