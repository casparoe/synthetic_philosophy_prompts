I am a novelist, and next month I am to give the opening address at a university colloquium on self-knowledge. My thesis is unfashionable there: that the novelist's daily craft — constructing characters whose stated reasons diverge from their real motives, and trusting readers to feel the difference — presupposes that introspection, though fallible, is not the systematic fiction the experimental literature suggests, and that literary practice constitutes a kind of long-run, informal evidence for its qualified reliability. The audience is roughly forty experimental psychologists, most of whom take the confabulation and choice-blindness results to have settled the matter against introspection, plus a handful of philosophers of mind sympathetic to phenomenology who will nonetheless press me on whether 'it rings true on the page' is evidence of anything at all. Prepare me for the question period: the six or seven objections I will actually receive, ordered from most certain to most dangerous, each phrased as a psychologist in that room would phrase it; for each, what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in under two minutes. Identify the weakest joint in my own argument — the place where my analogy between fictional craft and first-person authority is most likely to snap — and tell me how to defend it rather than hope it goes unnoticed. Finally, give me the question I am privately hoping no one asks.
