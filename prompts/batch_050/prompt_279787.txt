I've been asked to circulate my jurisprudential profile before a reading group, and I'd like a consistency audit first. I hold all six of the following, and I want to know whether they actually hang together. (a) Exclusive legal positivism of the Razian sort: the existence and content of law is wholly fixed by social sources, so identifying what the law is never requires moral argument. (b) It is a conceptual truth about law that it claims legitimate moral authority over its subjects. (c) Hart was right against Fuller: the desiderata of legality — publicity, prospectivity, clarity, and so on — are conditions of effective guidance, techniques that serve evil purposes as well as good ones, and are in themselves morally neutral. (d) Constitutional clauses framed in moral language, like bans on cruel punishment or guarantees of equal protection, incorporate moral standards into the law itself: whether a statute comports with them turns on the moral facts, and the standard was part of existing law rather than a mere instruction to judges to legislate. (e) In the grudge-informer cases, the post-war courts were right to refuse enforcement of the Nazi-era statutes, but Hart's description was the correct one: they made new law retroactively rather than discovering that the statutes had never been law. (f) Governing through clear, public, prospective rules is, just in itself, morally better than governing by decree and terror, even when the regime's ends are wicked. Please identify which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; rank the tensions by severity; and for each, say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be, ending with the single change that would clean up the most.
