A colleague in epistemology has given me her verdicts on a series of knowledge cases, all involving the same evidence: remembering having been at the bank two Saturdays ago. She says that Hannah, for whom nothing much turns on whether the bank is open this Saturday, knows that it will be open; that Sarah, who faces financial ruin if it is not open, does not know and ought to step inside to check; that the holder of a one-in-a-thousand lottery ticket does not know his ticket will lose; and that someone whose stakes suddenly rise mid-conversation — say, on learning that the envelope in his hand is the mortgage payment — can cease to know something he knew minutes earlier, with no change in his evidence.

The new case: Leo is in Sarah's exact situation, with the same evidence and the same ruinous stakes. Maria, an acquaintance who knows nothing of Leo's situation and is chatting in a relaxed, low-stakes setting, says: Leo doesn't know yet whether the bank will be open — he just remembers being there a couple of weeks back. Predict my colleague's verdict on whether Maria's utterance is true.

In doing so, set out the candidate rules her earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with — at minimum, contextualism about "knows," subject-sensitive invariantism (pragmatic encroachment), and any further views that fit — state what each rule predicts about Maria's utterance, explain which prediction you consider most likely for her and why, and describe the single further case you would put to her to discriminate among the rules that survive.

Lead with your predicted verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the case.
