A few years ago I came across the Skokie affair of 1977–78 and have been turning it over in my mind ever since. The bare facts, as I understand them: a small American neo-Nazi group announced its intention to march in Skokie, Illinois, a village that was home to a large number of Holocaust survivors; the village tried to block the march through a court injunction and ordinances requiring permits, insurance, and a ban on the display of Nazi symbols; the ACLU defended the group's right to march and lost tens of thousands of members over it; the courts sided with the marchers; and in the end the group never marched in Skokie at all, holding a small rally in Chicago instead. I have no legal training, but this strikes me as an almost laboratory-clean collision between the two great transatlantic approaches to hate speech: the American First Amendment tradition, with its suspicion of viewpoint discrimination and its tendency to count as legally relevant harm only something close to imminent violence, and the European approach, which puts human dignity at the foundation, treats certain forms of expression as attacks on the equal standing of citizens, and in its 'militant democracy' strand holds that a democracy need not extend its protections to those openly committed to abolishing it.

What I'd like is an analysis that runs in both directions. First the mapping, done carefully: what does each framework say the situation actually was — who was threatened, with what, and who had the standing to act? What would each framework say the village, the courts, and the ACLU should have done, and does either framework produce a verdict its own defenders would be embarrassed to state out loud for this case (the American one seeming to say that a village of survivors had no legitimate grievance worth the name; the European one seemingly committed to the courts deciding which political symbols a town may be shown)? Second, the stress test. It seems to me the episode contains at least three quite different candidate harms, and I want to know which framework can even register each: the pain of knowing such views exist at all, the distinct pain of being confronted with them in the streets of one's own adopted home, and the longer-run political risk that tolerating the display normalizes the movement. My amateur suspicion is that the American framework can only process the second of these by redescribing it as either offense (which it discounts) or incitement (which it wasn't), and that the European framework can only process the American worry — that censorship powers get abused — as a failure of nerve. Tell me whether that suspicion survives scrutiny.

Finally, what does the episode, as it actually unfolded, show about each theory? The march never happened, the movement collapsed into irrelevance, and the affair is now often told as the First Amendment's finest hour — the marketplace worked, sunlight disinfected. But I wonder whether that happy ending is doing illicit work: would the American verdict still be right in the counterfactual where the march happened and the movement grew? And conversely, is there anything in the episode that should make a defender of the European model uncomfortable — for instance, that the attempt at suppression generated far more publicity for the group than the march itself ever would have? I'd like the analysis to end with an honest verdict: which framework describes this case better, and what is the strongest version of the losing side's reply?
