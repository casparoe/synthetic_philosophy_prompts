I spent my working life as a physical chemist, and since retiring I have been making my way through moral philosophy and the theory of value with the enthusiasm and, I suspect, the naivety of a newcomer. One issue I keep returning to is the transitivity of "better than, all things considered." My professional instincts rebel against giving it up: in the laboratory, an ordering that ran in circles would have been a sign of a faulty instrument or an inconsistent measurement protocol, not a discovery about the quantity being measured. I have now read enough to know about spectrum arguments -- sequences of cases in which each successive item seems clearly better (or clearly not worse) than its predecessor, while the endpoints of the sequence appear to stand in the opposite ranking. A series of lives, each a little longer and a little worse in quality than the last, where each adjacent trade seems favourable but where one would not dream of preferring a near-endless life barely worth living to a short and splendid one; or a series of headaches, each marginally milder than the last but one more in number, where the same structure appears. My settled position is that these arguments fail: all-things-considered betterness is transitive, and the appearance of intransitivity always dissolves on inspection -- either the comparisons smuggle in shifting respects of value between adjacent pairs, or the judgments about the endpoints rest on an aggregation mistake, or the predicates involved are vague in a way that produces Sorites-style slippage rather than genuine cyclical rankings.

What I would like from you is a map of what would count against this position. Not merely a restatement of the spectrum arguments themselves, but an account of what would have to be true for me to be wrong: which kinds of cases would be genuinely decisive rather than merely uncomfortable, and what feature would a case need to have so that my usual dissolving moves (shifting respects, aggregation error, vagueness) demonstrably do not apply to it. I would also like to know which defeaters a committed defender of transitivity would themselves concede as damaging, as opposed to those only critics treat as damaging. Relatedly, I want an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated against refutation: if the transitivist can always respond that "all things considered better" just means whatever yields a transitive ordering, and that any residual ranking is therefore a comparison in some single respect rather than overall, then the thesis risks being true by stipulation. Is that a fatal objection to a thesis of this kind, or a legitimate claim that transitivity is constitutive of the concept of overall betterness rather than a contingent fact about it? Finally, say something about what results from neighbouring fields -- for instance, how economics and psychology treat cyclical preferences, including the standard argument that an agent with intransitive preferences can be exploited through a sequence of trades each of which they accept -- actually bear on the philosophical question, and whether they bear on it at all or merely on questions about rational preference, which may come apart from questions about value itself.

I should add what I personally would find most persuasive: a single, concrete case in which the transitive verdict is not merely counterintuitive but would commit me to something I can see is absurd on my own terms, rather than a case where the intransitive reading is simply available alongside mine. If no such case exists and the strongest counterevidence is a contested intuition about exotic lives and populations, I would like that said plainly. Please write your response as continuous prose in the manner of a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
