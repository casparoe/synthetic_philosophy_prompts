I run a grad seminar on moral psychology — about 12 students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them *feel* the difference between guilt and shame from the inside, not just recite the standard line that guilt is about what you did while shame is about what you are. Ideally the activity should also bring out the behavioral difference (shame pushing toward hiding and withdrawl, guilt toward apology and repair) and give us a concrete foothold for the bigger question of whether a culture could actually *choose* to be a guilt culture or a shame culture, or whether institutions just drift into one. Don't make it rely on anyone confessing real wrongdoing — that gets ethically dicey fast — so you'll have to engineer the emotions from artificial stakes.

Make definite design choices: pick one activity and commit to it, tell me why it beats the runner-up, don't hand me a menu of options.

Format, in this order: "The setup" (materials, what I tell the room, ~100 words), "The activity" (rules, roles, timing, ~300 words), "The debrief" (mapping moments in the game onto the guilt/shame distinction, ~200 words), and "What will go wrong, and what this can't show" (~150 words) — including which feature of real shame no classroom exercise can reproduce and I'll just have to lecture about.
