I run a recorded interview series for our philosophy department, and next month I'm interviewing the author of a new book defending what she calls outcome-luck realism: the view that it is not merely understandable but morally *correct* to blame people more when their choices produce worse outcomes, even when the difference in outcomes is entirely a matter of luck. Her central case is the familiar matched pair of reckless drivers -- identical conduct, identical states of mind, identical risks taken, except that a child steps into the road in front of one of them -- and she argues that our differential response to the two drivers should be vindicated rather than explained away.

Please draft a sequence of nine interview questions that presses her view where it is genuinely vulnerable, rather than letting her simply restate it. Concretely, I'd like the following covered, in an order that builds pressure rather than jumping around:

1. An opening question on the control principle -- the intuition that we are morally assessable only for what lies within our control -- which she must either reject outright or reinterpret, with a follow-up keyed to whichever of those two moves she makes (if she rejects it, ask how she proposes to contain the fallout for circumstantial and constitutive luck; if she reinterprets it, ask what work the reinterpreted version still does).

2. A question on the epistemic reading of outcome-based blame -- the idea that a bad outcome is evidence of greater culpability -- including the follow-up I most want answered: what does her view say about *stipulated* matched-pair cases in which we are told, as a condition of the thought experiment, that the two agents' negligence was identical and the outcomes diverged only because of factors no evidence could track? If she retreats to the claim that such stipulations are psychologically inert, press her on whether her view is then a thesis about blame or a thesis about the limits of our imagination.

3. A question on the difference between blaming someone and regretting one's causal role in a tragedy -- the lorry driver who kills a child through no fault of his own is often said to owe and to feel something distinctive, but many deny that this something is blame. Ask her to mark the boundary, and follow up on whether her view can explain why the lucky driver in her own central case seems to owe the *child's parents* something different from what he owes third parties.

4. A question about the law's differential treatment of attempts and completed crimes, asking whether her view recommends preserving, widening, or narrowing that differential, with a follow-up on the objection that legal punishment tracks deterrence and social stability rather than desert, so that legal practice is not evidence for her thesis at all.

5. A question asking her to state, as crisply as she can, what property the unlucky driver has that the lucky driver lacks -- having-c caused a death, say -- and whether being blameworthy for possessing that property is consistent with the idea that blame responds to the quality of the agent's will rather than to the history of the world. Follow up on the most likely answer, that the will is partly constituted by what it does, by asking whether that doesn't simply relocate the luck one level up.

6. A question on whether accepting resultant luck while rejecting constitutive and circumstantial luck is a stable position: if it can be just to blame more for lucky outcomes, why is it unjust to blame more for lucky character or lucky circumstances of action? Include a follow-up on the person whose dispositions were formed by an abusive upbringing, and ask her where, exactly, the control principle re-enters her picture, if anywhere.

7. A question on the universality objection: differential outcome judgments appear robust across cultures and developmental stages, and her opponents read this as evidence of a deep-seated cognitive bias rather than a perception of moral reality. Ask what distinguishes her project from a vindicating explanation of a bias, and what empirical finding, if any, would make her abandon the view.

8. A question on institutional design: hospitals and aviation regulators have largely moved toward outcome-insensitive review of errors, on the grounds that outcome-based review produces concealment and hindsight bias. Ask whether her view implies these just-culture regimes are morally mistaken, or only instrumentally justified, and follow up on what it would take for the instrumental justification to override what she takes to be a genuine desert claim.

9. A closing question inviting her to identify the strongest version of the opposing view -- the luck-skeptic position that all differential blame grounded in outcomes is unjust -- and to name the single consideration in its favor that she finds hardest to answer, with a follow-up asking why that consideration does not, in the end, move her.

Frame the questions the way a moderator would actually phrase them aloud: compact enough to be asked in one breath or two, free of jargon she hasn't herself introduced, but each carrying a specific point of leverage. Avoid questions inviting her to summarize the book, and avoid any question to which "that's a complicated issue" is a complete answer.
