Analyse the 1969 United States draft lottery — in which birth dates were drawn from capsules in a rotating drum to determine the order in which men would be conscripted, and which was soon shown to be badly biased, with dates late in the year disproportionately likely to be drawn early — through the lens of algorithmic randomness. I am not interested in the statistical forensics as such, which are well documented. What I want is a careful working-out of what the episode does to the question of whether a finite sequence can be random. The standard algorithmic accounts (Kolmogorov complexity, Martin-Löf tests) make randomness a property of the sequence itself, not of the process that produced it; the intuitive verdict on the lottery, by contrast, is that something went wrong with the process — inadequate mixing — whatever the resulting sequence happened to look like. So: map the episode onto the theory. Does a sequence produced by a biased mechanism thereby fail to be random in the algorithmic sense, or only relative to a chosen measure, and does that qualification rescue or undermine the theory's ambition? Could the 1969 lottery have produced exactly the same sequence by a genuinely fair mechanism, and would that sequence then have counted as random on your favoured account? Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: does the episode show that sequence-internal notions of randomness miss something essential — that randomness is partly about how a sequence was generated — or does it instead show that our intuitions about rigged lotteries conflate randomness with fairness, two things the theory was right to keep apart? Say where the theory is silent rather than forcing a verdict from it. Please write in British English.
