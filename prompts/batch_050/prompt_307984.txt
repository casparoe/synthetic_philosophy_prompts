I build fraud and pricing models for a living, so I keep running into a puzzle about what my models are even claiming. Consider how insurers actually set premiums: they sort people into classes — age band, ZIP code, annual mileage, credit-based insurance score where it's legal — and charge you the loss frequency of your class, even if your own record is spotless. Meanwhile regulators forbid certain classifiers outright: race everywhere, genetic information in health insurance, and so on. And customers protest: "Price my risk, not my group's." The insurer's honest reply is that there is nothing else to observe — you have no loss history that isn't a sample of one.

John Venn opened his Logic of Chance (3rd ed., 1888) with an epigraph that seems aimed at exactly this practice:

So careful of the type she seems
So careless of the single life.

Venn's book defends the view that probability statements properly attach to series — to types — rather than to single events. My question: what does the underwriting practice described above presuppose about probability? Reconstruct its implicit theory. Does it strictly presuppose something like Venn's frequentism — that "this driver's probability of a crash next year" is only meaningful relative to a chosen reference class — or does it merely fit that view well while remaining compatible with, say, a propensity reading on which each driver has a true individual risk that classes merely estimate? On the frequentist reading, is the customer's complaint simply confused, or is it a real grievance about which class was chosen, given that every person belongs to indefinitely many classes and the choice among them is underdetermined by the data alone? And what further commitments do the regulatory bans add: do they treat class selection as partly a moral or political decision rather than a purely statistical one, and can a pure frequentist coherently ground that distinction? Finally, say what would concretely change in pricing and regulation if we replaced the implicit theory with a Bayesian subjectivist view of the underwriter's credences — would individualized pricing become obligatory, impossible, or just differently licensed? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and flag where it is genuinely underdetermined. Answer in American English.
