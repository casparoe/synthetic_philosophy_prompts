I am trying to determine whether there is a workable version of Kant's universalizability test — not as an exercise in exegesis, but as the best available procedure for checking maxims — by running it against my own verdicts on cases. Here they are.

1. Borrowing money on a lying promise. The test condemns it: universalized, the practice of promising collapses and the maxim defeats its own purpose. I agree with the verdict, and this strikes me as the test at its best.

2. Becoming a professional violinist. If everyone acted on the analogous maxim, no one would farm, and the resulting world is unlivable. Yet the choice is plainly permissible. My verdict: any application of the test that condemns it has misformulated the maxim.

3. Reclining one's airplane seat fully. A world in which everyone reclines fully is conceivable and, I find, willable, so the test passes the maxim. My gut says the act is at least mildly inconsiderate. I am keeping both verdicts until something forces me to drop one.

4. Remaining childless by choice. Universalized, humanity ends with the present generation. I judge childlessness permissible, so if the contradiction-in-conception test condemns it, I would sooner abandon that reading of the test — though I keep going back and forth, because this universalized world does seem defective in a way the violinist world does not, and I cannot articulate the difference.

5. Lying whenever one is certain the lie will remain undetected. Universalized, the practice of trust apparently survives precisely because the lies are never discovered, so the maxim seems to pass the contradiction-in-conception test. The maxim is plainly wrong, and parasitic on the honesty of others. Perhaps the contradiction-in-will test catches it; I am not confident that it does.

6. Borrowing with no intention to repay, but only on Tuesdays and only from people whose surnames begin with R. The universalized world is perfectly conceivable — a bizarre but stable custom — so the conception test passes the maxim. It is plainly wrong. The test needs a rule against rigged maxim descriptions, and I want to know what that rule is.

7. Helping others only when doing so costs one almost nothing. The universalized world is conceivable, but I find I cannot will it without willing away the possibility of receiving costly help should I ever need it. Verdict: the maxim fails the contradiction-in-will test, and that verdict feels exactly right.

8. Lying to the murderer at the door. The test condemns the lie, and my gut refuses to follow it. Here I side with my gut: either the correct maxim must be permitted to mention the circumstances — but see case 6 — or the test overgenerates.

What I want is a statement, as precise as you can make it, of (a) the rule governing how a maxim may be formulated — the permitted level of generality, and what, if anything, may be built into the description — and (b) which contradiction test applies to which kind of maxim, together with the case above that motivates each clause of your answer. Cases 6 and 8 seem to me to pull the formulation rule in opposite directions, and cases 3 and 7 seem to demand a principled distinction between two maxims of mere selfish convenience; please address both pairs directly. Then tell me what your account predicts for two cases I have not listed: the maxim of letting one's talents rust undeveloped, and the maxim of buying up more than one's share of a scarce but replenishable good during a shortage. Finally, name honestly which of my eight verdicts your account cannot accommodate, rather than bending the principle to save them all.
