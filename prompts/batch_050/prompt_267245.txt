Yesterday I had a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about the philosophy of mind, and I'm not fully satisfied with how I defended my own position. She pressed me on whether the things I said over the course of the evening actually cohere with each other, and I want an outside audit before I talk to her again.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in functionalism and the metaphysics of mental states. Think carefully and methodically; before giving your verdict, briefly map out the logical dependencies among my commitments so I can see your working.

Here is roughly where I stand:

(1) I am a functionalist: what makes a state the mental state it is (a belief, a pain, a desire) is its causal and functional role — its typical causes, its effects on behavior and on other states — not the stuff it's made of.

(2) I think multiple realizability is the strongest argument for functionalism over the type-identity theory: since creatures with wildly different physical constitutions can presumably all be in pain, pain can't simply be identical to any one physical state.

(3) I accept the standard chip-replacement intuition: if my neurons were gradually swapped out for silicon components that preserved the input-output organization of my brain, my mentality would be preserved throughout, and any intuition to the contrary is just carbon chauvinism.

(4) I think the Chinese Room argument basically succeeds: a system that merely manipulates symbols according to a program, with no more to it than that, does not thereby understand anything. Genuine understanding requires more than implementing the right program.

(5) I am a physicalist: there is nothing ontologically special about biological tissue, and no non-physical ingredients are needed for a mind.

(6) I deny that simple artifacts like thermostats literally have beliefs; ascribing beliefs to them is at best a useful façon de parler.

My questions: Are these six commitments jointly tenable? For each pair (or larger cluster) that is in genuine tension, explain the tension precisely, rank it by severity, and identify the cheapest revision that would resolve it — cheapest in the sense of giving up the least of what I clearly care about. Please also flag any pair that merely looks inconsistent but dissolves on closer inspection, since my friend accused me of contradicting myself in at least one place where I think she was wrong. Finally, tell me which single revision would do the most to clean up the overall position.

Throughout, I want epistemic candor rather than a show of decisiveness: say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and at the end, single out the one point in your analysis where you are least sure you have it right.
