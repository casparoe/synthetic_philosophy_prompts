I'm a robotics/control systems engineer, mostly I work on motion planning for warehouse robots, and I got into an argument at a party last weekend about the sphex wasp. For those not familiar: the digger wasp does this elaborate routine where it drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, drops it at the entrance, goes inside to check things, comes back out and drags it in -- and experimenters found that if you move the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside, she drags it back to the threshold and repeats the whole entrance-check routine from scratch, over and over, apparently without ever noticing the loop she's stuck in. Philosophers (I think Dennett and Hofstadter talk about this) use "sphexishness" as this worry that maybe human behavior is the same kind of thing, just with more elaborate routines, and that our felt sense of freedom is what it feels like to run a very complicated script.

Here's my request. Restate the sphexishness argument and the free will worry it generates entirely in the vocabulary of control theory and embedded systems: open-loop vs closed-loop control, state machines, sensor feedback, interrupt handling, hysteresis, model-predictive control, adaptive gain, that kind of thing. I'm fluent in that stuff, so be as precise as you want -- e.g. my first instinct is to say the wasp is running a fixed sequence with no exception handler for a displaced-prey condition, and the whole "is she free" question dissolves once you see it's just a badly designed state machine.

But then do the honest part: give me a residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' worry translate cleanly into control vocabulary, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts seem to have no counterpart at all. In particular I'm suspicious of two things: first, whether "the system can modify its own control policy" (like gain scheduling or online system identification) actually captures what people mean by flexibility, or whether it just pushes the script up one level. Second, whether anything in the original debate -- the felt perspective, the reasons-responsiveness, whatever -- simply cannot be expressed as a property of a controller, and if so whether that's a limitation of control vocabulary or evidence that those notions were confused to begin with. Basically I want to know if my engineering instinct (humans are just very deeply nested feedback systems and there's no puzzle left) is right or is quietly throwing away the interesting part.
