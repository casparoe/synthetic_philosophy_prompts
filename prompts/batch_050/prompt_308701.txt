A bunch of institutions have settled on the same fix for using AI: let the model do the work, but make a credentialed human sign off. Lawyers have been sanctioned for filing briefs full of cases an AI invented, and the professional response was basically that whoever signs the filing vouches for every word. Hospitals run scans through detection models but a radiologist has to approve the read. And the people drafting rules for autonomous weapons keep converging on some version of "meaningful human control" as the core requirement.

What does this whole human-sign-off model presuppose about responsibility and understanding? It seems to assume you can be answerable for output you didn't produce and often can't fully check -- that merely having the competence to catch the system's mistakes is enough for responsibility to stick. But the entire economic point of these systems is that they beat human checking: a radiologist who re-reads every scan from scratch defeats the purpose, and people's skills atrophy the more they defer. So the practice seems to presuppose a competence it's busy eroding, and to park responsibility in the one place it increasingly can't live.

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. Does sign-off actually presuppose a control-based view where responsibility requires real capacity to verify -- or is it compatible with a purely forward-looking view where we just assign liability to whoever's best placed to reduce errors, and the "understanding" talk is decoration? When the oversight is ceremonial and the AI screws up, is there a genuine responsibility gap, or does the practice just hide it? And if responsibility really has to track understanding and control, what would these institutions concretely have to change -- ban the tools, treat them like products with strict manufacturer liability, or invent some new category of answerability?
