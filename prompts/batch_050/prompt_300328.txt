Critics of impartial consequentialism influenced by Bernard Williams often move freely among three complaints: that the theory is too demanding, that it alienates agents from the ground projects and commitments that make their lives theirs (the integrity objection), and that an agent who acted as the theory recommends would sometimes have, in Williams's phrase, one thought too many. Please write an essay explaining, as precisely as you can, why these are three distinct objections rather than one objection stated three ways, and what philosophical work each does that the others cannot.

Your essay should do the following. First, construct a single case — not one of Williams's own examples — in which the demandingness complaint has no grip at all (the agent sacrifices nothing she cares about) but the one-thought-too-many complaint still seems to apply, and explain what this shows about the logical independence of the two objections. Second, get clear on what the one-thought-too-many complaint is actually about: is the objection that the agent entertained a particular thought at all, that she entertained it at the wrong moment in deliberation, or that the theory must regard that thought as the one that justifies her action? Distinguish these readings and say which is strongest. Third, address the standard reply that the objection conflates a criterion of right action with a decision procedure — that a consequentialist can consistently hold that the right action is whatever maximizes value while also holding that agents should rarely deliberate in those terms. Explain why Williams (or a sympathetic reconstruction) would regard this reply as inadequate rather than merely incomplete; I want the strongest version of the rejoinder, not a dismissal. Fourth, consider whether the objection generalizes: does an analogous complaint about the structure of justification — as opposed to its content — arise for Kantian ethics or contractualism, or is there something specific to consequentialism's justificatory shape that generates the problem? Finally, assess a more speculative possibility of your own construction: suppose a theory keeps consequentialist outcomes as its standard of rightness but takes the primary bearers of moral evaluation to be an agent's dispositions rather than her occurrent deliberations. Does this escape the one-thought-too-many objection, relocate it, or merely hide it? 

Throughout, prioritize analytic precision over textual exegesis; do not worry about citations. Please write in American English.
