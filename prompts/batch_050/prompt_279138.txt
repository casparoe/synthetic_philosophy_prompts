Okay, some background so you know where I'm coming from. I spent 35 years as a biologist, mostly studying social insects and collective behavior, and only picked up philosophy books properly after I retired. What got me hooked was the question of whether a group can be a *moral* agent the way a colony is, in some sense, a *behavioral* agent -- whether blame can stick to a company the way it sticks to a person. So I did what I'd do with any new phenomenon: I wrote down cases and my gut verdicts, and now I want to know if there's a principle underneath. Here they are:

1. An engineer at a carmaker notices a brake defect, writes it up, and her managers quietly shelve the report because a recall would be expensive. Two years later people die in a crash caused by exactly that defect. Verdict: the company is blameworthy, full stop, not just the managers.

2. A mid-level trader at a bank secretly rigs a benchmark for years. Nobody else knew; compliance was genuinely reasonable by industry standards. Verdict: blame him, not the bank. The bank is more like a victim of him.

3. Same brake-defect story as case 1, except by the time the crash happens, literally everyone involved has left -- new CEO, new board, new engineers, new owners. Same name on the building, same product line. Verdict: still blameworthy. This surprises me but it's what I find myself thinking.

4. A bank's pricing algorithm quietly ends up charging worse rates to customers from certain postcodes, which correlate with ethnicity. Nobody designed it to do that, nobody knew, and arguably no single person *could* have predicted it. Verdict: the bank is blameworthy even though no individual is.

5. A two-person plumbing company where the owner personally orders the corner-cutting. Verdict: here it's odd to blame "the company" -- it's just the guy. The corporate talk adds nothing.

6. A mining company poisons a river doing things that were legal and standard practice at the time; the science showing the harm wasn't available then. Verdict: genuinely torn. Responsible for cleanup, yes, but *blameworthy*? My gut flips depending on the day.

Cases 2 and 4 are the ones that fight each other in my head. In 2 I refuse to blame the organization because no one could have caught it; in 4 I blame the organization *precisely because* no individual could have caught it. Same for 3 and 5 -- if turnover of every person doesn't dissolve the company's guilt in 3, why does smallness dissolve it in 5, where after all both people could also be replaced?

So: is there one principle of corporate blame that delivers most of these -- some condition on decision structures, information flow, whatever the right organizational analog of mens rea is -- or am I just pattern-matching on vibes like bad regulatory outcomes? If a clean principle has to sacrifice one of my verdicts, tell me which one you'd throw out and why. And then apply the winning account to the case I can't settle: my old university's biology department, which harrassed a junior researcher out of her job twenty years ago via a hiring committee that has since completely turned over -- is the department blameworthy, or only the long-retired professors?
