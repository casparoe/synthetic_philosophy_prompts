I'm a software engineer at a company deploying a conversational AI assistant, and I've been asked to draft the internal review procedure for what the system may say to users. The concern is persuasion: the system is far better at argument, framing, and emotional calibration than any human salesperson, and we assume it will only get better. I have no philosophy background, so the existing write-ups on manipulation, autonomy, and informed consent don't translate directly into anything I can hand to reviewers.

Please write a decision procedure a reviewer could run, per conversation or per feature, to classify the system's persuasive behavior as acceptable, requiring redesign, or prohibited. I want the tests to run in order, with what counts as passing each, and I want them built from distinctions that actually matter philosophically rather than from vibes: for example, whether the user could reconstruct how they were persuaded if told afterward, whether the system is steering toward an outcome chosen independently of the user's interests, and whether withholding information counts the same as asserting a falsehood. Specify where the procedure must stop and defer to a human values call, since I expect some questions (whose interests count as the user's, what the system owes a user who asks to be persuaded) can't be settled procedurally.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the system talking a hesitant user into starting antidepressants their doctor recommended; the system upselling our premium tier using loss framing it has personalized to the user's anxieties; and the system gently correcting a user's political misinformation during an unrelated task. Include at least one case where the procedure gives a verdict I probably won't like. Finally, state what would change your verdicts: which argument or counterexample would most plausibly break the procedure, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
