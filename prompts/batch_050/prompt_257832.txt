Two members of a decision theory reading group — call them the growth optimiser and the risk-weighting theorist — have argued for two years about rational choice under uncertainty and have finally agreed to stop talking past each other and produce a joint statement. Draft the document their adversarial collaboration should produce. Write in British English throughout.

Their shared example, which the statement should use as its anchor: a fair coin is offered on favourable terms — each round, the agent may stake any fraction of current wealth on a bet that pays even money and wins with probability 0.6. The gamble may be repeated indefinitely, with wealth compounding multiplicatively; there is also a one-shot version in which the bet is offered exactly once, and a variant in which actuarially unfair insurance against a small probability of ruin is available for purchase.

The growth optimiser's position: the uniquely rational strategy for the repeated gamble is the Kelly rule — staking the fraction of wealth that maximises the expected logarithm of wealth (here, 20 per cent) — because that rule almost surely accumulates more wealth than any materially different strategy as the rounds accumulate. This justification, the growth optimiser insists, rests on no assumptions about utility or attitudes to risk; what is ordinarily called risk aversion is really rational sensitivity to the time-average growth rate of one's own wealth, and expected value is an ensemble average that no single agent ever receives.

The risk-weighting theorist's position: rational choice consists in maximising risk-weighted expected utility — a rank-dependent generalisation of expected utility in which outcomes are weighted by a risk function as well as a utility function — and the agent's risk function is a matter of attitude, not of rational requirement. Kelly behaviour is merely the special case of logarithmic utility, so its appeal derives from a particular utility function after all rather than from any argument that bypasses utility; and the asymptotic dominance argument has normative force only for an agent who cares about almost-sure long-run outcomes, which is itself an attitude to risk.

The document should have four sections.

1. Agreed claims: a numbered list of propositions both parties can sign — about what the Kelly rule in fact achieves, about what expected-utility and risk-weighted agents would actually do in the three variants of the case, and about what is not in dispute. Nothing vacuous, nothing slanted towards either side.

2. Disagreements: a table in which each residual disagreement is stated in neutral wording, with a sentence alongside giving the formulation each party would accept as a fair statement of its own view.

3. Cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — I expect candidates include whether asymptotic almost-sure dominance has normative force for finite agents, whether the time-average framing adds anything beyond concave utility (consider what the growth optimiser should say about the one-shot case), and whether the choice of risk function is rationally adjudicable (consider whether the insurance buyer and the insurance refuser can both be rational). For each crux, specify a concrete case or calculation both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each predicts about it.

4. Verbal disputes: identify any part of the disagreement that dissolves on inspection — for instance, whether 'risk aversion' is being used in two senses — and show that it does.

Close with a short methodological note in your own voice, not either party's: state which agreed claim you are least confident both parties would actually sign on reflection, where your reconstruction of either position is most likely to be unfair, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about where the real crux lies.
