I am a novelist, and I lead a monthly workshop for eight other fiction writers, most of whom have published at least a little. We meet for two hours. For our next session I want to design a hands-on exercise that makes the participants feel the force of origin essentialism -- the thesis that a person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones they actually came from, so that asking "what would my life have been like if I'd been born to other parents, in another country, another century?" is not asking about you at all, but about someone else entirely. My motive is craft, not metaphysics: novelists constantly write sentences like "had she been born in Lagos instead of Leeds, she would still have..." and I suspect these sentences smuggle in an illicit assumption about what persists across a change of origins, and that noticing the smuggling would make us better at character.

Please design the exercise with the following shape. In the first phase, each participant should build a character concretely enough to care about (specify what they produce, and how long this takes). In the second phase, impose the counterfactual: the same person, but conceived from entirely different gametes, raised by different parents -- and then let the rules of the exercise force them to confront what they are actually keeping fixed as they write this "same" person, since nothing biological or biographical can carry over. In the third phase, I want some structured exchange among participants with small stakes attached -- points, a token economy, a vote with consequences -- so that each writer's hidden choices about which traits or qualities they smuggled across the change of origins get exposed and judged by the others, rather than remaining private. The group should discover, in the act of writing and judging, the thing the metaphysicians argue about: either there is some persisting essence that survives the change of origins, or there is not, in which case the exercise's instruction was incoherent and they have been writing a stranger all along.

Then give me the debrief: how to map specific moments of the exercise onto the philosophical positions (origin essentialism, counterpart-theoretic ways of talking about "you in another life," and the idea that narrative or psychological continuity is doing the work that biology cannot), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I suspect participants will cheat by keeping a gene-level trait like temperament or a birthmark, or will simply refuse the premise -- and how to handle each failure mode without lecturing. Finally, tell me frankly which feature of the philosophical problem the exercise cannot reproduce: I suspect fiction lets an author stipulate identity across worlds by fiat in a way that paper over whether the stipulation is coherent, and I would like that acknowledged rather than hidden. Keep the whole thing runnable within our two hours, name the philosophical thesis only in the debrief, and assume no prior philosophical training in the room.
