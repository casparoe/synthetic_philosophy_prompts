I'm taking an epistemology seminar this semester and we just finished Hume's essay on miracles, and it's gotten me thinking about an argument I keep having with my roomate. Her position is that once something gets labeled a conspiracy theory, you're basically justified in not engaging with it at all — the whole category is a red flag, since these theories supposedly have recurring features (they absorb counterevidence as proof of the cover-up, they need implausibly big groups to stay quiet, they thrive on gaps in the official account) that let you rule them out before ever looking at the specifics. I keep objecting that this seems rigged: actual conspiracies do get exposed from time to time (Watergate, the Tuskegee experiment, the NSA surveillance programs), and writing off a whole class of claims in advance seems to clash with the basic empiricist principle that you should proportion your belief to the evidence in front of you rather than to a label someone slapped on it.

So: if Hume were around today and got asked to adjudicate this, what would he say? I'm genuinely torn. His famous maxim about proportioning belief to evidence sounds like it supports my side — assess each claim on its merits, no exceptions. But his actual procedure in the miracles essay looks a lot closer to hers: he doesn't weigh miracle reports one by one, he argues that an entire genre of testimony can be discounted because of its recurring features (the human love of wonder, credulous audiences, reports coming from distant and ignorant times and places) and because uniform experience stands against it. That seems structurally the same move as dismissing conspiracy theories as a class. Does Hume have a principled way to separate the two cases — maybe that miracles violate uniform experience in a way that secret plots by powerful people simply don't? Or is he actually committed to category-level dismissal in both cases, which would mean my roommate is the better Humean here?

When you answer, please keep three things clearly seperate: what's solidly established about Hume's actual views, what's genuinely disputed among people who study Hume, and what's just your own speculation about how he'd extend his framework to an issue he never addressed. I really don't want a contested reading of Hume, or a guess about his counterfactual opinion, presented as if it were the consensus.
