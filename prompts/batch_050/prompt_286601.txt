At a recent family lunch, a discussion about inheritance tax turned heated, and my brother-in-law — who has strong libertarian sympathies — pressed on me a popular book about political philosophy, telling me its chapter on Nozick would settle the matter. I have since read the chapter, and I am trying to work out whether I can trust it, because it seems to extract a much stronger conclusion from the famous Wilt Chamberlain example than I had understood the example to support. The chapter summarises the argument as follows: 'Imagine a society whose wealth is distributed according to your favourite pattern — perfect equality, or distribution by need, or by merit; call this D1. Now suppose a brilliant basketball player negotiates a contract under which a small extra sum from every spectator's ticket goes directly to him. A million fans gladly pay the surcharge to watch him play, and by the end of the season he is far wealthier than anyone else: a new distribution, D2. Since D1 was just by hypothesis, and every transaction leading from D1 to D2 was entirely voluntary, D2 must be just as well, even though it matches no pattern whatsoever. Nozick thereby refutes every patterned theory of justice: maintaining any pattern would require the state constantly to forbid or undo voluntary exchanges, so egalitarian and need-based principles are really nothing but a standing licence for state interference with liberty. Taxation of earnings is morally equivalent to theft, and because people deserve whatever they acquire through voluntary exchange with others, redistribution violates their rights. The only legitimate state is accordingly a minimal one, restricted to protecting property and enforcing contracts.' I confess I am half-persuaded, which is partly why I am suspicious of it. Is this a fair account of Nozick's argument? Please distinguish what is accurate, what is a common simplification a defender of Nozick would tolerate, and what he would object to as a misrepresentation of his view. In particular, does the chapter's conclusion — that patterned theories stand refuted and that taxation is equivalent to theft — actually follow from the Wilt Chamberlain example, or does the example only establish something more modest, with the stronger claims depending on premises the summary has quietly imported or omitted? And what would a defender of a patterned conception of justice say is the weak point in the reasoning, once the summary is corrected? I should like to be armed with something more rigorous than instinct before the next family lunch. Please write your answer in British English.
