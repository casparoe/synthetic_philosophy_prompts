So here's some background: I spent 35 years as a research chemist (enzyme kinetics, mostly) at a midwestern state school, retired three years ago, and since then I've fallen hard for philosophy of science, which I used to think was a waste of good brain cells. Live and learn.

Anyway, there's something that bugged me even back when I was working, I just didn't have the vocabulary for it. Scientists have a very particular way of handling their own history, and I did it myself for decades without thinking twice:

1. Textbooks and review-article introductions narrate the past as a series of correct steps leading up to us, with wrong turns relegated to footnotes or comic relief ("people once thought the pancreas secreted directly into the nerves, ha ha").
2. We say certain dead people were "ahead of their time" — Mendel is the classic case — as if time itself were the variable and the guy just got to the right answer early.
3. We name laws, units, and constants after whoever's judged to have gotten it right, and everyone else in the priority dispute just evaporates from the record. Nobody remembers the other three people circling the same result.
4. And yet the very same scientists, over a beer, will happily tell you that all our current theories are provisional and probably wrong in ways we can't even see yet.

Now that I've read a bit, I gather historians call #1–3 "Whig history" — writing the past as progress toward the present — and mostly consider it a sin. Here's what I want to know.

What does this whole practice actually presuppose about what scientific knowledge IS? Does narrating history as a buildup toward us only make sense if there's a fixed truth out there and later science is genuinely closer to it — i.e., does it quietly commit you to a realist, cumulative picture and rule out the Kuhn-style view where paradigms replace each other rather than converge? Or is the practice compatible with alot of different philosophies and I'm over-reading it?

Second, the tension in #4. If current theory is provisional, then the present seems like a strange tribunal for judging the past — a good Kuhnian should expect our "ahead of their time" verdicts to get reshuffled at the next revolution. Is the practice internally coherent, or does it presuppose for the past a realism it explicitly denies for the present?

Third, what would history-writing even look like if we dropped the presupposition? Could you teach enzyme kinetics without narrating it toward the current model? Or is a bit of Whiggery functionally required for pedagogy — in which case the real question becomes something else (what?).

One request: please be committed. Don't give me the "realists say this, Kuhnians say that, constructivists say the other, you decide" tour. Pick the reconstruction you think is actually correct, say plainly whether the practice strictly presupposes it or merely sits most comfortably with it, take a definite stand on whether the practice is coherent as it stands, and give me your reasons. If you think I'm wrong somewhere in how I've set it up, say that too.

American English, please — my eyes glaze over at "whilst."
