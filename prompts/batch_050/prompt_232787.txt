Hey, i'm setting the final exam for my third-year seminar on moral progress and i want to retire my usual "compare Williams and Appiah" essay, since half the class answers it from half-remembered summaries anyway. Help me build a replacement around a case i invented (it's fictional, not a real place, so nobody can just recall the textbook treatment):

The Republic of Sorrea, an island trading nation, ca. 1955. For over a century, when a man's honor is insulted (an accusation of cheating, a slur on his family), the offended party can demand "walking the sand": a refereed bare-knuckle bout on the beach at dawn, to first blood or submission. Refusing the walk marks you as "pale": the bank won't extend you credit, nobody will witness your contracts, and your kids' marriage prospects collapse. Deaths are rare, maybe one in two hundred bouts, but injuries are routine. Here's the kicker: for sixty years there have been sermons, pamphlets, and a rationalist society publishing what everyone concedes are *excellent* arguments against the practice. Most participants agree in the abstract that it's barbarous. The custom persists anyway. Meanwhile: a railway is being built, a new merchant class trades with foreigners who find the custom absurd, and a popular young schoolteacher just died from a bout.

Write me one 50-minute essay question (40 marks) built on this case. The question should force students to (a) explain, using Williams on slavery and Appiah's The Honor Code, why sixty years of conceded arguments haven't ended the custom, (b) predict which of the developments above would actually kill it and why, and (c) take a stand on whether its ending would count as moral *progress* or merely change, given Williams' skepticism about self-congratulatory "vindicatory" histories.

Then give me a model answer (~700 words, the kind of thing your best student would produce) and a marking scheme out of 40. The scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make AND the mistakes that should cost marks. Make sure it catches at least: students who read Appiah as saying arguments play *no* role (his claim is that the winning arguments usually predate the revolution; honor is the lever); students who treat "honor" as just any social sanction rather than a specific code governing entitlement to respect; students who paint Williams as a flat relativist who thinks abolishing slavery wasn't progress; and students who recite dueling and footbinding without ever touching the Sorrea case (the details about credit, marriage, and the foreigners are there to be used). Roughly, how many marks for each part, and what does a 28/40 answer typically get wrong?
