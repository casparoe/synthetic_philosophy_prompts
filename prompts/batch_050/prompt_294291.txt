Write a satirical piece, in the deadpan form of an investor prospectus (with the usual sections: market opportunity, business model, risk factors, regulatory outlook), for a firm whose entire revenue model is the money pump. The firm has identified a population of consumers with transitive-violating preferences and sells them, one at a time, a sequence of small trades that each consumer gladly accepts and that together return them to their starting position minus a service fee, repeated indefinitely. The prospectus should take completely seriously, as settled accounting fact, the textbook argument that anyone with cyclic preferences can be turned into an infinite source of free money, and build its business plan on that premise.

The humor must come from exaggerating real features of the money pump argument and the diachronic rationality literature, not from inventing implausible ones. In particular, the piece should quietly land the standard objections without ever stating them as objections: the Risk Factors section should disclose, in liability-limiting legalese, that revenue projections assume customers will accept each trade as offered rather than recognize and decline the cycle; that the model presumes preferences are read off choices and that the customer's choices at step three are made by the same agent, with the same tastes, who chose at step one; that some customers inexplicably start planning ahead, treating the whole sequence as a single choice, which catastrophically impairs margins; and that a small but growing cohort of philosophers maintains these customers are rational all along, since each individual trade improves their position by their own lights. The Regulatory Outlook section may note pending guidance from the Bureau of Preference Hygiene and a forthcoming academic monograph, cited only as a threat, arguing that transitivity of preference is not a requirement of rationality at all but a consequence of bundling what are really separate choice situations.

Keep the tone straight throughout: no winking, no editorial aside, just a prospectus whose own disclosures, read closely, dismantle the company. Around 550 words.

After the piece, add a short author's note (separate from the prospectus, around 100 words) stating where the satire has flattened the actual debate -- for instance, what a defender of acyclic preferences or of sophisticated choice would say the parody got wrong, and which premise of the money pump argument your own treatment leans hardest on -- and what consideration, if you took it seriously, would make the satire miss its target.
