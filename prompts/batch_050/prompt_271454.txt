I have been a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and one community practice I have always wanted to see exported into academic philosophy is the adversarial collaboration: instead of two camps writing dueling papers, they co-author the single document both can sign, so the disagreement has to become exact rather than rhetorical. My philosophy-of-love reading group has been stuck for months on the question of love's reasons, and I think the group is ready to produce such a document. One camp holds a bestowal view in Frankfurt's mold: love is not a response to reasons or to perceived value in the beloved; rather, loving someone is itself a source of reasons, conferring importance on the beloved and their interests, with a parent's love for a child as the paradigm case. The other camp holds an appraisal view in Velleman's mold: love is a response to value already present in the person, an arresting awareness of the beloved's worth that suspends our ordinary emotional defenses against another person's inner life, and a love that answers to no value at all is at best arational. Kolodny's relationship-based view keeps surfacing as a would-be compromise that each camp privately suspects of collapsing into the other side.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps would produce after a serious, good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a preamble stating the shared question in wording neither side finds slanted — something in the neighborhood of whether love is a response to reasons, a source of reasons, both, or neither, but phrased better than I just phrased it; (2) a numbered list of claims both camps can genuinely sign, which I suspect will include claims about the paradigm cases (parental, romantic, and friendship love), about the datum that lovers do in fact give, request, and resent reasons, and about why the story that love is justified by the beloved's valuable qualities cannot be the whole story; (3) a set of disagreement clauses, each stated twice — once in wording the bestowalist would accept as a fair statement of their own position, once in wording the appraisalist would accept for theirs — so I can see where the formulations genuinely diverge rather than talk past each other; (4) a list of cruxes, and for each crux at least one concrete case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's predicted verdict recorded in advance — for instance, whether the two camps really do disagree about the thoroughly ordinary beloved whom no inventory of qualities would pick out, about continuing to love someone after dementia has erased the traits that occasioned the love, about "trading up" to a more accomplished partner, and about loving a person who is plainly bad; and (5) a closing note identifying which portions of the dispute, if any, the collaborators came to suspect were merely verbal, with reasons. If one of my seeded shared claims or cruxes would not actually survive contact with both camps, say so and substitute a better one rather than forcing it in, and if the relationship view turns out to be the honest description of some crux, the drafters may note that — but the signatories remain the two camps.
