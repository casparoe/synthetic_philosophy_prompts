You are a philosopher of perception with expertise in the epistemology and metaphysics of hallucination. Before writing, take a moment to brainstorm the strongest moves available to each side of the debate, so that neither character is a strawman.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers, Dr. Verity Hale (a disjunctivist) and Dr. Marcus Brandt (a defender of the "highest common factor" view of experience), disputing the following case. A subject, seated before a lemon, undergoes a procedure in which her brain's visual processing is slowly and seamlessly taken over by a neural stimulator: at the beginning she enjoys a wholly veridical perception of the lemon, at the end she suffers a pure hallucination, and each momentary stage is introspectively indistinguishable from the adjacent ones. Brandt presses a sorites-style argument: since no single step can mark the loss of the common experiential element, the same kind of mental state must be present throughout, which the argument from illusion has always assumed. Hale must respond without simply denying the continuity of the case; she may, for instance, retreat to a merely epistemological form of disjunctivism, reject the principle that indiscriminability transmits sameness of mental kind, or contest the case's coherence — but whatever she chooses, Brandt should anticipate and answer her actual reasons, not caricatures of them. Let the exchange also touch, at least briefly, on whether Hale's view can still explain the causal role of hallucination (why the hallucinating subject behaves just as the perceiver does) and on whether Brandt's common factor is explanatorily idle in the veridical case.

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. Preliminaries (roughly one short paragraph): stage directions establishing the case and each character's position.
2. The Dialogue (roughly 700–900 words): at least ten substantive exchanges, with each speaker directly engaging the other's most recent point.
3. Assessment (roughly 150–200 words): in your own voice as the course instructor, identify the single strongest move made by each side and state which character, on balance, emerged in a better dialectical position, and why.
