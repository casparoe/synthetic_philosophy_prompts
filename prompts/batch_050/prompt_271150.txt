A friend argues that Adam Smith’s critique of empire commits him to a conclusion he plainly would resist. (1) For Smith, a political arrangement is corrupt when it systematically sacrifices the interests of the ruled to a privileged order that writes the rules: monopoly merchants at home, chartered companies and planters abroad. (2) Colonies under the mercantile system are ruled in exactly that way, since trade routes, offices, bounties, and taxes are arranged so that the many in the colony serve a narrow metropolitan interest. (3) If an arrangement is corrupt in that sense, continued rule over unwilling subjects is wrongful domination, and the only just remedies are immediate withdrawal or full equal incorporation into one representative state. (4) So Smith must favor either instant colonial independence or equal parliamentary incorporation for the colonies. (5) But this proves too much: every eighteenth-century state, Britain included, contained unrepresented taxpayers, regulated workers, and subjects who never consented to the crown, so by (1)-(3) ordinary sovereignty itself is wrongful domination unless every subject consents. (6) Smith does not require unanimous consent for legitimate law, defense, taxation, or even burdensome regulation when these belong to a general system of justice and prosperity. (7) Therefore his colonial critique has no critical edge unless it collapses into an anarchic demand for universal consent. Which premise or inference should be rejected? Lead with the rejected step in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and no restating of the question: explain why the step tempted, distinguish domination from nonconsensual but legitimate authority, and say what Smith can still condemn about colonies without licensing (5). No citations, quotations, or reading list.
