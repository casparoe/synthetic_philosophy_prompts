Assigned question: "In the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defection is a dominant strategy, yet cooperation is widely observed among humans and other organisms. Explain how repeating the interaction changes the strategic picture, describe what Axelrod's tournaments suggested about which strategies succeed, and identify one important limitation of Tit for Tat."

Participant's answer: "In the one-shot game, defecting dominates no matter what the other player does, so mutual defection is the only Nash equilibrium. Once the game repeats with no certain endpoint, though, the shadow of the future changes the incentives: current cooperation can be rewarded later and current defection punished later, so mutual cooperation can be sustained as an equilibrium provided players value the future enough. Axelrod ran tournaments where submitted strategies played iterated prisoner's dilemmas against each other, and Tit for Tat — cooperate first, then copy the opponent's previous move — won. Its success was attributed to being nice (never defecting first), retaliatory (punishing defection), forgiving (returning to cooperation quickly), and clear (easy for opponents to read). Tit for Tat is a subgame perfect equilibrium of the iterated game, and Axelrod's results established it as the evolutionarily stable strategy in this setting. One limitation is that Tit for Tat can be exploited by strategies that alternate defection and cooperation in ways it can't respond to optimally, though it still performs well overall because its losses to exploiters are bounded."
