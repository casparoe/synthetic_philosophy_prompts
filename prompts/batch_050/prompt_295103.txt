I'm a PhD student in machine learning, working on evaluation methodology, and I've been reading philosophy of science on the side. I've run into the problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory, and I want to understand it in the vocabulary I actually think in.

As I understand the setup: Bayesian confirmation says that evidence E confirms hypothesis H just when conditioning on E raises H's probability, which requires that E be more probable given H than it is outright. But if E was already known — part of the accepted background corpus before H was even formulated — then E has probability 1, conditioning on it changes nothing, and H receives no credit at all, no matter how elegantly it accounts for E. The standard illustration is general relativity and the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion: the anomaly was decades-old data by 1915, yet scientists plainly took it as strong support for Einstein's theory, and the formalism apparently cannot explain why. The replies I've encountered fall into a few families: relocate the confirmation in the discovery of the entailment rather than in the evidence itself (the agent updates upon learning that H entails E, and logical learning is treated as a genuine credal event); recompute with a counterfactual prior purged of the old evidence (ask what your credences would have been had you never learned E); or concede that old evidence does not confirm and argue that scientific praise is tracking something else, such as whether the theory was deliberately constructed to fit the data rather than merely accommodating it after the fact.

Here is the vocabulary I want all of this translated into, and in which I am fully fluent: training and test sets, held-out evaluation, cross-validation, data leakage and contamination, double-dipping, prequential or sequential evaluation, pre-registration, and the general distinction between fitting and predicting. Translate the problem itself and each family of replies into that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which moves translate cleanly, which you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression at all in the language of evaluation methodology.

Two specific things I want the residue report to adjudicate. First, my field's instinct is that the problem has a trivial engineering solution — set aside data the model never saw and evaluate on that — and yet my field has also, apparently independently, reinvented the philosophers' distinction: we treat results on contaminated test sets as worthless, and we treat pre-registered analyses as more credible than exploratory ones. Does this mean the old-evidence problem dissolves into a practical problem of data hygiene, or does the fact that we need these institutional fixes show that the problem is real and that our hygiene practices are its operational shadow? Second, one family of replies depends on agents not knowing the logical consequences of their own theories — on logical learning being a real event that shifts credence. As far as I can tell, nothing in my vocabulary corresponds to an agent's changing epistemic relation to entailments it has not yet derived. Is that a gap in my framework, or a sign that this reply describes something no formal theory of confirmation can actually deliver?

Finally, after the translation and the residue report, I want your verdict on the direction of explanation. If some element of the philosophical debate has no counterpart in evaluation methodology, should I conclude that my vocabulary is missing a genuine phenomenon, or that the philosophers are building historical facts — when the evidence was learned, by whom, in what order relative to the theory's construction — into a concept of evidential support that ought to be indifferent to them?
