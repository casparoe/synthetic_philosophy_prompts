So I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a chapter. The book has this running argument between two characters -- an aging hardware engineer and a young AI researcher -- about whether an AI's "thoughts" actually do anything in the world, or whether they're just a story we tell ourselves about voltage patterns sloshing around in silicon. I've decided the engineer should win this round by springing a thought experiment on the researcher, and I need the thought experiment itself to be genuinely good, because my readers will include actual philosophers and I will absolutely hear about it if it's sloppy.

Here's the assignment: construct a thought experiment about an ordinary classical computer -- no quantum anything, no exotic physics, no homunculus hiding inside the box -- that bears on the question of whether symbols push the substrate around. The careenium question, basically: if you watch the system at the level of the marbles you see one causal story, at the level of the symbols another, and I want a case where those two stories collide instead of peacefully coexisting. The case should be engineered so that someone who says the symbol level is epiphenomenal (all the real causation happens down among the simmballs; symbols are just a handy redescription for creatures too slow to track electrons) and someone who says symbols do genuine causal work come away with different verdicts about the very same machine -- different predictions, or at minimum different judgments about what the correct explanation of the machine's behavior is. Self-reference is allowed and maybe even encouraged: I've got a hunch that a machine reading or rewriting its own code is where the two levels really crash into each other, but if you think there's a better pressure point, use that instead and tell me why.

Then prove to me the contraption works. Run the scenario at both levels, careenium-style: first as a pure story about marbles and voltages, then as a pure story about symbols, and state the verdict each side gives and why. Identify the single feature of the design that's doing the work, and tell me what happens to the case if I remove it -- does it collapse back into the boring both-descriptions-are-fine truce? And because my researcher gets a comeback in the next chapter: how would the epiphenomenalist attack the experiment? I want the argumet to feel fair, not like I set up a strawman for my favorite character to knock down.
