Write an essay that takes as its text the following sentence from G. K. Chesterton's Varied Types (1903):

"He is only a very shallow critic who cannot see an eternal rebel in the heart of the Conservative."

The essay should run to roughly 1,200–1,600 words (the sentence above does not count toward that length) and should do the following things, in whatever order you find most natural.

First, interpret the sentence. A rebel needs something to rebel against: what, on the most defensible reading, is the conservative's rebellion aimed at? Consider at least the possibility that the conservative's enemy is the present itself — that he fights on behalf of the dead and the unborn against the accidental majority of people who happen to be alive now, and that in an age of triumphant innovation it is the defender of old things, not the reformer, who occupies the position of the insurgent. Chesterton himself was a vigorous polemicist who thought many features of his own time needed to be resisted or even reversed, so do not settle for a reading on which the "eternal rebel" is a merely decorative flourish.

Second, test the sentence against Chesterton's own most famous piece of practical advice. In The Thing (1929) he defends the principle now commonly called Chesterton's fence: if you encounter an institution whose point you cannot see, you are not entitled to clear it away until you have discovered why it was erected; only someone who understands the fence's purpose may be permitted to remove it. Set the two dicta side by side and state the apparent conflict as sharply as you can. The fence principle counsels patience, deference, and epistemic humility toward whatever has survived; the rebel in the conservative's heart sounds like a license for militant action. Make the tension precise rather than gesturing at it: is the fence principle a rule about ends or only about means? Does it say anything at all about building new fences or rebuilding fallen ones, or only about demolition?

Third, bring Burke's way of dissolving such tensions into the argument. Burke treated inherited institutions as an entailed patrimony held in trust across generations, insisted that sound statesmanship joins a disposition to preserve with an ability to improve, and — most strikingly — defended the English revolution of 1688 as an act of conservation rather than innovation, a forcible return to ancient principles against a crown that had itself become the innovator. Use this to explore whether a restoration can be simultaneously an act of rebellion and an act of conservation, and whether that fact rescues Chesterton's sentence or merely relocates the problem.

Fourth, force the issue with two hard cases. (a) A politician proposes abolishing a regulatory agency created fifty years ago, in the name of an older, freer settlement it displaced. The fence principle seems to protect the agency — it is the standing institution now — while the rebel reading of Chesterton seems to license its demolition. Who is the conservative here, and who is the shallow critic? (b) A movement proposes reviving a lapsed institution — a defunct guild, an abandoned constitution, a dead language. The fence principle, being silent about construction, gives no guidance; the rebel heart presumably applauds. Does the difference between these cases show that Chesterton's two principles govern different domains, or that one of them must give way?

Finally, reach a verdict and defend it. Either reconcile the sentence with the fence principle — showing, for instance, that the fence principle is a discipline of understanding rather than a ban on action, so that the rebel who has done the work of understanding is precisely the person Chesterton trusts to act — or convict Chesterton of equivocation, showing that the word "rebel" smuggles in romantic prestige that his considered position does not entitle him to. A mixed verdict is permitted (for example, that the reconciliation succeeds for restoration but fails for demolition), but an essay that ends by merely noting that both ideas have something to be said for them has not finished the job.
