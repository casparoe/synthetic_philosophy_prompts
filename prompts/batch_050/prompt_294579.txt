I'm a graduate TA leading a fifty-minute discussion section attached to a large undergraduate ethics survey, and my assigned job is to introduce the paradox of deontology — the puzzle that deontological constraints characteristically forbid violating a constraint even when doing so is the only way to reduce the total number of violations of that very constraint. The students have already encountered consequentialism and the basic idea of side-constraints, but none of them have met the paradox as such. Don't explain the paradox to me; plan how I should explain it. Lay out the dependency structure: which distinctions have to be in place before the paradox can even be stated (I'm assuming constraints-versus-goals and the agent-relative/agent-relative contrast belong here, but tell me if the ordering should differ), and why stating the puzzle too early makes it sound like a trivial restatement of the consequentialist objection to rules. Recommend the single case the whole session should be built around — I suspect it needs to be a case where the agent's own prospective violation prevents several violations by others, rather than the usual killing-one-to-save-five-from-a-trolley setup, but say what the case must include and what it must not include (e.g., uncertainty about outcomes, special obligations) so that the puzzle survives reflection. Map the misconceptions I should expect and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off: students will think this is just the familiar complaint that following rules sometimes yields worse outcomes; some will think the paradox is a quick refutation of deontology rather than an internal tension that deontologists take seriously; some will try to dissolve it by rewriting the constraint as "don't kill unless it prevents more killing," without seeing what that move costs. For each, give me the sentence or question that exposes the error. Finally, specify one question I can pose in the last five minutes that would show whether students grasp the specifically paradoxical structure — that the constraint resists minimization of its own violations — as opposed to merely knowing that deontologists sometimes accept bad outcomes, and note which further complications (threshold deontology, doing versus allowing) I should explicitly defer.
