So I've been reading up on this idea that something being valuable basically just means it's appropriate or fitting to value it. Seemed neat. But then apparently there's a standard objection: if a demon will torture me unless I admire him, I have a really good reason to admire him, but that doesn't make him admirable. Fine, I get that.

What I can't get a straight answer on is whether this is actually one problem or a bunch. Because the same trick seems to work for other attitudes. If someone offers me a million bucks to believe that 2+2=5, I have a reason to believe it, but that reason has nothing to do with whether it's true. And I came across this case where you get money for genuinely intending to drink a nasty toxin tomorrow, even though you know that once you have the money you won't actually have to drink it — so a reason to intend, but no reason to drink. Are the demon case, the money-for-belief case, and the toxin case all the same underlying glitch, just showing up in different attitudes? Like, is there one general split between "reasons to have the attitude" and "reasons that show the attitude would be correct," and the demon is just the value-flavored version of it?

And the follow-up I really want answered: when philosophers try to fix the demon problem (I think there's some distinction between object-given and state-given reasons? might be mangling the terms), does the fix automatically carry over to belief and intention, or does every attitude need its own seperate patch, and if so why? Please explain it intuitively rather than with formal machinery — I'm not a philosopher, just someone this is bugging.
