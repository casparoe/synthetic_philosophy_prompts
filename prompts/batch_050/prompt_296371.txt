I'm a PhD student in value theory and next week I'm running a session in an undergrad ethics-and-film course where I pair one film with one philosophical problem. I've picked A Clockwork Orange (thinking mainly of the Kubrick film, but with the novel's ending in the back of my mind) and the wrong kind of reasons problem. Quick setup so we're on the same page: the standard line is that an attitude like admiration or desire can be unfitting even when you have overwhelming reason to have it — the classic demon case, where a demon will torture you unless you admire him, gives you a reason of the wrong kind to admire him, because his capacity to punish you isn't the sort of thing that makes anyone admirable.

What I want is a reading of the film that takes the Ludovico technique as the philosophical crux. The state doesn't exactly give Alex wrong-kind reasons not to do violence — it bypasses reasons altogether and re-wires his responses, so that violence (and, as collateral damage, Beethoven's Ninth) now makes him physically sick. The prison chaplain objects that this isn't goodness, because goodness has to be chosen. My hunch is the chaplain is groping toward something the right/wrong kind of reasons distinction illuminates but also partly misses: conditioning isn't a reason of any kind, so the usual machinery — reasons to have an attitude vs. reasons grounded in the attitude's object — can't quite say what's gone wrong with Alex.

So give me a committed reading, not a menu of options. Take a definite stand on (1) whether the chaplain's objection is best cashed out in fitting-attitude/wrong-kind-of-reasons terms or whether it needs a different notion entirely — autonomy, endorsement, reasons-responsiveness, whatever you think actually does the work; (2) whether the state's interest in crime reduction counts as a right-kind or wrong-kind reason for making Alex unable to choose violence, and whether that even matters for judging the technique; and (3) what the difference between the film's ending and the novel's last chaper — where Alex, de-conditioned, just grows out of his violence and starts wanting an ordinary life — does to the whole reading: does natural maturation restore whatever Ludovico destroyed, in the philosophically relevant sense?

If you think the wrong-kind-of-reasons lens distorts the film rather than illuminating it, say so and say exactly where it breaks — I'd rather have that than a forced fit. Max 800 words.
