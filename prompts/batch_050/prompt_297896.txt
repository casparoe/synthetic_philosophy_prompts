I'm hosting a philosophy-of-science podcast and will interview the author of a forthcoming monograph defending an austere view in the metaphysics of causation: omissions, absences, and non-occurrences are never literally causes, only ever quasi-causes that we invoke pragmatically when moral or explanatory expectations are in the background. Her central claim is that causation is a relation between actual events involving transfers of energy or the instantiation of conserved quantities, and since nothing flows to or from a non-event, cases like the gardener's failure to water the plant cannot genuinely cause the plant's death — they merely let us describe real causal chains (dehydration, desiccation) while indexing which omissions we find salient.

She is nimble with a small set of stock replies: that the intuition that omissions cause is explained away by our interest in deviations from norms; that science only ever needs the positive causal relata; and that counterfactual dependence is a guide to explanatory relevance, not to causal reality.

Write me a sequence of six to eight interview questions that does the following. Begin by getting her to state precisely which theses she commits to (does she deny that omissions are effects as well as causes? does the view extend to double prevention and prevention generally?). Then press where the literature presses hardest: cases where an absence appears to be the only candidate difference-maker (e.g., a safety system whose non-activation is the sole explanatory factor); the worry that her positive-only criterion is itself hostage to contested physics rather than metaphysically neutral; the charge that "salience" and "norms" do covert causal work that her official view cannot acknowledge; and the problem that moral and legal responsibility ascriptions for negligence seem to require genuine causation by omission unless she bites a large revisionist bullet. For each challenge, anticipate her standard reply and build the follow-up into the same question so she cannot simply recite the reply and move on. End with one question about what observation or argument — from metaphysics, physics, or everyday explanatory practice — would in principle count as evidence against her view, and whether there is any such thing.

In your answer, please distinguish what is genuinely settled in this literature (e.g., that most theorists accept some omissions as causes, that the counterfactual analysis has known trouble cases) from what remains live and contested (e.g., whether any armchair criterion for genuine causation can be insulated from physics, how revisionary the austere view really is), and flag any point where you are reconstructing a position rather than reporting a consensus.
