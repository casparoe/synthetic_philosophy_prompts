I've been in this running argument with a guy in my reading group for months now about the units of selection debate, and honestly we've reached the point where we're just repeating ourselves. He thinks the gene's-eye view is the only coherent way to think about natural selection and that "group selection" is mostly confused bookkeeping; I keep insisting that multilevel selection explains things (eusocial insects, the evolution of multicellularity, cooperation in general) that gene-level talk either can't or can only handle in a really strained way. We finally agreed to try doing this properly as an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement we'd come out of it with.

So: you are a philosopher of biology who has deep sympathy for both traditions and has read widely in both, and your job is not to pick a winner but to write the document that a committed gene-centric selectionist and a committed multilevel selection theorist could both actually sign after working together in good faith for a year. Please think carefully about what each side *really* believes rather than the caricatures — the gene-centrist is not a naive adaptationist and the multilevel theorist is not claiming that groups have goals or that selection favors things "for the good of the species." Before drafting, briefly brainstorm what each side's strongest commitments are, so I can see your working.

The statement itself should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims — things like what both sides accept about kin selection and sterile castes in Hymenoptera, about selfish genetic elements like meiotic drive, about the fact that selection at one level can be opposed by selection at another, and about the Price equation as a piece of mathematics. Second, a disagreements section where each residual disagreement is stated precisely, with each side's position phrased in words that side would actually accept — I especially want this done for the "equivalence" dispute, i.e. whether the two frameworks are just different accounting schemes for the same math or whether there's a real empirical difference underneath. Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that answering them would move at least one side, and for each crux, a case, observation, or experiment that both parties agree in advance would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down next to it. If some disagreement turns out to be purely verbal, say so explicitly and dissolve it in the text rather than letting it sit there looking substantive.
