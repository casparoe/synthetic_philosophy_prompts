Two friends of mine, Leon and Sofia, got into a long argument about a predicament facing a third friend, Maya, and I've been asked to referee. I'd like you to do it instead.

The predicament: Maya regularly drives home late at night along a featureless highway with two turn-offs. The turn-offs look identical, she is always exhausted enough that by the time she reaches one she has no memory of having passed the other, and she has no reliable way to mark, count, or time them — she knows all of this in advance. If she takes the first turn-off she ends up stranded on an unpaved dead end and sleeps in her car (value to her: 0). If she takes the second she gets home (value: 4). If she misses both, she ends up at an all-night diner and waits for morning (value: 1). Whatever procedure she follows, she will follow it identically at any turn-off she reaches, since her situation and information are identical at each.

Here is the argument as I recorded it:

(1) Leon: Since Maya will face exactly the same situation with exactly the same information at whichever turn-off she reaches, any "decision" she makes at a turn-off is automatically made at both. The only genuine choice is a single probability p of exiting, chosen in advance. Her expected value is 4p(1−p) + (1−p)², which is maximized at p = 1/3, yielding 4/3 — strictly better than any pure policy (never exit yields 1; always exit yields 0). She should bring a die and exit when it shows 1 or 2. Deliberation at the wheel can only be a re-choosing of that policy, so nothing else can be rational.

(2) Sofia: At the wheel Maya doesn't face a policy choice; she faces an action under uncertainty about her location. Her evidence at the two turn-offs is exactly the same, so her credence should split evenly: 1/2 that this is the first turn-off, 1/2 that it's the second. Exiting then has expected value 2, while continuing has at most 3/2 (if she's at the first, her best continuation is worth 2; at the second, continuing is worth 1). So at the wheel she should exit for certain — which lands her at 0. The ex ante plan is not self-governing: it survives only if she refuses to deliberate.

(3) Leon: Even credence is the mistake. Rational credence should track the relative frequency with which her arrivals are first-turn-off versus second-turn-off arrivals, and the policy itself fixes those frequencies. Given the die, 3/5 of her turn-off arrivals are at the first turn-off and 2/5 at the second. With credences 3/5–2/5: exiting yields (2/5)·4 = 8/5, and continuing yields (3/5)·2 + (2/5)·1 = 8/5. Deliberation, properly informed, ratifies the plan rather than overruling it.

(4) Sofia: It ratifies it only at a knife-edge of indifference, which makes the ratification toothless: at p = 1/3, nothing in her deliberation favors exiting 1/3 of the time over any other mixture. Worse, any nearby plan actively unravels. Had she planned to exit 40% of the time, her at-the-wheel calculation would compare exiting (1.5) with continuing (1.75) and instruct her never to exit — collapsing the plan to continuing always, value 1. A policy that only a die can hold in place isn't a deliverance of her rationality; it's a substitute for it.

(5) Leon: That proves too much. Any pure recommendation is worse than the die, so if rationality must issue in a pure action it is worth at most 1 to Maya, while the die is worth 4/3; a theory of rationality that forbids the better course on grounds of self-governance is the theory that fails. And the knife-edge objection generalizes badly: mixed strategies in games are likewise sustained only by indifference, yet we don't conclude that rational play is impossible there.

(6) Sofia: Games pit you against another will; here the only other party is Maya herself at another occasion, so the analogy fails. This exposes the real disagreement. Leon counts the whole journey as one agent choosing once; I count each occasion of deliberation as the locus of rational assessment. On Leon's view the woman at the wheel is an executor of instructions, not a deliberator at all — which may be good engineering, but it isn't an account of rational agency.

Please adjudicate. Rate each of the six moves individually — clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken — with a brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both sides are missing.
