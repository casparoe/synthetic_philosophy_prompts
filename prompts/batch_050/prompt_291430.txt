I am a 52-year-old man, and my doctor has recommended that I take a daily medication for the rest of my life to reduce my risk of a stroke. I am trying to determine how much the medication would actually help me, and I have run into something I cannot sort out. Depending on which group I count myself as belonging to, my ten-year stroke risk comes out very differently. It is about 8 percent for men my age in general; about 4 percent for men my age who do not smoke and are not overweight; about 12 percent for men my age with mildly elevated blood pressure, which I have; and about 3 percent in one large study of men my age with my blood pressure who exercise regularly, as I do. Every figure comes from a reputable study, and I genuinely am a member of all of these groups, so they cannot all be the answer to the question of what my risk is.

The problem gets worse the longer I think about it. I can keep refining the description: men my age with my blood pressure who exercise regularly and whose father had a stroke at 61, as mine did. Eventually the group contains only me, at which point talk of frequencies within the group seems to lose its grip entirely. And not every refinement seems equally respectable: statistics on men my age whose surnames begin with the same letter as mine would obviously be meaningless, but I cannot articulate the principle that separates that way of carving things up from the blood-pressure one. So here is what I would like to know. First, is there a fact of the matter about what my risk is, and if so, how would I in principle identify it? Second, if there is not, on what basis should I decide whether to take the medication — and is a decision made on that basis rational, or merely arbitrary? Third, does whatever answer you give commit me to a particular view about what probabilities are in the first place?
