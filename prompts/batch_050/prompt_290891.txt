When principals delegate bargaining to artificial agents, a familiar worry is that the agents will settle on agreements that are Pareto-suboptimal from the principals' own point of view. One reason: a utility function is only defined up to affine rescaling, so a bargaining solution computed over the utilities as stated can depend on normalizations nobody chose for a reason; two pairs of agents facing the same underlying conflict can reach materially different deals because of how each side's utility happened to be written down. A safe Pareto improvement over a default agreement is a modification of that agreement — say, a renormalization of both sides' utilities before the bargaining solution is computed, or a coordinated switch to a different solution concept — such that every principal prefers the modification in expectation to the default no matter what they privately believe about the other side's utility scale, outside options, or instructions to their agent. The improvement is "safe" in that endorsing it requires no trust and no disclosure: endorsement is rational under every belief consistent with what the endorser knows.

Suppose a small research group — three people, two years, trained in game theory and decision theory rather than machine learning — wants to spend that time making this style of reasoning usable in practice. The candidate directions on the table:

1. Foundations under structural uncertainty. Existing treatments assume a commonly known game with uncertainty confined to payoffs or types. Real delegators are uncertain about the structure of the interaction itself — which moves are available, what an agreement's consequences are, even whether the other side represents the conflict in the same ontology. Can the safety condition be defined without a common prior, or even a common game form, and does anything interesting survive the generalization?

2. Selection and fairness. There are typically many safe improvements over a given default, and they are not interchangeable: some shift surplus toward parties with more flexible utility representations or more credible outside options. Which selection principle among safe improvements should the group defend? Is any selection principle neutral, or is choosing among improvements itself a bargaining problem one level up, in which case the approach is at risk of regress?

3. Establishing safety in practice. A modification is only safe relative to what each party knows, so agreeing on one requires each party to become confident that the others' endorsement conditions hold — apparently without revealing the private information the condition was designed to protect. Is this a genuine obstacle with a protocol-shaped solution, or does it collapse the distinction between safe improvements and ordinary negotiated improvements?

4. Iterated and dynamic settings. A one-shot improvement can be undone by later renegotiation, and the mere anticipation of future improvements changes what parties accept now. Does the concept extend to ongoing delegation relationships, or does safety become vacuous once the future enters the model?

5. Multi-party generalization. With more than two principals, a modification can be safe for the grand coalition while exposing subsets to losses, or vice versa. What is the right generalization — safety for every coalition, only for the whole, or something in between — and how much of the two-party theory carries over?

Which of these directions should the group pursue, and in what order? Defend the ranking rather than merely presenting it. For each direction you rank highly, identify what depends on it: which of the other directions presuppose its resolution, and whether any practical deployment of the idea can proceed without it. For each you rank low, say whether that is because it is intractable with the tools the group actually has, because its resolution would not change what anyone builds, or because it is less open than it first appears. If the best use of the two years involves a question not on this list, or a division of labor the list does not suggest, say so. Finally, for your top-ranked direction, state what result — positive or negative — arriving a year from now would most change the rest of your ranking.
