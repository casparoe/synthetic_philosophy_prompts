Last night a friend and I argued for hours about what "socially constructed" actually commits you to. The argument started over gender, wandered into race and money, and stalled completely when my friend claimed that calling something socially constructed is just a polite way of calling it unreal or arbitrary — so, on his view, the slogan that gender is constructed amounts to the claim that it is a fiction we could all collectively drop tomorrow. I pushed back, but I realized mid-argument that neither of us had any idea what a serious theory of social construction is even supposed to do. We were trading slogans with no standard in hand.

Before I have this argument again, I want the standard. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of social construction of kinds — a theory that purports to explain what it is for a kind (race, gender, money, diseases, genres, whatever else qualifies) to be socially constructed. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, not just the requirement itself. Specifically, I want the list to cover at least the following ground, though you should not limit yourself to it:

- Which paradigms the theory must be able to sort correctly — it should get the same verdict as common sense on things like money (plainly constructed) and, say, electrons (plainly not), and it should say something informative about the contested middle cases, such as psychiatric diagnoses or biological sex, rather than just gesturing at them.
- Which distinctions it must draw and refuse to collapse. For instance: constructing a kind versus constructing our concept or category of it; a kind being constructed versus any particular fact about who falls under it being constructed; and "constructed" versus "illusory," "arbitrary," "easily changed by individual choice," and "bad." My friend's conflation of several of these was the whole problem.
- What the theory must say about the causal reality of constructed kinds — the sense in which being constructed is compatible with being brutally consequential (money can get you fed or evicted; a racial classification can determine how you are treated), so that "it's constructed" cannot function as a debunking argument all by itself.
- How the theory handles feedback: the fact that classifying people in a certain way changes the people classified, which in turn changes the classification. Whether a theory is required to account for this, or whether it is a special feature of human kinds that a theory merely needs to leave room for.
- Whether an adequate theory must explain where constructed kinds get their stability — why money keeps working despite being constructed, why some attempted social changes to categories succeed and others fail, and whether the theory owes an account of who or what does the constructing.
- Whether the theory is required to deliver a verdict on the normative question — whether constructed kinds are things we should retain, revise, or abolish — or whether that is a separate project the theory should stay silent on.

Two further asks. First, flag any pair of desiderata on your own list that you suspect are in tension or cannot be jointly satisfied — for example, I have a hunch that wanting constructed kinds to be fully mind-dependent in some sense and wanting them to be causally robust and beyond individual control may pull against each other, but I cannot make the tension precise. If the list itself turns out to encode incompatible demands, that is the part I most want to see. Second, mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable for any theory worth the name and which are really preferences of one camp — say, people who want construction talk to do emancipatory political work versus people who want it to be a neutral piece of metaphysics.

Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases. A brief note at the end on what general shape of theory survives the list is welcome, but do not turn it into a survey of named positions — I will do that reading myself once I know what I am asking of them.
