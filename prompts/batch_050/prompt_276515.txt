I am an associate editor at a generalist philosophy journal, and a steady trickle of our submissions — perhaps two or three a month — deploy transcendental arguments: arguments that move from some allegedly undeniable feature of thought, experience, or practice to a substantive conclusion about what must exist or must be the case. Our referee pool is inconsistent at evaluating them. Some referees accept any argument with the right Kantian shape; others reject the genre wholesale. Decisions about whether to send these papers out, and how to weigh the reports that come back, currently rest on my judgment, and I would like to replace that with something my assistant editors can run.

Please write a decision procedure that an assistant editor with solid general training but no specialization in Kant or post-Kantian philosophy could apply to a submitted paper in under an hour, in order to reach one of three verdicts: send to referees, send out with a specific question referees must answer, or recommend desk rejection with reasons stated. The procedure should be a sequence of tests with explicit stopping points, grounded in what the philosophical literature actually treats as the live pressure points for this style of argument. At minimum it must:

1. Force a classification of the argument's conclusion — whether it purports to establish something about the world or only something about what we must believe or how we must conceive things — since the standard objection (associated with Stroud) is that the stronger reading needs a concealed bridge principle, typically a verificationist or idealist premise, and the weaker reading leaves the skeptic untouched. The procedure should tell the editor how to locate the bridge, or its absence, in an actual paper, and what verdict each outcome implies.
2. Probe the necessity claim: whether the alleged precondition of experience, discourse, or agency is genuinely indispensable, or whether the author has simply not imagined alternatives. Give the editor a concrete technique for generating counter-hypotheses rather than a bare instruction to "consider alternatives."
3. Check for uniqueness: even granting that some precondition is necessary, whether the author's favored explanation of it is the only one available, or whether a cheaper one (naturalistic, deflationary, merely psychological) does the same work.
4. Detect epistemic circularity: cases where the premise the skeptic is invited to grant already presupposes, in its justification, the conclusion under dispute.
5. Include a self-application step: what happens when the argument's own standards are turned on the argument itself, and how the editor should treat an author who anticipates this and one who does not.
6. Distinguish the genuinely modest uses of the form — Strawson-style mapping of our conceptual scheme with no anti-skeptical pretension — from arguments that wear modest dress but draw immodest conclusions, and say how to tell them apart in practice.

For each test, state what counts as passing, what counts as failing, and what to do when the paper is genuinely ambiguous — I want the points where judgment cannot be eliminated flagged as such, with an instruction about what the editor should write down for me rather than a pretense that the test settles it.

Then run the completed procedure on two hypothetical submissions: first, a paper arguing that the possibility of perceptual experience requires the existence of mind-independent causal order; second, a paper arguing that the norms governing assertion commit any speaker to the existence of other minds. Finally, identify one well-known style of transcendental argument that your procedure handles badly or delivers a verdict on that I am likely to find unwelcome, and explain why — I would rather learn the procedure's limits from you than from a referee's report.
