My flatmate and I argued about algorithmic fairness until two in the morning, and I want to know whether her position is as principled as she claims. Her verdicts on the screening tools we discussed: a tenant-screening tool that approved the same fraction of applicants from two demographic groups and was equally well-calibrated in both (a given risk score meant the same actual default rate), but whose false-rejection rates differed across groups — she called it fair. A tool whose false-rejection and false-acceptance rates were equal across groups, but which approved different fractions of each group and whose scores were not comparably calibrated — she called it unfair. A tool used in a population where the two groups had identical underlying default rates, which satisfied parity, calibration, and equal error rates simultaneously, but which leaned heavily on postal code as an input — she called it unfair, on the grounds that the postcode was doing the work of the protected attribute. Now consider a new case: a tool deployed where the two groups genuinely have different underlying default rates; it is well-calibrated within each group, approves substantially different fractions of the two groups, has unequal false-rejection rates, but uses neither the protected attribute nor any plausible proxy for it. Predict her verdict, identify the rules her earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with and what each predicts here, give your best guess and the reason for preferring it, and tell me which further case would best distinguish the rule you think she is running from its nearest rival.
