I am a third-year PhD student in molecular cell biology, and about twice a month my advisor forwards me a manuscript she has agreed to review, asking for a first-pass report she can build her referee comments on. The question she most wants answered is usually not whether the experiments are technically competent but whether the paper delivers what its title promises: an account of how the phenomenon is produced, rather than one more demonstration that some factor is involved in it, correlated with it, or necessary for it under the conditions tested. Her standard for this distinction comes, by way of the philosophy of biology she likes to cite, from Machamer, Darden, and Craver's 2000 paper in Philosophy of Science, Thinking about Mechanisms.

On my understanding of that account, a mechanism is not a parts list: it consists of entities and activities organized in space and time so as to be productive of the phenomenon in question, so both the doings and the things doing them have to be specified, the organization itself does explanatory work, and a genuine explanation exhibits continuous productive stages from some start condition to the phenomenon rather than mere input-output dependence. I also take them to hold that explanations in practice range from mechanism sketches, containing gaps and black boxes, to more complete schemata, and that what counts as bottoming out is relative to the explanatory purpose. If this gloss misrepresents the account in some way that matters for what follows, correct it before proceeding, and say briefly where the correction comes from in their framework.

What I want is that account turned into a decision procedure I can actually run. The constraints: no more than about forty-five minutes per manuscript, usable by a working scientist who reads figures and methods but not the philosophy literature, and ending in a one-paragraph verdict I can hand to my advisor, who makes the final call and whose name goes on the review. The deliverable is an ordered sequence of tests or gates, each with a question to answer about the paper and instructions for what to do with a yes, a no, and an unclear. I want you to decide which distinctions from the account must survive translation into the procedure — I would guess the entity-activity dualism, the requirement of organization, productive continuity versus mere perturbation-dependence, and the sketch-versus-schema spectrum as an output category rather than a pass-fail line — and which can be dropped without real loss, and to defend both choices. Wherever the procedure simplifies what Machamer, Darden, and Craver actually say, flag the divergence.

The procedure must handle, explicitly, the cases that come up constantly in my subfield: a genome-wide screen followed by characterization of a single hit; a knockout with only partial rescue; an optogenetic gain-of-function experiment showing sufficiency but nothing about the normal case; a computational model that reproduces the phenomenon in silico; and papers where mechanistic language appears only in the discussion while the results establish associations. Then run the procedure on three short hypothetical manuscripts you invent — give each a title and a one-sentence summary first — including one modeled on my own kind of thesis project, a single-cell transcriptomics study plus knockdown validation claiming to explain how a cell population chooses between two fates, where I expect the procedure to return a verdict I will not enjoy. Do not soften that one.

Finally, mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and only concealed — for instance, deciding whether a black box is a missing stage or a missing level of detail — and say what the procedure cannot do at all: I assume it cannot certify that a mechanism is true, cannot assess evidential quality, and may reward satisfying narratives over genuine productive continuity, but I want your own list of expected failure modes. Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each gate as a faithful rendering of the underlying account and in the procedure as a whole, and end by naming the single point — of interpretation or of design — you are least sure of, and what would change your mind about it.
