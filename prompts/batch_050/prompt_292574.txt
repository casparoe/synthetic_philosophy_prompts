I’m a PhD student in philosophy of biology trying to build an exam question for a seminar on functions, and I keep getting stuck on the usual split between causal-role accounts (function = what a part currently does that contributes to some capacity of the containing system) and etiological/selected-effect accounts (function = effect for which the trait was historically selected). Everyone has a stock case for “CR is too permissive” or “etiological is too backward-looking,” so I want a fresher thought experiment that forces the separation in the opposite-feeling direction: CR says the trait definitely has a function and can malfunction, while the selected-effect view says the very same tokens have no function and therefore cannot strictly malfunction, even though they are helping the organism stay alive. Please construct the scenario to order, not just describe the debate.

Constraints: near-future but boringly plausible biology; no aliens, nanobots, perfect gene drives, or magical Lamarckism; keep it under a page for the story itself; make it about an endangered mammal metapopulation in a conservation program if you can, because assisted reproduction is a nice way to uncouple contribution-to-survival from differential reproductive success without fantasy. Engineer two matched populations that are as identical as you can make them in mechanism, anatomy, current ecological role, and fitness contribution, but differ only in how the candidate trait got fixed and whether present reproduction is selection-sensitive. Required verdicts: in Population A, causal-role analysis must say trait T has function F and that a later damaged state of T is dysfunction/disease; selected-effect analysis must deny F in Population A and deny that the damaged state is malfunction in the strict sense, while still allowing some ordinary notion like harm, defect, or veterinary problem. In control Population B the two accounts should converge, so the case isn’t just trading on vagueness about organisms, traits, or environments.

After the scenario, do the audit: define T, F, the system level, and the relevant capacity precisely enough that the verdicts are generated rather than stipulated; state the nearby-history difference and show it is the only load-bearing variable; explain what happens if you remove assisted reproduction, if you let the trait spread by ordinary selection for a few generations, and if you make the trait merely neutral rather than beneficial. Then steal-man both losers: what should the CR theorist say to avoid calling every accidentally useful bit of junk a functional organ, and what should the etiological theorist say to avoid the embarrassing result that a life-saving, well-integrated trait in a managed lineage has no function at all? End by naming the single feature doing the work and whether your case collapses back into a standard selected-effect victory once fitness contribution and heritable differential success are re-coupled. Don’t give me citations; I want the design and why it meets the spec.
