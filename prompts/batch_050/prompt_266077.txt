I've been turning over the evidential-cooperation-in-large-worlds argument, and I think I've found a hole in it — or more likely a hole in my understanding of it. As I understand the idea: in a sufficiently large universe (or multiverse) there exist many agents whose decision-making is correlated with mine — near-copies, or just people running sufficiently similar reasoning — and this correlation makes my cooperating good evidence that they cooperate too, which can swamp the ordinary causal calculus for one-shot prisoner's dilemmas, voting, charity, and so on.

My worry is that the largeness of the world seems like it should destroy the correlation rather than exploit it. Each of those counterparts' decisions is produced by their own brain, environment, and sensory inputs — an enormous mass of causal factors that have nothing to do with me. My decision is at most one tiny piece of evidence about what any of them does, and as the world grows and the intervening factors multiply, that evidential weight should shrink toward zero. In the limit, I should be nearly certain that they'll do whatever they do regardless of my choice — and that sounds like a recipe for defecting and pocketing the locally higher payoff, not for cooperating. In short: doesn't a large world dilute the correlation across too much noise for it to bear any normative weight?

Where does this reasoning go wrong? Or, if it doesn't go wrong, why do proponents of the argument think that multiplying counterparts multiplies the reason to cooperate, rather than dissolving it? I'd especially like to know whether my mistake is in how I'm modeling the correlation between my decision and theirs, or in how I'm moving from "evidence about what they'll do" to "reason for me to act."
