I’m leading a one-evening climate reading group for smart non-specialists who understand the basics of greenhouse warming but mostly picture solar geoengineering as “spray particles, turn down the Sun.” I want to teach them one sharper idea: stratospheric aerosol injection is not a substitute thermostat but a borrowed-temperature commitment that has to be continuously maintained. Ideally they’ll see moral hazard, termination shock, and the question of who gets to decide as three parts of the same control problem rather than as separate objections.

Don’t just explain aerosol injection to me. Plan how I should teach it in about 50 minutes.

- Work out the dependency order. For example, what do they need to grasp about long-lived greenhouse gases versus short-lived aerosols before “masking” versus “removing” warming makes sense? When should I introduce the difference between the eventual temperature level and the speed of warming? At what point can termination shock be framed as accumulated climate debt rather than as stored heat suddenly coming back?
- Pick one carrying example and say why it should carry the session. I’m tempted by a loan analogy, but worry it makes the problem sound mainly financial. Should I use a medical treatment, a dam under pressure, an artificial organ, or something else? Specify what the example must include—maintenance, withdrawal, incentives, authority—and what it must not imply.
- Map the likely misconceptions in the order they’ll arise and where the sequence should defuse each one. I expect at least: aerosols clean up CO2; stopping just returns us gently to normal; termination shock requires some dramatic technical malfunction; moral hazard means ordinary people become careless rather than governments changing their incentives; gradual phase-out solves the problem automatically; and global average cooling means everyone’s climate improves.
- Include the governance layer without turning the session into generic politics. What distinctions should they be able to make between deciding to start, deciding to continue, deciding to change the target temperature, and deciding to stop? How do I get across that a system can be technically adjustable while politically hard to adjust?
- End with one check for understanding that could not be answered by repeating slogans. Maybe give them a fictional scenario involving a regional drought, an election, or an international dispute, and ask what they should be able to identify.

Also tell me what to deliberately leave out at this level—detailed atmospheric chemistry, specific deployment technologies, precise regional rainfall effects, treaty history, etc.—and what warning I should give so they know these omissions are simplifications rather than the whole subject.
