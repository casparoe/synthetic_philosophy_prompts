I'm prepping the methods week of an undergrad seminar on philosophical methodology, and I keep running into the same analogy in the textbooks and blog posts I'm assigning: the thought experiment as the philosopher's laboratory experiment. The idea goes roughly like this -- Galileo didn't need to actually drop anything off the tower of Pisa, he ran the experiment in his head and got a real result; Gettier cases, trolley problems, and Twin Earth work the same way. So the intuition you feel when you read the case is basically your observational data, the way a meter reading is a chemist's data. Armchair philosophy is therefore an empirical discipline, just one with very cheap equipment.

My worry is that this analogy is doing a lot of unearned work. Dennett's point about intuition pumps -- that these little stories are often engineered, sometimes deliberately, to channel your intuition in one direction by choosing what to make vivid and what to leave offstage -- seems to cut against the lab picture pretty hard, since an instrument that's been tuned to give one reading isn't much of an instrument. On the other side I've seen people argue (I think Norton has a version of this) that every thought experiment is really just a disguised argument, so the whole experimental framing is decorative anyway.

Pretend you're a professional philosopher who works on the epistemology of thought experiments and you're helping me get this right before I teach it. Think it through carefully -- brainstorm the candidate correspondences and disanalogies first, then organize. Specifically I want:

1. An explicit mapping of the analogy: what plays the role of the apparatus, the measurement, the observation, the controlled conditions, replication? A table is fine.
2. Which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support (intuitions are data, so the armchair delivers evidence), and which are just superficial differences that every analogy has. I'm thinking here about stuff like: no blinding, no intersubjective calibration, framing and order effects, the fact that the "result" is produced by the very subject reporting it, no new information coming in from the world.
3. Whether the conclusion survives once you grant those disanalogies -- or whether there's a weaker version that does survive (maybe thought experiments are evidence-*adjacent* without being experiments, like proofs, or like legal hypotheticals).
4. If the laboratory analogy fails, what analogy would you put in the syllabus instead, and what would it get right that the lab one gets wrong?

Try to steelman the textbook version before you knock it down, I don't want to teach my students a strawman. And flag any place where you think I'm misstating Dennett or Norton -- I'm going from memory and might be garbling them.
