Write a dialogue, roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, between two political philosophers who have been appointed as advisers to the constitutional convention of a newly founded island state. The island's actuaries and economists have declared that no reliable probabilities can be assigned to how rival basic structures will affect citizens' life prospects: the economy, climate and demography are too novel for the models to yield anything beyond best-case and worst-case ranges. The convention must nevertheless choose, now, the distributive principles to entrench in its constitution. One adviser is a Rawlsian, who argues that the choice should be made on maximin grounds: the convention should select whichever arrangement makes the worst-off position as good as possible. The other is a Harsanyian, who argues that rational choosers behind a veil of ignorance would instead maximise expected utility, treating the prospect of occupying each social position as equally probable.

The dialogue should satisfy the following conditions:

1. Both advisers are equally competent. Neither may be a strawman, and each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talk past them. Exposition should emerge through disagreement, not through either character lecturing the reader.
2. The Rawlsian must invoke and defend the three conditions under which Rawls took maximin to be the rational choice: that knowledge of probabilities is impossible or extremely insecure; that the chooser cares little for gains above the minimum that maximin secures; and that the rejected alternatives involve outcomes one could hardly accept. The Harsanyian must probe whether the island situation really satisfies all three.
3. The Harsanyian must press the charge that maximin is extreme — that applied consistently it would sacrifice arbitrarily large gains for everyone else to avoid a marginally worse minimum — and must defend the equiprobability assumption against the objection that it smuggles unwarranted information into the veil of ignorance. The Rawlsian must answer both points.
4. At some stage one adviser proposes minimax regret as a compromise principle. The other objects that regret is menu-dependent: adding or removing an option nobody would ever choose can reverse the recommendation. The first must either defend regret against this objection or concede the point and explain what follows for the debate.
5. The dialogue should end without a cheap victory: either each adviser states what evidence or argument would change their mind, or they jointly identify the single proposition on which their disagreement ultimately turns — for instance, whether the veil of ignorance models a decision under ignorance or a decision under risk.
6. Set the exchange in a lightly sketched scene (a committee room, cooling coffee, a map of the island pinned to the wall) with naturalistic speech.

Use British English throughout.
