I've got to hand in a short position statement for a workshop on interpersonal comparisons of welfare (long story, the organizer is an economist and I refuse to be the philosopher who shows up with nothing commital), so let me dump my current view on you and you can tell me whether it hangs together before I embarrass myself.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Welfarism: for policy purposes, the betterness of outcomes is entirely a matter of individual welfare. Desert, rights side-constraints, procedural values — real, maybe, but not part of the betterness ranking.

2. Welfare is preference satisfaction, and preference is revealed by choice. Utility functions are bookkeeping devices for orderings. Even the von Neumann-Morgenstern stuff only gets you cardinality relative to lotteries, which encodes attitudes toward risk, not how much someone wants something. There is no psychological magnitude, no "intensity of desire," sitting behind the numbers.

3. And yet interpersonal comparisons are obviously possible in ordinary life. When I give the last slice to the friend who's been talking about this pizza place all week rather than the friend who shrugs at pizza, more preference gets satisfied, and I can know that.

4. Because of 2, the utilitarian sum-ranking has no fact of the matter behind it. But prioritarianism is still coherent and correct: weighting the worse off only requires knowing who is worse of than whom, not by how much, and ordinal level comparisons give me that.

5. The economist trick of rescaling everyone's utility onto a 0-1 scale over their own feasible set and then adding across people is circular: the choice of normalization smuggles in exactly the cardinal comparisons it pretends to deliver.

So: does this package survive contact with itself? My own anxiety is mostly about 4, but I might be wrong about that, so audit the whole set rather than just humoring me. Tell me which pairs genuinely conflict, how bad each conflict is, and what the cheapest fix for each would be. Also flag any pair that looks like a problem but dissolves once you're careful, because I'd rather not revise things that aren't broken. And if you had to pick exactly one commitment to revise or drop to get the most consistency per unit of philosophical pain, which would it be and why?
