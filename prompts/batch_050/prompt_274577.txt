I’m preparing the opening exercise for an upper-level seminar on moral ideals, partiality, and demandingness. Every year my students treat moral sainthood as a simple yes-or-no question about whether an extraordinarily altruistic person would be pleasant company, which flattens the discusion badly. Design a sorting instrument, best understood as a decision tree, that takes someone from the thought that morality matters enormously to a specific position on whether maximal moral goodness is desirable. Use nine diagnostic questions, each phrased as a concrete case or forced choice that someone could answer without already knowing the relevant terminology. Among the cases, please include a concert cellist who could do more good by abandoning music, a parent deciding between a special benefit for their child and a slightly greater benefit for strangers, a saintly agent who reliably does the right thing but feels no affection for anyone, a person whose moral efforts leave room for one otherwise useless private hobby, and an agent for whom perfect impartial concern is psychologically impossible.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from famous names. The branching questions should separately test whether we are evaluating an action, a character, a whole life, or a state of the world; whether moral goodness is something to maximize directly or merely a regulative ideal; whether moral reasons override all other reasons; whether losses of love, creativity, spontaneity, and personal projects count as moral costs or as nonmoral costs that can still matter; whether partial attachments are independently valuable, instrumentally useful, or just excusable weaknesses; and whether an ideal can be worth admiring even when nobody could or should try to realize it. End with exactly eight leaves, giving each a descriptive name and one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment. The leaves should include an unqualified defender of sainthood, a tragic sainthood view on which sainthood remains best despite real nonmoral losses, a two-level view on which sainthood is a compass but not a decision procedure, a partiality-constrained saint view, a pluralist moderation view, an integrity-based anti-saint view, a feasibility skeptic who doubts that maximal moral goodness is coherent for human beings, and one position that emerges from your own questions rather than from my list. Note any coherent-looking combination of answers that remains unoccupied and briefly explain whether it is unstable, unattractive, or simply neglected. If two of the eight leaves cannot actually be separated by the nine cases, say so instead of pretending the tree is sharper than it is. Although this is a classification exercise, please answer in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or ASCII flowchart. Just narrate the branches in the way one might explain the tree in a long email to a colleague.
