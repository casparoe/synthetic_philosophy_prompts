My reading group has spent most of the semester stuck on the demarcation problem, and two members in particular have been going in circles: one is a committed Popperian who thinks falsifiability is the line between science and non-science, full stop, and the other is a Bayesian-ish Kuhn fan who thinks the whole idea of a sharp demarcation criterion is a mistake and that what actually distinguishes good science is a cluster of practices (progressive research programs, honest updating, resisting ad hoc rescues) rather than any logical property of the statements themselves. They've agreed to try an adversarial collaboration instead of another shouting match, and I've volunteered to help, so I want you to draft the document they'd produce.

Here's what I think it needs to contain. First, the claims both of them can actually sign, which is harder than it sounds: things like unfalsifiable doesn't mean false (meaningless metaphysics vs. true-but-untestable claims), astrology does make risky predictions and keeps losing them, so naive falsifiability classifies it as science and that's a problem for someone, both sides hate ad hoc rescue maneuvers but disagree about whether they're a logical sin or a practical one, and both accept that no theory is ever falsified by a single observation without auxiliary assumptions (the Duhem problem), though they draw very different morals from it. Then the disagreements, stated precisely enough that each would accept the wording of their own position: is demarcation a property of statements or of historical practices and communities, is the Duhem problem a fatal blow to falsificationism or just a reason to treat falsification as a methodological decision rather than a logical event, and what is demarcation even for -- a court deciding whether to admit expert testimony or a funder deciding between string theory and parapsychology needs something usable, and the two camps may be answering different questions without noticing.

The most important part: for each crux, name a concrete case or piece of evidence both agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. I've suggested they use how each criterion handles three cases -- parapsychology experiments that keep getting negative results and keep generating excuses, string theory's critics calling it unfalsifiable and its defenders saying that's a caricature, and the replication crisis in psychology, which arguably shows falsificationist machinery working as advertised in a field some people still want to call a science. If part of their dispute turns out to be verbal (I suspect the word criterion is doing a lot of work), say which part and why. One format request, since this will be a long email to the group rather than a formatted document: write it entirely in continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables -- just paragraphs, like a letter, even for the parts where a table would be tempting.
