I am a novelist revising an ensemble novel divided between a depopulating agricultural county and a metropolitan hospital. I want the central conflict to arise from two defensible ways of life rather than from caricatures of rural traditionalism and urban sophistication. To that end, compare the following ideal types as ethical outlooks, not as sociological descriptions or partisan labels:

1. Rooted stewardship. A person’s identity and dignity are formed through durable obligations to particular people, places, and practices. Dependability, inherited knowledge, care for land, and willingness to sacrifice individual preference for communal continuity are central virtues. Mobility and credentialism are suspect when they detach people from relationships for which no impartial institution can substitute.

2. Mobile professionalism. A person should be free to revise inherited commitments and develop transferable abilities wherever they are most useful. Dignity consists in self-authorship and competent contribution rather than ascriptive belonging. Impartial standards, credentials, and portable rights are valuable because local attachments can become parochial, coercive, or unfair to outsiders.

Do not assume that the first outlook is simply rural and working-class or that the second is simply urban and professional-class. Treat geography and class as pressures that may make one outlook more available, while allowing either to be adopted elsewhere.

First, reconstruct the strongest version of each outlook: its conception of dignity, its account of what makes an obligation real, the kind of knowledge it trusts, and the injury it considers most characteristic of a bad life. Then compare their verdicts in four cases: an adult child leaves a struggling family business for an excellent distant career; trusted local knowledge conflicts with credentialed medical advice; a town can preserve its central employer only by accepting environmental damage and excluding newcomers from the benefits; and an informal network of mutual aid is replaced by fairer but impersonal professional procedures. In each case, distinguish disagreements about facts from disagreements about value, identify what each outlook might rationalize, and ask who is made to bear the cost.

Finally, evaluate the two outlooks with respect to stable belonging, individual self-authorship, duties to strangers, resistance to local prejudice, correction of false beliefs, care for future generations, and the tendency of each side to conceal class power as virtue. Conclude whether one outlook is more defensible as a general ethic or whether each has authority over different kinds of decisions. If you propose a hybrid, state a non-slogan rule for adjudicating conflicts between rooted obligation and mobile freedom, and specify what each outlook would have to surrender.
