A friend and I spent most of last Saturday arguing about the Special Composition Question — under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? She defends unrestricted composition (any plurality whatsoever forms a sum, including one whose members are her left shoe, the moons of Jupiter, and the year 1066), and I kept insisting that composition must be restricted somehow, but every time she asked me what a *good* restricted answer would even have to look like, I retreated to examples. I want to stop doing that. Before we argue about which answer is right, I want the standard of adequacy itself written down.

Please respond as a metaphysician who has thought carefully about material constitution and mereology, and take your time. Begin by brainstorming a long list of candidate requirements — anything anyone has ever demanded of an answer to the question — and only then prune, consolidate, and organize that list into a final specification. The final specification is the deliverable; the brainstorm can be brief.

The specification should state, for each requirement: what it demands, and the case or consideration that motivates it. Along the way, the following test cases must appear somewhere, since they are what my friend and I actually fought over: (a) a pocket watch assembled screw by screw, where at no single step does it seem to go from non-watch to watch; (b) a swarm of midges that coordinates its flight for ninety seconds and then disperses, where I claimed the swarm exists during those ninety seconds and she claimed nothing new comes into being; (c) the pages of my late grandfather's diary, now distributed among four relatives' attics, which she says still compose a (scattered) object and I say no longer compose anything. For each case, say which requirement it motivates or which candidate requirements it discriminates between.

For every item on the final list, mark it as either non-negotiable (an answer that fails it simply fails to answer the question) or as a preference of a particular camp (something a nihilist, a universalist, an organicist, or a brutal-composition theorist would insist on but that an opponent can coherently waive). I am especially suspicious that some popular demands — e.g., that an answer "respect common sense" about ordinary objects, or that composition never be vague — are camp preferences masquerading as ground rules; say explicitly if you think so.

Two further demands. First, if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, identify it and show the conflict — my working hypothesis is that one cannot simultaneously have restricted composition, absolutely no vagueness about when composition occurs, and no arbitrary-looking cutoff in a continuous series like the watch assembly, and I want to know whether that triad really is impossible or whether there is a way out. That is the part I most want to see. Second, name the single requirement that excludes the most live contenders, but keep this brief — the criteria and their motivations matter to me, not a scorecard of existing positions.

Finally, on your own commitments: for the two or three requirements you mark non-negotiable whose status is most contestable, state what consideration or counterexample would lead you to move them into the camp-preference column, and indicate where your specification as a whole is most likely to be smuggling in my friend's universalism or my own folk conservatism. I would rather have a specification with its biases confessed than one presented as neutral.
