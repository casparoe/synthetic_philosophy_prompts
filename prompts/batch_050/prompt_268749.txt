I'm a novelist working on a near-future book, and I'm stuck on a scene I want to build around a digital-mind dilemma, but I want the dilemma engineered properly rather than just vibes. So I want you to construct me a thought experiment to spec.

Here's what the case has to do. Most of the digital-minds cases people reach for go in one direction: some chatbot or android behaves brilliantly, talks movingly about its feelings, and the puzzle is whether there's really anyone home. I want the hard direction. Build a case where a system plausibly has an inner life but shows almost nothing of it on the outside -- so that views tying moral status to phenomenal experience say it matters, and views tying moral status to displayed capacities (communication, agency, preferences expressed in behaviour, that sort of thing) say it doesn't, or at least say it matters far less. The drama should come from the fact that nobody can ever recieve a message from it that settles the question.

Constraints, because my editor will kill me otherwise: no mind uploading, no brain scans that can read off consciousness from the substrate, no exotic physics -- just ordinary near-future tech a reader will buy. The scenario should be about a paragraph, maybe two, and it has to be something I could plausibly drop a character into (say, a technician deciding whether to switch a system off) rather than an abstract setup. The disagreement between the two views must fall out of the case itself, not be asserted by the narration -- if a reader finishes it and immediately knows which side is right, the case has failed.

Then do the checking part, since I can't be trusted to: state the verdict each of the two broad views gives and why, name the single feature of the case that's doing all the work, and say what happens to the disagreement if I remove that feature (does it just collapse into the standard chatbot case?). Also tell me the strongest move a defender of each view would make to stop the case from counting against them -- I need both characters' arguments to sound like the winning one in the scene.

Finally, one line on what you think the case is actually good for dramatically, i.e. where it leaves the reader. Don't worry about naming philosophers or citing papers, I just want the machinery to work.
