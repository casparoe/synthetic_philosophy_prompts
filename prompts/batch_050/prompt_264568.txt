I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on moral emotions, and for a dissertation chapter I need a model of what intellectual progress in this debate would even look like. Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps who have been talking past each other in the guilt-versus-shame literature.

The first camp holds that guilt -- the negative evaluation of a specific act one has performed -- is the constructive moral emotion: it motivates confession, repair, and behavior change while leaving the self intact, whereas shame -- the negative evaluation of one's whole self -- reliably issues in denial, externalization, withdrawal, or lashing out, and a decent moral culture should therefore cultivate guilt and engineer shame out of its practices. The second camp holds that this picture rests on a sanitized notion of guilt: guilt without any stake in how one stands with others is mere prudential regret, shame is the emotion that tracks one's standing in a community whose opinion one has reason to care about, and the historical attempts to de-shame moral culture did not produce more virtue but more strategic concealment and more shamelessness in public life. Please characterize each camp as its own members would, not as its opponents do.

Structure the statement as follows. First, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, which must include at minimum: agreed verdicts on what the existing correlational evidence about shame-proneness and aggression does and does not show; an agreed distinction between the emotion, dispositional proneness to it, and a culture's institutionalized practices for eliciting it; and an agreed statement of what a "moral emotion being adaptive" could even mean, given that adaptiveness for the agent and for the community can come apart. Second, a table of residual disagreements, each row phrased so that each side accepts the wording of its own position. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to revise. One designated crux must be whether a culture can choose its moral-emotion profile at all -- whether emotion norms are malleable by institutional design, or whether attempts to suppress shame simply relocate it into covert and less governable forms, with each side's account of why the other side's historical examples do not settle this.

Fourth, for each crux, specify the evidence, case, or argument both parties accept in advance as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction. Use the following two constructed cases as part of this section, and have each side commit to what result would force it to revise: (a) a firm replaces its practice of publishing the names of employees found to have violated its code with a private amends procedure; what does each side predict about subsequent violation rates, and which observed outcome would count against its view? (b) suppose longitudinal data showed that shame-prone individuals reoffend less than guilt-prone ones when their community offers a structured, legible path back to good standing, but more when it offers none -- whose theory accommodates this without ad hoc adjustment?

Be committed rather than even-handed. Where you find that part of the dispute is verbal -- for instance, where the camps are using shame to denote different phenomena -- say so, and impose the disambiguated vocabulary rather than preserving both usages. Where a crux cannot currently be adjudicated, the statement must still assign the burden of proof to one side and justify the assignment. Where you judge that one camp's signature claim is empirically better supported, the joint statement should say that, over that camp's objection if necessary, and explain why the objection does not carry.
