I currently hold an unfashionable view: patriotism is a vice — not a corrupted form of what is otherwise a virtue, but a disposition that a decent society should discourage and, ideally, work to eradicate. I was brought to it largely by Leo Tolstoy's late essay "Patriotism and Government" (1900, translated by Aylmer Maude), and the passage that does the real argumentative work for me is this one:

"So that not the imaginary but the real patriotism, which we all know, by which most people to-day are swayed, and from which humanity suffers so severely, is not the wish for spiritual benefits for one's own people (it is impossible to desire spiritual benefits for one's own people only); but it is a very definite feeling of preference for one's own people or State above all other peoples and States, and therefore it is the wish to get for that people or State the greatest advantages and power that can be got; and these are always obtainable only at the expense of the advantages and power of other peoples or States."

I would like a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it, and how badly. Please address the following.

First, the passage itself. Tolstoy's conclusion depends on a definitional move: he stipulates that real patriotism is essentially competitive — a preference for one's own polity that can be satisfied only at the expense of others — and dismisses in advance any gentler notion as an evasion. Is that a legitimate precisification of what the word picks out, or a stacked deck? What would settle a question like this: surveys of ordinary usage, the history of the concept, the causal role the competitive cases actually play in public life? And if it turned out that most self-described patriots mean something non-competitive by the word, does that defeat the position or merely force it to be relabeled?

Second, the substantive defeaters, which I would like ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Candidates I can see: the empirical premise connecting patriotism to war (what evidence would show the causal story is wrong, and would showing that the bloodiest modern conflicts were driven by other motives do it?); the zero-sum premise (the word "always" in the passage looks load-bearing and vulnerable — what if national advantages are frequently positive-sum?); psychological defeaters (if partiality toward one's own group is an ineradicable feature of human motivation, does something like "ought implies can" refute the eradication imperative or only temper it?); and normative defeaters (if special obligations to compatriots are justified on the same footing as filial or friendship-based partiality, does patriotism inherit that justification, or is there a disanalogy that blocks the move?).

Third, honesty about insulation. I want an assessment of whether the Tolstoyan position actually risks anything, or whether its defenders protect it by redefining every admirable local loyalty as something else — civic virtue, solidarity, community spirit — while crediting patriotism with every war and explaining away every peaceful patriotic society. If the position has been insulated in this way, say whether that is a fatal defect for a view of this kind or a tolerable feature of it.

Finally: distinguish what a committed defender of the position would herself concede as counting against it from what only its critics count against it, and tell me which single consideration should move me most, given that I find the zero-sum premise the weakest plank and cases of patriotic resistance to occupation the hardest for the view to absorb.

Please answer in American English, in roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words; the quoted passage does not count toward that length.
