ok so background: longtime LW reader, was there for the foom debates, read the sequences when they were still on overcome bias, etc. Lately I've been arguing with a friend about whether "the alignment problem" is actually one problem or like three problems in a trenchcoat, and in the course of this argument I went back and reread Turing's Computing Machinery and Intelligence (Mind, 1950) and got stuck on a bit near the end, in the learning machines section, that I don't think I've ever seen anyone quote in alignment discussions.

To paraphrase it, since I'm pasting from a paywalled scan: Turing says that an important feature of a learning machine is that its teacher will often be very largely ignorant of what's actually going on inside it, though he may still be able to predict its behavior to some extent. And he says this applies most strongly to a machine that's been educated up from some child-machine design rather than programmed directly.

Here's why this broke my brain a little. On the one hand this is Turing basically conceding, in 1950, that if you *train* a machine instead of programming it, you get something whose internals you can't inspect and whose behavior you can only partially predict. That sounds a LOT like the modern inner/outer alignment split: even if you solve the outer problem (figure out what objective you actually want to train toward), there's a further problem that the trained thing might end up pursuing something else entirely, and you can't check by looking inside. In the same section he even sketches the evolutionary version of the idea, with random changes to the child-machine playing the role of mutations, which reads today like a weirdly prescient description of SGD.

But on the other hand, this is also Mr. Imitation Game, the guy whose whole move in that paper is that we should stop asking what a machine "really" thinks and only care about how it behaves. So maybe he'd say the "inner" problem is exactly the kind of metaphysical confusion he was trying to get rid of, and that a model that behaves aligned in every situation you can test just is aligned, full stop?

So the question: if you could sit Turing down in front of the current debate, what do you think he'd say about whether alignment is one problem or several? Would the teacher-ignorance remark have pushed him to predict something like mesa-optimization, or would he have dug in on the behavioralist reading? Ground your answer in the actual paper (the child machine stuff, the education-via-reward-and-punishment stuff, whatever's relevant), not just general Turing vibes. And if you think the two readings I just gave are both wrong and the passage is doing something else entirely, say that instead.
