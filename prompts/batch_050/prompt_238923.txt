I am assembling the problem set for a graduate seminar on scientific realism, and I want one exercise devoted to verisimilitude — the notion that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another. My interest was rekindled by Peirce's entry on truth in Baldwin's Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology (1901), where he suggests that an abstract statement may possess truth partly by confessing its own inaccuracy and one-sidedness, as if admitting error were itself an ingredient of truth rather than a surrender of it. I want the exercise to test whether that concession can be made rigorous, against the background of the two standard objections: the result associated with Tichý that Popper's content criterion in Conjectures and Refutations cannot order two false theories at all, and David Miller's 1974 argument that comparative closeness-to-truth judgments are not invariant under a mere change of primitive predicates.

Construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. (a) Fix an everyday subject matter — the week's weather in one town, the contents of a colleague's desk drawer, a village bus timetable — and state the complete relevant truth about it as a short conjunction of atomic claims in a fixed vocabulary; then state two rival false theories, each a conjunction in the same vocabulary, such that the first gets strictly fewer of these claims wrong than the second, so that any competent reader's intuitive verdict is that the first theory is closer to the truth. (b) Apply Popper's criterion — theory A is at least as close to the truth as theory B just in case B's true consequences are included among A's and A's false consequences among B's, with strictness in at least one direction — and derive, for your two theories, the result that the criterion fails to rank them; do not cite Tichý's theorem, reproduce the reasoning on the constructed case itself. (c) Introduce a second vocabulary for the same subject matter by redefining predicates in Miller's manner (for example, a predicate that applies to a day just in case it is hot if and only if it rains), translate the truth and both theories into it, and show that the naive error count now reverses, ranking the second theory as closer. Constraints: no probabilities, no similarity measures over possible worlds, no formalism beyond elementary propositional logic, and the scenario itself should run to no more than 120 words. No verdict may be stipulated; each must be derived from the criterion in question. The purpose of the problem set is to decide whether verisimilitude should be presented to students as a live formal notion or as a cautionary tale, so what counts as cheating is a construction that asserts the instability rather than generating it.

Finally, give a committed diagnosis rather than a menu of options. Take a definite position on the standard rescue, which restricts admissible vocabularies to those whose predicates are natural in something like Lewis's or Sider's sense, so that Miller-style translations are ruled out as gerrymandered: does this rescue objective verisimilitude, or does it merely relocate the problem into the metaphysics of naturalness, where it fares no better? Identify the single structural feature of your constructed case on which the instability depends, and state what happens to your conclusion if that feature is removed — in particular, whether the case collapses back into a straightforward illustration that Popper's criterion was simply too weak. And say, in light of all this, whether Peirce's idea that a statement can earn its truth by confessing its own inaccuracy admits of a rigorous sense or should be retired as a suggestive metaphor. I would rather have a position I can argue against in class than a balanced survey.
