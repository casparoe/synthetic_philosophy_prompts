I help run a medieval philosophy reading group, and for the past year our two most committed members — one a convinced occasionalist in the tradition of al-Ghazālī's Ash'arite theology, the other an Aristotelian causal realist of roughly the Thomist/dispositionalist stripe — have been rehearsing the same argument about fire and cotton without progress. I've asked them to stop debating and instead produce a joint statement, adversarial-collaboration style: a document both could actually sign. They are hopelessly deadlocked even over how to open it, so I'm outsourcing the drafting to you.

Write that joint statement. It should contain, first, a numbered list of claims both parties can accept — about what observation actually delivers in a case like fire contacting cotton (paraphrase the famous example; do not quote any text), about what Hume later established regarding our epistemic access to necessary connection, about the difference between regularities and the metaphysical ground of regularities, and about what each side's view does and does not imply for the possibility of miracles, scientific prediction, and human responsibility. Second, a section stating their remaining disagreements, each formulated in wording that both would accept as a fair statement of the position being attributed to them — this is where the work is, since the realist keeps accusing the occasionalist of making the world a puppet show and the occasionalist keeps accusing the realist of idolatry of secondary causes, and neither formulation is one the accused would sign. Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect, but want you to test, that one crux is whether denying necessary connection entails denying real efficacy, another is whether the realist's causal powers are anything more than the occasionalist's divine custom redescribed, and a third is the status of human action — whether occasionalism can accommodate agency without collapsing into either determinism by God or an ad hoc exception, and whether the realist's own account of will fares any better. For each crux you endorse, specify what argument, thought experiment, or consideration both parties agree in advance would bear on it, and record what each side predicts or concedes about it beforehand.

Finally, end the statement with a section both sign reluctantly: an honest accounting of which portions of their year-long dispute were, on reflection, verbal rather than substantive — for instance, whether they ever actually disagreed about what happens when fire touches cotton, as opposed to about who gets to call what a cause. If you conclude that some portion of the disagreement is merely terminological, name it and justify the verdict; if you conclude that a residual, genuinely metaphysical disagreement survives all disambiguation, state it as precisely as you can in one or two sentences.
