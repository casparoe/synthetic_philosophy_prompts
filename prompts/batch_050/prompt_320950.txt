I have recently taken a seat on the grants committee of a foundation that funds projects justified by long-horizon forecasts -- demographic projections, resource-depletion models, technology-adoption curves, that sort of thing -- and I have discovered that the committee has no principled way to evaluate the track records cited in applications. The same few episodes keep being invoked as precedent: Malthus, whose population argument was mocked for a century and is now periodically declared vindicated; Ehrlich's population-bomb forecasts; the Club of Rome's limits-to-growth scenarios; Keynes's prediction about the fifteen-hour week. Every applicant claims their preferred failed-looking forecast was merely early, and every skeptic claims that "early" is what people say instead of "wrong." I cannot adjudicate these claims without a standard, so I would like you to write the standard first.

Give me the desiderata that an adequate account of long-run predictive success and failure would have to meet -- the requirements for any principled answer to the question of when a prediction counts as falsified, when as merely premature, when as rescued by an auxiliary assumption, and when as genuinely vindicated. For each desideratum, state the requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it, and whether you take it to be non-negotiable or instead a preference of one school of thought. Specifically, I would like the list to address at least the following:

- The historical cases the account must classify in a way that survives scrutiny. I assume any account that declares Malthus simply right, or simply wrong, has failed to do the work; likewise for Ehrlich and for Keynes on leisure. State what the correct classification of each must at least be sensitive to, without prejudging the verdict.
- The distinctions the account cannot collapse: between a conditional prediction whose antecedent failed and an unconditional one that missed; between a wrong point estimate and a wrong trend; between a prediction that failed because the mechanism was mistaken and one that failed because a parameter moved; between a theory that was rescued and a theory that was retrofitted.
- The results from the philosophy and practice of science the account cannot contradict -- for instance, what follows from the fact that any failed forecast can be preserved by adjusting background assumptions, and what follows from the calibration literature's insistence that a forecaster who is eventually right about everything is usually right about nothing.
- The constraint I care most about: the account must give "right but early" real content without making it unfalsifiable. If some subset of your desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable -- for instance, if rewarding patience toward delayed predictions is in tension with penalizing vagueness about horizons -- then say so explicitly and identify the minimal conflict. That is the part I most want to see.

Conclude with two things. First, which single requirement knocks out the largest number of the informal standards my committee members actually use. Second, a brief scorecard -- no more than a paragraph each -- of how a strict falsificationist standard, a Lakatos-style research-programme standard, and a pure calibration/scoring-rule standard perform against the list. The scorecard is secondary; the specification is the deliverable, since I intend to bring it to the committee as the basis for policy.
