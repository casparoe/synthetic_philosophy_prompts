I'm a retired field biologist who's fallen down the philosophy-of-history rabbit hole in my old age, and I'm drafting an essay arguing that non-human things -- germs, climate swings, volcanic eruptions -- should count as real actors in history, not just scenery. Before I write any more, I want a spec to write against. So: what does an account of historical explanation that grants causal (maybe even agential?) roles to things like the 1815 Tambora eruption or mosquito-borne disease have to deliver to count as adequate? Give me the desiderata -- which historical cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect (trigger vs. cause, enabling condition vs. agent?), which objections it has to survive, above all the environmental-determinism charge I keep getting hit with -- with the consideration behind each requirement, and say which are non-negotiable. As a test case I keep circling: does a late frost that ruins a harvest get a line in the causal story of the riot that follows, or does all the explanatory work belong to the rioters' grievances? Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- if treating a volcano as an actor while keeping human choice explanatorily central is incoherent, I'd rather know now. Then a quick note on how the main existing approaches do against the list. American English please, and keep it conversational.
