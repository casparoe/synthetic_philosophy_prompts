I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of language, and I'm trying to settle my commitments on inferential-role semantics before I commit to them in my qualifying paper. Roughly, I hold all of the following:

(1) The conceptual content of an expression is constituted by its inferential role — by the inferences it licenses as a conclusion and the ones that license it as a premise, including the material (non-logical) inferences embedded in the practice.
(2) The norms governing these inferences are social and instituted by practice: understanding a claim is a matter of being able to keep score on the commitments and entitlements of interlocutors, not of grasping abstract objects.
(3) Logical vocabulary is semantically special but unproblematic: its role is to make antecedently material inferential relations explicit, and connectives can be given a purely inferential definition via their introduction and elimination rules without further constraint.
(4) A semantic theory must be compositional: it has to explain how the contents of novel sentences are determined by the contents of their parts.
(5) Observation reports like "that is red" are fully covered by the same story — their content is just as inferentially constituted as anything else's, with no non-inferential contribution doing semantic work.
(6) Speakers with substantially different inferential commitments — say, users of "mass" before and after Einstein — can nonetheless genuinely disagree with each other rather than merely talking past one another.

Which of these are actually in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each genuine tension? Please rank them rather than treating them as on a par, and for each say what the least costly revision would be. I especially want to know whether there's a single commitment whose abandonment would resolve the most.
