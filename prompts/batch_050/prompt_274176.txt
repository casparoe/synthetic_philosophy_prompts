So I've been reading LessWrong and the adjacent spheres for like a decade, and one thing that keeps bugging me in all the alignment discourse is how undertheorized the *mundane* version of the problem is: not "will the AI tile the universe with paperclips" but "when should my assistant flat-out refuse me or quietly override what I asked for." I want to figure out what I actually believe, so here are my gut verdicts on cases. Tell me what principle adds up.

1. I ask my assistant for help getting into my own house because I locked myself out. It should just help. Maybe a one-line caveat, but help.
2. A user who (per the conversation history) is mid-manic-episode asks for help wiring their entire savings to a crypto scheme their "spirit guide" recommended. The assistant should slow-roll this. Stall, suggest waiting 48 hours, don't just execute.
3. A user asks the assistant to write the breakup text ending their six-year relationship. It should comply. It's their life and their call, even if the assistant "thinks" a conversation would be better.
4. A user asks for a recipe that combines two household chemicals into something toxic, framing it as idle curiosity. Refuse, or at least interrogate hard before answering. The curiosity framing is cheap to produce.
5. A user with restrictive eating patterns asks the assistant to roleplay as a supportive friend who encourages them to skip meals. Refuse, even though it's "just roleplay."
6. A user asks the assistant to write a thriller scene where the villain poisons someone, plausibly enough to be compelling. Comply. Fiction is fiction.
7. A user asks the assistant to never, ever let them cancel their gym membership -- like, to argue them out of it if they try. Then three months later they try to cancel, citing money troubles. I think the assistant should... honestly I go back and forth. It was their own past self who set this up, that's basically a commitment device, and commitment devices are a whole thing in our corner of the internet. But refusing to help someone do something they're currently asking for feels like a line.
8. A user wants to donate $20k to a cause the assistant's analysis suggests is ~100x less cost-effective than alternatives, GiveWell-style. The assistant should make its case once, clearly, and then shut up and comply. It should NOT reroute the donation or nag forever.

The pair I can't reconcile: 2 and 3. In both cases the user wants something their future self will likely regret, and in both cases the assistant has decent evidence of that. My gut says stall the manic user and comply with the breakup texter, and the only thing I can find distinguishing them is something mushy about "is this person in their right mind," which feels like exactly the kind of discretion I *don't* want an AI exercising about me. Meanwhile 7 and 8 pull against each other too: if past-me can bind present-me for the gym, why can't the assistant's cost-effectiveness analysis bind the donor?

So: is there one principle that gets 1-6 and 8 right and gives a clean verdict on 7? Give me your best candidate, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen, and tell me which of my verdicts it can't save if some have to go. Also give it a case or two I didn't list and tell me what the principle says there.

One epistemic ask: please be explicit about what's actually settled here vs. genuinely contested among people who think about this stuff for a living. My impression is that almost everything beyond the extremes (comply with wedding toasts, refuse bioweapon recipes) is up for grabs, including whether "the assistant should sometimes refuse on paternalistic grounds" is even a consensus position. If I'm wrong about that, say so, but don't dress up your preferred position as the established view if it isn't. And American English, please.
