I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm applying for my employer's six-month research sabbatical to work on a philosophy project. Here's my half-formed idea. Most discussion of brain–computer interfaces and mental privacy frames the problem as protecting a specially sacred zone: the contents of your mind, the last place that is truly private. I suspect this framing is a mistake. The thing that actually distinguishes consumer neurotechnology from, say, my browsing history is not that it exposes mental content but that it lets a third party run statistical inferences about my mental states and dispositions that I could not run about myself -- so the interesting asymmetry is one of inferential power, not of access. I want the proposal to test whether cognitive liberty is better understood as a right against certain kinds of inference than as a right against access to one's thoughts, and what follows for how regulation should be designed. Write the proposal: a precise statement of the question, an explanation of why both the 'inner sanctum' framing and the 'neural data is just ordinary sensitive data' framing leave it open, the method and what I would actually do month by month, and what would count as the project failing. One firm request: don't hedge. Take a definite position on which framing is most promising and build the proposal around that choice, giving reasons, rather than presenting the options neutrally and leaving me to pick.
