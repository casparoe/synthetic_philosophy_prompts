I work in machine learning, mostly on training dynamics and interpretability for large neural networks. I'm fluent in that vocabulary -- loss landscapes, gradient descent as an optimizer, architectural priors and inductive biases, regularization, spurious correlations, emergent capabilities that nobody trained directly, feature attribution methods. I've recently been reading about the debate in evolutionary biology between adaptationism and its critics, especially the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique: the charge that biologists too often assume every trait is an adaptation shaped directly by selection for its current function, tell "just-so stories" about what each trait is for, and neglect traits that arose as byproducts of other structures, as consequences of developmental or architectural constraint, or through drift rather than selection.

Here is what I want. Restate that debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in. My sense is that the adaptationist is like someone who assumes every behavior of a trained network is there because it minimized the loss, and the spandrels critics are like interpretability researchers pointing out that plenty of what networks do is an artifact of architecture, initialization, optimizer quirks, or the structure of the training distribution rather than anything the loss selected for. But I want this done properly, not as a loose analogy. Specifically:

1. Translate the core adaptationist commitments (selection as the dominant explanation of traits, the heuristic of asking what a trait is for, optimization thinking) into claims about trained models.
2. Translate the main critical categories -- spandrel/byproduct, exaptation (a trait co-opted for a function it didn't arise for), developmental constraint, drift -- into ML counterparts, one by one. For byproducts in particular, say whether emergent capabilities, spurious features, and double descent each count, or only some.
3. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the biological debate map cleanly onto training dynamics, which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all. For example, I suspect that "function" and "trait inheritance" do real work in biology that has no clean counterpart in a model trained from scratch, and that natural selection and gradient descent differ in ways (lineages, populations, heritability) that break the analogy somewhere. Tell me where.
4. Finally, give me your verdict: does the residue show that my ML framing is missing something the biologists are genuinely tracking, or that part of the biological debate is less substantive than it looks? And state explicitly what would change your mind -- what empirical result or conceptual argument, from either field, would lead you to reverse that verdict or to relocate the residue to a different part of the mapping. Where your reasoning most plausibly fails, say so.

I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the translation and the residue analysis to stand on the concepts themselves.
