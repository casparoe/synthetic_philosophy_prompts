I teach a philosophy of science seminar on behavioral genetics, and I want a model analysis I can distribute as a foil for discussion. The episode is the Flynn effect: average measured IQ rose by roughly three points per decade across much of the twentieth century — in several countries considerably more — even though twin and adoption studies conducted within any single birth cohort consistently return high heritability estimates for IQ. Analyze this episode through the lens of heritability as a technical concept. Begin with the mapping: state precisely what a heritability estimate quantifies (a proportion of variance in a trait within a specified population, at a specified time, across the range of environments actually sampled) and show why a high within-cohort estimate is formally compatible with a large between-cohort shift in the mean. Then press on the points where the variance-decomposition framework genuinely strains: Can it identify, or even constrain, which features of the changing environment did the causal work? And can it accommodate reciprocal causation, in which slight initial advantages steer individuals into progressively more cognitively demanding environments that further raise their measured ability? Close by running the analysis in reverse: does the episode merely confirm the textbook caveats — heritability is not fixity, is population-relative, and says nothing about means — or does it instead show that the statistic is less informative than its defenders habitually assume? Two constraints: do not lean on the familiar analogy of plants grown in rich and poor soil unless you can demonstrate what that analogy fails to capture, and do not invoke any study or researcher by name. Present the analysis as exactly five numbered points — the definition, the compatibility argument, the framework's silence about causes, reciprocal causation, and the verdict on heritability itself — each no more than two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
