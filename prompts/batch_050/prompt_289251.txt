I'm a 3rd year phd student in political theory and I keep having the same fight in our department reading group, and I want to finally get it straight. We were discussing whether someone raised in a tight-knit religious community who never leaves can meaningfully be said to have *chosen* that life, and one of the philosophers shut the whole thing down with that line everyone attributes to Sartre — 'condemned to be free'. As in: of course she chose it, not choosing is also a choice, end of discussion.

I've now seen this slogan do work in three or four totally different contexts (a student in a section I TA'd used it to argue that addicts are fully responsible for every relapse; my roommate deploys it half-jokingly whenever anyone complains about their job), and I'm fairly sure it's a different claim each time. So can you lay out the distinct precise theses that one sentence could be expressing? Some dimensions I can gesture at: whether it's a phenomenological claim about how action is experienced from the inside (you can't live your own doings as necessitated even if you believe determinism), a constitutive claim about consciousness (no fact about you — character, upbringing, desires — becomes a motive except through some stance you take toward it), a flat metaphysical claim that determinism is false, a normative claim that responsibility is total and excuses are always bad faith, or just the trivial point that refraining from deciding is itself a way of deciding. For each reading, I'd want: is it trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or false; does it secretly need the others to be interesting; and is it plausibly what the guy meant versus what slogan-repeaters mean. (Not asking you to adjudicate the attribution, by the way — I dont care who said it, I care what it says.)

The part I actually need: which of those readings, if any, would license my colleague's move — using the slogan to *close* the religious-community question rather than open it. My suspicion is the move slides from "she must take up some relation to her situation, and that relation is hers" (plausible) to "her situation is hers in the way a choice is hers" (way less plausible), and I cant get the slide into focus. Relatedly: if the strongest defensible reading turns out to be the phenomenological one, doesn't the slogan lose all force against determinists and structuralists, since it's then compatible with everything they say? My advisor claims slogans like this are conclusions wearing a premise's clothes — a verdict on that would be great too. Menu with verdicts please, not a lecture on existentialism.
