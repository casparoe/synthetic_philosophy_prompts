So there's a move I keep seeing and I want to know if philsophy of language (or anywhere else) already has a name for it. Please answer in British English btw.

The move: someone says "tigers have stripes", I point out albino tigers don't, and instead of conceding the sentence is false they say the albino ones "don't count" — not because the claim is merely statistical, but because an albino tiger is allegedly a defective tiger, not the real thing the sentence was about. Same shape with "mosquitoes carry malaria": it survives even though only a small minority of mosquitoes carry it, because the rest get treated as irrelevant to what the sentence was really claiming.

Now, this smells like the no true Scotsman move, but I don't think it quite is one, because sometimes the exclusion seems totally legitimate — "ducks lay eggs" really does stay true even though male ducks don't, and nobody's cheating when they say so. Whereas the albino-tiger case feels like a dodge. So the phenomenon is: treating some members of a kind as the ones a generalization is really about, such that counterexamples get reclassified as defective or non-central members rather than as evidence against the claim. Sometimes fine, sometimes a con.

Is there an established term for this — for the move itself, or for the line between when it's legitimate and when it's Scotsman-ing? I know people have written about generics like "ducks lay eggs", but I'm after the specific carving: what makes "male ducks don't lay eggs" a fine exception and "albino tigers lack stripes" a cheating one. If there's no existing name, propose one and define it tightly enough to sort the cases, with a couple of boundary cases where your definition has to work hard.
