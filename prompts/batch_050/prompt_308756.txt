I've been a reader of rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one thing the usual Schelling-point talk never quite settles for me is the normative status of everyday coordination conventions: tipping, queueing, jaywalking, quiet cars, and so on. I want to know whether my case intuitions add up to a coherent position. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like you to tell me what principle — if any — generates them.

1. Cutting into a queue at a bus stop in London: wrong, and I'd feel genuinely guilty doing it, not merely embarrassed.
2. Stiffing a server at a sit-down restaurant in the US, where wages are built around tips: wrong, and it feels wrong in the same register as breaking a small promise.
3. Jaywalking across a completely empty German street at 3am, no cars, no children watching: fine. The convention exists, but violating it here costs nobody anything.
4. A tourist in a country where tipping is mildly insulting, who tips anyway on the grounds that extra money can't hurt: not fine. He has an obligation to conform to the local norm once he could reasonably have known it.
5. A first-time visitor who leaves a tip in that same country out of genuine ignorance: excused. But a first-time visitor to Britain who pushes past a queue, pleading that people don't queue where he's from: not fully excused — he should have noticed. (I realize these two sit in tension; both involve ignorance.)
6. Talking at normal volume in a train's quiet car: wrong, though less gravely than queue-cutting, and I'm not sure why the magnitudes differ.
7. Sneezing without covering your mouth: wrong, but for the harm, not the convention — this one feels categorically different from the others.
8. A German visitor in Rome who waits at the red light while locals stream across against it: not only permissible but mildly admirable. Conformity doesn't obligate him to join a convention he finds objectionable — yet in case 4 I said the tourist must conform.

My suspicion is that 3 and 4 are the hard pair (when do conventions bind absent harm?), with 5's two halves and 8 complicating any answer about whether visitors must adopt local norms. Please state a principle — or two or three candidates — precisely enough that each clause can be tied to the cases motivating it; tell me which of my verdicts the simplest version of your principle has to overturn, and whether you'd overturn them or accept the complexity; and apply the principle to cases I haven't listed: haggling in a market where locals expect it, whether a delivery driver must take his shoes off at the door of a house whose occupants do, applauding between movements at a classical concert, and a foreigner in the US who tips 10% because that's generous by his home standards. Finally, is the moral obligation / mere etiquette distinction doing any real work here, or is it a label we paste on after the verdicts are already in?
