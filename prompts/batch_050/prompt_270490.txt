I am a novelist revising a chapter set in two imaginary countries, Alder and Bracken. In 1900 they have similar coastlines, soils, climates, populations, and resource endowments. A boundary commission then places Alder under a legal-administrative system with independent courts, secure property rights, and constrained taxation, while Bracken falls under personalist rule in which officials may confiscate assets and judges serve at the ruler’s pleasure. By 2000 Alder is far richer. My draft lets one character infer that institutions caused the divergence, but a historian friend objects that this is a just-so story: institutions were not assigned randomly, their survival depended on prior social coalitions, prosperity may produce good courts rather than the reverse, and successful societies often rewrite their histories to make later wealth look inevitable.

Please take the institutionalist side in a live exchange. Play a sophisticated first-person defender of the view that institutions can causally shape national prosperity, not a libertarian caricature: you recognize selection effects, reverse causation, measurement error, historical contingency, and the possibility that geography and culture operate through institutions rather than independently of them. Engage my fictional evidence on its own terms, distinguish partial causal influence from a total explanation of national wealth, and concede any part of my challenge you find convincing. Do not cite scholars, historical cases, statistics, or further reading; rely on the scenario and ordinary causal reasoning. Keep each reply under four paragraphs, remain in character until I write curtain, and never treat the fictional example as evidence that could settle the real-world dispute by itself.

My opening move is this: your claim that Alder’s institutions caused its prosperity mistakes survival for causation. Even granting the initial boundary settlement, outsiders may have imposed extractive rule on Bracken because its elites were already too fragmented to resist, while Alder’s courts endured because merchants, farmers, and officials had enough independent wealth and trust to defend them. If institutional quality reflects preexisting culture and bargaining power, and if richer societies can subsequently purchase better administration, what observation in my fictional history could possibly falsify your position rather than be absorbed into it? Begin by answering as the institutionalist.
