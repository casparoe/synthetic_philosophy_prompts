Last month I lost about $340 in an online auction for a camera lens worth maybe $250 new, and my brother, who teaches game theory at a community college, told me I'd been "throwing good money after bad" and should have dropped out early. I pushed back: at every single bid, it was worth bidding a little more to avoid forfeiting what I'd already committed, which is exactly the trap of the dollar auction setup he himself once explained to me. His response was essentially that the sunk-cost slogan settles it -- the money already bid is gone either way, so rational bidding means ignoring it and dropping out the moment the price exceeded the lens's value to me. But that strikes me as too quick, because if everyone followed that rule the auction dynamics would change, and anyway "ignore what's already spent" seems to describe the paradox rather than dissolve it: each individual bid was locally defensible, yet the aggregate was ruinous.

So here's what I'd like. Take the slogan "don't throw good money after bad" and unpack the distinct precise principles it could be expressing. Candidate readings I can already glimpse: a decision-theoretic axiom that sunk costs are irrelevant to forward-looking choice; a piece of psychological hygiene against loss-chasing and escalation of commitment; a prudential rule about predictable competitive dynamics (i.e., the problem isn't the sunk money but the structure of the contest); a reputational or consistency norm against appearing erratic; and maybe a purely statistical point about expected value at the margin. For each reading, say whether it's trivially true, substantively defensible, or actually false, and -- crucially -- whether that reading would actually have justified my brother's advice in my case. I suspect the slogan is doing different work in ordinary consumer contexts than in genuine escalation scenarios like the dollar auction, bidding wars, or continuing to fund a failing project, so please address whether any reading of it resolves the escalation paradox or whether they all merely rename it.

Finally, my brother's unstated view seems to be that the only correct move in a dollar auction is never to enter, which is itself a slogan of sorts. If the various readings of "don't throw good money after bad" don't deliver that conclusion on their own, say what additional premise is needed to get from the slogan to "don't play at all," and whether that premise is something the slogan's everyday users would accept if it were made explicit.
