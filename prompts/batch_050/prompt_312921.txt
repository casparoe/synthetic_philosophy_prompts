Write a satirical commencement address of roughly 600 words, delivered by the founder and director of the fictional Kavka Institute for Advanced Intention to its newest graduating class. The Institute owes its existence to a standing offer from an anonymous benefactor, modeled on Kavka's toxin puzzle: anyone who, at midnight tonight, genuinely intends to drink a vial of toxin at noon tomorrow will wake to find a large sum already transferred to their account, the sincerity of the midnight intention having been verified by an infallible brain-scan. The toxin causes a day of severe illness and nothing worse, and — this being the clause on which everything turns — the money, once paid, is the recipient's to keep whether or not the vial is ever touched. The Institute charges steep tuition to teach people how to win this prize, and the founder's speech must be a masterpiece of confident nonsense that nonetheless gets the philosophy exactly right. The humor should arise from the real structure of the puzzle: the obstacle is not weakness of will, fear, or insufficient motivation, but the fact that a rational person cannot intend at midnight to do what she already knows she will have no reason to do at noon. Have the founder boast about techniques that transparently cannot work — visualization exercises, accountability partners, precommitment contracts, drinking the toxin early to get it over with — and have him celebrate the Institute's rare success stories in terms that reveal, to a careful reader but never to him, that every winner won by misunderstanding the rules. Include one tortured paragraph in which he fields the question he always gets from the floor: why none of the Institute's own faculty, who understand the contract best, have ever claimed the prize. The address should stay deadpan throughout; the founder never suspects that his industry is built on selling a solution to a problem that admits no solution, and the satire should ultimately land on the broader willpower-and-precommitment coaching industry, which treats every failure of follow-through as a mere technique problem. Format the piece as continuous prose, a speech as it would actually be delivered, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or other formatting devices.
