I have been reading rationalist blogs and their comment threads for the better part of fifteen years, and one habit that community instilled in me early was a suspicion of ancient philosophical disputes: if intelligent people have failed to settle something for two thousand years, the odds are decent that they are arguing about words rather than about the world. I was therefore gratified, on finally reading the primary text instead of the summaries, to find Hume making a recognisable ancestor of this very move at the start of Section VIII of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, "Of Liberty and Necessity", Part I: "From this circumstance alone, that a controversy has been long kept on foot, and remains still undecided, we may presume, that there is some ambiguity in the expression, and that the disputants affix different ideas to the terms employed in the controversy." Hume goes on to suggest that the whole controversy between liberty and necessity has turned merely upon words, and that a few intelligible definitions would have ended it. A reading group I belong to has asked each member to write up their considered position on free will and moral responsibility for the others to attack, and before I submit mine I would like to know whether it hangs together — and, specifically, whether the passage above can bear the weight I am putting on it.

Here is the position as drafted. (1) Frankfurt-style cases persuade me that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise: an agent can be genuinely blameworthy even when they could not have acted differently. (2) I find the Consequence Argument compelling, so I accept that if determinism is true, no one ever could have done otherwise in the incompatibilist's sense. (3) I am nonetheless a compatibilist about moral responsibility: a fully causally determined agent can be genuinely blameworthy. (4) I judge that an agent whose character and decisions were covertly engineered by manipulators is not responsible for the engineered wrongdoing — and, having thought it through, I cannot identify any principled difference between such manipulation and an ordinary causally determined upbringing. (5) In the spirit of the quoted passage, I hold that the compatibilism dispute is at bottom a confusion about the meanings of "free" and "could have done otherwise", to be dissolved by careful definition rather than settled by substantive argument.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me on the topic. For every pair of commitments that genuinely pulls against another, say how severe the tension is, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost the rest of the position; for every pair that merely looks tense, explain why the tension dissolves on inspection. I would like the tensions ranked by severity rather than presented as equally bad, and I have no prior preference about which commitment should go, so do not soften the verdict. Separately, tell me whether the quoted passage actually establishes what I cite it for in (5) — that long-lived disputes may be presumed verbal — or only something weaker, and whether the strength of Hume's inference there matters for the rest of my position or is independent of it. Throughout, distinguish what is genuinely settled among philosophers who work on this from what remains contested, and do not present a contested verdict as though it were the consensus. British English, please.
