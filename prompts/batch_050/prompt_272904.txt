After a career in experimental biology I have, in retirement, begun reading the philosophical and scientific literature on consciousness in non-human animals, and I find myself in a position I recognize from teaching: I have confident judgments about particular cases but no articulated principle that generates them. I would like you to supply the principle, or tell me honestly that my judgments do not add up. Here are my verdicts, roughly in order of confidence.

1. The common octopus is conscious. Its problem-solving, play, apparent preference formation, and the way it trades off pain against reward seem to me far beyond anything a stimulus-response reading could cover.
2. The honeybee is probably conscious. The waggle dance, cross-modal learning, and judgment-bias results (pessimism after agitation) strike me as the right kind of evidence, despite the nervous system being only about a million neurons.
3. The fruit fly is not conscious. Sleep-wake cycles, courtship learning, and attention-like states do not move me here; the whole animal has perhaps a tenth of the bee's neurons and its behavior looks compiled rather than deliberated.
4. Adult fish such as trout are conscious; the trade-off and wound-tending evidence persuades me, and I am unmoved by the objection that they lack a neocortex, since convergent evolution can surely build the relevant capacities from different parts.
5. Newly hatched zebrafish larvae are not conscious; their behavior is close to purely reflexive, and I see no reason to attribute experience where a simple control circuit explains everything.
6. Individual ants are not conscious, whatever one says about the colony; a single ant's repertoire seems to me a lookup table.
7. I am genuinely uncertain about jumping spiders, whose hunting behavior involves apparent planning with detours out of sight of the prey.

Now, the difficulty I can see myself. Verdicts 2 and 3 appear to turn on neuron count, but I doubt I actually believe in a neuron threshold, and if the real difference is behavioral sophistication, the fruit fly's case is better than I admitted. Verdicts 4 and 5 are awkward for the opposite reason: development is continuous, so any line I draw between the larva and the adult seems arbitrary, and yet I want the line. And verdict 6 makes me uneasy next to verdict 2, since I am not sure the ant's repertoire is qualitatively poorer than the bee's, only that I feel differently about it.

Is there a single defensible criterion -- some combination of integrated, flexible behavior, valenced learning, self-maintenance, or whatever you think does the work -- that recovers most of these verdicts? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause of it is motivated by which case, and say what it implies about the cases I have not listed: pill bugs, nematodes with their 302 neurons, the severed arm of an octopus, and cerebral organoids grown in vitro. If no principle recovers everything, tell me which of my verdicts it is cheapest to abandon and what I lose by abandoning it.
