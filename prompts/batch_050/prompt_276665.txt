Write an original short story of roughly 1,200–1,800 words on the question of whether dreams are experiences we undergo whilst asleep, or merely false memories fabricated at the moment of waking.

The setting: a near-future sleep laboratory has developed a technique that appears, at last, to settle the question empirically. Participants are trained to give pre-arranged signals during REM sleep — a sequence of eye movements answering yes/no questions, or a murmured running commentary — and their real-time responses match the dream narratives they produce on waking. A journalist, a research participant, and the laboratory's director become entangled in a dispute over what, exactly, the experiment has shown.

The story must be engineered so that the experiment, despite working perfectly, fails to settle the dispute. The denouement should turn on the underdetermination itself: one character argues that a signal given during sleep, corroborated by a report on waking, is straightforward evidence that an experience was occurring at that moment; the other argues that the signal and the report alike could be the first manifestation of an experience created only in the instant of signalling or waking, with a spurious impression of prior duration — and that no conceivable further data could distinguish these hypotheses, since any new test would simply reproduce the same structure. The twist must not depend on the device malfunctioning, on anyone lying, or on the dream turning out to be 'real' in some supernatural sense; those endings are forbidden.

Further requirements: (1) include at least one sustained scene in which two characters argue the point, each giving the strongest available version of their side, including a reply to the other's best objection; (2) work in, somewhere, the awkward case of dreams nobody remembers — if dreams are experiences, does sleep contain unremembered suffering, and does it matter?; (3) the philosophy must emerge from the narrative, so do not name real philosophers and avoid the words 'verificationism', 'behaviourism', and 'functionalism'; (4) after the story, append a short afterword of at most 150 words, clearly separated from the fiction, explaining how the laboratory dispute maps onto the actual debate between Norman Malcolm's view that the notion of an experience had during sleep is unintelligible because the waking report is the sole criterion for dreaming, and Daniel Dennett's cassette theory, on which dreams are composed and inserted into memory at or around waking rather than experienced during sleep.
