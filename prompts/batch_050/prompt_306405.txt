Two colleagues of mine have been going back and forth about reparations for historical injustices like slavery. (1) Anna argues that no living person is owed compensation for such wrongs, because compensation is owed only to people whom the wrong made worse off than they otherwise would have been — yet present-day descendants of victims owe their very existence to the chain of events the injustice set in motion, so they cannot have been harmed by it. (2) Ben replies that this uses the wrong baseline: a person can be wronged by being deprived of what they were entitled to, and the comparison should be with their entitlements, not with nonexistence — a thief doesn't escape liability for stealing a child's inheritance by pointing out that the child would never have been born in a world without the theft. (3) Anna counters that this move collapses the case for reparations into an ordinary argument about present-day inequality: if the relevant standard is what people are entitled to now, then the history drops out of the justification entirely, and one might as well defend redistribution on independent grounds without any historical claims. (4) Ben responds that history still does essential work even on his view: it identifies who owes, namely persisting institutions that committed or profited from the wrong, and a duty of compensation is morally different from a duty of general redistribution, since it binds the wrongdoer specifically, survives changes in the current distribution, and is not discharged simply because the victims' descendants happen to become well off. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
