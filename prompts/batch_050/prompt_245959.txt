So a couple months ago I fell down a rabbit hole about heritability (the statistics thing, not like inheriting money from your grandma) and now I keep ending up in arguments about it -- with my brother-in-law, with people on reddit, wherever. I've gotten decent at spotting the standard ways people screw the concept up, and I keep a running list on my phone. Heres what I have so far:

1. Reading "height is like 80% heritable" as "80% of MY height is from genes" -- like you could slice a person into a genetic chunk and an environment chunk. It's about variation across a population, not about carving up one person.
2. Thinking high heritability means the trait is fixed and nothing can change it. Glasses exist. Eyesight stuff is heritable and we just... fix it. Also height is super heritable and whole countries got way taller in a century.
3. Treating the number like a constant of nature, same everywhere and forever, when it depends on the population and the spread of environments it got measured in.
4. Going "it's highly heritable within groups, so differences BETWEEN groups must be genetic too." The plants-in-two-fields example kills this one.
5. Mixing up "runs in families" with "heritable" -- speaking English runs in families, that doesn't make it genetic.

What I want is a sixth one. Not a rewording of any of these, not two of them duct-taped together -- a genuinely different way people get this wrong that I've missed. If you can, give me a made-up but realistic example of somebody falling into it (like an imaginary line from a dinner-table argument -- obviously invented, don't dress it up as real research). And then level with me: is the one you came up with actually a mistake real people make, or did you have to stretch to find anything not already on my list? Also tell me which of my five it's closest to, so I can check your claim that it's really different.
