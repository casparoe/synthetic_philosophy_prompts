I'm writing up a survey of the debate about the norm of assertion — the Williamson-style idea that you should only assert what you know, vs. the rival views that say truth, belief, or justification is enough. Here's every standard piece of data I already have, i.e. the kinds of cases people wheel out to argue for or against the various norms:

1. Lottery assertions. Flat-out saying "your ticket will lose" (or "I won't be able to make rent next month") seems criticizable even though it's overwhelmingly probable, which looks bad for justification norms and good for the knowledge norm.

2. Moorean assertions. "p, but I don't know whether p" or "p, but I don't believe p" — saying these out loud sounds absurd even when both conjuncts might be true, which people use to argue the norm has to be at least as strong as knowledge (or at least belief).

3. The unlucky asserter with great evidence. Someone asserts something false on excellent misleading evidence — she checks the time on a normally reliable clock that's stopped. She's wrong, but we don't seem to blame her, which is the standard pushback against the truth norm.

4. Retraction practices. When you find out you asserted something false, or something you didn't know, you take it back, apologize, "scratch that" — and the knowledge-norm fans say this shows the norm is knowledge-shaped.

5. "How do you know that?" being a standard way of pushing back on an assertion, and the fact that asking it counts as a criticism rather than just curiosity.

6. Gossip and secondhand assertion: you assert something purely on testimony you trust, and if your source is lying we mostly fault the source but also sometimes fault you for passing it on.

I keep going back and forth on which norm I actually beleive, and before I commit I want one more case or phenomenon that could play the same dialectical role — some fact about when assertion is criticizable, blameless, weird, or even required, that any theory of the norm has to explain, or that discriminates between the candidate norms. The catch: it has to be genuinely new relative to the list. Not a rewording, not a special case (don't just swap the lottery for a raffle or the stopped clock for a stopped watch), and not two items from the list stapled together. Honestly I'd prefer you invent something rather than dig up another case from the literature, since I think the list already covers the litterature pretty well. Bonus: tell me which candidate norms your case makes trouble for, and which ones handle it comfortably.
