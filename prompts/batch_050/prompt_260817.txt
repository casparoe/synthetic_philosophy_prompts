I'm a PhD student in population ethics writing a paper on Alfonso Cuarón's Children of Men, and I want to read the film through the procreation asymmetry: the view that while we have strong moral reasons against bringing miserable lives into existence, we have no comparable reason to bring happy lives into existence, since merely possible people are not deprived of anything by never existing. The film's premise — a humanity nearly two decades into global infertility, suddenly confronted with one pregnancy — seems to dramatize precisely the side of the asymmetry that standard treatments neglect: what is actually lost when no one can be created? My question is whether the film treats Kee's child as a benefit to the child herself (which the asymmetry denies existence can be), or only impersonally, as hope for the species and a political prize — note how every faction in the film wants the baby for what she represents. Please work through the key scenes (the public mourning for the world's youngest person in the opening, the barn revelation, the soldiers' ceasefire at the sound of the baby) and say where the film resists the reading: is the grief it depicts really grief over nonexistence, or over extinction, sterility, and lost futures — and is the film even interested in that distinction? I want criticism that is faithful in both directions, not the film flattened into an illustration of Benatar.
