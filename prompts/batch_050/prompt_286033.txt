I clerk for the European affairs committee of a national parliament. Twice a year we run the subsidiarity early-warning exercise: for each new legislative proposal from the Commission, the committee decides whether to issue a reasoned opinion objecting that the proposal infringes the subsidiarity principle — the principle, roughly, that the Union should act only where the member states cannot achieve the objective sufficiently well acting alone and where Union-level action would do better by reason of scale or effects.

I would like to be able to anticipate the committee's votes before its meetings. Its record across the last six exercises is as follows; the proposals are paraphrased, but the votes reflect the committee's settled positions.

1. Caps on the retail roaming charges that mobile operators may levy on customers using their phones in another member state. No objection.
2. Binding national emission ceilings for certain air pollutants, with common monitoring obligations. No objection.
3. Mutual recognition of specified professional qualifications, so that an architect or pharmacist licensed in one member state may practise in another subject to limited conditions. No objection.
4. CO2 emission standards for new passenger cars sold anywhere in the Union. No objection.
5. A common framework for the teaching of twentieth-century European history in secondary schools, with Union-level curriculum benchmarks. Objection issued.
6. A Union-wide prohibition on smoking in bars, cafés and restaurants, justified on public health grounds. Objection issued.

The next exercise will cover a regulation of short-term accommodation rentals arranged through online platforms. It would oblige platforms to obtain a registration number from each host in a standardised Union-wide format, to share booking data automatically with municipal authorities, and to honour caps on rental nights per property imposed by cities formally designated as housing-stressed under criteria set at Union level.

I am less interested in your own view of the proposal's merits than in extrapolating the committee's practice. Please answer the following, in British English:

(a) Set out the two or three principles the committee might plausibly be running, each of them consistent with all six verdicts above — for instance, a test that asks whether the harms at issue physically or economically cross internal borders, a test that asks whether divergent national or municipal rules would fragment the single market, and a comparative-efficiency test that asks who could in fact regulate the matter most effectively. For each principle, show how it accommodates the six verdicts.

(b) State what each principle predicts about the short-term rentals proposal. At least two of them should diverge on it; make the divergence explicit rather than gesturing at it.

(c) Give your best guess, with a rough probability, of how the committee will in fact vote, identifying which of the six precedents is the closest analogue and whether the analogy favours objection or acquiescence.

(d) Say whether this case forces the committee to commit to a reading of subsidiarity it has so far managed to leave ambiguous — in particular, whether a patchwork of municipal rules burdening cross-border platform services counts as a single-market problem at all, and whether a purely local harm can ever justify Union action when the instrument causing it operates across borders.

(e) Describe the further hypothetical Commission proposal whose treatment would most efficiently discriminate between the principles you left standing after (c), and say what each possible verdict on it would rule out.

Finally, if you were advising the committee on how to vote so as to keep its practice maximally coherent, what would you advise, and what principle would that vote commit it to going forward?
