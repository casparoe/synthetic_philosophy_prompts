I'm a novelist, and my first reader June did a philosophy degree before she went into publishing, which is a mixed blessing. Her whole editorial philosophy comes from one moment in Bertrand Russell's On Denoting, where he's analyzing "the father of Charles II was executed" and grumbles: "Now the, when it is strictly used, involves uniqueness; we do, it is true, speak of 'the son of So-and-so' even when So-and-so has several sons, but it would be more correct to say 'a son of So-and-so'." June applies this to every "the X" in my manuscripts. Her rulings so far:

- "The Duke of Almaville's son rode ahead" — my Duke has three sons, estabished in chapter one. June: false as written; you mean "a son."
- A character says "my husband will be furious," and the story makes very clear she was never married. June: false — the character is lying or confused, which is fine, keep it.
- The narrator says "the golden mountain does not exist." June: true. Keep.
- In a fantasy draft, "the Empress of the Southern Isles signed the treaty," the Empress being an actual person in that world. June: true in the story, obviously fine.

Now the new case. In my current manuscript the regime has built its propaganda around a war hero, Captain Vey, and I have deliberately left it unsettled in the text whether he ever existed — some characters swear they served with him, others say he was invented whole cloth, and I refuse to decide. Halfway through, the in-world newspaper reports: "Captain Vey crossed the Renn at dawn."

Predict June's verdict on that sentence: flat-out false, neither true nor false, or "it's a novel, stop asking"? Say which rule best explains her track record — strict Russell applied to fiction word for word, Russell's analysis but with truth relativized to the story, or something with presuppositions and gaps — where the runner-up rules part ways with it over Captain Vey specifically, and what single test sentence I should slip into the next draft to force her hand.
