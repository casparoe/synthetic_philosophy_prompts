Our reading group has been working through Hofstadter's Atlantic piece on Google Translate -- the one where he worries that for all its fluency, statistical translation doesn't understand anything, because the words are never hooked up to the world. We got into a proper argument about whether his worry even survives contact with the newer models, and I've been asked to write down where I stand so the others can have a go at me next week. Here's my position, such as it is:

(a) Hofstadter is basically right: however good the output gets, a system trained on text alone doesn't understand what it translates, because its symbols are ungrounded -- never connected to perception, action, or anything outside language.
(b) On meaning, I'm an inferentialist: to grasp what a word means is to master its role in reasoning -- what follows from it, what it follows from, what it rules out. Meaning is use in that sense, not a mental tether to objects.
(c) For technical prose -- legal boilerplate, software documentation, that kind of thing -- current machine translation is now better than what most bilingual humans would produce, and I'd say so without hedging.
(d) Understanding comes in degrees, and the top degrees essentially involve connotation: getting the associations and overtones right, not just the truth conditions. A translator who renders every word of a poem correctly but flattens its register hasn't fully understood it.
(e) That said, for anything high-stakes like a contract, I wouldn't sign off on a machine translation without a human who genuinely understands both languages checking it -- because it's the human's understanding that confers correctness on the output, not the other way round.
