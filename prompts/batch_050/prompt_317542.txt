I've come around to the view that conceivability, done carefully, is real evidence for possibility. Not the crude version where picturing something settles it — I mean ideal conceivability: if a scenario survives maximal reflection, no contradiction turning up no matter how hard you push on it, then it's metaphysically possible. My stock example is the usual one: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me with no consciousness seems describable down to the last neuron, so a zombie world is possible, so physicalism about consciousness is false. I've also built in what I take to be the standard safety valves — the claim is about ideal reflection, not about what I can picture on a Tuesday afternoon, and a posteriori necessities like water being H2O don't refute it, because seeming to conceive of water without H2O is really just conceiving of watery-looking stuff under a different description.

What would count against this? I'm asking because I want a map of my exposure, not because I'm having a wobble today. A few things I specifically want covered:

- Which cases, if any, would show ideal conceivability and possibility coming apart — and how could such a case ever be established, given that a proof of the scenario's impossibility is exactly what would supposedly reveal it was never ideally conceivable in the first place? I worry the view is built so no counterexample can stick, and I want to know if that worry is fair or if there genuinely are loading-bearing places it could break.
- Are there episodes elsewhere (mathematics, logic, old debates about space and geometry) where conceivability-based reasoning delivered a verdict we now reject, and do those count against my view or only against the sloppy version I already disown?
- Does the zombie argument stand or fall with the general principle, or could the principle surive while the zombie case specifically fails (or vice versa)?
- Rank these from fatal to merely awkward: (a) a demonstration that every attempted zombie description smuggles in a contradiction nobody has spotted yet; (b) a case where something is ideally conceivable by everyone's lights but we have strong independent reason to think impossible; (c) a psychological result showing that conceivability judgments mostly track how often you've heard a description, not anything modal.

One test case of my own I want folded into the assessment: take an open mathematical conjecture the experts think is probably true but unproved. I find I can "conceive" of it being false — I can picture the counterexample announcement, the retracted textbooks, the whole movie. On my view, does the possibility of its falsity follow, and if that answer is embarrassing, which clause of the position has to give — or is math just a different game where conceivability was never supposed to apply?

And the honest bit: has my view been qualified into unfalsifiability, with the word "ideal" doing all the protective work, so that any failed conceivability claim gets reclassified as not really ideal? If so, is that fatal for something meant to be an evidence-principle, or is it just what all defeasible principles look like up close? And what would a committed modal rationalist concede as counting against them — as opposed to what critics say should? If the whole thing quietly collapses into "whatever is possible is possible," I'd rather be told now.
