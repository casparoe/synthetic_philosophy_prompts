ok so i have to circulate a "position statement" for my political theory reading group next week and i want a consistency audit before i embarass myself. here's roughly where i've landed on the democracy vs epistocracy stuff:

1. The case for universal suffrage is deontological, not epistemic. The vote is grounded in the equal moral status of persons, so competence-based exclusion (lottocracy, Brennan-style restricted franchises, voter exams) is wrong *in principle*, even if it would produce better outcomes.

2. Democracy should be defended instrumentally: it's the system that tends to produce the best decisions, full stop. There's nothing intrinsically fair about counting everyone's preferences equally, since preferences about things like monetary policy or tariff schedules aren't the kind of thing that command equal respect.

3. The Condorcet jury theorem can't do any work defending real elections. Votes aren't independent, and big blocs of the electorate are systematically below chance on policy-relevant facts, so aggregation doesn't launder ignorance.

4. The actually-good epistemic defense of mass participation is Hong–Page type results: cognitive diversity beats raw individual ability on hard problems, so a diverse mass public can outperform a small group of the highly competent.

5. Estlund is basically right that any workable epistocracy fails his demographic objection — the class of the "qualified" would be skewed by race, class, and education, and the arrangement couldn't be justified to the people it excludes.

6. Most referendums are a bad way to make policy (brexit being exhibit A), and representative institutions are epistemically superior to direct democracy.
