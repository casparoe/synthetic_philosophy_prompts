I am preparing an application for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship in comparative philosophy, and I need the research-proposal section drafted for me. It should run to about 1,800 words. My project concerns the Four–Seven debate of Joseon-dynasty Korea, and I will give you the background as I understand it, so that the proposal does not presuppose anything beyond what is stated here.

The background: in sixteenth-century Korea, two Neo-Confucian philosophers, Yi Hwang (pen name Toegye) and Yi I (pen name Yulgok), conducted an extended controversy over the origin of the emotions. On one side were the Four: the moral emotions Mencius identified as the sprouts of virtue — commiseration, shame and dislike, deference, and approval and disapproval. On the other were the Seven: the ordinary emotions enumerated in the ritual classics — joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hatred, and desire. The debate turned on the two constituents of Neo-Confucian metaphysics, i (principle or pattern, the normative structure of things) and gi (material force or psychophysical stuff). Toegye held that principle and material force can each act as a source of issuance, and so the Four issue from principle while the Seven issue from material force — a position usually summarized as the mutual issuance of principle and material force. Yulgok objected that issuance is always an affair of material force and that principle never moves on its own but only rides on material force; the Four differ from the Seven not in what issues them but in the purity and balance of the material force through which principle is expressed. The controversy was conducted through letters over many years and became the defining dispute of Korean Neo-Confucianism.

Here is my half-formed idea. The standard presentation of the debate — in English-language surveys especially — treats it as a dispute in moral psychology, as if the question were whether the Four and the Seven form two natural kinds of emotion with different etiologies. I suspect this misdescribes it. On my reading, the real question is whether normative principle can be causally efficacious in the genesis of a mental state: Toegye's insistence that principle issues the Four is a claim that normativity itself can move the mind, and Yulgok's refusal is a claim that only concrete psychophysical processes move anything, with principle functioning as a constraint on, rather than a source of, motion. If that is right, the debate is an early and independently developed instance of a problem that Western philosophy knows under headings like the causal role of reasons, or how facts about what ought to be can figure in explanations of what happens. But I also suspect something stronger and more uncomfortable: that once the debate is recast this way, much of it may turn out to be merely verbal — that Toegye and Yulgok could each accept the other's central formulations under a disambiguation of what issuance means, and that the residue of genuine disagreement is smaller and stranger than the tradition assumes. I want to test both the reframing and the dissolution hypothesis.

The proposal should do the following, in roughly this order. First, state the research question precisely: not whether Toegye or Yulgok was right about the emotions, but whether their disagreement, once reconstructed as a disagreement about the causal efficacy of principle, remains substantive or collapses into equivocation over issuance. Second, explain why the existing interpretations leave this open — why neither the moral-psychology reading nor purely philological treatments of the correspondence can settle whether the parties were disagreeing about one thing. Third, lay out the method. The core of it is what I want to call concession mapping: reconstruct each philosopher's position as a set of conditional commitments (if issuance means such-and-such, then the following holds of the Four; if it means something else, this instead), identify the strongest formulation of each side that the texts support, and then determine whether there exists a single disambiguated model of emotion-genesis — for instance, a two-factor model distinguishing the source of a state's normative content from the source of its motivational force — that both parties would have to accept, and what, if anything, they would still be disputing. The proposal should say concretely what doing this involves across the two years: which stages are textual reconstruction, which are model-building, which are comparison with Western treatments of reasons and causes, and roughly how the months divide. Fourth, say what a result looks like in each direction: what the project establishes if the disagreement survives disambiguation, and what it establishes if it does not — and why either outcome is philosophically interesting rather than deflating. Fifth, give an honest account of failure conditions: for instance, that the texts underdetermine the reframing, that the causal-efficacy vocabulary turns out to project an alien fact-versus-value framework onto i and gi, or that concession mapping yields only an uninformative standoff. The risk of anachronistic projection is the most serious one, and the proposal should contain a real strategy for controlling it, not just an acknowledgment. Finally, scope: say what this project deliberately will not attempt (no comprehensive history of the debate's later reception, no new translations of the correspondence) and why two years is the right amount of time.

Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review by author and title — refer to positions and interpretations generically and I will supply the references myself. Write it in the first person, in the register of a serious fellowship application: confident but without overselling, and specific wherever the alternative is a gesture.
