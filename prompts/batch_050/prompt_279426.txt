I'm in a philosophy of history seminar and we've just spent two painful weeks on the covering-law vs. narrative explanation debate — Hempel and the positivists on one side, people like Dray and Gallie on the other. My friend insists the debate is dead and that narrative obviously won decades ago; I think covering-law explanations get unfairly mocked. To settle it we agreed on a test case: the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991. Neither of us is a Sovietologist, but the broad facts are common knowledge — stagnating planned economy, Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika, the arms race burden, Eastern Europe peeling off in 1989, nationalist movements in the republics, the failed August coup, dissolution by December.

Here's the exercise. Explain the collapse twice, properly. First, as a covering-law explanation: what would the general law actually have to be for the collapse to fall under it? Give me real candidate laws — something about imperial overstretch, about legitimacy decay under prolonged economic stagnation, about multiethnic federations, about what happens when an authoritarian regime liberalizes from above — and then be brutally honest about how lawlike each candidate really is, and whether adding the "law" to the explanans actually improves the explanation or just dresses up a hunch in formal clothes. Second, as a narrative explanation: construct the story version, where the collapse becomes intelligible by placing events in a sequence so that earlier ones set up later ones, and then do the part narrativists hate — identify where the narrative covertly smuggles in general claims (e.g., about how top-down reform destabilizes autocracies), i.e. where it relies on exactly the law-governed machinery it claims to replace.

Then the payoff: rank the two explanations. Which one actually explains the Soviet collapse better, and by what standard — retrodictive power, understanding, causal identification? Also tell me what the case shows about each model: is this just the kind of event (singular, overdetermined, easily narrated in hindsight) where covering-law was always going to struggle, or does the fact that almost nobody predicted the collapse cut against narrative too, since good narratives are supposed to make outcomes feel inevitable only after the fact? And if you think some third option — like mechanisms-based explanation — beats both on this case, make that argument instead of copping out with "it's complicated."
