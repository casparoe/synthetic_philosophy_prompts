I'm a junior in high school and part of my school's European politics debate club, and next month we're arguing a motion on the future of European integration, roughly along the lines of: this house believes the EU should recommit to the goal of an ever closer union. In a practice round last week, the student arguing the pro side kept leaning on a comparison I've also seen in opinion pieces: European integration is like riding a bicycle, because a bicycle stays upright only while it keeps moving forward, so the EU must keep deepening -- more shared competences, more common institutions, more pooling of sovereignty -- or else it will wobble and collapse into crisis, fragmentation, and eventually exit referendums. I suspect this is the central rhetorical move I'll have to answer, and I want to be fair to it before I attack it, since some very serious people have used it and it clearly gestures at something real about how the eurozone crisis, for instance, forced further centralization that nobody had planned.

Could you audit the analogy for me? Specifically: what is supposed to map onto what (the rider, the pedaling, the forward motion, the falling over, and things like steering, brakes, and the destination -- which parts of the bicycle picture actually have counterparts in the EU, and which parts of the EU, like 27 veto-wielding member states, elections, and public opinion, have no counterpart on a bicycle)? Where does the comparison break down in ways that matter for the conclusion, as opposed to the trivial ways every analogy breaks down? What does it quietly smuggle in -- for example, the assumptions that "forward" can only mean more integration rather than, say, consolidation, that there is no stable resting point short of a federal state, that slowing down is the same as reversing, and that collapse is the default outcome rather than one possibility among several? And how does it interact with the democratic-deficit objection, since my instinct is that "we cannot afford to stop pedaling" is a way of saying we cannot afford to wait for public consent, which seems less like an argument for ever closer union and more like an admission about its politics?

Finally, once the disanalogies are on the table, does the bicycle picture still support anything -- maybe a weaker claim about momentum, sequencing, or sunk institutional costs, even if it can't carry the full ever-closer-union conclusion -- and is there a better analogy I could offer in the debate that captures whatever truth it has without the teleology? Please write your answer in American English.
