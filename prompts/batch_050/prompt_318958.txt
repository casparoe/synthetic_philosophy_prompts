I'm writing my undergrad thesis on counterpart theory and I'm stuck on the section where I motivate it. My supervisor says my list of arguments for Lewis's counterpart theory over transworld identity is already "the standard canon" and if I want a decent grade I need to either find one more argument in the literature or come up with one of my own. Here's what I have:

(1) The hubris argument — whether some merely possible person would have been *me* can't just be a brute fact about the universe, independent of what I care about; the counterpart relation being a matter of similarity means it's up to me, in a way, which possibilities count as possibilities for me, and that seems right.

(2) The flexibility argument — which of my properties count as essential to me seems to shift with context (I couldn't have been a poached egg, but maybe I could have been taller, and in some contexts even that gets doubted). Counterpart theory handles this by letting the counterpart relation vary with conversational context, while transworld identity has to pick one fixed answer.

(3) The duplicate problem — a perfect atom-for-atom duplicate of me in another world: transworld identity has to say it's *me* or *not me*, and both answers look arbitrary. Counterpart theory just says he's a very close counterpart and the question of identity doesn't arise.

(4) The parsimony argument — counterpart theory needs no mysterious cross-world identity facts or haecceities, just worlds and similarity relations between their inhabitants, and we believe in similarity anyway.

(5) The quantified modal logic argument — counterpart semantics gives a cleaner, more general semantics for modal logic and doesn't force the awkward commitments (like everything existing necessarily) that constant-domain semantics with transworld identity drags in.

So: is there a sixth argument for counterpart theory, genuinely different from these five? Not a restatement or a special case of one of them — e.g., don't just give me another version of "essentialism is messy." If it's something from the literature tell me roughly whose idea it is; if it's your own construction, say so, and tell me whether you think it actually works or whether Lewis already has an answer to it. Also happy to hear if you think one of my five is secretly two arguments in a trenchcoat.
