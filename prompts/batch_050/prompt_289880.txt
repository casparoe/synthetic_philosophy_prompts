I am assembling the final examination for my graduate seminar on comparative theories of moral desert, and I need a thought experiment constructed to specification. The case must do real philosophical work: I intend to use it to test whether students can hold three theories of desert apart while running them over the same scenario.

First, some stage-setting to fix the terms. For the purposes of this exercise, understand karma not as a religious doctrine but as a naturalized theory of moral desert with three commitments: (i) the mechanism is impersonal — there is no judge, no ledger-keeper, and no moment of verdict; desert is realized through the ordinary causal consequences of action, above all through what an action does to the agent's own character and dispositions; (ii) desert accrues to what the agent actually brings about across the full causal career of the act, not merely to what she intended at the moment of choice; and (iii) because there is no moment of judgment, desert unfolds across a life rather than being assigned at a point. Set this alongside two more familiar positions: outcome-insensitive retributivism, which fixes desert at the moment of action and so treats resultant luck as morally irrelevant, and outcome-sensitive retributivism, which holds that what in fact happens can make a genuine difference to what the agent deserves.

The task: construct a single thought experiment in which these three theories deliver three pairwise-distinct verdicts about the same two agents, subject to the following constraints.

1. Two agents whose choices, intentions, and degrees of negligence are stipulated to be identical; luck alone breaks the symmetry between their outcomes. The setting must be realistic — a road, a hospital, a firm, a courtroom — with no trolleys, demons, mind-reading devices, or other philosophical machinery.

2. The karmic mechanism must be shown operating through ordinary psychology and social causation: habituation, reputation, the entrenchment or erosion of dispositions. A naturalist who grants nothing supernatural should be able to follow the karmic verdict without protest. Do not narrate the mechanism from outside the story; let it fall out of the events themselves.

3. The verdicts must be derived, not stipulated. For each theory, state in two or three sentences what it says about the two agents and why. The karmic theory must deliver a verdict available to neither retributivism: I will accept either a reversal (the agent whom outcome luck spared ends up worse off, desert-wise, than the agent it did not) or a dissolution (the theory declines to assign the two agents different deserts at all, but for reasons unavailable to outcome-insensitive retributivism).

4. After the derivations, identify the load-bearing feature of the case: what does your scenario contain that a standard moral-luck case of the familiar kind lacks, such that the three theories come apart here? State what happens to each of the three verdicts if that feature is removed.

5. Conclude by steelmanning the strongest objection on behalf of whichever theory fares worst. The attack I anticipate is that the karmic verdict tracks the agents' well-being rather than their desert — that showing an agent is made worse off by his own success is not the same as showing he deserves more. Say whether your case survives this objection, and if not, concede precisely where it fails.

Format: the scenario in a single paragraph of at most 200 words; the complete answer, derivations included, under 800 words. The scenario should be followable by a strong undergraduate who has never encountered the word karma; the analysis should reward a reader who has thought seriously about desert.
