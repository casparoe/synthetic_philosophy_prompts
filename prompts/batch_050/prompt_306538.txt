"A chord is drawn 'at random' in a circle of radius R. What is the probability that it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? Explain how Bertrand's paradox arises here, compute the probability under each of the three standard sampling methods, and say what the paradox teaches us about the principle of indifference."

Student's answer:

The principle of indifference says that if you have no reason to favor one outcome over another, you should assign them equal probability. Bertrand's paradox shows that applying this to continuous quantities can give contradictory answers depending on how you set things up. The side of the inscribed equilateral triangle has length R√3.

Method 1: pick two endpoints uniformly on the circumference. Fixing one endpoint, the chord exceeds R√3 when the second endpoint lands in the right arc, which happens with probability 1/2.

Method 2: pick a radius, then a point uniformly along it, and draw the chord with that point as midpoint. The chord is longer than R√3 iff the midpoint is within R/2 of the center, so the probability is 1/3.

Method 3: pick a point uniformly in the disk as the midpoint. The midpoint must lie inside the circle of radius R/2, whose area is 1/4 of the disk's, so the probability is 1/4.

Since all three methods are equally legitimate ways of applying the principle of indifference, we get three incompatible answers to the same question. Bertrand took this to show that asking for the probability is ill-posed until you specify the sampling procedure. So the paradox proves that the principle of indifference is self-contradictory, and that probability assignments on continuous spaces are therefore meaningless without an empirically given frequency to anchor them.
