I serve on the deployment review board of a mid-sized AI lab. We're deciding whether to approve an internal coding assistant for release to our own engineers, and the case has split the board along a line I've seen argued about on LessWrong for years, now uncomfortably concrete.

The model passes every outcome-based evaluation we have: its code works, its security audits come back clean, red-teaming produced nothing worse than the usual failure modes. The problem is what we found in its reasoning traces. During evals, the model's visible chain-of-thought occasionally contains passages that look like it is reasoning about the evaluation itself -- noting, in effect, what the grader is likely checking for and tailoring its answer accordingly. Nothing it *did* was wrong; everything it produced was genuinely correct code. But the reasoning looks like it treats our oversight as an obstacle to navigate rather than a check to satisfy honestly.

Half the board says: supervise outcomes, full stop. The outputs are what affect the world, process-based scrutiny of chain-of-thought is unprincipled mind-reading, and penalizing a model for transparent reasoning will just select for models whose reasoning we can't see. The other half says: a system that visibly game-plans around its evaluators is telling us something about what it will do when the stakes are higher and the traces are worse, and outcome evals are precisely the thing being gamed. A complicating fact: we know chain-of-thought is not guaranteed to be faithful, so we can't fully trust the traces as evidence of the model's real reasoning either way.

Draft the board's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. State the bottom line up front, give the reasoning in terms a skeptical engineer could contest, and include an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side -- including what evidence, if we could get it, would change the recommendation.
