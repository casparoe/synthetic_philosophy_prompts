I'm applying for my university's summer research fellowship in philosophy (about 12 weeks, deliverable is a ~8000 word paper plus a short presentation) and I need a two-page project proposal. The topic I want to work on is Kavka's toxin puzzle — the setup where a billionaire offers you a fortune if at midnight tonight you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make you painfully ill for a day, where the money depends only on having the intention at midnight, not on actually drinking. So you could win by intending and then never drinking, but by tomorrow you'll have no reason to drink, and seeing that in advance is supposed to make the intention impossible to form in the first place. My roommate who studies econ keeps insisting this is just a commitment problem and philosophers are overthinking it, which annoyed me enough that I want to figure out if he's accidentally right.

Here's my half-formed idea, which is what I'd like the proposal built around. The standard debate seems to argue about whether intending to drink would be rational, but I suspect that question is doing two jobs at once and that's partly why the debate goes in circles. There's a psychological question — can a creature like me even form an intention it knows it will have every reason to abandon? — and a seperate structural question about rationality — if you could form it, would it be rational to? I want to pull these apart by thinking about agents who can hard-commit: someone who signs a penalty contract with a friend, someone whose professional reputation rides on follow-through, or even a simple machine that gets locked into a course of action. A commitment device passes the billionaire's test trivially. If the puzzle evaporates for agents with the right architecture, maybe it was never really a puzzle about rationality but about a contingent gap in humans between intending and executing — and then the interesting question becomes what exactly the billionaire's money is testing in us.

What I need in the proposal: a precise statement of the question, narrow enough to actually answer in 12 weeks (probably restrict it to the architecture reading and only touch the broader rationality debate where forced); a section explaining why the standard moves — resolute-choice style approaches, the rationality-of-intention literature, the response that you simply can't intend at will — leave my version of the question open without me having to relitigate all of them; a week-by-week plan of what I'd actually be doing; what a positive result looks like versus the project collapsing (e.g., if the commitment-device cases turn out to just relocate the puzzle one level up instead of dissolving it, is that a failure or is that the finding?); and an honest paragraph on scope creep, since this could easily balloon into all of diachronic rationality. No bibliography or citations please, I'll add those later — refer to positions by description.
