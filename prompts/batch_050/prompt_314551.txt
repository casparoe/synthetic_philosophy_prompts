My two housemates got into a surprisingly heated argument last week and I want to settle it, partly because I think they were both half-right and talking past each other. Background: one of them, Priya, is choosing between accepting a funded philosophy PhD offer and taking a decently paid job at an environmental nonprofit. She's been making pros-and-cons lists for weeks and keeps saying she genuinely cannot decide, that it's not 51/49, more like the two options refuse to be compared at all. That's what started it.

Our other housemate Marcus said her indecision is purely epistemic: if she somehow knew exactly how each life would unfold, one option would turn out better, or at worst they'd be precisely equal in value, and "hard choices" are really just choices where we lack information. Priya pushed back with a thought experiment. Suppose the nonprofit job paid $500 more per year. The choice would still be agonizing, right? But if academia had been strictly better all along, then nudging the nonprofit job slightly upward should at some point tip it from "clearly worse" to "clearly better," passing through a zone of rough equality -- it shouldn't stay maximally hard the whole time. So, she said, the two options must stand in some fourth relation that's not better-than, worse-than, or equal. Marcus called that spooky metaphysics and said a fourth sui generis value relation is just giving up and naming your confusion. Then he added what he thought was the killer move: if neither option is better and they're not equal either, then picking one is arbitrary, like a coin flip, and Priya obviously doesn't think flipping a coin would be a fine way to decide. Priya said no, reasons can genuinely count in favor of both options without adding up to a verdict, and choosing can itself be the exercise of rational agency rather than the tracking of a pre-existing fact -- but she admitted she couldn't spell that out very well. Then Marcus tried one more angle: on her view, if she picks academia, she could never rationally regret the choice OR feel vindicated by it, since there's no fact of the matter about whether she chose well. Priya said that's just false, you can throw yourself into what you chose and create new reasons that weren't there before, and Marcus said now she's just making it up as she goes.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Specifically: (1) Does Priya's small-improvement thought experiment actually show that a fourth value relation exists, or does Marcus's epistemic reading survive it? (2) Does the arbitrariness objection land, or is there a coherent way to say both options are supported by reasons without a coin flip being just as good? (3) Is Marcus right that incomparability would make regret and vindication irrational, or is Priya's "create new reasons" response defensible, and does it secretly smuggle comparability back in? And if you think both of them are missing something about what makes the choice hard in the first place, I'd like to hear that too.
