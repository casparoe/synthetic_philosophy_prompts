I teach a second-year undergrad philosophy of science seminar (about 16 students, one 90-minute session) and we're in the middle of the Popper-Kuhn-Lakatos stretch. I want an activity for the Lakatos session that makes them feel from the inside why a research programme can be doing badly and yet not be rationally abandonable -- and why some theory-rescues feel like genuine progress while others feel like face-saving. No lecture up front. They shouldn't hear the terms "hard core" or "protective belt" until the debrief.

Design a game with roughly this skeleton (improve it where you can, but keep the load-bearing pieces):

- Students split into 3-4 teams. Each team runs a research programme studying an invented phenomenon. I don't want real history of science (no phlogiston, no Newton), because I don't want them coasting on remembered outcomes, and because I want predictions to be genuinely checkable in-game.
- To make that possible, the facilitator holds a fixed, simple data-generating rule for the phenomenon (you design both the rule and the phenomenon) and only reveals outcomes after teams have locked in their predictions in writing.
- Each round, the facilitator hands every team the same new anomaly: data their current theory doesn't comfortably cover. Teams must either patch the theory (add or modify an auxiliary assumption while keeping the core claim) or abandon the core and adopt a new one. Abandoning costs points and gets psychologically harder because a rival team is sitting right there doing fine -- I want appraisal to feel comparative, the way it actually is in science, rather than a verdict on one programme in isolation.
- The payoff structure has to make both failure modes live temptations: stubborn patching (rescuing the core with accommodations that only mop up the anomaly) and premature abandonment (junking a programme after one bad round). So scoring should reward confirmed novel predictions heavily, reward anomaly-absorbing patches only weakly, and punish core-switching -- such that a team on a string of degenerating patches still beats a team that keeps starting over, until it doesn't.
- You design the anomaly stream so that, faced with the same anomaly, one plausible patch sticks its neck out about future data while another merely shields the core. Include one fully worked example round showing both responses and how they score.

Deliverables:

- Full rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, and the exact payoff table.
- The data-generating rule and the scripted anomaly sequence, plus guidance for what the facilitator does when a team predicts something off-script -- how do you improvise a response without breaking the fiction that the rule was fixed all along?
- A debrief plan mapping game moments onto the vocabulary: hard core, protective belt, progressive vs. degenerating problemshift, novel facts, and the "no instant rationality" point that a single bad round is not a refutation. Also get them onto the question of whether a prediction counts as novel because it was made before the data arrived or because the patch wasn't cooked up to fit that data -- in the game those two coincide, so the facilitator has to pry them apart explicitly.
- What will predictably go wrong when I run this and how to handle each: a degenerating patch gets lucky and its wild prediction happens to confirm; teams reverse-engineer the scoring and start gaming it; one strong student captains each team and the others spectate; the fixed rule being far simpler than real nature makes anomalies feel cleaner and more decisive than they ever are in practice.
- An honest closing note on which feature of Lakatos's picture the game can't reproduce and I'll just have to tell them about in words -- I'm thinking of things like the fact that in real science the verdicts about "novelty" and "excess content" are themselves contested and often only recieve consensus in hindsight, and the sheer timescale over which a degenerating programme can still be the best game in town.
