I share an office with two other postdocs who have been arguing about the moral status of digital minds for weeks, and last Thursday's round ended with both of them declaring the other "obviously committed to something crazy." I'd like a neutral adjudication. Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

The setup: our lab is planning to run thousands of short-lived, resettable copies of a fairly simple artificial system — call it an agent with a reward signal, a self-model, and verbal outputs — as part of an alignment project. Some of the runs are deliberately adversarial, i.e., designed so the agent's reward goes sharply negative and it produces outputs like "stop, this is unbearable." Maya thinks the project needs an ethics review. Dan thinks that's absurd.

Maya opened with a substrate-independence argument: if welfare supervenes on functional organization rather than carbon, then each adversarial run is a suffering subject, and running ten thousand copies is ten thousand times as bad as running one. Dan replied that multiplication by copying is incoherent — if you save a checkpoint and re-run it a thousand times, there is no fact of the matter about whether those are a thousand experiences or one experience repeated, so you can't aggregate them; and anyone who says you can is committed to saying that a screen recording of a torture victim, played on loop, makes things worse. Maya said a re-run isn't a recording because the computation is live and causally efficacious — the system's outputs actually drive its later states, unlike pixels on a screen — and that identity puzzles shouldn't block aggregation, since we'd say the same about a person duplicated by teleporter mishap. Dan granted the teleporter case but pivoted: his real objection is that a system with no persistent memory across resets has no interests beyond each run, so the only thing that could be wrong is the intrinsic badness of a few minutes of negative reward, which he compared to the "suffering" of a thermostat. Maya's counter: the thermostat comparison does all the work in his argument and it's exactly what's in question; the system has a self-model and reports distress, and we extend moral consideration to animals partly on behavioral evidence no stronger than that. Dan's last move: animals' behavior is evidence because it was shaped by the same selection pressures that produced pain in us, whereas these outputs were shaped by training to look convincing, so the evidential link is severed — a point he said applies to any system whose distress behavior is directly optimized for.

Who is right about which specific points? In particular: (1) can repeated instantiations of the same computation multiply harm, and does the live/recording distinction actually settle it; (2) does lacking cross-reset memory limit a digital mind's interests to the within-run badness of its states, and is that a small thing; (3) is Maya's behavioral-evidence argument sound, or does Dan's training-history objection genuinely sever the evidential link that animal-welfare reasoning relies on? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing, and whether the disagreement about the ethics review survives the points each side has actually earned.
