Warren Quinn's famous self-torturer puzzle describes a person hooked up to a device with 1000 settings, where each step up adds a bit more pain but so little that you can't actually feel the difference between any two neighboring settings, and each step also pays you a nice chunk of money. Taken one step at a time, each upgrade seems clearly worth it, yet the end result is obviously horrible.

Please describe the most important implications of this puzzle for everyday decisions that involve lots of tiny, individually imperceptible tradeoffs, like gradually sacrificing sleep for work, letting hobbies slide one skipped week at a time, or accepting small privacy losses for convenience. In particular, I want you to get into whether the puzzle really shows that rational preferences can be intransitive, or whether it instead shows something about how we should handle vague boundaries and incremental choice. Also adress the follow up question: if each step genuinely feels no worse than the last, is there any principled way to pick where to stop, and do popular proposals like picking a threshold in advance, or treating the whole sequence as one big decision rather than many small ones, actually solve the problem or just move it somewhere else?

Please answer in American English.
