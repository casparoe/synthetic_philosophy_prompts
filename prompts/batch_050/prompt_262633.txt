I'm preparing a graduate seminar on semantic externalism, and I want the final session to be a workshop in which the students construct their own Twin-Earth-style thought experiments rather than simply rehearsing the classics. To calibrate the exercise, I've collected the canonical cases that are supposed to establish that mental and linguistic content depends on factors outside the speaker's head: (1) Putnam's original Twin Earth (1975) — Oscar and Twin-Oscar are molecule-for-molecule duplicates, but the stuff called 'water' on Twin Earth is XYZ rather than H2O, so their thoughts differ in content even though nothing inside them does; (2) Putnam's elm/beech case — I cannot tell an elm from a beech, yet my uses of the two words refer to different trees because experts in my linguistic community can, via the division of linguistic labor; (3) Burge's arthritis case (1979) — a man sincerely reports that he has arthritis in his thigh, and in a counterfactual community where the word is used more broadly his utterance expresses a different belief entirely, so content varies with communal usage even for a term whose application the speaker takes himself to understand; and (4) the switching cases, in which a person unknowingly transported between Earth and Twin Earth slowly acquires thoughts with Twin-Earthly content without any internal change that registers the fact.

Each of these fixes content through one of two mechanisms: the subject's causal contact with samples in the physical environment, or deference to the usage of a surrounding linguistic community. What I'd like from you is one more case for the seminar that supports an externalist conclusion (or embarrasses internalism) through a different mechanism — not a natural kind with a hidden microstructure, not deference to experts, and not an undetected change of environment. It might turn on the subject's own causal history, on perceptual rather than linguistic content, on normative or mathematical vocabulary, or on something else entirely, so long as the content-determining factor lies outside the subject's skin and operates in a way the four listed cases do not. Swapping XYZ for some other substance, or arthritis for some other technical term, would be a rewording rather than a new case; a special case or a hybrid of the listed mechanisms wouldn't count either.

For the case you construct: set it up carefully enough that a student could work out the intended verdict on their own; say precisely what it shows that my four cases do not — which externalist thesis it would establish, or which internalist commitment it would put under pressure, that the canonical cases leave untouched; and then assess it honestly. Does it survive the standard externalist replies and the standard internalist countermoves — for instance, the charge that it confuses reference-fixing with content, or that narrow content was doing all the real psychological work? If the case is interesting but ultimately fails, that is worth knowing too, since half the point of the seminar is to show why these constructions are harder than they look.
