I cover atrocity crimes and international justice for a general-interest magazine. Next month I'm giving a talk at an interdisciplinary conference on the politics of naming mass violence, defending this thesis: the word genocide has become so loaded in public debate that arguing over whether an atrocity "counts" now crowds out description of what is actually happening, and journalists should therefore reserve the term for conduct a competent court has found to be genocide, otherwise writing descriptively — systematic killing of a group as such, deliberate destruction of a group's cultural institutions, and so on. I am not claiming the 1948 Convention definition is wrong; my claim is about journalistic usage tracking findings rather than accusations.

The audience: about half international criminal lawyers who work with the Convention's definition daily; several historians and political scientists in the Lemkin tradition who regard the legal definition as a political compromise that gutted Lemkin's broader conception (which encompassed cultural destruction and arguably targeted political groups); and a contingent of senior editors who enforce newsroom style rules on contested terminology.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the half-dozen objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way its faction would phrase it. For each: whether it has real force or is mainly rhetorical, what I should concede, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply, with a note on where that reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and my best answer to it anyway.
