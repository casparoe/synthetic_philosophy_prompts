So I spent fourty years as a field entomologist, and now that I'm retired I've fallen into philosophy the way you fall into a pond — accidentally, and now I'm in over my head. My reading group just finished a stretch on concept learning and induction, and the thing that grabbed me were Bongard problems: those little puzzles where you're shown six figures that obey some rule and six that don't, and you have to infer the rule from nothing but the examples. What I love is that the puzzle is basically the whole problem of induction in miniature — any finite set of examples fits infinitely many rules, so actually solving one is a small miracle of picking the right hypothesis. (I gather Goodman made a version of this point with his grue business, and my granddaughter, who does machine learning, tells me her entire field lives inside this problem.)

Here's what I want: write me a poem that is itself a Bongard problem. Twelve lines, of which exactly six belong to the positive class and six to the negative, interleaved so the reader can't cheat off the layout, with the rule being something about the lines themselves — their words, their grammar, their imagery, whatever you like, so long as it's inferable from the examples alone and a clever reader could genuinely crack it. At the same time the poem should be about the very thing it enacts: concept induction, the puzzle of how anyone ever latches onto a rule from a handful of cases. Form and content pulling in the same direction, like a good analogy. And I'd rather the rule not be a cheap trick along the lines of "starts with a vowel" — make it something with a bit of conceptual bite, the way the good Bongard puzzles have, where once you see it you can't unsee it.

Two more asks. Keep the underdetermination worry humming in the background without stating it outright — someone who knows the grue problem should feel it in the poem rather than be lectured about it, and someone who doesn't should just get a good poem about the strangeness of learning. Then, after the poem, give me the answer separately: state the rule plainly, mark which lines are which, and own up to one alternative rule that also fits your twelve lines, since being honest about the imposter rules is half the point. No citations or reading lists please — I've got my own library for that, and I'd rather have the thing itself.
