I've been caring for my aging father for the past two years -- managing his finances, driving him to appointments, fielding his daily calls -- and in working through what I actually owe him I've come around to something like Jane English's friendship model of filial obligation. As I understand it, her claim is that adult children do not owe their parents repayment for the sacrifices of child-rearing, since those sacrifices were freely given rather than loans, and that whatever grown children owe their parents is instead grounded entirely in the ongoing relationship: roughly, what a good friend would owe a good friend, given the history and the present state of the friendship. Where the relationship has gone cold, or was never warm, or was actively harmful, the obligations shrink accordingly. I find this liberating and basically right, and I've started using it to push back on siblings who invoke what Dad did for us as if it were an outstanding debt.

But I want to stress-test the view before I lean on it any harder, so: what would count against it? I'm not asking for a survey of alternative theories of filial duty (I know the gratitude model and the special-goods model exist); I'm asking what specifically the friendship model risks, and where. Which cases does it handle badly or fail to describe at all -- for instance, obligations to a parent with dementia who can no longer participate in anything resembling friendship, or duties owed to a parent whose sacrifices were extreme even by parental standards? Which intuitions about desert, vulnerability, or the sheer asymmetry of the parent-child history does it have to dismiss, and are those dismissals costs the view can afford? If it turns out the model needs heavy qualification -- say, a floor of duties that persists even when the friendship is dead -- does it still say anything distinctive, or has it collapsed into the rivals it was meant to replace?

I'd like the potential defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest sense of which of them a committed defender of the friendship model would concede as real damage and which they'd wave away, and why. Please take a definite stand rather than laying out possibilities and leaving me to choose: tell me which objection you think actually lands hardest, whether the view survives it and in what form, and what I should say to my siblings when the debt talk starts up again.
