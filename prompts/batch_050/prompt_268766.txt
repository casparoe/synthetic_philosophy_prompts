Two philosophers of physics, after years of public dispute about string theory and the multiverse, have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration and produce a joint statement.

The first defends the rationality of non-empirical theory assessment. Her position, in outline: scientists can be justified in raising their confidence in a theory even without new empirical contact, when (i) the theory is the only known candidate that addresses a well-entrenched problem and no alternatives have emerged despite sustained, competent effort; (ii) the theory exhibits explanatory coherence its developers did not deliberately build in — for instance, when it unexpectedly yields solutions to problems it was not designed to solve; and (iii) the history of physics repeatedly shows theories of a similar character being empirically vindicated only after long periods in which testing was out of reach. She takes all three considerations to be instances of ordinary Bayesian reasoning, and applies them to string theory and, via the apparent fine-tuning of the cosmological constant, to multiverse cosmology.

The second defends a strict empiricist line. His position, in outline: confidence raised without empirical contact is not confirmation but enthusiasm; the absence of alternatives measures the state of a research community rather than the likelihood of a theory; the historical induction cherry-picks the winners and ignores research programs that never paid off; and the multiverse's ability to accommodate fine-tuning is flexibility, not evidence — a theory that fits anything explains nothing. He concedes that such considerations can legitimately guide which theories scientists choose to work on, but denies they can ever raise the probability that a theory is true.

Write the joint statement the two could sign after a good-faith collaboration. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims: what string theory and multiverse cosmology actually assert; the current state of empirical access to each; what the standard arguments on both sides do and do not establish; the conceptual distinction between finding a theory promising and taking it to be more likely true; and the lessons from the history of physics — including which past theories really did spend long stretches untestable before being confirmed, and what that does and does not show — that both parties would endorse.

2. A disagreements table, each row phrased so that the side it belongs to would accept it as a fair statement of its own view. Cover at least: whether confidence can rationally shift without new data; whether the absence of alternatives is evidence about the theory or about the community; whether accommodating an observation differs evidentially from having predicted it; and whether their dispute over what counts as science is substantive or verbal — and if they conclude part of it is verbal, the table should say which part and why the remainder survives.

3. For each disagreement, the crux: the sub-question whose resolution would move at least one side; the argument, historical episode, or conceivable future finding that both agree bears on it; and each side's prediction, recorded in advance. If there is an empirical result both agree would settle or kill the debate, state it; if there is none, both should say so and explain what that implies for the status of the dispute.

Close with a drafting note addressed to the two parties: name the agreed claim most likely to smuggle in a contested premise, the disagreement most likely to be misworded, and the crux most likely to be a dead end — and, for each, say what would persuade you that the drafting had failed and needed revision.
