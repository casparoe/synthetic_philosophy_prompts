I'm auditing my own gut intuitions about fiction, specifically Sherlock Holmes, and I want to know what principle (if any) I'm actually relying on. Here's where I land, case by case:

1. "Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street." True, at least in some sense. I'd correct someone who said he lived in Manchester.

2. "Sherlock Holmes exists." False. Flat out false, I'd say.

3. "Sherlock Holmes never existed." True.

4. "Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character." True. But notice this sits weirdly next to 2 and 3 — if there's no such person, who exactly is it that's a fictional character?

5. "Conan Doyle invented Sherlock Holmes." True, obviously.

6. "Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any real detective who ever lived." True, I'd say, even though the comparison crosses some kind of line between fiction and reality.

7. "Sherlock Holmes is cleverer than Hercule Poirot." Feels like it could genuinely have an answer?? At least it feels more meaningful than asking whether Holmes is cleverer than my cousin Dave.

8. "Sherlock Holmes was born in 1854." Neither true nor false — the stories don't say (as far as I know), so there's just no fact of the matter.

9. "Sherlock Holmes had a pet canary as a kid." Same as 8? Or is there a difference — 8 feels like it has a determinat answer we merely don't know, while 9 feels like there's no answer at all. Honestly I can't tell if those are actually different.

10. "Sherlock Holmes is my favourite detective." True. But again — favourite *detective*? He isn't a detective. On my own verdict in 3, he's nothing. So what am I even saying?

11. "221B Baker Street is Sherlock Holmes's address." True... but 221B Baker Street is also a real spot in London with a museum in it. So is this a claim about fiction or about London?

My tentative principle going in was: a claim about Holmes is true iff Doyle's stories say so, false iff they contradict it, and neither otherwise. But this clearly chokes on 4, 5, 6, 10 and 11, all of which are true and none of which are in the stories. Is there a better principle — or a small handful of principles for different "modes" of talking about fiction — that captures all or most of my verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which verdicts your best candidate has to abandon and whether that's a price worth paying, and say what it implies for cases I haven't listed. Most of all I want to know what it says about the one I keep flip-flopping on: my friend insists "Sherlock Holmes is the greatest detective in history" is *false* because he isn't real, I keep saying it's true, and neither of us can figure out what we're actually disagreeing about.
