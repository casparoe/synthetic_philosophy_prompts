I am a second-year undergraduate taking a course on African American aesthetics, and I keep running into the slogan 'all art is propaganda', which everyone in the class associates with W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 address Criteria of Negro Art, published in The Crisis. It came up most recently when a classmate deployed it to shut down a discussion: I had suggested that a particular protest novel felt artistically thin precisely because its political message was doing all the work, and she replied that since all art is propaganda anyway, there is no coherent standpoint from which to criticize a work for being propagandistic. That struck me as too quick, but I found I could not say exactly why, because I could not pin down what the slogan actually claims.

Please respond as a professional philosopher of art who is also expert in Black intellectual history and the Harlem Renaissance debates Du Bois was intervening in (including the pushback from advocates of art for art's sake, who took propagandistic aims to corrupt art). Think carefully rather than reaching for the standard summary. I would like you to begin with a short brainstorming pass listing every distinct thesis the slogan could plausibly be expressing, and then organize your answer around the most defensible four to six of them.

For each reading, state it as a precise claim and give a verdict: is it a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something closer to false? To get you started, I can already see several candidates, which you should refine, reject, or add to: first, a descriptive claim that all art as a matter of fact serves some social or political function, whether or not its maker intends this; second, a stronger constitutive claim that serving such a function is part of what art is, so that apolitical art is not even coherent; third, a normative claim that artists, at least Black artists in Du Bois's position, ought to make their art serve the cause of justice; fourth, a deflationary claim about the word itself, namely that propaganda is not a term of abuse and that propagandistic art can be aesthetically excellent; and fifth, a reflexive claim that the purists' insistence on pure, disinterested art is itself propaganda for a particular social order.

Two further asks. First, say which reading or readings Du Bois himself most plausibly needed for his actual argument in the address, given that he went on to describe his own writing as having always served propagandistic ends — it matters to me whether the strongest version of his position is the provocative constitutive one or something more modest. Second, return to my classroom exchange: which readings of the slogan, if any, would actually vindicate my classmate's move of dismissing the thinness worry, and which readings would in fact leave my criticism intact or even support it? I would rather get an honest menu with verdicts than a single authoritative disambiguation.
