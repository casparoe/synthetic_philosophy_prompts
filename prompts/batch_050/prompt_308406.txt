I'm setting the qualifying paper for our grad seminar on the metaphysics of time and I need one essay question on Gödel's rotating universes. The problem is that all my students can rattle off the headline -- Gödel found solutions to Einstein's field equations in which matter rotates and closed timelike curves exist, and concluded that time is "ideal" rather than objective. I want a question they can't answer with that summary.

What I actually want tested is whether they understand where the argument's real action is, which I think is the modal step. Gödel's move isn't "time travel is physically possible, spooky!" It's roughly: in the rotating worlds you can't carve the cosmos into a consistent global series of "nows," so there's no objective lapse of time *there*; but those worlds obey the very same laws as ours and differ only in contingent facts about how matter happens to be distributed and moving; so the reality of time in our world would rest on a cosmic accident, and Gödel thought no genuinely objective feature of reality could depend on a contingency like that. Whether or not that reconstruction is exactly right, a good candidate has to state the inference precisely and then evaluate it: does "time is real" have to mean "there's a global temporal ordering," or could time be real without objective lapse? Does the mere existence of physically possible CTC worlds threaten time in the actual world, or is there a principled difference between laws and boundary conditions that blocks the modal step -- and does it matter that Gödel universes aren't expanding while ours apparently is? Candidates should also have to say why the conclusion doesn't already follow from the bare possibility of local time-travel loops, since plenty of philosophers accept those without giving up on time.

So please give me:
1. The exam question itself, answerable in about 75 minutes, pitched so that reciting "Gödel + CTCs = time is ideal" gets you a failing answer.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words.
3. A marking scheme with five must-hit points, marks allocated out of 20, and for each point a one-line note on the specific way students typically go wrong on it (e.g. confusing the ideality claim with the claim that time travel is actual, or treating the argument as if it were just about whether CTCs are paradoxical).
