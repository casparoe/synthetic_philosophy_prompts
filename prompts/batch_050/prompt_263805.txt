Design an original thought experiment that dramatizes why the question "Who started the Cold War?" may be badly posed as a question about blame. The scenario should not itself be about the Cold War: set it in an ordinary, realistic setting — two neighboring businesses, two academic departments, two households in a dispute over shared property, or something similar — but engineer it so that three different frameworks for assigning responsibility, loosely mirroring the three main schools of Cold War historiography, deliver three different verdicts about your case:

1. A first-move framework (responsibility falls on whichever party performed the first openly hostile act) assigns blame to party A.
2. A structural-provocation framework (responsibility falls on whichever party created the conditions that made the other's hostile response nearly inevitable, whether deliberately or as a foreseeable byproduct of pursuing legitimate aims) assigns blame to party B.
3. An interactionist framework (responsibility lies in the escalating feedback loop between the parties rather than in either one) concludes that the blame question is itself misleading in this case.

Constraints: the scenario must be describable in a single paragraph; neither party may be cartoonishly malicious or irrational — every step each party takes should be defensible from that party's own point of view, given what they could reasonably believe at the time; and you may not stipulate the three verdicts by fiat. The verdicts must fall out of the events you describe, such that a reader applying each framework in good faith would reach the verdict you claim for it.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. (a) Walk through what each of the three frameworks says about your case and why, showing the reasoning step by step rather than merely asserting the verdicts. (b) Identify the single structural feature of your case that makes the three frameworks diverge, and say what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed. (c) Explain how a committed defender of the first-move framework — the one your case treats least kindly — would try to resist your construction: what would they say to keep the blame on party A, and does their objection expose a genuine weakness in the case or merely restate the framework's commitments? (d) Conclude with a short paragraph on what the exercise suggests about whether "Who started the Cold War?" is the right historiographical question to ask, and formulate at least one better-posed replacement question that a historian could actually set out to answer.
