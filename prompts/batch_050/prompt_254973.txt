I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy (my home turf is really decision theory, but I've drifted into population ethics) and in three weeks I'm presenting at our department's works-in-progress series. The paper defends a view I'm calling difference-set ethics for infinite worlds: roughly, that when we're comparing actions in a universe that contains infinitely many happy and unhappy lives regardless of what we do, we shouldn't try to sum or average total welfare across all of spacetime (which just gives undefined or equal infinities), but should instead compare actions only over the regions where their outcomes actually differ -- the "difference set" -- and rank actions by the finite comparisons available there, staying silent where no such comparison exists. So in the case I keep using, where a god offers you a deal that moves one galaxy's worth of flourishing civilizations from one side of an infinite, uniformly happy cosmos to the other without changing anyone's wellbeing, my view says the deal is neither good nor bad but simply not ranked, whereas total-utilitarian summing either crashes or says the swap is a matter of indifference, and I think not-ranked is the right verdict because the alternative lets you be "paid" in infinitely offsetting compensations. The audience is about fifteen people: three population ethicists who've thought hard about infinite populations and will know the standard paradoxes, two formal epistemology people who will want everything precisified, a couple of utilitarians of the classical totalist stripe, and one cosmologist who sits in on everything and loves asking whether any of this survives contact with an actually infinite universe with no privileged foliation.
