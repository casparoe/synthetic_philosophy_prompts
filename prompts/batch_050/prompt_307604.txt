I’m giving a 35-minute talk next week to a formal epistemology reading group on van Fraassen’s Reflection principle. My thesis is that Reflection is usually stated too broadly: a rational agent does not have to defer in advance to every future credence she can predict. She owes reflective deference only when the future credence results from what I’m calling lossless learning, where the later information state refines the earlier one and the change comes from conditioning on evidence the present self already regards as relevant. I want to exclude predictable memory loss, rational information compression, changes in the question being asked, and cases where the future self is forced to coarse-grain. I’m calling the restricted view checkpoint reflection.

The example I’m building the talk around is this: at noon I have detailed evidence supporting a hypothesis, including knowledge that it came from a highly reliable instrument. At 6 p.m. I will retain the numerical result but predictably forget which instrument produced it. My 6 p.m. credence will therefore be lower than my noon credence. I claim that if I know this in advance, I should not lower my noon credence merely because I can predict the later one. Reflection applies only to admissible checkpoints in an information-preserving chain, not to every later stage of my own cognitive life.

The audience is small but dangerous: one hard-line Bayesian who thinks rational belief change is just conditionalization; one accuracy-first epistemologist who cares about diachronic rationality and will press me on whether my view licenses avoidable inaccuracy; one philosopher of probability who is suspicious of Dutch-book arguments but knows exactly how they work; one philosopher of physics who thinks treating agents as cleanly separated information states is a convenient fiction; and a couple of grad students who enjoy extremely artificial counterexamples. None of them needs Reflection explained from scratch.

Please make me a Q&A prep sheet, not a general essay. Give me the eight or so objections I’m most likely to get from this particular audience, ordered by a combination of likelihood and damage. For each one: say who is most likely to raise it, phrase it the way they would actually phrase it, explain what the strongest version of the worry is, tell me what I should concede, what I should refuse to concede, and give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds. Also mark where each reply is weakest, and where I should bite a bullet instead of pretending the objection is confused.

Seperately, I want help with three pressure points in my own formulation. First, is lossless learning doing real work, or is it just a label for cases where Reflection happens to give the answer I want? Second, can I distinguish rational compression from ordinary evidence gathering without making the principle dependent on controversial claims about cognitive limitations? Third, if I say the future self can be perfectly rational while still not worthy of present deference, am I giving up the idea that rationality is temporally unified in a way the Bayesians will rightly object to?

End with the question I’m hoping nobody asks, the best bad answer I could give if it comes up, and one place where I should probably soften the thesis before the talk rather than defend it to the death.
