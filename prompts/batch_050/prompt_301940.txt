I am a novelist researching a thriller, and I have spent months reading the literature on torture and the ticking time bomb. The debate strikes me as frozen. One side holds that in the idealized case — a captured terrorist, a bomb known to be planted, a known deadline, no other options — torture is obviously justified, perhaps obligatory, and that anyone who denies this is in the grip of a rule worship they would abandon in the situation itself. The other side replies that the case is a fantasy rigged to force that answer: in reality one never knows there is a bomb, never knows the suspect knows where it is, never knows torture will extract the truth in time, and an institution licensed for the idealized case will in fact be used on the non-ideal ones. Both sides have been making exactly these moves for decades, and neither has conceded an inch.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the dispute is not really about torture but about which cases count as evidence in moral reasoning — the vivid singular hypothetical versus the statistical reality of institutions. As someone whose trade is the vivid singular case, I know where my own bias lies.

Acting as a moral philosopher with experience in applied ethics, please think carefully about this. Before answering, briefly brainstorm the candidate diagnoses, then settle on one: is this a bedrock clash between deontological and consequentialist starting points, a hidden empirical disagreement about what interrogation actually yields, a disagreement about which cases count as data, or two parties answering different questions under one name — one about what to do in the case, one about what rules to enact? Support your diagnosis from what each side actually says, and tell me what, given it, would count as progress — an empirical result about interrogation, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — or else argue honestly that no progress should be expected, and why.
