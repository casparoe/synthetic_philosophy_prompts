I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on the philosophy of archaeology, and I am setting the final essay paper. The recurring difficulty I face is that students can recite the standard vocabulary — equifinality, formation processes, analogy, the so-called Pompeii premise — without being able to put it to work on an actual assemblage. I therefore want one essay question built around a constructed scenario, which I give here for you to embed in the question: a survey team uncovers a riverside settlement of roughly forty dwellings occupied for about two centuries; one house is twice the size of the rest and contains worked bone, copper ornaments, and storage pits; a cemetery a short walk away contains ninety graves, of which six (all of children) hold copper ornaments identical to those in the large house, whilst the remainder hold only plain pottery or nothing; and a scatter of deliberately broken copper objects lies along the riverbank downstream of the settlement. The question must ask candidates to say what, if anything, this evidence warrants about the community's beliefs and institutions — for instance, whether the large house signals hereditary hierarchy, whether the child graves licence inferences about ascribed rather than achieved status, and whether the riverbank scatter is evidence of ritual deposition rather than discard — and, crucially, to show their working: which background assumptions (ethnographic analogy, uniformitarian principles about how deposits form) each inference presupposes, and which alternative processes could have produced the same material pattern. The question should be unanswerable by reciting definitions; it should reward candidates who notice, for example, that the six child graves cut both ways (hereditary rank, or special funerary treatment of children tout court) and who can say what further evidence would discriminate between the readings.

Please write, in American English: (1) the exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, for a 60-minute essay; (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme totalling 30 marks, organised around the specific moves a first-class answer must make, with the marks attached to each, a note on how the boundary between a first-class and an upper-second answer should be drawn, and a one-line remark per move on the characteristic way weaker candidates fumble it (for instance, treating deliberate breakage as self-evidently ritual, or assuming that grave goods transparently record social rank rather than the mourners' choices). Keep the scenario self-contained: no citations, no reading lists, and no reliance on particular named sites or texts, since candidates sit this paper without materials.
