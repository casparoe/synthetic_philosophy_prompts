So I retired two years ago after forty years as a marine biologist and have fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, as you do. One thing I keep circling back to is the prisoner's dilemma stuff -- Axelrod's tournaments, tit-for-tat and its more forgiving cousins, the whole evolution of cooperation literature, which I half-knew already from the biology side. Meanwhile I somehow got elected to the disputes commitee of our allotment association (community garden, 60 plots, endless feuds over shared tools, water rosters, whose compost is whose). The same people clash again and again, which strikes me as exactly the iterated case the theory is about, and I'd like to run things on something better than vibes. Write me a procedure I can actually follow when a dispute lands on my desk: the questions to ask, in order -- is this really one-shot or will these two keep meeting for years, how much noise and misperception is in play (did he actually take the spade or did someone just move it), is the relationship about to end because someone's giving up their plot, does either party have a real exit option, and crucially, is cooperation here even the goal, since two plot-holders cooperating to hog the communal mower is collusion, not harmony. Ground each step in what the tournament and evolution results actually support -- nice, provocable, forgiving, clear, generous when there's noise -- and flag where I'd be stretching a result about populations of strategies into advice for individuals that it can't bear. Then run the procedure on two made-up allotment disputes, including one where it tells me to recommend something I'll hate, and be honest about where the procedure has to stop and judgment take over.
