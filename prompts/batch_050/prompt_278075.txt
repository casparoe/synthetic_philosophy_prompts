So my company just announced a pilot where warehouse staff can voluntarily wear these EEG headbands that track focus and fatigue, and it's got me spiraling about what mental privacy even should cover. I've been polling my own gut on cases and the pattern is confusing me. The headband thing, even fully voluntary with a pay bump: feels not fine, like there's pressure baked in. My friend with a medical BCI implant for epilepsy who wants to share her neural data with researchers to help develop better devices: totally fine, obviously her call. Police asking that same friend to let them scan her implant's logs because she witnessed an assault and her device might have recorded stress-response timestamps that place her at the scene, and she consents: I think fine? but I feel weird about it. A car insurance app that uses one of those consumer focus trackers to prove you weren't drowsy in a crash, opt-in for a discount: not fine, even though it looks exactly like the headband case plus a discount instead of coercion. Spotify-style mood playlists driven by a cheap brainwave reader: fine, whatever, it's just vibes. An employer using that same mood data to decide who seems "engaged" in meetings: absolutely not. A mom reading her teenager's sleep-and-attention dashboard from his study headband because she paid for it: not fine, though I know plenty of parents who'd disagree. The case I keep flip-flopping on is neuromarketing: a company testing which ad images trigger craving responses in people's brains, consented, paid, anonymized -- part of me says that's just a focus group with better instruments, part of me says it's extracting something people can't meaningfully consent to giving away because they don't know what it reveals about them.

Is there a principle that captures most of this? My tentative theory is something like "your brain data is yours to share but no one with power over you gets to ask," but that breaks on the police case (cops have power, she consented, I still think it's ok) and maybe on the mom case (parents have power over kids but also legit interests). Maybe the real distinction is about what the data gets used for, or whether it's inferences versus raw signals, or something about voluntariness being impossible under dependence. Give me your best principle (or two or three candidates if one won't cut it), stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each of my cases motivates, tell me what it says about cases I didn't list (like subpoenaing neural data, or advertisers buying aggregated brain-data sets), and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save -- I'm prepared to abandon one or two if the simpler principle is good enough.

Lead with the principle itself in your first sentence -- like, just state it -- and then walk through the cases and the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my cases back at me.
