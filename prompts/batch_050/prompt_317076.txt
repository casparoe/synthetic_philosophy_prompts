I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm drafting a long piece about Daniel Dennett's notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity — the idea that a self is not an inner pearl or homunculus but an abstract point, fixed by the stories a person tells about who they are, in roughly the way a center of gravity is fixed by the distribution of mass in an object. While workshopping the draft with a colleague, she pressed an argument on me that she claims shows the view proves too much, and I've been turning it over for a week without finding a satisfying exit. Here it is as she stated it. (1) A center of gravity is a calculational device: positing one lets you predict how an object will behave, but the center is not an additional item in the world over and above the object and its parts — nothing is located there, and you could in principle do all your physics without mentioning it. (2) Dennett's view says the self stands to a person's narratives and self-ascribed mental states exactly as a center of gravity stands to a physical body's mass distribution. (3) So the self, too, is merely a calculational device, not an additional item in the world. (4) But things that are merely calculational devices cannot be harmed, wronged, praised, blamed, or owed anything; it makes no sense to say you wronged a center of gravity or that one deserves compensation. (5) So if Dennett is right, no one is ever really wronged, no one is genuinely responsible for anything, and moral practices that presuppose persons are built on a bookkeeping convention we could discard without loss. My colleague takes this as a reductio of the view. I find the conclusion unacceptable — it seems to dissolve the very phenomenon Dennett was trying to explain — but I can't cleanly name the step that fails, and I suspect the trouble hides in the move from (3) to (4), where "merely a calculational device" may be doing more work than (1) and (2) entitle it to.

I'd like your help diagnosing this. Tell me which premise or inference you would reject and why, but also explain why that step looked innocent — I want to understand what made it seductive, not just where it broke. In particular, I'd like you to consider whether the flaw lies in an equivocation about what kind of non-thing a center of gravity is (there seems to be a difference between a fiction, in the sense of something claims about which are not literally true, and an abstraction, in the sense of a pattern that is really there even though it is not a separate ingredient), and whether Dennett himself would grant premise (1) as stated, given his insistence elsewhere that patterns can be perfectly real without being extra furniture. Then price the alternatives: what does it cost to reject (1), what does it cost to reject (4), and is there a reading on which my colleague's argument is valid but the least painful exit is to deny (2) and say the self is an abstraction of a different, more committed kind than she assumed? I'd also like your honest assessment of whether the argument exposes a genuine soft spot in Dennett's view — for instance, whether his account can underwrite moral responsibility as robustly as commonsense does, or only a deflated version of it — rather than a mere verbal slip on her part. Please answer in continuous prose, as if writing me a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
