I'm a retired experimental physicist and lately I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole about evidence, and I keep going back and forth on where I actually land. So here's a bunch of cases and my gut verdicts, and what I want is: what principle, if any, gets me most of these without tying itself in knots?

1. The famous blue bus setup, roughly: a bus ran someone off the road, and 80% of the buses in town belong to the Blue Bus Company, but that's all we know. My verdict: not enough for a finding against the company, civil or criminal. Even though 80% would be a landslide in my old field.

2. Same accident, but now an eyewitness who knows buses says it was a blue one. I think that IS enough to find against the company, or at least a lot closer. What's odd is I don't think eyewitnesses are actually more reliable than the 80% statistic -- probably less! Yet my gut treats them completely differently.

3. A thousand people attend a rodeo, only 400 tickets were sold, and a random attendee is sued for gatecrashing on the ground that there's a 60% chance he didn't pay. Not enough, says my gut. But here's the wrinkle: if the organizers sued ALL thousand attendees and won 60% of the time, they'd be right about 600 people -- and somehow that seems fine to me as an aggregate outcome while each individual win bothers me. I can't tell if that's inconsistent or if there's something real there.

4. DNA match at a crime scene, one in a million match rate, in a country of 300 million, defendant picked up purely because of the database hit, no other evidence. My gut says: not enough for conviction. But my physicist brain says one-in-a-million against priors that low still comes out strongly against him, and I don't fully trust either side of that argument.

5. Fingerprint match plus the defendant was seen near the scene and had a motive -- suddenly a much weaker-looking case per piece feels like plenty. So combining evidence seems to do something that a single statistic never does, and I can't articulate what.

6. A plane crashes and there's no investigation possible. I would NOT want a court to find the airline liable just because most crashes are pilot error and this airline has more pilot-error crashes than average. And yet in my old life I'd have called that the rational bet. I'm fine with this tension, I think -- betting and verdicts are different games -- but I couldn't defend that under pressure.

So: is there one principle here (something about specific vs. statistical evidence, or about what a court owes the individual), or two principles, or am I just inconsistent? Which of my verdicts would a good theory force me to give up, and what's it predict for cases I didn't mention -- say, an AI system flagging someone as 90% likely to reoffend at sentencing? And please be straight with me about which parts of this are basically settled in the literature and which parts people who actually work on it still fight over -- don't dress up one side of a live dispute as the consensus view, I'd rather know where the cracks are.
