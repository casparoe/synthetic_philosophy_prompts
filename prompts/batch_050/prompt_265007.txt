so I TA an undergrad ethics seminar and the students keep catching me out, so let me write down where I actually stand and you can tell me whether it hangs together. My commitments:

(a) I think the separateness-of-persons objection is basically knock-down against classical utilitarianism: a burden on Alice being "outweighed" by a benefit to Bob is nothing like a burden on Alice now being outweighed by a benefit to Alice later. Nobody experiences the sum, so adding across people is bookkeeping, not compensation.

(b) Prudential tradeoffs within a single life really are as unproblematic as the utilitarians say -- and that, for me, is exactly what makes the interpersonal case different.

(c) I'm also pretty convinced by Parfit-style reductionism about personal identity: what matters in survival is psychological continuity and connectedness, identity is not what matters, and the boundaries between selves are less deep than common sense assumes.

(d) In rescue cases I think you should just save the greater number. Views where the numbers don't count (Taurek-style) seem to me borderline crazy.

(e) Even so, I don't think the surgeon may carve up the one healthy patient to save five in the transplant case.

(f) When my utilitarian friends say "you already aggregate, you just do it within lives, so extend it," I tell them that misses the point of the objection entirely.

Which of these are genuinely in tension and which only look like it? Please rank the real tensions by severity, say for each one what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs, and tell me if theres a single revision that would clean up the most. For what it's worth, my students' favourite move is to claim (d) and (e) contradict each other, and I assume they're wrong about that -- but I'd like to be able to say exactly why instead of just asserting it.
