I'm a PhD student in political theory and this term I'm TAing a first-year intro course on justice. In a couple weeks I get one 80-minute section to teach the "equality of what?" debate -- the basic idea that egalitarians can agree that something should be equalized while disagreeing about what the currency is (welfare, resources, capabilities), and that the choice of currency isn't a technicality but changes who you think is badly off.

Don't give me a mini-lecture I can memorize; plan how I should run the explanation. Specifically:

- The order. What do students need in place before the "currency" question even makes sense to them? My sense is most of them arrive assuming egalitarianism just means equal money, and if I jump straight to Sen's critique it'll feel like hair-splitting. Tell me what has to come first and why the ordering matters.
- The carrying example. I want one concrete case that does the whole job -- something like a person with a disability, or a person with expensive tastes, where the three currencies visibly diverge on what she's owed. Should I use one person and run all three currencies over her, or three different people? And what's wrong with the "expensive tastes" example as the lead case (I have a feeling it makes welfarism look sillier than it is)?
- The misconception map. What confusions should I expect, and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off? I'm guessing at least: (a) that this is a debate about whether equality matters rather than what it amounts to, (b) that capabilities are just "resources plus extra stuff," (c) that picking a currency settles policy questions directly.
- The check. One question I can pose at the end where a wrong answer tells me which misconception they're still holding -- not just whether they can recite the three labels.
- What to deliberately leave out. There's obviously a ton more (luck egalitarianism, the metric vs. distributive-rule distinction, Dworkin's auction) -- what should I openly bracket and how do I say so without making it sound like the interesting part is elsewhere?

The students are mostly not philosophy majors and a lot of them did the reading at 2am, if at all. Keep the plan realistic for that.
