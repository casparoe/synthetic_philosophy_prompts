Eighteen months ago my mother had a stroke. She recovered fluent, grammatical speech, but with a twist the neurologists find benign and I find agonizing: she uses ordinary words in systematically nonstandard ways, perfectly consistently. Talking with her now feels like fieldwork — I have had to build, half-consciously, a working translation manual for her idiolect from her patterns of assent and dissent. I am also her healthcare proxy, and a decision about a major elective surgery is due within the month. Her answer, when the surgeons ask her directly, is consistent every time — but what it means depends entirely on which manual is right.

Construct a thought experiment, set in a situation recognizably like mine — a hospital, a proxy decision, no science fiction, no mind-reading devices, no omniscient interpreter — meeting these specifications. Two rival translation manuals for one speaker's idiolect must: (1) fit her observable verbal behavior equally well, including her answers to every follow-up question anyone could realistically pose, so that no further questioning could decide between them; (2) diverge systematically rather than ad hoc — the disagreement is generated by a global reassignment of what her words refer to, compensated for elsewhere in the manual, in the manner of Quine's gavagai argument; and (3) deliver opposite verdicts on a single sentence the proxy must act on, translating it on one manual as consent to the surgery and on the other as refusal.

Give me the scenario itself in under two hundred words. Then demonstrate that it works: state the two manuals compactly, with enough of the compensating reassignments visible that I can see the machinery rather than just the punchline; walk through two or three exchanges where both manuals predict identical behavior; name the single feature of the construction that is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed; and sketch the strongest available objection from someone who insists there is still a fact of the matter about what her words mean — fixed, say, by her decades of causal history with the wider speech community — such that the case shows only my ignorance, not genuine indeterminacy.

The hardest constraint will be keeping the rival manual psychologically plausible rather than obviously gerrymandered; if you think full realism and full systematicity cannot both be had, say so plainly and show how close you can get.

Finally, commit. The case is not an academic exercise for me: I need to know whether the question of which manual is correct must be answered before I can act, or whether the proxy decision can legitimately proceed on other grounds. Take a definite position on whether my situation involves true indeterminacy of meaning — no fact of the matter either way — or merely an inaccessible fact I cannot reach, give your reasons, and then tell me concretely what I should do this month: sign, refuse, or something more specific, with the reasoning a conscientious proxy could actually stand behind. Do not hand me a menu of options; I have enough of those already.
