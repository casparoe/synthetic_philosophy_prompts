**Death in Damascus is a control problem, not a prediction problem**

The standard setup: a man meets Death at the market in Damascus, Death looks surprised to see him, the man concludes Death has an appointment with him there, so he flees to Aleppo overnight. But the appointment was always in Aleppo — Death was surprised because she expected to meet him tomorrow, not today. Whatever the man does, the prediction about where he'll be tracks his choice, so his reasoning is always ex post wrong. The usual lesson people draw is that this is a problem for causal decision theory: CDT tells you to act on your best causal hypothesis about the prediction, but the prediction is a function of the very deliberation CDT is running.

I think this misdiagnoses it, and the right analogy is a financial market with price impact. A small trader can ask "what will the price be tomorrow?" and trade on the answer. A big enough trader can't, because her own order moves the price — she has to ask "given that the price is a function of my order, what order do I want to place?" That's control theory, not forecasting. The man in Damascus is a large trader: Death's prediction has perfect "price impact" on the outcome. CDT's failure here is like a naive trading strategy failing when you *are* the market — not because causal reasoning is broken, but because the model treats an endogenous variable as exogenous.

Consequence: maybe you don't need to abandon CDT for evidential or functional alternatives. You need CDT plus an honest model of which variables depend on your choice. Then Newcomb-flavored problems collapse into a modeling question: is the predictor's output endogenous to your deliberation? If yes, pick the policy you'd want to be predicted as having. If no, just follow the causal arrows.

One more thing, a variant I came up with — call it Death in Basra. Suppose Death is a sloppy predictor: she flips a coin biased 90% toward wherever your deliberation is currently trending, and announces the result. Now there's no guarantee you're wrong whatever you do, no perfect tracking. But CDT still has no obvious way to reason about the biased coin, which suggests the original puzzle was never really about guaranteed regret at all — it's about endogeneity, and the perfect-predictor version just makes the endogeneity impossible to ignore.
