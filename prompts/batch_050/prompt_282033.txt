I run a small futurism reading group (six people, mixed backgrounds, nobody's read the superintelligence literature cover to cover) and I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts each of us into a position on the singleton hypothesis before we start arguing about it. Write me that tree.

Start at the node 'a single stable world-level decision-maker, with no effective outside rivals, is at least physically possible within the next few centuries' and end at named positions. Roughly six to eight leaves, five to seven branching nodes. The leaves must include at least: singleton inevitable and desirable; singleton inevitable and disastrous; singleton possible but contingent on how particular technologies shake out; singleton impossible because coordination at that scale always fails (permanent multipolarity); singleton impossible because no single will survives at that scale (the thing always fragments internally); and 'the singleton/multipolar distinction collapses' as a position in its own right. Give each leaf a name I can print on the handout — a real position name where one exists, your own coined label where nobody's claimed the spot — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

One hard rule: every branching node has to be phrased as a concrete question my group can answer without knowing any of the labels. Not 'do you accept orthogonality?' but a scenario you can react to. To force this, work these two cases into the tree somewhere:

1. The Consensus Engine: one person's mind is uploaded and copied a million times; the copies deliberate and vote, the winning faction's decision becomes world policy, and no human institution can override it. One agent or many? If your answer is 'many', does the singleton hypothesis become trivially false no matter what happens?

2. The Thermostat: a system controls every meaningful parameter on Earth — climate, arsenals, compute, borders — but it has no preferences of its own beyond keeping those parameters inside preset bands, and any government can adjust the bands. Is that a singleton? If the answer here splits from the Consensus Engine case, the node that seperates them has to be about what a singleton is, not about when one might arrive.

Build the tree from the logical space first: list the questions, note which combinations of answers have no defender at all, and for each empty combination say briefly whether it's empty because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored. And if two leaves on your tree can't actually be separated by your questions (say, they only differ on how the singleton comes about, which the tree never asks), say so explicitly rather than papering over it.

Answer in British English please. Start with the tree itself — no preamble, no 'here is the tree you asked for', nothing before the first node.
