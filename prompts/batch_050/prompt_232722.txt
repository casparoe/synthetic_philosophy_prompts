I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and a philosopher I interviewed last month walked me into an argument I've been chewing on since, because the conclusion seems insane but I can't spot the slip. It's about deflationary theories of truth -- the idea that truth isn't some deep property, calling something "true" is just a way of endorsing it. Here's his chain, as best I reconstructed it in my notebook:

(1) To say "it's true that snow is white" is to say nothing more than that snow is white. The truth-predicate adds no content, it's just a device for endorsing things, or for generalizing when you can't list them all ("everything in the report is true").
(2) Any piece of language that adds no content beyond that kind of bookkeeping is, in principle, eliminable with no loss in what we can say.
(3) But the exact same move works for "refers": to say "snow" refers to snow is again just a disquotational device, no substantive word-world property involved.
(4) So reference is a bookkeeping device too, eliminable without loss.
(5) And if neither truth nor reference names any real relation between words and the world, then there is no substantive relation between words and the world at all.
(6) If there's no such relation, then nothing anyone says is genuinely about anything -- including premise (1).
(7) So deflationism about truth refutes itself.

He grinned when he got to (7) and said the deflationist has to give up somewhere around (3). I feel like the damage is actually done later, somewhere between (4) and (6), but I can't articulate why. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what it costs to reject the neighboring steps instead. Fair warning: I'd much rather keep (1) than accept (6), so if your answer is "reject (1)," you need to tell me what I'm actually paying for it.
