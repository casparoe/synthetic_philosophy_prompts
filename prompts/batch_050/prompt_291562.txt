I am a third-year philosophy student enrolled in a seminar on population ethics and axiology, and last week our discussion of suffering-focused views left me genuinely unsettled rather than merely unpersuaded. We covered tranquilism, the view associated in the literature with Lukas Gloor and with certain strands of Buddhist thought, according to which positive experiences have no value in themselves and what ultimately matters is only the absence of suffering and of craving. One classmate argued that tranquilism has an embarrassing implication: an empty universe, or a world of dreamless meditators, ranks no worse than a world full of joyful lives, since neither contains suffering or craving. My professor replied that this is a bullet one can bite, and that the apparent asymmetry in our intuitions might just reflect how we experience pleasure from within, as craving-relief rather than as a positive addition. I went home unable to tell which of these moves is doing real work.

Here is what I want from you: respond as a philosopher specializing in axiology and population ethics, think carefully, and give me your actual credence that tranquilism is correct, where I define tranquilism precisely as the claim that the value of any state of affairs is fully determined by how much suffering and craving it contains, so that experiential states free of suffering and craving are all equal in value regardless of how pleasurable they are. Do not just give me a number; decompose the credence. Some sub-questions I think it depends on are: whether positive experience has any intrinsic value at all or only instrumental value; whether craving is a genuine bad over and above suffering; whether the empty-universe implication is a decisive defeater or an acceptable cost; and whether axiology is even the kind of domain with determinate answers, or whether tranquilism versus its rivals is partly a standoff of intuitions with no fact of the matter. Assign a rough probability to each, say in one line why, show how you combine them into the final number, and identify which sub-question the result is most sensitive to and what argument or consideration would move it most.

Two constraints. First, please separate what is broadly settled among people who work on this (for example, that suffering-focused views form a coherent family, or that the empty-universe implication follows from the view) from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested claim as if it were the consensus. Second, keep the entire response to at most 450 words; I would rather have a tight, explicit decomposition than a long survey.
