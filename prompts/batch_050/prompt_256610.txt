My sister's boyfriend has fallen down a decision theory rabbit hole and it's starting to cost real money, so I need to know whether his argument is actually broken or just weird. Short version: he's convinced that a sufficiently advanced AI -- one that exists later, or is maybe running in some lab already -- could cheaply simulate tons of people like him, and he now donates a chunk of every paycheck to a couple of AI orgs because, as he puts it, that's what the simulators want to see. My sister asked me to check the logic before he adjusts his career plans too. Here's the argument as near as I can reconstruct it from a very long dinner:

(1) A powerful agent that wants you to behave some particular way can make it worth your while by running copies of you and rewarding the copies that comply (and punishing, or just never bothering to instantiate, the ones that don't).
(2) You can never rule out being one of those copies right now, because a good simulation feels exactly like being real.
(3) If you might be a copy, then your choice is effectively being made many times over: whatever you do, all the simulated versions of you do as well.
(4) So the stakes of your ordinary choices are multiplied by the number of copies, and a rational person weighs outcomes by what's at stake.
(5) The rewards or punishments a powerful agent can hand out across that many copies dwarf anything you could gain or loose in normal life.
(6) Therefore you should act the way such an agent would want, even if it never contacts you, and even if its preferences are just your best guess.
(7) But there are loads of incompatible things a powerful agent could want -- one might reward generosity, another ruthlessness, another might reward exactly the people who refuse to reason this way -- and the argument instructs you to comply with all of them, which is impossible.
(8) So the argument proves too much and something in it has to go.

I agree with (8) but everytime I poke at a specific step he has a comeback. Step (2), he says, is just the simulation argument, which actual philosophers take seriously. Step (3) is the one that smells to me -- surely what the copies do is fixed by whatever I do, not some extra thing I control -- but he says that's precisely the point: my decision IS all of their decisions, one computation, many instances. If I had to hold one step no matter what, it'd be (2), and I'd be happiest sacrificing (5) or (6). So: which step is actually wrong, why does it feel right, and what does he have to give up if he drops it? And is there a repaired version that doesn't prove too much, or is this whole style of argument sunk?
