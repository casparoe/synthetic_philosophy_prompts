I run a small book club (we meet in a pub, very informal) and we're doing Kafka's Metamorphosis next month. One of our members is a primary school teacher who's got really into how kids learn categories, and she gave us a potted version of something she half-remembers from a podcast: apparently there are three main theories of what a concept even is. Either you've got a prototype in your head (so a bird is robin-ish and a penguin barely counts), or you store a bunch of remembered individual examples and match new things against those, or you've got a kind of mini-theory about what makes the thing what it is underneath, its hidden essence or causal story. Fine. But rereading the story I couldn't stop noticing that the whole plot is the Samsa family deciding, week by week, whether the thing in the bedroom still counts as Gregor. Early on Grete feeds him and figures out what food he likes, which looks like exemplar thinking, matching him against the remembered brother. Later the father throws the apple and it feels like the prototype view has won: nothing shaped like that could be my son. And the theory view would say the real question is what's going on inside, whether Gregor's mind is still in there, which is weirdly the question the family keeps circling but never quite settles, until Grete basically announces it isn't him anymore and the cleaner treats the body as rubbish to be cleared out.

So here's what I'd like: read the story through this three-way lens. Which theory best describes each family member's reasoning, and how their thinking shifts over the story? Does the story itself seem to have a verdict on when something stops counting as one of us, and is that verdict that none of the three theories apply because it's actually settled by social practise and usefulness, not by any mental file? Where does the lens strain? One thing bugging me: Gregor's inner voice is present the whole time, and none of the three theories seem to know what to do with that, since concepts are things the family has about him, not things he has about himself. And what does the story add that a psychology paper wouldn't? My hunch is it shows the concept "person" is less a stored representation and more a decision you keep remaking until remaking it gets too costly, but I might be overreading.
