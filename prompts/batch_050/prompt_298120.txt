Write a dialogue between two political philosophers debating the following policy proposal, which is fictional but designed to isolate what exactly makes kidney markets repugnant.

The proposal: a national kidney authority holds a legal monopsony on live kidney purchases. It pays every seller the same fixed price — set generously, say at three times median annual income — plus lifetime health insurance and priority status should the seller ever need a transplant themselves. Sellers undergo mandatory psychological evaluation, a six-month cooling-off period, and independent advocacy to confirm the decision is not coerced by debt collectors or family pressure. The authority allocates all purchased kidneys by a public algorithm that ignores ability to pay, ranking recipients only by medical urgency, expected benefit, and waiting time. No private buyer may purchase a kidney; no private seller may choose the recipient. Proponents note that under this scheme, no rich person can outbid a poor person for an organ, exploitation safeguards exceed those in any existing labor market, and the supply of kidneys would plausibly end the waiting-list deaths entirely. Opponents concede all of this and still object.

Construct a dialogue between two characters — call them whatever you like, but make them equally sophisticated — in which the opponent of the scheme tries to articulate what remains wrong with it once exploitation, crowding-out, and unequal access have been engineered away, and the proponent presses each candidate objection in turn. The opponent should seriously develop at least two distinct lines of resistance: first, the claim that treating the body as alienable property corrupts the norm of unconditional solidarity that the health system partly embodies, even when no individual is harmed; and second, the claim that the scheme will in practice sort by economic desperation no matter the safeguards, so that kidneys flow systematically from poor bodies to rich bodies even if money flows back — and that this direction of flow is itself objectionable. The proponent should respond to the *content* of these objections rather than to weaker versions of them: for instance, by asking whether the corruption argument proves too much (does it also condemn paid surrogacy, paid clinical trials, or professional soldiers?), and by asking whether the directional-flow objection would still have force if the poor were also net recipients of kidneys, as transplant data suggest they would be.

The dialogue should run to roughly 600–900 words. Each character should concede points when genuinely pressed rather than repeating slogans, and neither should win cleanly: end the dialogue at a point where the two have located their actual, irreducible disagreement — which may turn out to be an empirical conjecture, a difference in how they weigh symbolic against welfare considerations, or a dispute about whether institutions have expressive meanings at all. Make that terminus explicit in the final exchanges.
