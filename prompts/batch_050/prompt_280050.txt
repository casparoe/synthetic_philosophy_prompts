I coordinate volunteers for a community kitchen, and over the past few years I keep running into a pattern that I can't find a word for, and it has started to feel less like a management problem and more like a philosophical one. One of our volunteers began staying late on her own initiative to deep-clean the storage room -- nobody asked her to, it wasn't part of anyone's role, and for the first months everyone was simply grateful. But after a year or so, something shifted. Newer volunteers assumed the clean storage room was just how things were. When she went on a long trip and the room reverted to its old state, people were genuinely annoyed -- some even muttered that standards had slipped, as though she had stopped doing something she owed us. Meanwhile, volunteers who have never once stayed late receive no criticism whatsoever. She is, in effect, being blamed for ceasing to do more than her fair share, while those who never did more than their fair share are blameless. I've seen the same shape elsewhere: an office colleague who once covered holiday shifts out of kindness is now resented for refusing to, and a friend who lent money freely in her twenties is treated as stingy now that she has stopped.

I know that moral philosophers discuss acts that are good to do but not required -- heroism, great generosity, and so on -- and I'm aware there's debate about whether such a category is even coherent, since once an act becomes expected it starts to look obligatory. But my case seems to be a specific mechanism rather than the general category: the free extra, repeated reliably, becomes the new baseline, and the person who supplied the extra ends up under a heavier de facto obligation than people who never supplied anything at all. It's not quite the puzzle of whether beyond-duty acts exist, because the act clearly wasn't required at the start; it's a claim about what reliably performing such acts does to their status over time. Nor does it seem to be simple ingratitude or exploitation, because the blamers often sincerely believe a duty is being shirked.

Does this phenomenon have an established name in the ethics literature, or is it discussed only in passing under some broader heading? If there is a literature, I'd like to know how it analyzes the transition -- does the optional act literally become obligatory, or do people merely come to treat it as obligatory, and is the volunteer's resentment-proofing her own fault for creating expectations? If there is no established name, I'd welcome a coined one, but with a careful definition: I want it to cover the temporal ratchet from praiseworthy to expected, and to be distinguished both from ordinary cases of duties changing as roles change and from the mere fact that generosity can be taken advantage of. A bare label without the conceptual carving wouldn't help me much.
