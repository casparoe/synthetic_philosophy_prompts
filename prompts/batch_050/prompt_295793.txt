In my undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of fiction we have spent several weeks on the puzzle of imaginative resistance: readers who will happily imagine, in fiction, that animals speak, that time runs backwards, or that the laws of physics differ, nonetheless balk at imagining that cruelty for its own sake is morally good, even when a story flatly asserts that it is. I am preparing a lecture that surveys the standard explanations of this asymmetry, and I want to be sure the survey is genuinely exhaustive before I admit to my students that I find none of the familiar accounts wholly satisfying.

The explanations I currently intend to cover are these. First, inability accounts: we cannot imagine the deviant moral claim, either because an author lacks the authority to make moral propositions true in a fiction simply by asserting them, or because the claim is conceptually incoherent once our moral concepts are held fixed. Second, unwillingness accounts of the sort associated with Gendler: we could imagine the deviant claim but refuse to, because doing so would require taking up an evaluative perspective we do not wish to inhabit, and because we suspect an invitation to export the deviant morality to the actual world. Third, Weatherson-style distinctions between alethic and moral resistance, on which genuine resistance arises only for higher-order or thick evaluative claims rather than for all fictionally deviant content, so that the phenomenon is narrower than the puzzle suggests. Fourth, genre- and convention-based explanations, on which resistance tracks our expectations about what kind of story we are reading and what the author is licensed to stipulate, rather than anything deep about morality or the imagination. I take these four to cover the familiar ground fairly well. My question: can you propose a fifth explanation — a genuinely different diagnosis of the phenomenon, and not a rewording, special case, or mere hybrid of the four above? Please state the proposal clearly, show how it explains the asymmetry between freely imagined impossibilities and resisted moral deviations, and indicate where it generates predictions or handles cases that the listed accounts do not.

Finally, I would like the answer to be self-critical about its own proposal. Identify the strongest objection to it, whether philosophical or empirical, and say explicitly what consideration would lead you to a different verdict: what evidence or argument would persuade you that the proposal collapses into one of the existing accounts, that it explains only a marginal subclass of cases, or that it fails outright? If, on reflection, you judge that no genuinely new fifth explanation survives scrutiny, please argue for that conclusion instead, with the same candour about what would change your mind.
