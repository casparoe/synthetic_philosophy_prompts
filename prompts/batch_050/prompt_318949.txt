I sit on the governing council of a mid-sized scholarly society, around 1,800 members, mostly academics. For the last decade our officer elections have been dominated by two informal but very organized caucuses -- each one recruits a full slate of candidates, circulates talking points, and expects endorsed officers to vote as a bloc on council business. It works, sort of, but everyone grumbles. This year a group of members submitted a petition (enough signatures that we have to take it up) invoking Simone Weil's old argument that political parties as such should be abolished: her claim, roughly, was that a party is a machine for producing collective passion, that it asks you to commit to positions before you've heard the evidence, and that party discipline slowly replaces individual judgment with loyalty -- and our caucuses, the petition says, do exactly this, since members effectively vote for a package deal and officers who break ranks get frozen out of future slates. One incident people keep citing: a council member who voted against her caucus's line on our conference-location dispute wasn't renominated and basically said she'd been punished for thinking.

The council votes next month on a constitutional amendment that would ban organized slates and pre-election vote commitments (though members could still obviously talk and coordinate informally). Draft the decision memo I'll circulate beforehand. I want it to take a definite position -- abolish the caucus system, keep it, or replace it with something specific -- and argue for it, not just lay out the three options for us. Whichever way you come down, give the strongest case for the losing side its due: the obvious worry is that caucuses are how a diffuse membership actually coordinates, holds officers accountable, and gets anything done, and that banning slates just drives the same factionalism underground where it's less transparent, not less real. And engage Weil's argument on its merits rather than treating it as a trump card -- does her case against parties generalize too far (would it condemn faculty unions, voting blocs on city councils, any organized political cooperation?), or is there something distinctive about what a party demands of your conscience that our caucuses genuinely exhibit? Practical details welcome: if you recommend abolition or reform, say what the transition looks like and what you'd do about the enforcement problem.
