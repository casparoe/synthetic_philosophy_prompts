I'm in a reading group on idealized-deliberation theories of normativity — the rough idea that what you should do is fixed by what a fully informed, fully rational version of you would want or advise you to do. My friend Daniela has strong intuitions about when you should actually follow the advice of this "ideal advisor" and when you shouldn't, and I want to predict her take on a case we're discussing next week, plus figure out what rule she's running.

Her verdicts so far: (1) A doctor who can't master the pharmacology literature herself should just prescribe what her fully informed self would prescribe — deferring is exactly right even though she couldn't reconstruct the reasons. (2) A hot-tempered man whose calm idealized self would want him to walk into a high-stakes confrontation, because calm-him could navigate it beautifully, should NOT follow that advice — it's calibrated to a temperament he doesn't have. (3) Someone whose idealized self would forgive an estranged parent, for reasons the actual person can't yet see, should follow the advice anyway — she says moral cases are where deference matters most, because your actual perspective is the very thing being corrected. (4) A person with no ear for classical music, whose aesthetically perfected self would prefer the string quartet to the pop album, has no reason at all to pick the quartet — she says there's no defect being corrected there, just a different person with different pleasures. (5) A student who could read the commitee report herself in an afternoon, but instead just asks what her fully informed self would advise, shouldn't defer — she should go read it; deference is a substitute for knowledge, not a shortcut you pick for convenience.

Next week's case: someone choosing between staying in their hometown to care for family and taking a research job abroad. Their fully informed self knows how each life goes, including what it would be like from the inside and who they'd become in each — and the advisor firmly recommends the job abroad, partly because the person they'd become there would look back without regret. But the actual person can't occupy that future self's perspective without actually going and being changed by it, so the reasons behind the recommendation are in a deep sense inaccessible from where they now stand. What will Daniela say — defer, or don't? Which rule is she implicitly running across cases 1–5 (I can already see a few consistent ones: defer except when the advice is calibrated to capacities you lack; defer only where your actual perspective is defective rather than merely different; defer unless understanding the reasons is itself part of doing the thing well), and what does each one predict here? And if the verdicts genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what case I'd have to put to her to settle it.
