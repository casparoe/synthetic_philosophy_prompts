So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and over the holidays I fell down a weird rabbit hole of medieval Islamic philosophy after a podcast mentioned Ibn Rushd (Averroes). I've been slowly picking my way through his Decisive Treatise (Kitab fasl al-maqal) in the Hourani translation, and early on he makes this move that I can't stop thinking about:

"If the activity of 'philosophy' is nothing more than study of existing beings and reflection on them as indications of the Artisan, i.e. inasmuch as they are products of art (for beings only indicate the Artisan through our knowledge of the art in them, and the more perfect this knowledge is, the more perfect the knowledge of the Artisan becomes), and if the Law has encouraged and urged reflection on beings, then it is clear that what this name signifies is either obligatory or recommended by the Law."

So basically he defines philosophy in a way that makes scripture itself command it, and then later in the treatise (if I'm reading it right) he handles apparent clashes between philosophy and scripture by saying the scripture must be interpreted figuratively -- but only qualified philosophers are allowed to do that interpreting. Here's what I want: compare Ibn Rushd's strategy for harmonizing religion and philosophy with Stephen Jay Gould's "non-overlapping magisteria" idea, where science and religion supposedly can't conflict because they govern totally separate domains (facts vs. values, roughly). Both are obviously "peace treaties" between reason and faith, but they seem to work in opposite directions: Ibn Rushd harmonizes by merging the subject matter (both study the same beings) and then adding an interpretive hierarchy, while Gould harmonizes by building a wall and insisting neither side ever trespasses. From my day job this feels like the difference between making two systems compatible by tightly integrating them vs. by defining a strict API boundary between them, and in my experience tight integration always breaks in more interesting ways.

Concretely, I'd like you to compare them on at least these points: (1) Which strategy is more fragile when an actual hard conflict shows up, like a scriptural claim about the natural world that contradicts the best available demonstration? Walk me through what each framework does with that case. (2) Does Ibn Rushd's definition in the passage above do too much work -- like, he defines philosophy so broadly that religion has to endorse it, but then the "philosophy" he defends later (Aristotelian demonstration) is way narrower, so is there a bait-and-switch going on, or am I being uncharitable? (3) Does his interpretive rule secretly give philosophers the final authority over what scripture means, so the "harmony" is really philosophy winning, whereas Gould at least tries to keep both sides sovereign? And (4) is the wall version even livable -- can a religious tradition actually promise to never make factual claims? If it helps, feel free to tell me where my API analogy is actually misleading rather than helpful, I suspect it breaks down somewhere around the interpretation-hierarchy part.
