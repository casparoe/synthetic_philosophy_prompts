A friend and I got into a long argument after watching a recent wave of films about famous dead people, and I've been trying to pin down whether the thing that bothered me is a recognized phenomenon with a name. Here's the pattern I keep noticing. A film depicts a real historical figure, and every individual scene it shows is documented: the harsh letter really was written, the cruel remark at dinner really was made, the affair really happened. Nothing is invented. And yet the overall portrait feels like a smear, because the film selects only the subject's worst documented moments and omits everything that would contextualize them -- the reconciliations, the generosity, the provocations they were responding to. If you fact-check scene by scene, the filmmakers are untouchable; if you ask what a viewer walks away believing about the person, they've been misled as thoroughly as if the events had been fabricated. My friend says this is just dramatic license, the same thing as compressing timelines or inventing composite characters. I think it's almost the opposite: the usual dramatic license announces itself as invention, while this phenomenon borrows the authority of accuracy precisely in order to mislead.

I have some vague sense that adjacent concepts exist -- defamation, except it usually requires false statements of fact and the dead mostly can't be defamed in the legal sense anyway; lying by omission, except that suggests withholding one particular fact rather than building a whole character out of curated truths; and there's the general philosophical distinction between lying and merely misleading, which captures the mechanics but not what's distinctive here, namely that the target is a real person's posthumous reputation and the instrument is a mass-audience dramatization claiming documentary fidelity. Is there an established term for this specific thing -- reputational harm to the dead through selection among exclusively true facts in a work that trades on its accuracy -- and if so, where has it been discussed? If there isn't, I'd like you to coin and define one, but please don't just hand me a label: I want the concept carved properly. Where are its boundaries? Does it require an intent to mislead, or is negligent selection enough? Does it differ if the omissions are boring rather than exculpatory? Does the subject's being long dead, or recently dead, or a public versus private figure, change the category or only the moral severity? And how does it relate to, and differ from, each of: dramatic license, ordinary editorial bias in nonfiction biography, defamation, and truthful-but-misleading assertion?
