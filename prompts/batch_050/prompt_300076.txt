You're an expert in bargaining theory writing for someone who knows the basics of the Nash bargaining solution but has never really internalized Kalai-Smorodinsky. Think carefully about the geometry before you write anything, and brainstorm two or three candidate setups before committing to one.

Construct a bargaining problem in which the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution deliver clearly different outcomes, and where the KS outcome is the one that looks obviously fairer to an ordinary person, while the Nash outcome looks exploitative of one party. (KS is usually presented as the odd one out because of its monotonicity demands, so I want the reverse.) Constraints:

- Realistic setting: two people dividing something concrete -- siblings splitting an inheritance, a freelancer and a client dividing revenue from a joint project, a divorcing couple splitting proceeds from selling the house. No abstract "players 1 and 2 bargain over a pie."
- The geometry must stay computable by hand: a linear or piecewise-linear Pareto frontier, small whole numbers throughout, and utilities that are plausibly just money or simple functions of it.
- The disagreement point has to be earned by the story: whatever each party can guarantee themself without agreement must come from the scenario (outside options, legal entitlements, whatever), not just be stipulated as "d = (8, 0)".
- Heads up: a symmetric setup with a symmetric disagreement point makes the two solutions coincide, so you'll have to break symmetry somewhere. Figuring out where, and justifying the asymmetry from within the story, is part of the exercise.

After the scenario: compute both solutions explicitly and show the arithmetic, then describe the geometry in words (ideal point, disagreement point, the KS ray, where the Nash product gets maximized). Say which axiom-level difference between the two solutions is doing the work in your case, and steelman the partisan of the Nash solution arguing that your "fair-looking" KS outcome is actually the unfair one. Finally be honest about the epistemics: say how confident you are that the case genuinely makes KS look better to a neutral observer rather than just baking that verdict in, and single out the one step in the construction or analysis your least sure about.
