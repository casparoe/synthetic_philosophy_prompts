I lead the design team at a small studio that makes abstract strategy games. Our pipeline depends on taking an existing game -- usually one of our own, sometimes a classic -- and producing a genuinely new game from it, and we do this often enough (roughly one derivation project per quarter, each given about six weeks) that I want to stop relying on whoever happens to have a good day. I've been reading about the view, associated with Douglas Hofstadter, that creativity is fundamentally a matter of taking a theme and varying it: treating the features of a thing as "knobs" that can be twiddled, where the hard part is less the twiddling than perceiving which knobs a concept has and which of its features are load-bearing rather than incidental. I'd like you to turn that view into a working procedure my team can run on a source game.

Concretely, I'm asking for a written protocol, usable by designers who have never read the philosophy, that covers at least the following:

1. How to produce the initial inventory of a source game's features, and how to tell which features belong on the knob list at all -- in particular, how to distinguish a parameter of the game (something with a range of values or alternatives) from a fixed property that only looks variable.
2. A method for sorting knobs into those whose twiddling yields superficial variants (reskins, rebalanced numbers) and those whose twiddling changes what the game is fundamentally about -- the model suggests this distinction is where creative depth lives, so the procedure needs a test for it, not just an exhortation to "go deep."
3. Guidance for the move the model treats as the real crux: realizing the inventory itself is incomplete, that is, discovering a knob nobody had noticed because it was invisible in the source (a feature so taken for granted that no one thought of it as a feature). What, if anything, can be proceduralized here -- systematic questions to ask, analogies to force, inversions to try -- and what resists proceduralization entirely?
4. Stopping and selection rules: how many variants to generate, how to evaluate them, and when to abandon a derivation project rather than keep twiddling.

The protocol must fit inside our constraints: a team of three, about six weeks per project, no outside consultants, and the final call on what ships rests with me. Wherever a step is really a judgment call wearing procedural clothing, mark it as such rather than pretending the method settles it -- I would rather know where the procedure will misfire than be told it won't.

Then demonstrate the protocol by running it, in compressed form, on two worked cases: first, deriving a new game from chess (a well-worn exercise, which is precisely why it's a useful test of whether the procedure produces anything beyond the usual variants), and second, deriving one from a cooperative deduction game in which players share incomplete information under a communication restriction. Include in at least one case a point where following the procedure honestly leads to a conclusion I will not like -- for instance, that a promising knob is actually load-bearing and untwiddlable, or that a project should be abandoned -- and show the reasoning rather than softening it. Close with a candid account of what the procedure cannot do: which parts of creative derivation it leaves untouched, and where it might actively mislead a team that follows it too faithfully.

Throughout, keep the engagement with the underlying theory substantive rather than decorative: I want to know what has to survive the translation from philosophical picture to studio protocol, and what you had to drop or distort. Say how confident you are in the main claims you make about the model and its operationalization, and single out the single point -- whether about the theory, the procedure, or one of the worked cases -- that you are least sure of. Please write the whole response in American English.
