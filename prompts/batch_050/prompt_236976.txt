Next month I am presenting a paper at a philosophy colloquium whose audience I know fairly well: mostly metaphysicians and normative ethicists, including at least two senior people on record as defending the Epicurean conclusion that death is not bad for the one who dies, plus a cluster of graduate students trained in population ethics who are fast with counterexamples and intolerant of hand-waving about counterfactuals.

My thesis, in outline: Lucretius's symmetry argument -- the observation that the long stretch of nonexistence before my birth seems no worse than the long stretch of nonexistence after my death, so the latter cannot be bad for me either -- fails because of a modal asymmetry between the two stretches. I argue that I could not have been born significantly earlier while remaining numerically the same person, because a person's origins are essential to their identity, whereas I could have died significantly later while remaining myself. The counterfactual comparison that deprivationism needs -- weighing my actual life against the longer life I would otherwise have had -- is therefore available for the postmortem period but not for the prenatal one, and the symmetry breaks. On that basis I defend standard deprivationism: death is bad for its victim insofar as it deprives them of goods their life would have contained. I also commit to a concurrentist answer to the timing problem: the badness of the deprivation is located at the time the loss begins, not before, and not at no time.

Prepare me for the question period. Specifically, I want:

1. The objections I should expect, phrased the way this audience will actually phrase them -- they will know the standard moves (mirror-image cases, the charge that origin-essentialism is doing no real work, the point that our asymmetric attitudes toward past and future might be mere bias rather than evidence, the timing problem in its other forms) -- ordered first by how likely each is to be raised and then by how much damage it does if it lands.
2. For each objection, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, plus a candid note on where that reply is weakest and what a hostile follow-up would target.
3. What I should concede up front, before anyone asks, so I do not look evasive.
4. The objection I should hope nobody raises -- the one I have no good answer to -- stated as sharply as my most informed opponent would state it.
5. The single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session intact.

Finally, tell me which objection you yourself judge most likely to be fatal if pressed hard, and be explicit about what would change your assessment: which argument, distinction, or counterexample -- if someone produced it in the room -- should make me retreat to a weaker thesis, and what that fallback thesis would be.
