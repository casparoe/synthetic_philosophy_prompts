After forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I have spent my retirement catching up on philosophy, and I now convene a reading group at our local lifelong-learning institute: twelve of us, mostly retired professionals, none with formal training in the subject. We have just finished a unit on rational choice, and I want to close it with a session on the money pump argument and diachronic rationality. Design a 90-minute activity that makes the participants feel the force of the argument rather than merely follow it on the page.

Two requirements shape the design. First, the standard pump targets synchronic intransitive preferences, but I want the session to stage the diachronic version as well: an agent whose preferences are perfectly consistent at every instant, yet who can still be exploited across time because the plans they endorse in the morning and the choices they make in the afternoon come apart. Both versions should be run as separate rounds, and the debrief should draw out how they differ and which is the deeper problem. Second, the pump must operate through the rules of the game itself, before anyone names it: no lecture up front, and the words "transitivity," "consistency," and "money pump" are not to be uttered until the debrief.

Practical constraints: no real money changes hands; use tokens redeemable for something small but genuinely wanted (I can supply chocolate and first pick of next term's readings). Each individual trade offered must look attractive by the participant's own lights, while the sequence is guaranteed to leave anyone who follows their revealed preferences strictly worse off than someone who sat on their hands. Specify the full rules, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline.

The debrief must do three things: map each round onto the premises of the argument (every step acceptable, the whole dominated); address the predictable response of participants who smell exploitation and adopt a blanket policy of refusing all further trades — does that defense concede the argument's point or dodge it, and what does the policy cost; and state candidly which feature of the philosophical argument the game cannot reproduce, given that in the classroom the pump is run by a deliberate adversary whereas the argument is meant to expose a defect in the preferences themselves.
