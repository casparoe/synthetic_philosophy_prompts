For a graduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, I want to fix the terms of the debate before any readings are assigned. Write the desiderata that a theory of moral responsibility for action must satisfy once Frankfurt-style cases are on the table -- that is, cases in which an agent deliberates and acts entirely on her own, meeting every ordinary condition on responsible agency, while a counterfactual intervener (or some other fail-safe mechanism) stood ready, but never needed, to guarantee that she choose and act exactly as she did. The deliverable is the specification itself: what such a theory has to be able to do, with the case or consideration that motivates each requirement.

The list should address at least the following, though I want you to reorganize, subdivide, or add to them as the logic of the problem demands. First, verdicts: which Frankfurt-style scenarios a theory must classify as cases of responsibility, and whether it is permissible for a theory to deny the standard verdict, or whether doing so simply changes the subject. Second, the dilemma concerning the prior sign: in many versions of the case, whatever would have triggered the intervention either causally guarantees the agent's choice or it does not, and a theory must say which horn it accepts and absorb the cost -- the first horn appears to assume what the incompatibilist denies, while the second leaves a residual alternative open. Third, robustness: if a theory responds to Frankfurt cases by insisting that some alternative possibility remains, it must explain what makes an alternative substantive enough to ground responsibility rather than a mere flicker, since the intuition driving the cases seems to be that the agent would be responsible even if every flicker were removed. Fourth, tracing: the theory must handle cases in which an agent lacks alternatives at the time of action precisely because of earlier culpable conduct, without letting all responsibility become merely derivative. Fifth, extension beyond the canonical setup: responsibility for omissions and for the consequences of action, where constructing the analogous intervener is less straightforward. Sixth, question-begging: the theory should not simply build in the principle of alternative possibilities or its denial as an unargued premise. Seventh, distinctions it must not collapse -- between moral and causal responsibility, between responsibility and blameworthiness, and between the claim that responsibility requires alternatives and the claim that freedom does.

Two further asks, which matter most to me. (a) For each desideratum, mark whether it is a genuine constraint any adequate theory must meet or a commitment of one camp in the literature -- and be alert to the possibility that some of my requirements above are camp-specific prejudices rather than neutral demands. (b) Identify any subsets of the desiderata that are jointly unsatisfiable; if, for instance, the requirement that the Frankfurt agent be responsible cannot be combined with the requirement that responsibility demands robust alternatives, then the specification itself yields a result and I want that made explicit. Conclude with only a brief note on how the main families of views -- actual-sequence or source accounts, strategies that defend alternatives via flickers, and semi-compatibilist positions that sever responsibility from free will -- stand against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I will do the full scoring in the seminar.
