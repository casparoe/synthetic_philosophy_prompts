I work as a software engineer, with no formal philosophy background, and I've ended up in a disagreement I can't resolve. It started at our team's retrospective: one of my colleagues, Priya, routinely takes weekend on-call pages nobody asked her to take, rewrites flaky tests outside her remit, and spends Fridays mentoring juniors. Everyone describes her contribution as "above and beyond". My flatmate Dan, overhearing me say this, insisted the category is incoherent and walked me through an argument that I found worryingly persuasive, because its conclusion seems to prove far too much. Here it is, as I reconstruct it. (1) An act counts as above and beyond the call of duty only if it is morally better than what duty requires, yet omitting it is not wrong. (2) If doing A is morally better than doing B, then A is what you have the strongest moral reason to do; reasons simply track betterness. (3) If you have the strongest reason to do A and do B instead, then you have acted worse than you could have, and knowingly acting worse than you could is always open to legitimate moral criticism. (4) So omitting a supposedly supererogatory act is legitimately open to moral criticism. (5) But an omission that is legitimately criticisable just is a moral failing, and a moral failing is nothing other than a breach of duty -- so the act was a duty after all, contradicting (1). (6) Therefore either morality demands that each of us always do the best available action (nothing is genuinely optional, and Priya is merely doing her job), or else our habit of praising people for going beyond duty is confused sentimentality.

Dan leans towards the first horn: he says that on reflection there is no "extra credit" in morality, only people who do their duty and people who fall short, and that our praise for the former is just surprise at how rare duty-fulfilment is. I strongly suspect something slips between (4) and (5) -- that being open to criticism of some sort is not the same as having failed a duty -- but I can't articulate the difference without making "criticisable" so weak that (3) stops looking plausible, which would leave me unsure why I can't similarly defuse (3) directly. I'd also like to preserve the category, since Priya plainly deserves special praise that merely adequate colleagues don't; but I don't want to rescue it by fiat. Please tell me which step you would reject and why it looked fine in the first place. In particular, I'd like you to price the alternatives: if you reject (2), does that sever the link between value and reasons in a way that makes morality unmoored? If you reject (3) or the move from criticism to blame, what account of "good but optional" do you need, and does it risk collapsing back into a duty by another name? And if you instead accept (6), how demanding does morality become -- are we all, on that view, breaching duty constantly -- and is that a bullet worth biting?
