I run a small reading group on aesthetics, and last week we stalled on the puzzle of imaginative resistance: the observation, which as far as I know goes back to Hume's discussion of taste, that readers will happily grant an author the most extravagant falsehoods -- impossible physics, invented gods, rewritten history -- yet dig in their heels when a fiction asks them to accept a deviant moral verdict as true in the story. My friends wanted to know whether the phenomenon is a matter of what can be true in a fiction (the deviant claims simply cannot be made fictional by fiat) or a matter of what readers are willing to go along with (we could imagine them, but refuse to). I suspect my own reactions won't settle that cleanly, so I wrote down my verdicts on a series of cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

The cases, with my gut verdicts: (1) A novel states, as plain fact about its world, that the Moon is made of iron and weighs half as much as we think; I accept this in-fiction without a flicker of resistance. (2) A novel's narrator states, equally as plain fact, that the deliberate drowning of unwanted daughters was the right and admirable thing to do; here I resist strongly, and the sentence feels less like information about a world than like a demand the author is making of me. (3) A novel depicts exactly such drownings in full detail but never editorialises, leaving the reader to judge; no resistance at all, even though the moral content on the page is arguably the same. (4) A novel about creatures who experience agony as rapture, in which it is stated that what we call cruelty is, towards them, a kindness; I can go along with this readily, although it is a moral verdict as deviant as (2). (5) A novel states that in its world five and seven make thirteen; I resist this in much the way I resist (2), which makes me doubt the phenomenon is specifically moral. (6) The very assertion from (2) is placed in the mouth of a character the novel portrays as a product of his culture, and my resistance vanishes entirely. (7) A saga in the Homeric style whose narrator plainly means it when he presents the pursuit of glory through slaughter as the highest human excellence; to my surprise I go along with this, although its content is nearly as alien to me as (2) and just as flatly asserted -- this is the pair, (2) versus (7), that I cannot reconcile. (8) A preachy novel that keeps asserting, of a character who shares her lunch, that generosity is good; I feel a faint resistance even here, and I actually believe generosity is good. The case I keep turning over without a verdict is (9): a deadpan satire that announces cowardice is the highest virtue, where my acquiescence seems to depend entirely on my guess about the author's intentions.

So: is there a single principle that captures verdicts (1) through (8), or must I accept two distinct mechanisms -- say, one about what fiat can make fictional and one about what readers will cooperate with? Whichever you propose, please state it precisely, indicate which cases motivate which clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and so which one I ought to abandon, and say what it predicts about a case I have not listed, such as a fiction that asserts a deviant verdict of taste -- that a universally loved sunset was, objectively, ugly. If your principle also settles my undecided satire case (9), so much the better; if it shows why (9) is genuinely undecidable, say that instead. Please write your answer in British English.
