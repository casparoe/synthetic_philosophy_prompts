I've been chewing on Sidgwick's discussion in The Methods of Ethics of what later got called the paradox of hedonism — roughly, the claim that pleasure often comes best when you're aiming at something else, so making your own happiness the direct target can be self-defeating. I don't want a literature survey; I want a defended research agenda. Suppose a small interdisciplinary group (three philosophers, one psychologist, one decision theorist) has two years to work on this. Which open questions should they prioritise, and in what order?

To keep it concrete, here are the sub-questions I keep circling, but feel free to restructure them or add your own: (a) is the paradox really about motivation (desire for pleasure crowds out the absorption that produces pleasure), about attention (monitoring your own enjoyment disrupts flow), about identity (being the kind of person who aims at pleasure repels the goods that give it), or are these three different problems that keep getting conflated?; (b) does the paradox generalise to other self-referential goods — status, authenticity, being seen as rational — and if so what's the shared mechanism?; (c) what does it imply for rationalist-flavoured life optimisation: if happiness is best pursued obliquely, what does a sensible agent actually do — pick goals for their expected hedonic side-effects while sincerely committing to them, even though the sincerity seems to require not treating them as means? Is that a stable motivational arrangement or does it collapse into self-deception?; (d) are there cases where the paradox fails — where deliberate, explicit pleasure-seeking works fine — and do the exceptions carve out a principled boundary?

For each question you prioritise, say why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers versus empirical people, and what would change your ranking. Also please seperate what's genuinely settled (e.g. where the empirical evidence on flow and affective forecasting is solid) from what's still contested among people who've actually thought about this, and don't present a contested reading of Sidgwick as the consensus view. Maximum 700 words.
