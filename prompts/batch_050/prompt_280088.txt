I'm a high school senior taking an elective seminar called "Philosophy of Biology," and next week we have a structured debate about whether natural selection acts on genes, on organisms, or on groups. Our teacher, Mr. Okafor, will moderate, and I want to anticipate how he'll approach the new cases he's promised to bring. He has never lectured us on any theory of this directly. Instead, all semester, whenever a case came up, he gave a verdict about "what's really being selected here." Here is his track record as I recorded it in my notes:

1. A mutant gene in fruit flies that cheats during sperm production, so it ends up in about 90% of a male's sperm instead of the usual half. His verdict: the gene itself is what is being selected.
2. Ant colonies in which the workers are sterile and spend their whole lives defending a nest they can never personally reproduce from. His verdict: the colony is being selected, not the individual workers.
3. A bacterium that survives an antibiotic because it carries an enzyme that chemically destroys the drug. His verdict: the individual bacterium is the unit being selected.
4. The same kind of resistance carried on a small ring of DNA that bacteria pass directly to their neighbors, so that it spreads sideways through a hospital ward even when the bacteria carrying it are not reproducing especially fast. His verdict: here it is the gene that is being selected, not the bacteria carrying it.
5. Cancer: a cell lineage inside a person's body that multiplies out of control and evolves resistance to chemotherapy. His verdict: the cells are the units of selection, and the patient's body is effectively their environment.

These are the two scenarios I expect him to use in the debate:

Scenario A: A seabird species nests in colonies on cliffs. In every colony, some individuals serve as sentinels, watching for predators while the others feed; sentinels feed less and leave fewer offspring than the non-sentinels in their own colony. However, colonies with more sentinels lose far fewer chicks to predators, and those colonies grow faster and split off new colonies more often.

Scenario B: A contagious cancer that passes directly from one wild animal to another when they bite each other, so that a tumor's "offspring" are tumors growing in other bodies. It has been spreading through the population for decades and keeps evolving.

You are a professional philosopher of biology with long experience teaching the levels-of-selection debate to beginners. Please think carefully about this, and brainstorm candidate interpretations before settling on your answers. I'd like your response to cover, in order:

(a) Identify at least two, and ideally three, distinct and internally coherent rules a person could be following that would produce all five of Mr. Okafor's verdicts. State each rule in a couple of sentences of plain language, without relying on jargon I haven't met in class.
(b) Under each rule, predict his verdict on Scenario A and on Scenario B, including at which level or levels he would say selection is acting. Then give your single best guess for what he would actually say in each scenario, with your reasoning, and name which of his five earlier cases is the closest analog for each scenario.
(c) Tell me honestly whether his five verdicts are genuinely consistent with one another, or whether he has quietly switched principles at some point during the semester. If they are consistent, say what makes them cohere; if not, say where the seam is.
(d) Propose one new biological case, different from everything above, whose correct classification would best distinguish the competing rules from one another, and state what verdict each rule would give for it.

Please be precise and concrete throughout, and where the evidence in his track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so plainly rather than forcing a prediction.
