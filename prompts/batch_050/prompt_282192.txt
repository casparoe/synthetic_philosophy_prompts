Two colleagues of mine -- both in a reading group on formal epistemology that I sit in on -- got into an extended argument after last week's session over whether Solomonoff induction actually succeeds in justifying Occam's razor, or merely restates it in fancier notation. I stayed neutral during the discussion and genuinely can't tell who came out ahead, so I'd like an outside verdict. Here is the exchange as I remember it. (1) Ana argued that Solomonoff induction gives Occam's razor its first real justification: if you take the prior probability of a hypothesis to be determined by the length of the shortest program that generates its predictions, then simpler hypotheses automatically receive exponentially greater prior weight, and the preference for simplicity is no longer an aesthetic add-on but a consequence of requiring that predictions be computable from the data. (2) Bruno replied that the construction smuggles the arbitrariness back in through the choice of universal machine, since a hypothesis that has a short description on one machine may require a long one on another, so the razor ends up ranking hypotheses relative to an arbitrary encoding rather than objectively, and the justification is therefore circular -- simplicity means whatever your machine says it means. (3) Ana responded that this is exactly what the invariance theorem is for: any two universal machines can simulate each other via a fixed-length interpreter, so the complexity of any hypothesis differs across machines by at most a constant, the priors differ by at most a corresponding constant factor, and with accumulating data the posteriors converge -- machine-dependence is a nuisance that washes out asymptotically. (4) Bruno countered that the constant can be made arbitrarily large by a hostile choice of machine -- one could build a machine on which a favored hypothesis has a trivially short program -- so two reasoners using different machines can be driven arbitrarily far apart on any finite amount of data, and since every actual act of induction takes place on finite data, an asymptotic convergence guarantee rescues a situation no one is ever in, leaving the practical use of the razor exactly as unjustified as before. (5) Ana's final move was that the objection proves too much: every method of induction makes some background commitment, ordinary subjective Bayesianism lets you pick any prior whatsoever with no accountability at all, and Solomonoff at least localizes the entire subjective element into one explicit, bounded, well-understood choice, so Bruno is holding the formal theory to a standard of assumption-freeness that no rival account of induction meets either.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and then give an overall verdict -- who is right about which specific point, whether Ana's final tu quoque actually answers Bruno's finite-data objection or merely changes the subject, and what, if anything, both parties are missing.
