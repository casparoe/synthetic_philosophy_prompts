I am a philosopher preparing to present a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on consciousness and moral status. The paper defends the following argument. Suppose constitutive panpsychism is true: the fundamental physical entities described by physics have proto-experiential properties, and the experiences of complex systems like brains are constituted by these micro-experiences rather than emerging from nothing. Now add two further premises. First, valence is not restricted to complex systems: micro-experiences possess at least some minimal positive or negative hedonic character, and there is no principled line at which valence switches on. Second, impartial welfarist aggregation counts every subject's experience, however simple. The conclusion I defend is what I call the dominance thesis: because the number of fundamental entities bearing micro-experiences vastly exceeds the number of macroscopic subjects, the overwhelming majority of morally relevant experience in the universe resides in dense, high-energy matter -- stellar interiors, planetary cores, the early universe -- and consequently the moral priorities generated by conventional ethics (animal welfare, human suffering, existential risk) are, on these premises, a rounding error. I treat this not as a reductio but as a live implication that ethicists must either accept or locate precisely where the argument fails.

My audience is a workshop of about twenty people: roughly half are moral philosophers specializing in population ethics and animal ethics (people comfortable with totalism, averagism, the repugnant conclusion, and speciesism debates, but with no particular sympathy for panpsychism); most of the rest are philosophers of mind who have worked on the combination problem and the boundary problem for composite subjects; and there are two physicists who work on statistical mechanics and have been invited to keep the metaphysics honest. The room is therefore not hostile to speculative metaphysics, but it is hostile to arguments that smuggle in controversial premises under technical vocabulary.

Draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I want the objections this specific audience will actually raise -- not generic objections to panpsychism from a textbook -- ordered by how likely each is to be asked and how much damage it would do to the dominance thesis if left unanswered. Phrase each objection the way that audience member would phrase it (a population ethicist's challenge will sound different from a physicist's), and for each one give me: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; an explicit statement of what I should concede rather than fight; a note on where my reply is weakest; and whether conceding that objection costs me the dominance thesis itself or only forces a reformulation of it. Include at least one objection turning on the question of whether micro-experiences can bear valence at all without a subject capable of caring, at least one turning on the boundary problem for macroscopic subjects, and at least one a physicist would raise about whether talk of hedonic character in thermal systems is even physically coherent. Finally, identify the single question I am most hoping nobody asks, explain why it is dangerous, and tell me honestly whether the paper can survive it.
