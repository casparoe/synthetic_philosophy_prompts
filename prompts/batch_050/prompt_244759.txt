The wrong kind of reasons problem for fitting-attitude accounts of value has been going for roughly twenty years now -- the demon who'll punish everyone unless you admire him, the saucer of mud that's worthless but that someone will richly reward you for wanting, all the usual cases -- and from the outside the literature just seems to keep subdividing: one camp splits reasons into object-given and state-given, another relativizes the fittingness relation, another bites the bullet and says the attitude is fitting in these cases but that value was never fully analyzable anyway, and every move gets answered by a slightly tweaked demon. I have a hypothesis I want tested: the debate is stalled because both sides treat 'there's a reason to have this attitude' as a single relation, when two different questions are actually being asked at once -- what is the object like, and what would having the attitude accomplish -- and no refinement of one unified relation will ever pull those apart. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a genuine first-order disagreement underneath all the taxonomy that one side could in principle win? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is (verbal, substantive, two debates under one heading), and what would even count as settling it -- a new distinction, a decision procedure for which intuitions count as data, or an admission that 'reason' is just ambiguous here. Engage with what the main positions actually say, not just their slogans.
