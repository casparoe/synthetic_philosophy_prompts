So my friend and I just spent basically all of last night arguing about teleosemantics — the idea that what a mental state is about gets fixed by what the underlying mechanism was selected (by evolution) to do. The frog's snap-detector means fly because snapping-at-flies is what it was selected for, etc. She thinks this is basically the only serious way to naturalize content; I think it collapses once you poke at it. Neither of us won and it's still bugging me.

Here's where we need a tiebreaker. During the argument we cooked up an objection I haven't seen anywhere (maybe it exists in the literature, whatever), and I want to know how it stacks up against the classics. Our objection, roughly:

The content-drift problem. Selection pressures change gradually. Say some detection mechanism originally got selected for detecting A (a particular predator's silhouette), but over a long stretch the environment shifts, and the very same mechanism — physically unchanged — starts doing real work detecting B (a new predator with a similar silhouette), and eventually B-detection is the only thing keeping it around. So on teleosemantics the content of the same internal state drifts from "A" to "B". But when? Any cutoff in the selection history looks arbitrary, since selection is a gradient, not a switch. Either content flips at some arbitrary threshold (weird), or it changes gradually (but "it's sort of about A and sort of about B" seems like a bad thing to say about meaning), or you bite some bullet we haven't thought of. My friend says this is just the old indeterminacy problem dressed up; I think it's new and worse.

The classics I want it compared against (tell me if I've mangled any of these):

1. Indeterminacy: the frog's mechanism was selected "for" catching flies, but equally for responding to small dark moving dots, or for obtaining protein — nothing in selection history privileges one description, so content is indeterminate.
2. Swampman: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person assembled by sheer chance would, on this view, have no contentful states at all (no selection history), which seems nuts.
3. Novel environments: the view's whole selling point is explaining error/misrepresentation, but critics argue it either can't handle representations deployed in radically new environments or else makes everything the creature does come out correct by definition.
