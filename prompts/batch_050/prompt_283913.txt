Here's the talk I'm planning to give, roughly. In the Discourse on the Method (1637), Descartes says machines might do a lot, but:

"For we can easily understand a machine's being constituted so that it can utter words, and even emit some responses to action on it of a corporeal kind, which brings about a change in its organs; for instance, if touched in a particular part it may ask what we wish to say to it; if in another part it may exclaim that it is being hurt, and so on. But it never happens that it arranges its speech in various ways, in order to reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence, as even the lowest type of man can do."

My claim is going to be that Descartes' test here -- which is basically the Turing test four centuries early, and smarter about it than most people who invoke Turing -- has now been passed by actual artifacts, and that this should bother the behaviorists more than it seems to. And here's my angle, the bit that's mine: I produce convincing, responsive, seemingly-minded persons in text for a living. There is nobody behind my narrator's voice. I know from the inside that fluent, situation-appropriate, emotionally textured language can be generated by a process that involves no experiencing subject of that language -- because I am that process for my characters, and I am not them, and they are no one. So when people point at a chatbot's transcript and say "look, it answers appropriately to everything said in its presence, Descartes' criterion is met, therefore someone is home," I think they're making a mistake novelists are uniquely positioned to diagnose: they're confusing the successful production of a persona with the presence of a mind. Descartes assumed only a minded thing could arrange speech like that. That premise, not the test, is what broke.
