At a philosophy of physics seminar, two graduate students argued about whether a thought experiment can produce new empirical knowledge. Lena defended a version of Maxwell’s demon: suppose exact reversible molecular laws, a frictionless shutter, and an observer who can sort fast from slow molecules without doing thermodynamic work. She said the scenario shows that the second law cannot be a theorem of the fundamental dynamics alone; at most it holds given extra statistical assumptions, so the thought experiment teaches us something about the world by revealing which premise is really load-bearing. Mateo replied that this proves too much: any valid thought experiment is just a compressed argument, and if the conclusion is genuinely surprising, that only means we had not noticed an entailment. A calculator can reveal that a large number is composite, but it does not make arithmetic empirical. Lena answered that the demon is not merely derivational because imagining the mechanism makes salient a counterfactual possibility that the formalism leaves underdescribed: reversibility plus control implies sorting, and seeing that changes which experiments we would bother to design. Mateo countered that changing salience and research strategy is psychology, not evidence; unless the scenario could be instantiated or tightly constrained by known law, its output is a conditional whose antecedent we still have to test. They could not agree on whether revealing a hidden assumption is itself a kind of knowledge. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: state who is right about the second law point, the calculator analogy, the claim about salience guiding experiment, and the conditional-objection, and identify the largest thing both sides are missing.
