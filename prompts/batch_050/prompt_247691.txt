Consider the question of what exactly Descartes's cogito establishes — not what role it plays in the architecture of the Meditations, but the precise proposition (or propositions) a thinker is entitled to assert on its basis alone. I am familiar with the standard answers to this question, which I take to be the following, and I do not need any of them rehearsed:

1. The substantialist reading (Descartes's own): the cogito establishes the existence of an enduring thinking thing, a res cogitans whose whole essence is thought.
2. The Lichtenberg deflation: it establishes only that thinking is occurring; the inference from there to an "I" that does the thinking smuggles in a subject that the datum itself does not contain.
3. The minimal epistemic reading: it establishes merely that at least one thought exists, here and now, and licenses no conclusion whatever about minds, persons, or owners of thoughts.
4. The momentariness restriction: even granting that it establishes that I exist, it does so only for the instant at which the thought is entertained, and yields nothing about persistence from one moment to the next.
5. The performative reading (associated with Hintikka): the cogito is not an inference at all but an existentially self-verifying performance, since the thought "I do not exist" defeats itself in the thinking; accordingly, what it "proves" is better characterized as indubitability-upon-thinking than as a conclusion derived from premises.
6. The anti-skeptical reading: its genuine yield is not any claim about the self but simply that something exists, thereby refuting the hypothesis that nothing does and placing a floor under universal doubt.

I would like you to supply one further answer — that is, one proposition the cogito can plausibly be taken to establish — that does not appear on this list and is not a rewording, weakening, special case, or conjunction of items already on it. A new answer might concern, for instance, what the cogito shows about something other than existence (about a thinker's access to her own mental states, about the content or structure of thought itself, about the limits of doubt rather than its refutation), but you need not follow those suggestions. Then do three things: first, state your candidate in a single precise sentence; second, explain why it is genuinely distinct from each of the six readings above, paying particular attention to whichever one it most closely resembles; third, make the strongest case you can that the cogito really does establish it. Finally, give me your honest verdict on whether the candidate survives scrutiny, or whether it collapses into one of the listed readings under pressure.
