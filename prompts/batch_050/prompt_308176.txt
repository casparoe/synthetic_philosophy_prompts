So I'm a philosophy prof and next semester I'm teaching my third-year seminar on democratic theory, and I want to open the unit on the boundary problem — the question of who gets to count as the people in the first place — with chapter XVI of Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, the one on nationality. I was griping about this to a colleague over coffee last week: everytime I've taught it, the students do one of two things. Either they nod along with Mill's thought that free institutions need a people with some pre-political fellow-feeling to hold them together, as if he'd stated an obvious truth, or they write him off as a Victorian nationalist and move on. Both reactions skate straight past the thing I actually care about, which is the circularity: democratic procedures can't settle the boundaries of the demos, because any vote on who belongs already presupposes an answer to that question — you can't vote on who gets to vote without having already decided who's voting.

Don't explain the boundary problem to me; I know it fine. Plan how I should explain it in one 80-minute session built around that Mill chapter. I'm specifically interested in: the order the pieces have to come in, i.e. what has to be in place before the circularity is even visible to them (my hunch is they first need to see that "democracy = majority rule" only makes sense against a constituency that's already been fixed by non-democratic means, but maybe you disagree); the one carrying example to anchor the whole session — I'm leaning toward a single concrete secession-referendum case where the fight is precisely over the electorate rather than the outcome, rather than starting with immigration, but tell me what the example needs to have and what it needs to lack; the misconceptions they'll arrive with or form on the spot (I'm guessing: it's just gerrymandering, universal suffrage dissolves it, it only comes up when new states are born, and the all-affected-interests principle answers it by definition) and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off; and how I'll know at the end whether they can port the idea to a fresh case, like a city-region demanding its own referendum, rather than just parroting Mill back at me. Also what I should definately leave out at this level and say so out loud.

Give me the plan as a numbered list of exactly five items — one for the order of ideas, one for the carrying example, one for the misconception map, one for the check for understanding, one for what to cut — each a sentence or two, no introduction, no conclusion.
