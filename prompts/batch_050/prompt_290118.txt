**Exam question:** Drawing on Machamer, Darden, and Craver's account of mechanisms: (a) explain what characterizing mechanisms in terms of entities and activities adds to the simpler idea that a mechanism is a set of parts that interact; (b) explain how mechanistic explanation, so understood, differs from explaining a phenomenon by deriving it from a general law together with initial conditions; and (c) discuss whether their account can accommodate mechanisms that are stochastic or that produce their characteristic phenomenon only occasionally. Support your answers with an example from biology.

**Student's answer:**

On Machamer, Darden, and Craver's account, a mechanism consists of entities and activities organized so as to produce a phenomenon. The key addition beyond talk of "interacting parts" is that the parts alone do no explanatory work: it is the activities, the ways in which entities produce changes in one another, such as binding, pumping, or opening, that carry the productive continuity from the mechanism's set-up conditions to its termination conditions. Entities and activities are thus explanatorily on a par. One cannot state what the parts of a mechanism are doing merely by listing their structural properties, since the same collection of entities could be organized and active in different ways and thereby produce different phenomena.

This differs from law-based explanation because the explanation does not consist in showing that the phenomenon was to be expected given general laws plus initial conditions. Instead, one explains neurotransmitter release, for instance, by describing how synaptic vesicles dock at the presynaptic membrane, how calcium influx triggers their fusion with the membrane, and so on: a description of a causal process rather than a derivation. Generalizations may appear within such a description, but they function as summaries of what the organized entities do, not as the explanatory core.

As for irregular mechanisms, MDC's own characterization speaks of mechanisms as productive of regular changes from set-up to termination conditions, and I take this to commit them to the requirement that a mechanism produce its phenomenon in the same way on every occasion. A genuinely stochastic process, for instance a particular point mutation arising in one cell lineage, therefore cannot count as a mechanism on their view. This is a real cost of the account, since much of molecular biology deals in precisely such processes.

Because mechanistic explanation analyzes a phenomenon in terms of its lower-level components, Craver's work in particular treats it as a form of reduction: the behavior of the whole is explained exclusively by the organized activities of its parts.

Finally, since every activity is the activity of some entity and can only be described by reference to the entities engaged in it, the entity/activity distinction does not mark a genuine ontological difference; it is a useful terminological convention for organizing our descriptions of mechanisms.
