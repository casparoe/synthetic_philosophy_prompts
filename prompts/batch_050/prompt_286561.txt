So I've been obsessing over the "cluelessness" problem for consequentialism — roughly, every little thing I do has unpredictable knock-on effects rippling into the far future (the person whose life I save today might have a grandson who invents something terrible, or whatever), so "add up the expected consequences of your options" starts to look like a joke. I've been collecting the standard replies and I want to know if there's one I've missed. What I already have, please don't just repeat these:

1. Symmetry — the unpredictable future effects are as likely to be good as bad, so they cancel out in expectation and I should just compare the consequences I can foresee.
2. It's everyone's problem, not just consequentialism's — deontologists also can't predict what keeping their promises leads to down the line, so cluelessness can't be a reason to pick one moral theory over another.
3. Bite the bullet the other way — the far future actually dominates the math, so instead of ignoring it we should focus on the few things that robustly shape it, like preventing extinction.
4. Split the problem in two — when your uncertainty about the long-run effects is symmetric, fall back on foreseeable consequences; when you have even a slight reason to think one option does better long-run, go with your best guess.
5. Consequentialism is a theory of what makes acts right, not a step-by-step decision procedure — nobody said you have to consciously compute the outcome, so being unable to compute it doesn't refute the theory.

As far as I can tell that covers the usual menu. Is there a genuinely different reply — not a rewording, blend, or special case of those five? If so, explain it, tell me honestly whether it actually holds up, and say how it relates to the ones above. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, no preamble, and don't restate my question.
