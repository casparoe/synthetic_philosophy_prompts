PhD student in ergodic theory here, so foundations isn't really my home turf, sorry if this is basic. Over the years I've collected a bunch of existence results that all feel "fake" in the same way, and I want to know if there's an established name for what they share. Examples: Banach limits, nonprincipal ultrafilters on N (the kind you use in ultraproduct arguments), a well-ordering of the reals, the Vitali set. In each case the theorem says the object exists, everyone cheerfully invokes it, but nobody has ever written one down and, as I understand it, nobody could even in principle give a complete description of one.

What's bugging me is that this seems strictly stronger than ordinary nonconstructive proof. Take the classic proof that there are irrationals a, b with a^b rational — it doesn't exhibit witnesses either, but you walk away knowing the witnesses live inside a tiny explicit set of candidates. With my examples there are no candidates at all. It also seems different from mere non-uniqueness: an isomorphism between two algebraic closures of Qp is non-canonical and depends on choices, but that's a failure of canonicity, not of describability — unless actually those collapse into the same thing?

So: is there a standard term for this category of "existence with no possible witness," and a principled test for what belongs to it? Does a Hamel basis of R over Q? Does a maximal ideal in some big non-noetherian ring? If the literature has no name for it, what would you call it, and how would you draw the boundary — what would be a clear case just outside the phenomenon on each side?
