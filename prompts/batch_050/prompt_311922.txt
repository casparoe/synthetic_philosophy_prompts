So I'm a novelist (sci-fi mostly) and I'm stuck on a scene and need help getting the philosophy right. My protagonist is a decision theorist who gets visited by an Omega-style predictor — your classic counterfactual mugging setup. Omega tells her: I flipped a fair coin before coming here. If it had come up heads, I would have given you a million dollars, but only on the condition that you would have paid me $10,000 in the world where it came up tails. It came up tails. Hand over the $10,000.

Here's the twist I want the scene to turn on. Earlier in the book, she and a perfect duplicate of her (long story, teleporter accident) faced what I think is a structurally identical game: a coin flip decided which of the two copies gets the million, but only if the other copy pays the $10k. Crucially, at the moment they had to decide, neither copy knew which one would turn out to be "the tails one." In THAT case, both copies agreeing to pay was obviously right — each was basicly buying a 50/50 shot at a million for ten grand.

I need her to argue, out loud, partly to Omega and partly to herself, that the original one-person case is the same problem wearing a disguise, so she should pay. Specifically:

1. Make the case that the two-copy setup and the single-person counterfactual setup are decision-theoretically equivalent — that "you might be the other copy" and "you might have been in the other branch of the coin" should move her in the same way, and explain why the difference between self-locating uncertainty and counterfactual uncertainty shouldn't matter here.

2. Then handle the obvious objection: in the two-copy case the coin hasn't landed yet from her epistemic perspective, but in the Omega case it HAS landed, she knows she's in tails-world, so there's no uncertainty left to hide behind — paying just sets ten grand on fire. Give her best response to this. I assume it runs through something about what policy she should have adopted before any coins were flipped anywhere, and why agents who hold that policy end up richer on average than agents who always reason from the moment they find themselves in — but make it concrete, not hand-wavy.

3. Bonus beat for a later chapter: she once also received an XOR-blackmail letter and refused to pay. I want one paragraph where she explains why the very policy that makes her pay the mugger makes her stiff the blackmailer — that paying the blackmailer rewards the wrong kind of counterfactual. If you actually think consistency forces her to pay the blackmailer too, argue that instead, but pick a lane and stay in it.

No citations or name-dropping papers, please — she's a character, not a lit review. Plain speech, the way a genuinely smart person talks when cornered. Maybe 400-600 words, I can trim. Thanks!!
