Here it is:

THE CATASTROPHE THAT WOULDN'T BE

Frege built his life's work on an axiom letting you form the set of all things with a given property, and Russell showed him a contradiction drops straight out of it. Frege added a devastated appendix and by most accounts never recovered. That's the standard story about what inconsistency does to a formal system: it kills it. I want to argue the story is mostly myth, and that if someone posted a correct derivation of a contradiction from ZFC tomorrow, the actual consequences would be boring.

First point. Classical logic has explosion: from a contradiction, everything follows, so in an inconsistent system every sentence is a theorem. But that's a fact about the consequence relation, not about what mathematicians would do. Nobody would start publishing proofs that 1 = 2. They'd do triage: work out which results route through the contradiction and which don't. Practice is already effectively paraconsistent in this sense — when the early set-theoretic contradictions surfaced, nobody concluded that 2+2=5 had been a theorem all along; they quarantined the guilty axiom and kept the rest. The formalism explodes, the practice doesn't. "The system is inconsistent" and "everything is now provable" are not the same event.

Second point. Almost all ordinary mathematics uses a minuscule fragment of ZFC's strength. Number theory, analysis, the stuff of physics and engineering — it lives far below the reaches where an inconsistency would presumably be lurking. Bridges would not fall down, your bank statement would be fine. It'd be a problem for set theorists the way a bug in an obscure kernel module is a problem for the three people who maintain it.

Third point, the one my mate really hates. Suppose PA itself is inconsistent. Then there's a proof of 0=1 from the Peano axioms. But proofs have lengths, and nothing guarantees the shortest one is short enough for anyone to find, or even to write down within the lifetime of the universe. It's perfectly possible we live in an inconsistent arithmetic right now and no physically possible computer could ever exhibit the contradiction. In that world everything we believe about numbers would be, strictly, theoremhood-in-an-inconsistent-system — and it would make no detectable difference, ever. Kicker: if that scenario is genuinely possible, then consistency can't be doing the work in our justification for trusting arithmetic that philosophers say it does. We don't trust arithmetic because we know it's consistent — Gödel blocks us from proving that from inside anyway, and Gentzen's proof just shifts the bet onto transfinite induction. We trust it because it's never bitten us and the short proofs we actually run keep working. An undiscoverable contradiction changes neither of those.

Conclusion: inconsistency would be a maintenance problem, not a metaphysical crisis. Frege's appendix is the outlier, not the template. What maths runs on isn't consistency in the logician's sense but a long unbroken streak of small checkable successes — and a contradiction, even a real one, is just one more bug report in the tracker.

That's it. If the third point is rubbish, tell me plainly. Also tell me honestly whether the whole thing is interesting or whether I'm just being contrarian because I wanted to win.
