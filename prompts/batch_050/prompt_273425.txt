I'm a grad student TAing for my advisor's seminar on the ethics of influence, and I want to use her as a test case for a paper idea about how people actually draw the manipulation/persuasion line. All term I've been collecting her spontaneous verdicts on cases students bring up, and they're consistent enough that I think she has an implicit theory, but I can't figure out which one. Here's the data. One: a car salesman notices a middle-aged customer keeps glancing at the sports car and starts talking about what the car says about its driver, deliberately working the guy's insecurities -- she said manipulation, obviously. Two: a debate coach teaches students to open with their strongest, most vivid argument and to structure the rest around it -- she said that's just craft, totally legitimate. Three: a charity A/B tests its appeal letters and switches entirely to the version with a photo and name of one specific child because it crushes the version with statistics, even though everything in both letters is true -- she called that manipulation, and when a student objected that nothing was false she said truth isn't the issue. Four: a mother deliberately waits until her son is anxious about his friends' college plans to raise the subject of his own applications, because that's when he'll listen -- manipulation, though she stressed the mother meant well and the son really should apply.

Now here's the new case I want to spring on her, and I want to predict what she'll say before I do. A surgeon genuinely believes her patient needs an operation, and the patient is hesitating out of what she judges to be irrational fear. She tells him the five-year mortality figure rather than the equivalently true survival figure -- one in ten dead in five years without surgery, instead of nine in ten alive -- deliberately picking the loss frame because she's read that loss frames move hesistant patients, and she picks it for exactly that extra push. Everything she says is true, it's in the patient's own interest, it's arguably decision-relevant information, but the frame selection is covert and done for its psychological punch rather than its content, and it happens in a context (doctor-patient) where the patient isn't braced for persuasion the way the car buyer is.

What I want from you: (a) lay out the plausible rules that fit all four of her verdicts -- I can see at least a mechanism-based one (does it work through reasons or around them?) and a lever-type one (does the persuasive force track the evidential relevance of what's presented?), and probably others I'm missing; (b) say what each rule predicts about the surgeon case, since I suspect they diverge there even though they agree on all four old cases; (c) give your best guess at her actual verdict and which old case is the closest analog, with the reasoning; and (d) if her track record genuinely underdetermines it, say what principle she'd be committing to with each possible verdict, and describe the one further case that would settle which rule she actually holds. Don't just say "it depends" -- I need the divergence mapped precisely, because that's basically the whole paper.
