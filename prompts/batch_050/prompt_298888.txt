I co-run a small philosophy reading group (about 12 people, we meet in a seminar room for 75 minutes) and we're on torture vs. dust specks this month. Everyone's read the usual setup -- one person tortured horribly for decades versus a googolplex of people each getting a barely-perceptible dust speck in the eye -- and the discussion always goes the same dead way: half the room says "obviously the specks, no amount of tiny harms adds up to torture," the other half says "aggregation is just arithmetic," and nobody actually *feels* anything. It's all lip service to intuitions. I want to run an activity at the start of the session that forces the problem into their bodies before we talk about it.

Design me a game/simulation with real (small, harmless) stakes -- think things like waiting extra minutes before the group can leave, holding an ice cube, getting your preferred snack swapped for a bland one, losing points that convert into who has to stack the chairs -- where one participant or the facilitator repeatedly faces a choice between imposing one concentrated unpleasantness on a single person versus distributing a tiny unpleasantness across everyone, and where the "tiny" option can be chosen *again and again* so its cumulative total quietly overtakes the concentrated one. Some mechanism I'd like: nobody, including the person choosing, should be able to see the running total unless they choose to track it (that's part of the point -- I want someone to feel the floor give way when the tally is revealed). Give me concrete rules, materials, exact stakes and quantities, and how to handle consent/ opting out without letting one refusenik collapse the whole thing.

Then the debrief: map specific moments of the game onto the actual philosophical fault lines -- the "specks never sum" intuition, the separateness-of-persons objection, the spectrum/sorites worry that there's no sharp line between a speck and real suffering -- and tell me how to handle the predictable failure modes (e.g., people heroically volunteering to absorb harm, which ruins the data; or the room turning it into a joke so nobody engages). Finally, be honest with me about which feature of the original thought experiment the game structurally *cannot* reproduce -- the googolplex-scale numbers, the fact that real specks land on real distinct strangers, whatever you think it is -- and how I should say that out loud without deflating what just happend.
