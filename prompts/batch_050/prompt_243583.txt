Here is the student's answer, in full:

"Constitutive panpsychism, defended by Galen Strawson among others, says our consciousness is constituted by the consciousness of fundamental particles. The combination problem, a term coined by William James and developed by David Chalmers, asks how many small minds could add up to one big mind like ours. Chalmers distinguishes the subject-summing problem — how many microsubjects could yield one macrosubject — from the phenomenal bonding problem, which is essentially the same question restated in terms of phenomenal properties rather than subjects. Russellian monism helps at this point: physics describes only the structure of matter, so proto-phenomenal properties must fill in its intrinsic nature, and once the microsubjects exist with their proto-experiences, their combination into a unified experiential field is straightforward. Cosmopsychism avoids the combination problem entirely, because if the cosmos is the fundamental conscious subject and our minds are aspects or parts of it, there is no need to combine many minds into one — the problem never arises. Cosmopsychism therefore looks strictly better motivated than constitutive panpsychism."
