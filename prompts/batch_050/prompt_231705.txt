So I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, three books out, and I keep getting invited to do these "the writer on film" nights at a local arts cinema where I intro a movie and then do a short talk after. I picked About Time (the Richard Curtis one, 2013, Domhnall Gleeson learns from his dad that the men in the family can slip back into their own past). I picked it half for sentimental reasons, but on rewatch the middle section genuinely stopped me cold: Tim goes back to fix something for his sister, comes home, and his toddler daughter has been replaced by a different baby -- a boy -- because arriving back before a child's conception scrambles which sperm meets which egg. He panics, undoes the whole trip, and gets his daughter back. And later this rule is what forces the ending: once another kid is on the way, visiting his dead father in the past becomes impossible, so they get one last trip together. It hit me that this movie has basically dramatized Parfit's nonidentity problem -- the thing from Reasons and Persons about how the people who exist depend on our choices, so you can't have wronged a person by an act that's the very reason that person exists -- and I want the talk to be about that.

Here's my ask. Act as a philosopher who genuinely knows population ethics (Parfit, the nonidentity problem, the person-affecting intuition, the "no-difference view," all of it) and who also takes film seriously as a way of doing philosophy, not just illustrating it. Before writing the talk, brainstorm in private about where the mapping between the movie and the nonidentity problem is exact and where it's sloppy -- I want you to think this through carefully rather than reach for the obvious take. Then give me a reading I can build a twenty-minute talk from. Specifically: does the baby-swap scene actually stage the nonidentity problem, or does it stage something the problem says is confused -- namely, treating the replacement of one possible child by another as a loss or a death, when by Parfit's lights no one has been harmed, since the daughter who "disappears" couldn't have existed under the altered circumstances anyway? Notice the film is emotionally on the side of the confusion: it makes us feel Tim's daughter was almost killed, and the horror only makes sense if she's the kind of thing that could persist across possible worlds. Is the movie thereby refuting Parfit, exposing something his framework can't say about love being love of particulars, or just relying on a metaphysical fudge about personal identity (the kids' souls are apparently swap-proof while everyone else's timeline is play-doh)?

Also tell me where the film resists the philosophical reading and I should be honest about that in front of the audience -- I'm thinking of the ending, where the moral turns out to be "live each ordinary day as if you'd deliberately come back to savor it," which seems to abandon the metaphysics entirely for a kind of carpe diem sentimentality, and I can't decide if that's the film chickening out or actually landing on a position (roughly: gratitude toward the actual over regret about the merely possible) that the nonidentity problem itself points toward. As a novelist I keep noticing that fiction individuates characters by stipulation -- I decide who persists across drafts in a way the real world doesn't let anyone do -- and I'd love one paragraph on whether that authorial god's-eye view is exactly what the film is smuggling in and what Parfit denies we ever have. End the reading with whatever the movie adds to the debate that the journal articles don't, if you think it adds anything, and don't be afraid to tell me the film is just incoherent if that's your verdict -- I'd rather intro a flawed film honestly than oversell it. The crowd is about a hundred civilians plus a handful of philosophy grad students who show up to everything, so pitch it accordingly.
