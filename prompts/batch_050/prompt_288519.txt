I am a PhD student in moral philosophy writing a dissertation on the conditions under which right actions possess moral worth, and a case came up in our reading group last week that we argued about for an hour without resolution. I would like to see it argued out properly rather than summarised. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers, Ada and Yusuf, both working ethicists and both at the top of their game, debating a single case: Miriam, a palliative care nurse, stays two hours past the end of her shift to sit with a dying patient who has no family. She does not deliberate about her duties before deciding to stay; it simply never occurs to her to leave, because she is fond of the man and cannot bear the thought of his dying alone. Ada defends the view that an action has moral worth only insofar as it is done because it is right, for the reasons that make it right, so that Miriam's staying, however kind, lacks moral worth, or at least has less of it, insofar as it sprang from affection rather than from a recognition of what morality required. Yusuf defends the view that acting directly from love or concern for another person can confer full moral worth, and he presses the further point that Miriam's affection was itself responsive to precisely the features of the situation that made staying the right thing to do, so she did act for the right reasons, even if she would not have described them as reasons at all.

Neither character should be a strawman, and I want genuine back-and-forth in which each replies to the other's actual arguments rather than restating an opening position. In particular, make them work through the following complications, in whatever order arises naturally. First, motives are opaque and mixed: Miriam herself could not say whether duty or affection moved her, and it is plausible that each motive, on its own, would have been sufficient to keep her there, so the question of which motive was operative may have no determinate answer. Second, Ada's view appears to make moral worth hostage to facts about an agent's own psychology that the agent cannot access, which sits oddly with the idea that worth is something the agent is responsible for. Third, there is the question of whether acting for the right reasons requires conceiving of those reasons under an explicitly moral description, since Yusuf thinks Miriam's responsiveness to the man's need suffices while Ada thinks something more reflective is required. Fourth, Yusuf should press the worry that the duty-centred picture alienates the agent from the very people morality is about, and Ada should have a serious reply to this rather than conceding it. Let the exchange end not with one side winning but with the two of them identifying, as precisely as they can, where the real disagreement lies and what would have to be true for each side to give ground.

I would like the dialogue to run to roughly a thousand words and to be written as plain continuous speech, each turn beginning with the speaker's name, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or stage directions; the turns should follow one another the way a conversation would read in a transcript or a letter. Please keep it first-order: no name-dropping of real philosophers and no references to the literature, since I want to test whether the arguments stand on their own before I connect them to anything published. I intend to use the result as a foil in my second chapter, so the sharper and more honest the disagreement, the better.
