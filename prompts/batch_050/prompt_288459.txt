I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a long piece on the role of obviously false assumptions in successful models across the sciences -- frictionless planes, ideal gases, point masses, and, my main case study, the perfectly rational agents of economics. A colleague who edits our economics desk has offered me a capsule account of the standard methodological defense of such assumptions, which he traces to Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics. He writes:

"Friedman's line is that the descriptive realism of a theory's assumptions is simply not the thing to test. A theory is an instrument, and like any instrument it is judged by whether it works -- that is, by whether its predictions survive confrontation with evidence -- not by whether the picture of the world embedded in its assumptions is accurate. Indeed, Friedman insisted that the more significant a theory is, the more wildly unrealistic its assumptions will typically be, since a theory that explains much by little must leave most of reality out. Hence his famous 'as if' move: expert billiards players behave as if they solve the relevant physics equations; firms behave as if they maximize profit. So when economists assume perfectly rational, perfectly informed agents -- beings who demonstrably do not exist -- the complaint that this is unrealistic simply misses the point. The only legitimate question is predictive: does the theory behave as if such agents existed, and do its implications check out? Samuelson later dubbed this maneuver the 'F-twist' and treated it as a respectable, if contentious, position in the philosophy of economics."

I'm inclined to trust the broad outline -- the colleague has covered economics for years -- but two things nag at me. The inference from "predictions are the test" to "realism complaints miss the point" strikes me as suspiciously convenient for practicing economists, and I vaguely recall Samuelson being hostile to this move rather than conceding it respectability. Since the summary will anchor a section of my article, I need an audit I can act on: which parts are accurate and safe to run, which are defensible simplifications that need a qualifying clause, and which are straightforwardly wrong or contested readings presented as settled. In particular, I want to know whether Friedman's actual position grants theories the blanket immunity from realism-based criticism that my colleague's version implies, or whether he left openings -- for instance, ways in which the content of assumptions can be probed indirectly, or contexts in which realism matters for knowing where a theory applies. And I need to know whether a further claim I plan to make on the back of this -- that invoking Friedman lets economists dismiss behavioral critiques of rational-agent assumptions -- actually follows from the accurate parts of the summary.
