In my school's philosophy club we recently got into an argument about whether it makes sense to say that one person's choice "doesn't matter" when millions of people face the same choice. A friend of mine defended the following analogy, which I've since heard several times: eating factory-farmed meat is like voting. A single vote essentially never decides an election, and a single chicken purchase essentially never changes how many animals are raised, since supply responds to demand in large batches, not one unit at a time. Yet most of us think you should vote anyway, and some of us even think voting is a duty. My friend concluded that, by parity, either you must also think abstaining from meat is a duty, or you have to admit that voting is pointless too. Someone else at the table said the analogy is lazy and that voting and meat-eating work completely differently, but couldn't say exactly how.

I'd like you to audit this analogy properly. Give me the explicit correspondence table first: what plays the role of the election, the vote, the margin of victory, the electorate, and so on, on the meat-eating side, and note where the mapping has to stretch. Then identify the disanalogies that actually matter for my friend's intended use — licensing a duty to abstain (or debunking the duty to vote) — and separate them from disanalogies that are real but irrelevant to that conclusion. For instance, does it matter that elections have thresholds and ties while animal agriculture has supply chains with probabilistic responsiveness at some unknown scale? Does it matter that voting is coordinated by a shared institution with a public rule, whereas meat-buying is not? Does it matter which side is supposed to learn from which — maybe voting is the problematic case and meat is the clear one, rather than the other way around?

Please take a definite position: tell me whether the analogy survives as an argument for my friend's conclusion, whether it instead supports the opposite conclusion about voting, or whether it collapses entirely, and give your reasons rather than laying out the options for me to pick. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, with no preamble and no restating of my question, and then give the correspondence table and the reasoning. If you think a better analogy exists for the inefficacy worry in either case — one that captures what the voting comparison misses — name it and say briefly why it's better.
