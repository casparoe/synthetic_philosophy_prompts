I teach a 75-minute upper-level philosophy seminar with 16 students. Before lecturing on Russell’s theory of descriptions, I want a classroom game organized around this passage from Bertrand Russell’s “On Denoting”:

“Everything, nothing, and something are not assumed to have any meaning in isolation, but a meaning is assigned to every proposition in which they occur. This is the principle of the theory of denoting I wish to advocate: that denoting phrases never have any meaning in themselves, but that every proposition in whose verbal expression they occur has a meaning.”

Design a competitive card-and-hidden-information game in which teams must use definite descriptions to identify objects or evaluate claims, then encounter cases with no satisfier, multiple satisfiers, and negation outside or inside the description. Participants should initially argue from ordinary intuitions about “what the phrase means” and only later discover why Russell analyzes the entire sentence. Provide materials, rules, scoring without money, a timeline, and a debrief connecting particular rounds to existence, uniqueness, predication, and scope. Also explain what will predictably go wrong on a first run and which part of Russell’s theory the game cannot settle.
