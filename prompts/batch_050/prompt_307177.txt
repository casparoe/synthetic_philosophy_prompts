My roommate is in a decision theory PhD course and over beers last week she told me my attitude toward risk is "all over the place," so I've been trying to write down my actual gut verdicts on a bunch of cases to see if there's any pattern to me. Here they are.

(1) Someone offers me a coin flip: $100 if heads, $0 if tails, or I can just take $45 and walk. I take the $45. (2) Same offer scaled up a thousand — $45,000 for sure vs. a flip for $100,000 or nothing — and there I wouldn't even hesitate, I grab the sure money, and it honestly doesn't feel like it's just "money matters less the more of it you have," it feels like a differnt kind of caution kicks in at that scale. (3) But if I got to play the original $100/$0 flip a hundred times in a row and keep the total, I'd play every single time, since the averages bail me out. (4) I pay a bit more than the actuarially fair price for fire insurance on my apartment every year and consider it money well spent. (5) And yet a couple times a year, when the jackpot gets huge, I buy a lottery ticket, fully aware it's a bad deal on average — it doesn't feel irrational, it feels like buying a tiny shot at a different life. (6) Meanwhile, with losses I turn into a gambler: if I owed a fine and could either pay $45 flat or flip a coin to pay $0 or $100, I'd flip in a heartbeat. (7) Last one, and this one feels like the key to me: what bothers me about the flip in (1) is specifically the chance of walking away with nothing. A flip between $200 and $300 has the exact same spread, and I'd happily pay $240-something for it. So I don't hate spread as such — I hate a bad floor.

So my tentative principle is that I'm some kind of pessimist: I give the worst available outcome extra weight beyond its probability and more or less let that drive the decision. My roommate said this sounds like something called risk-weighted expected utility, where your choices come from discounting outcomes through a "risk function" rather than through how much the money itself is worth to you, but she was three beers deep so who knows if that's a real thing. Does the pessimism principle actually deliver all seven of my verdicts? If not, is there a cleaner principle — or a small set of them, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is handling which case — that gets most of them, and which verdict would you throw out first to buy tidiness? Also tell me what your best candidate says about a case I didn't list and keep flip-flopping on: a coin flip where I win $10,500 on heads and lose $10,000 on tails. One more thing: please seperate what's just math here — what the standard expected-utility picture can and can't reproduce, what the risk-weighting picture actually predicts — from what's a genuine live dispute among decision theorists, like whether any of my verdicts are flat-out irrational. Don't present either framework as the settled consensus if it isn't one.
