You are a professional speechwriter with a solid working knowledge of constitutional theory, and I want you to approach this carefully: first spend a few sentences thinking through the rhetorical problem I describe below, then write the speech itself, then append the short note requested at the end.

The occasion: I am a political theorist in a European constitutional monarchy. Every year the country's main republican association holds a dinner and, by long tradition, invites one defender of the monarchy to give the after-dinner speech. This year they have invited me. The room will hold about two hundred people: mostly committed republicans who know the standard arguments cold, plus a handful of journalists and a few of my colleagues from the constitutional law faculty. The speech should run about twelve minutes when read aloud.

Here is the complication, and it is not incidental decoration — it should shape the entire speech. My actual position, developed over three books, is that a constitutional monarchy is defensible only on the condition that it is politically mute. Its legitimacy rests on the fact that the head of state wields no partisan power, takes no public positions, and never needs defending the way an elected politician does. On my view, the moment the monarchy becomes an object of ordinary political contest — the moment it has defenders and opponents trading arguments the way parties do — the one thing that made it worth keeping is already gone. A defense of the monarchy that succeeds as political rhetoric is, on my own theory, a symptom of the monarchy's failure.

So the speech has to thread a needle. It cannot be a rousing partisan defense, because on my own view that would be self-undermining. It cannot simply concede to the republicans, because I accepted the invitation in good faith and I do in fact hold the position I hold. And it cannot be a meta-speech that merely talks about this paradox at the audience, because after-dinner speeches that lecture the audience about the impossibility of addressing them are insufferable. What I want is a speech that takes the paradox as its subject without disappearing into it: warm, occasionally funny, honest about the genuine strengths of the republican case, and structured so that by the end the audience has received a real argument — roughly, that the strongest case for the monarchy is that a well-ordered country should almost never have occasion to talk about it — delivered in a way that enacts the point rather than violating it.

A few further constraints. The room contains people who will catch every allusion, so the argument must hold up at the level of constitutional theory: you may draw on the familiar distinction between a constitution's ceremonial and working parts, on the logic of reserve powers that retain their authority only so long as they are never exercised, and on the idea that hereditary succession functions as a device for removing the head of state from contestability rather than as a claim about anyone's personal merit. But the room also contains people who have never read a word of this literature, so none of these ideas may appear under a technical label; each must be carried by a concrete image, anecdote, or example. Invented anecdotes are fine, provided they are plainly the kind of thing that could happen rather than claims about real individuals — please use no named monarchs, historical episodes, or dated events, since I do not want to have to fact-check the speech. And the republican case must be stated at its strongest, never as a straw man; the audience will know immediately if I flinch.

Since this is a speech rather than an essay, I cannot ask you for a verdict, so I will ask for the nearest equivalent: after the speech, append a note of a few sentences saying where the speech takes liberties with the constitutional ideas it draws on, which contested theoretical claim it leans on hardest, and which single moment in the speech you are least confident will land with this particular audience, and why.
