I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of cognitive science, and I'm writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on the frame problem that students answer in two hours, and I need it to be impossible to answer by reciting the standard potted history (McCarthy and Hayes's formalization, the qualification and ramification problems, Dennett's robot stories). Specifically, I want the question to force students to confront a crux: whether the frame problem is essentially an artifact of logic-based AI -- a problem about axiomatizing which facts survive an action -- or whether it is a deeper problem about relevance that any finite agent faces, such that systems which avoid explicit symbolic axiomatization altogether (say, a robot controlled by a learned predictive model) have merely relocated the problem rather than dissolved it. The question should include a short scenario of the student's own to analyze (I want the question to supply a novel robot scenario, roughly: a household robot that must decide, while cooking, which of the indefinitely many consequences of turning on the stove it needs to represent before acting), and require students to say what would count as evidence that a given system has genuinely solved the problem versus being lucky in its training distribution.

Please deliver the following, in this order:

1. The Question -- the exam question as it would appear on the paper, including the scenario, the tasks, and any guidance about scope (about 250 words).
2. Model Answer -- what a top answer looks like, about 700 words. It must take a position on the narrow-artifact versus deep-relevance dispute rather than surveying both, and it must explicitly address what a learning-based system changes and what it leaves untouched.
3. Marking Scheme -- five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, totalling 25. Each point should name the specific move a candidate must make (not a topic area but a move, e.g. of the form: distinguishes the representational version of the problem from the inferential version and argues the latter survives the former's dissolution).
4. Typical Errors -- one line per must-hit point describing the characteristic way a weaker candidate fails that point (e.g. treats the scenario as purely an engineering problem and never says what a solution would have to show).
