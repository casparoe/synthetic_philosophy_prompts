Please draft a retirement address of about seven minutes for Dean Elian Voss, the longtime admissions dean at the fictional Halden College. The occasion is the unveiling of the college’s new Admissions Data Wall, which Voss championed after a controversy over legacy admissions. The audience will include statisticians, admissions officers, alumni and their children, faculty, student journalists, and some rejected applicants who remain angry about the episode. The speaker is the chair of the faculty senate.

The controversy arose from two tables, both arithmetically correct. In the most recent cycle, legacy applicants were admitted to the Conservatory at a rate of 10 out of 50, or 20 percent, while non-legacy applicants were admitted at a rate of 7 out of 40, or 17.5 percent. In Data Science, legacy applicants were admitted at a rate of 80 out of 100, or 80 percent, while non-legacy applicants were admitted at a rate of 150 out of 200, or 75 percent. Thus legacy applicants had the higher admission rate in both programs. Yet because legacy applicants were concentrated in the less selective Conservatory and non-legacy applicants in the less selective Data Science program, the overall rates reversed: 90 of 150 legacy applicants, or 60 percent, were admitted, compared with 157 of 240 non-legacy applicants, or about 65.4 percent.

The speech should use this reversal to honor Voss’s commitment to publishing both aggregate and disaggregated figures, but it must not treat Simpson’s paradox as a magic acquittal. It should make clear that the aggregate result is not an illusion, that the program-level result is not automatically the causal truth, and that further reversals might appear under other relevant groupings. It should also acknowledge questions the tables leave open: whether the college influences which programs applicants choose, whether program-level criteria are themselves fair, and whether transparency about a process is compatible with responsibility for its outcome.

The address needs to work as a speech rather than a statistics lecture. Give nonspecialists one memorable way to understand how the whole can point in one direction while every examined part points in the other, without suggesting that statistical facts depend on how we choose to describe them. Give the statisticians enough precision that they will not wince. The tone should be ceremonially warm but morally restrained: Voss may be praised for refusing to hide inconvenient numbers, not declared innocent of every charge brought against the admissions system. End with a toast that follows naturally from that distinction.

Please add, after the speech, a short private note to me identifying any places where the address simplifies the statistics or takes a contested position about what the relevant comparison should be. If the assignment itself is confused—for example, if one cannot appropriately celebrate Voss’s transparency while remaining noncommittal about the fairness of the outcome—say so plainly instead of smoothing over the difficulty.
