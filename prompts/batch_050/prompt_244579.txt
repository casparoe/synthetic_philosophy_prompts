I am preparing the background handout for a graduate seminar on AI safety, and the final unit concerns the impact-measure research program: the attempt to make an agent low impact by penalizing side effects formally, rather than by specifying in advance which outcomes are good or bad. The handout already covers the following proposals, and I would like one more for the closing section.

1. Fixed-baseline distance penalties. Measure the divergence between the state of the world under the agent's policy and the state that would have obtained under a null policy, using some state-difference metric, and penalize the divergence (the low-impact proposal of Armstrong and Levinstein).

2. Stepwise inaction baselines. At each time step, compare the actual future with the counterfactual future in which the agent does nothing from that point on, and penalize divergence from this rolling baseline (stepwise inaction, as in work by Krakovna and collaborators on relative reachability).

3. Attainable utility preservation. Penalize changes in how well the agent could optimize each of a set of auxiliary reward functions, so that destroying future option value counts as impact even when no present-tense state comparison registers it (Turner and collaborators).

4. Reachability preservation. Penalize changes in which states are reachable from the current state, so that closing off regions of the state space is costly whether or not anything has yet been destroyed.

5. Reversibility and reset-based constraints. Penalize effects the agent could not subsequently undo, or require the agent to preserve its ability to return to the vicinity of the initial state.

6. Explicit whitelisting. Hand-code the permitted types of effect and treat everything else as forbidden.

I am not interested in a variant: a new baseline, a new state-difference metric, a different distribution over auxiliary reward functions, or a weighted blend of two items above all count as rewordings, not additions. Propose a seventh approach whose underlying structure differs from all six — it should answer the question of what makes an action high-impact in a way that is not naturally expressible as a distance from a counterfactual, a change in an ability or reachability set, or an explicit list of permitted effects. Sketch concretely how the penalty would be computed in a toy gridworld or similarly simple setting, and then evaluate your proposal against the three standard failure modes I plan to discuss in class: offsetting (the agent causes a side effect and then arranges matters so that the penalty does not register it, for instance by undoing the change along the measured dimension while the real effect stands); interference incentives (the agent acts to prevent humans or natural processes from changing the world, because change is precisely what it is penalized for); and value loading (the measure smuggles in the designer's substantive judgments about which effects matter, so that the program covertly concedes that avoiding side effects cannot be defined without a partial specification of value). Conclude by saying, in one or two sentences, whether your proposal supports or undermines the view that low impact can be defined in a value-neutral way.
