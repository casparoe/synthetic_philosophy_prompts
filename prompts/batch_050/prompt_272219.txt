I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been reading about Boethius and his treatment of divine foreknowledge in the Consolation. Out of that reading I assembled an argument, and it seems to prove far more than it should, but I can't locate the bad step. I'd like a diagnosis: which premise or inference should I reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost?

The setup as I understand it: Boethius holds that God is eternal, not merely everlasting. God does not wait for the future to arrive; he exists outside time and apprehends all moments in something like a single timeless present. The analogy that made this click for me is a completed log file: I can inspect it from beginning to end at once, rather than experiencing it line by line as it is written.

The argument:

(1) God, being eternal, knows in his timeless present everything I will ever do, including what I will choose to do tomorrow afternoon.

(2) Knowledge is factive: if someone knows that p, then p.

(3) So it is settled, from the standpoint of eternity, that tomorrow I will choose what I will in fact choose.

(4) What is present to an eternal knower is as fixed as the past is for us. The whole timeline is, from God's vantage, already complete; there is no "yet to be determined" anywhere in it, just as there is none in a finished log.

(5) What is as fixed as the past cannot be otherwise. I cannot now make it the case that I had a different breakfast this morning, and if tomorrow's choice is fixed in the same way, I cannot make it different either.

(6) Therefore I cannot choose otherwise tomorrow; and since nothing in the reasoning is specific to this particular choice, no human choice is ever genuinely open.

(7) Worse, nothing in the argument seems to depend on anything distinctively divine. Replace the claim that God timelessly knows what I'll do with the claim that it was simply true a thousand years ago that I would do it, and the same chain appears to go through — which would establish fatalism from the fixity of truth alone, with no theology required.

I can't accept (6), and (7) makes me suspect the machinery itself rather than any theological premise. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than keep the conclusion: the factivity of knowledge and divine foreknowledge both seem considerably more secure than my ability to find the flaw. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if the guilty step is one of (4) or (5), I'd like to understand why the move from "known, hence settled" to "settled, hence unchangeable" seemed so natural — since in engineering terms, a read-only view of a recording really does seem to fix what the recording contains.
