Two coworkers and I stayed late at the office debating something that started as a joke and turned into a real argument. Background: our team had to decide by Friday whether to migrate our payment service to a new database. One coworker, Dana, had been going back and forth all week and genuinely hadn't made up her mind. On Thursday the other coworker, Marcus, asked her, "What do you think the odds are you'll end up voting yes?" Dana said the question was malformed, and they argued for an hour. Here's the exchange as best I remember it, with numbered moves so you can go through them one by one.

(1) Marcus opened by saying the question is obviously coherent and answerable. Dana knows her own inclinations better than anyone; she could introspect and report something like 60/40 in favor of migrating. We assign probabilities to other people's decisions all the time, and she has strictly more information about her own case than she does about anyone else's. Refusing to put a number on it, he said, is just squeamishness about quantifying anything mental.

(2) Dana replied that the asymmetry between self and other is exactly the point. When she predicts someone else's decision, she treats it as an event to be forecast from evidence. But while she is still deliberating about the migration, the decision isn't an event out there to be forecast -- it's the thing she is in the middle of producing. If she concluded "I'm 70% likely to vote yes," she would be treating her own deliberation as a weather system to observe rather than a question to answer, and at that point she isn't really deliberating anymore. Probabilities about her own choice can only be formed from a stance that abandons the deliberative stance, so prediction and deliberation can't coexist for the same option at the same time.

(3) Marcus said this proves too much. Consider: Dana has been in four previous architecture debates, and in all four she initially resisted the change and eventually voted yes after the security review. On that track record, shouldn't she put a high probability on voting yes again? If she refuses, she's ignoring perfectly good evidence -- the same evidence she'd happily use for anyone else. And note she can use this evidence without abandoning deliberation: knowing her own pattern doesn't settle the technical question, which is what she's actually deliberating about.

(4) Dana answered that (3) equivocates on two different things. One is a probability about the *outcome* of her deliberation, formed by stepping back and viewing herself from the outside, the way she'd view a colleague. The other is a probability *within* deliberation, attached to an option she is still actively weighing. The first is legitimate -- she can predict her own future self the way she'd predict anyone -- but only by treating her current self as past, i.e., by no longer occupying the deliberating position. The second is incoherent: inside deliberation, the options have to be treated as genuinely open, and "genuinely open" is not the same as "has probability 0.6." A coin she's about to decide the outcome of isn't a biased coin.

(5) Marcus's last move: he said (4) draws the line in the wrong place, because we in fact run both stances simultaneously all the time, and engineers above all people should admit this. His example: our self-monitoring dashboards. When he writes a retry loop he models his own system as stochastic while still designing its behavior deliberately. Similarly, he said, he can note "I'll probably cave and say yes by Friday" *while still genuinely weighing the technical merits* -- the prediction is just one more input to the deliberation, the way knowing your own biases is. Dana said that if the prediction is an input to deliberation rather than a replacement for it, then it isn't a probability assigned to the choice itself; it's evidence about her dispositions, and he's confusing the two. Marcus said that distinction is doing no work, because dispositions just are probabilities of choosing.

Please adjudicate. For each of the five moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-incomplete, and justify each rating in a sentence or three. Then give your verdict on the overall dispute: is Dana right that a deliberating agent cannot coherently assign probabilities to the options she is still deciding between, or is Marcus right that self-prediction and deliberation can run in parallel without contradiction? If you think part of the disagreement is verbal -- for instance, that they're using "probability of my choice" to mean different things -- identify exactly which part, and say what each side would have to concede to resolve it.
