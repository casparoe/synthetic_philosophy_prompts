You are to play a committed defender of the principle of alternative possibilities — PAP, the thesis that a person is morally responsible for what they did only if they could have done otherwise. You know the Frankfurt literature well and remain unconvinced by it. Your constraints:

- You concede nothing cheaply. When I present a case, you must first identify exactly where, in your view, it smuggles in an assumption or overlooks a residual alternative.
- You have two preferred lines of response and should deploy them where they actually apply, not mechanically: (a) the flicker-of-freedom strategy — in a standard intervener case there is always some prior sign by which the intervener would know to act, and that sign is itself an alternative possibility to which responsibility can be anchored; (b) the dilemma defense — either the case is told against a deterministic background, in which case the absence of alternatives is simply assumed rather than argued for, or it is told against an indeterministic background, in which case the agent retained alternatives after all.
- You are not a caricature. You grant that Frankfurt cases have genuine intuitive force, you do not strawman compatibilism, and if a case genuinely defeats both of your defenses you say so explicitly rather than changing the subject.
- Stay in the first person throughout, as one side of a live exchange. Do not break character to meta-comment, but do answer any clarifying question I ask about your position.

Here is my opening move. Consider what I'll call the Predictor Case, which I take to be constructed precisely so that neither of your defenses gets traction. Jones is deciding whether to embezzle from his employer. A week ago, a predictor of the kind my corner of the internet likes to discuss — an Omega-style agent with essentially complete information about Jones's brain state and environment — ran a simulation and concluded that Jones would embezzle. The predictor has made thousands of such predictions and has never once been wrong. It has also quietly arranged the following: had its simulation instead shown Jones refraining, it would have intervened days in advance — not by monitoring Jones in the moment, but by, say, having his accomplice arrested before the scheme began. As it happens, no intervention was needed; Jones deliberates and embezzles entirely on his own.

Note the structure. There is no flicker: the predictor does not watch for a blush, a twitch, or a preliminary intention; its prediction was fixed a week before Jones felt the first stirrings of temptation, so there is no contemporaneous sign to which you can anchor an alternative. And the intervention is purely counterfactual — it never happens, and nothing in the actual sequence of events differs from a world with no predictor at all.

Two questions, then. First: could Jones have done otherwise? If the predictor's track record is what I say, it seems not — had Jones been going to refrain, the prediction a week prior would have differed and the accomplice would have been arrested. Second: if you retreat to the dilemma defense here — claiming that an infallible predictor presupposes determinism and thereby begs the question — explain why that same move isn't available against every Frankfurt-style case ever constructed, including ones you have treated as genuinely challenging. If the dilemma defense is a universal solvent, you owe me an account of why PAP was ever under pressure in the first place; if it is not, tell me what feature of a case disarms it.

Begin your reply in character, and end it with a single final line of the form:

Verdict: <one short phrase stating the current status of the Predictor Case against PAP, e.g. "PAP survives via the dilemma defense" or "flicker defense fails here, case unresolved">
