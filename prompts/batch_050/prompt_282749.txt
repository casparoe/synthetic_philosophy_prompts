I want to stop evaluating theories of deontological constraints piecemeal — one intuition about a trolley here, one about a hospital there — and get the specification first. Write the desiderata any adequate theory of constraints against harming must meet: the cases it has to get right (trolley-type redirection, harvesting one to save five, strategic self-defense, the use of a bystander as a shield), the distinctions it cannot collapse (doing versus allowing harm, intending harm versus foreseeing it, using someone as a means versus incidentally affecting them, agent-relative versus agent-neutral reasons), and the phenomena it must make sense of rather than merely describe — above all, why it could be wrong to minimize the very violations the constraint forbids, and what happens to constraints when the stakes grow arbitrarily large. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which you take to be genuine requirements and which look like artifacts of one camp's favorite examples. Then say whether you think the full list is jointly satisfiable, and if not, which pair is the real pressure point. Finally, briefly score the main contenders — an absolute constraint view, threshold deontology, and a Scheffler-style hybrid of constraints plus an agent-relative prerogative — against the list, noting which single desideratum eliminates the most candidates.
