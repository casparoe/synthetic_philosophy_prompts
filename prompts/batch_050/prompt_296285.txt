I just got moved onto the politics desk at a fact-checking site, and I've realized a huge share of the claims we get are conditionals, and nobody here has a consistent way to handle them. There's a big difference I want baked into the method between claims like "if the trade deal goes through, food prices will fall" (something you can sort of assess with evidence about mechanisms and base rates) and claims like "if the previous administration hadn't passed that law, we'd have avoided the shortage entirely" (where the whole question is which hypothetical world you're even comparing ours to, and how much you're allowed to hold fixed when you rewind history). I dimly remember from a philosophy elective that philosophers treat these two families of "if" sentences quite differently -- the indicative ones get tied to something like conditional probability, while the subjunctive/counterfactual ones get analyzed in terms of nearby possible worlds and similarity, and there's that famous pair about Oswald and Kennedy where flipping between the two moods takes you from obviously true to obviously absurd. But I can't just hand my editor a philosophy seminar. I need something I can actually run on a claim in under an hour, with my editor signing off at the end.

So: please turn whatever the actual theory says into a written decision procedure for rating conditional claims. It should be a sequence of steps in order -- first sorting the claim into the right family (including what to do when the grammar is ambiguous or the speaker is clearly using the "wrong" mood rhetorically), then for each family a different set of questions: for indicatives, what evidence would even bear on them and what to do when no conditional probability is remotely estimable; for counterfactuals, how to pin down what the speaker is holding fixed, how to catch "backtracking" readings where the claim quietly rewrites more of history than it admits, and when a counterfactual is just unrateable rather than false. Mark clearly which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls in a trenchcoat, because my editor will ask.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical claims and show the work: (1) "If the central bank cuts rates this quarter, mortgage applications will rise." (2) "If the senator's 2019 amendment had passed, the factory in Dayton would never have closed." (3) "If this drought is what climate models predict, the reservoir won't recover next year either." At least one of these should end up with a verdict I'll hate having to publish -- e.g. having to call a claim from a politician on "our side" unrateable or misleading -- and tell me honestly where the procedure breaks down entirely, like counterfactuals about long counterfactual histories or pure rhetorical flourishes ("if that's a tax cut, I'm a Dutchman"). Please don't pad it with citations, I can't check them anyway; I need the reasoning, not a bibliography.
