I have been reading an essay on algorithmic information theory which presents Solomonoff induction as the final word on Occam's razor. Its summary runs as follows: 'Solomonoff induction is the mathematically rigorous version of the principle that simpler hypotheses should be preferred. It assigns each possible sequence of observations a prior probability of 2^−K(s), where K(s) is the length of the shortest computer program that produces s on a universal Turing machine, and it predicts by conditioning this prior on everything observed so far. Thanks to the invariance theorem, the choice of universal machine makes no difference, so this prior is objective rather than conventional. Solomonoff proved that predictions made this way converge to the true distribution of any computable environment, which means the only thing standing between us and perfect inductive inference is that Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable. Practical methods such as minimum description length are approximations to this ideal, and as our approximations improve, scientific inference will in effect be automated.'

The essay is by a competent writer and most of it sounds right to me, but I have a nagging suspicion that it overstates things, particularly the move from the invariance theorem to objectivity and the claim about convergence. Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me: which parts a specialist in algorithmic information theory would accept as accurate; which parts are defensible popularisations that elide a qualification that actually matters; and which parts are straightforwardly wrong or rest on conflating distinct notions (for instance, description length of a sequence versus the length of programs that generate it, or convergence of prediction error versus identification of the true hypothesis)? In particular, is the universal prior really machine-independent in any sense that matters for finite data, and does the convergence theorem deliver what the final sentence needs? Finally, once the summary is corrected, does anything of its central claim — that Occam's razor has been made fully rigorous — survive?
