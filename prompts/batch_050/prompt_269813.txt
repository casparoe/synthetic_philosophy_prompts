I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consciousness (14 students, one 80-minute meeting), and next week we reach the distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness, with global workspace theory as the main access-first rival. In past years, students have read the partial-report argument and nodded along without it ever touching them: they treat "rich phenomenology, sparse access" as a view one might hold rather than something that could be true of them, right now, in this room.

Design a two-part in-class activity that makes them undergo the distinction before they hear its name.

Part one should recreate, with classroom materials and no special equipment, the structure of the partial-report paradigm: a briefly displayed array, a cue delivered after the display vanishes, and the gap between what students can report under full-report versus cued-report conditions. Specify the exact procedure (stimuli, timings that are achievable with a slide deck or held-up cards, how the cue is delivered, how many trials, how responses are collected and tallied so the class generates its own data), the roles (who displays, who cues, who records), and a timeline. The design should be arranged so that each student first insists they saw the whole array and then collides with their own failure to report it — the felt surplus over the accessible should arise in the first person, not be described to them.

Part two should stage the global workspace theorist's reply as something the class can enact rather than something I tell them: an activity in which the information from part one is shown to be unusable for the things access is supposed to underwrite — verbal report, reasoning across items, deliberate comparison — unless it gets selected and made globally available, and in which the "rich" residue begins to look either generic or like a memory artifact. Give the procedure, timeline, and the specific moments where students should feel the pull of the deflationary reading.

Then write the debrief plan: the questions I should ask, in order; how to map specific moments of the activity onto the phenomenal/access distinction and onto the workspace model; the two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students peeking, timings collapsing, a confident student derailing the data collection) and how to handle each; and a frank account of which feature of the philosophical dispute the activity structurally cannot reproduce — in particular, that behavioral report gaps leave open whether the unreported residue was ever phenomenally present at all — and what I, as facilitator, must supply by argument afterward.

No lecture beforehand, and the standard names for the positions should not be mentioned until the debrief.
