I'm co-leading a faculty reading group on judgment and decision-making in literature, and for our next session I've been assigned Pride and Prejudice. The frame I want to impose on the novel is the distinction between the inside view and the outside view -- the difference between predicting how things will go from the felt particulars of your own situation, and predicting from base rates, reference classes, and what generally happens to people so situated -- together with the doctrine of epistemic modesty, the idea that one should discount one's own confident impressions in favor of what the average observer or the statistical regularity would say. What strikes me, on rereading, is that Austen seems to run this experiment twice with opposite results. Elizabeth Bennet is the great inside-view reasoner of English fiction: she trusts her own readings of character, forms confident judgments of Darcy and Wickham from close observation, and is catastrophically wrong about both, corrected only when documentary evidence forces a re-reading. Charlotte Lucas, by contrast, makes what is essentially an actuarial decision about marriage -- she treats her own romantic prospects as a reference class rather than a love story, accepts Collins on base-rate grounds about what women in her position can expect, and the novel, surprisingly, seems to half-vindicate her: her arrangement is comfortable, her judgment of its costs lucid, and the narrator never quite punishes her the way the romantic reading would predict.

I'd like you to act as a literary critic who is also philosophically serious about epistemology -- someone who could publish in either venue. Before composing your answer, brainstorm at least three distinct readings the novel permits on this question, including at least one that cuts against the reading I've sketched above. Then write the reading you find most defensible. In particular, I want you to engage a distinction I've been toying with and that I haven't seen in the literature: between epistemic modesty proper, which defers to base rates and general opinion about how things usually go, and what I'd call interpretive modesty, which is not deference to anyone but a way of holding one's own readings of character loosely, treating them as revisable hypotheses rather than perceptions. My suspicion is that Austen rewards one of these and punishes the other, and that Elizabeth's arc -- whose correction, notice, comes not from deferring to base rates but from better first-person evidence -- is actually an argument against epistemic modesty in matters of character, while Charlotte's subplot is an argument for it in matters of predicting one's own future happiness. If that's right, the novel defends a domain-sensitivity thesis about when the inside view is legitimate, which would be a genuinely interesting position and not one I see the psychological literature on the planning fallacy taking seriously. But test my suspicion hard: steelman the opposite reading (that the novel is simply on the side of modesty everywhere, or that Charlotte is meant as a warning and I'm misreading the narrator's tone), and say plainly where the text resists being fitted into this frame at all.

Close by answering the critic's question: what, if anything, does the novel add to the inside view / outside view debate that the psychological and philosophical literature, proceeding by experiments and thought experiments, does not already contain? If your honest answer is that the novel is a less precise instrument than the debate and mostly dramatizes confusion, say that rather than manufacturing a contribution. Aim for something I could hand to the reading group as a discussion paper -- organized, argumentative, and faithful to the novel as a novel rather than a treatise in costume.
