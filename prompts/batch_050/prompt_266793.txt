I'm in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and philosophy of science, and for a class presentation I built the following argument against Bayesian confirmation theory. The trouble is that the conclusion is stronger than I can believe, and I cannot locate the weak step.

The setup: a research group formulates a new theory T to explain a dataset D that has been sitting in their archives for years — known to everyone, trusted, assigned probability one. Intuitively, T's ability to entail and systematize D counts in T's favor. This is the standard structure of, for instance, the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, known for decades, counting in favor of general relativity.

The argument:
(1) Evidence E confirms hypothesis H only if learning E raises the rational credence one ought to assign H.
(2) When E is already known with certainty, P(E) = 1, and conditioning on a proposition of probability 1 changes nothing: P(H|E) = P(H).
(3) All evidence actually in our possession is known with certainty in this sense; and the logical facts about which hypotheses entail which data, if known, have probability 1 as well.
(4) So no evidence we possess confirms any hypothesis; in particular, old data cannot confirm a new theory.
(5) The obvious repair — assess confirmation by the credences we would have had before learning E — fails whenever the evidence predates the theory or the theorist: there is no well-defined earlier credence function to rewind to.
(6) Therefore confirmation of theories by antecedently known data is impossible, and any theory choice driven by such data is irrational.
(7) Since a great deal of respectable science proceeds in exactly this way, either that science is irrational or the Bayesian framework is false.

Two questions. First: which premise or step do you reject, and why did it look compelling? Second: what does rejecting it cost? Concretely, what must a Bayesian who rejects (2), or (5), or the move from (3)–(4) to (6) say about logical omniscience, about which proposition is actually doing the confirming, and about which probability function underwrites the confirmation relation? If forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2), since they strike me as the core of the framework — but I want to know what I am paying for them.
