Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether a larger population is better, and I want an outside audit of the analogy he kept leaning on. His case for pronatalism came down to this: each additional person is a lottery ticket for genius. Most tickets lose, but the occasional Newton or Einstein hits the jackpot, and — this is what he stressed — the jackpot is non-rival: everyone alive, and everyone born afterward, benefits from one person's ideas at no extra cost, so the expected value of each additional ticket is positive and compounds across generations. I countered with the older picture of the world as a lifeboat or a fixed pie: more mouths, smaller shares. He replied that the pie picture assumes the stock of goods is fixed, whereas his lottery generates precisely the ideas that enlarge it. We parted without either conceding, and I do not want to concede now unless the analogy actually survives scrutiny.

Please audit the lottery analogy as an argument for pronatalism. Concretely, I want three things.

First, a correspondence table. Map each element of the lottery onto the population case — at minimum: the ticket price, the odds of winning, the jackpot, the fact that these winnings can be shared at zero marginal cost, the other ticket-buyers, the number of draws, and whoever profits from selling the tickets. Where an element has no natural correspondent in the population case, say so plainly rather than forcing one.

Second, the disanalogies. Test at least the following, and add any I have missed: (a) lottery tickets are identical and independent, but the talent of a marginal child plausibly depends on per-capita investment in children, which rapid population growth may dilute; (b) ordinary lottery winnings are rival while ideas are non-rival — note explicitly which side of our argument this cuts for; (c) a lottery's prize pool is fixed in advance, whereas the value of further ideas may be falling (the easy ones discovered first) or rising (ideas combine), and my friend's case needs the right one of these; (d) some tickets carry negative payouts — additional people impose congestion, emissions, and strain on shared institutions in a way no lottery ticket does; (e) buying more tickets leaves the rules of the lottery unchanged, but more people change the rules under which everyone lives, for better or worse.

Third, the verdict. Tell me which single disanalogy does the most damage to my friend's argument, which of my attempted counters actually fails (he will certainly ask), and whether the analogy, once audited, still carries the pronatalist conclusion, carries it only in a weakened form (specify the weakening precisely), or fails outright. If there is a better analogy for whatever is genuinely right in his position, name it and explain in one or two sentences why it fits better.

I want a committed answer, not a menu of options: take a definite position on whether the analogy earns its keep and defend it against the strongest objection. End with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...}, enclosing one short phrase — survives intact, survives weakened, or fails — followed by the single most damaging disanalogy in a few words.
