I serve on the clemency advisory board of a state that retains the death penalty, and we have a new petition before us. Our statute instructs us to recommend commutation wherever execution would be unjust, and the governor's office has made clear that it wants fidelity to our own past practice rather than any change of direction. Our record, with identifying details removed, is as follows.

Petition one: the prisoner tortured and murdered two strangers over several hours; guilt was established by continuous surveillance footage, corroborated confessions, and forensic evidence, and no serious claim of innocence has ever been raised. We declined to recommend commutation. Petition two: the prisoner committed a comparably brutal murder, but guilt rested on a single eyewitness identification and a circumstantial chain, and the prisoner has always maintained innocence. We recommended commutation, over a dissent that emphasized the brutality of the crime. Petition three: the crime was again maximally aggravated and the evidence of guilt overwhelming, but our own commissioned review had concluded that the state's capital statute produced no measurable marginal deterrent effect and that life imprisonment without parole incapacitated equally well. We nonetheless declined to recommend commutation. Petition four: the prisoner committed an intentional but unaggravated murder, a single killing in the course of an armed robbery, with guilt as certain as in the first petition. We recommended commutation.

The new petition: the crime is at least as aggravated as in petition one, involving the prolonged killing of a child; guilt is supported by a DNA match and a confession, but post-conviction review has turned up irregularities in the laboratory's chain of custody, and a portion of the confession was never recorded. Every member of the board concedes a small residual doubt, though none believes the prisoner is likely innocent.

Please tell me, first, what recommendation we should make if our aim is consistency with our own record, and which earlier petition is the closest analog. Second, set out at least two distinct principles that each rationalize all four prior petitions, show that they diverge on this one, and say which you take to be the best reconstruction of our practice and why. Third, for whichever recommendation you give, state candidly what principle we would in effect be adopting, and what we would be adopting if we went the other way. Finally, if our record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, describe the hypothetical petition that, had it appeared earlier, would have done the most to settle which principle we have actually been following.
