Next week I am presenting a paper provisionally titled The Audit Problem for Common Knowledge of Rationality to a twelve-person workshop on epistemic game theory and formal epistemology. My thesis is that, in extensive-form games, common knowledge of rationality is too coarse as a standard of interactive reasoning because it ignores whether a player’s higher-order beliefs would survive what I call justification resilience: a publicly announced requirement that each player state the counterfactual beliefs that make their planned continuation rational. The announcement changes no payoffs, introduces no cheap talk as a strategic move, assumes ideal reasoners, and is not meant as a tremble, a bounded-rationality device, or a psychological claim. I argue that rational players may satisfy ordinary choice-based and belief-based rationality conditions while relying on off-path beliefs whose justification would collapse once those beliefs had to be articulated and mutually examined. I use an ordinary finite centipede-style game and a coordination case to argue that common knowledge of resilient rationality sometimes supports a backward-induction conclusion more cleanly than common knowledge of rationality alone, but that it can also undermine a forward-induction argument whose intuitive appeal depends on keeping an implausible justification offstage.

The audience will include five epistemic game theorists fluent in type spaces, rationalizability, extensive-form reasoning, and backward- versus forward-induction results; three formal epistemologists who care about higher-order belief and public announcement; two game theorists suspicious of any solution concept that relies on agents introspectively explaining themselves; and two philosophers of action who will press whether my condition concerns rationality, communication, or merely equilibrium selection.
