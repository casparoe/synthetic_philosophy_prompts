I work as a journalist covering science and ideas, and two years ago I was appointed the lay member of my local hospital's clinical ethics committee, partly because of a long-form piece I wrote on the philosophy of personal identity. I now need help drafting a written opinion, and I would like the reasoning to be philosophically careful rather than merely procedural. The case: a 78-year-old retired palliative care nurse with moderate Alzheimer's disease has been admitted with pneumonia. Nine years ago, shortly after her diagnosis and while unquestionably competent, she executed a detailed advance directive stipulating that once she could no longer recognise her children or live outside institutional care, she was to receive no antibiotics for intercurrent infections and no artificial nutrition, comfort measures only. She added a pointed clause, apparently anticipating exactly our situation: she foresaw that her future self might appear content and might even ask to be kept alive, and she directed that such requests be set aside as, in her words, the utterances of a mind she would by then no longer govern. Both of the triggering conditions are now met. Yet by the unanimous account of her care-home staff she is a cheerful person who enjoys music, meals and visitors, and when a junior doctor gently explained that she had a chest infection she said she would like the medicine, please. Her daughter insists the directive be honoured; her son insists that his mother, the woman in front of us, plainly wants treatment.

The committee is split and I have been asked to draft the short written opinion we will adopt, which will be shared with the treating team and the family. Roughly half the members hold that a contemporaneous, informed refusal by a competent person is decisive, and that to override it now is to treat a past version of a patient as less authoritative than her present diminished self, something we would never do in reverse. The other half argue that the person before us has current, simple, real interests — in continued life, in small pleasures — that cannot be extinguished by instructions written by someone who could not have known what this life would actually be like; one member went further and questioned whether the patient is even, in the relevant sense, the same individual who signed the document. Our jurisdiction treats valid advance refusals as legally binding where they clearly apply, but there is genuine dispute about whether her present assent to treatment, however fleeting, changes the picture.

Please draft the opinion, of perhaps 600 to 800 words, in British English. It must reach a concrete and actionable recommendation — treat the pneumonia, withhold antibiotics, or some defined middle course such as a time-limited trial with explicit stopping conditions — and it must set out the reasoning in terms the family can follow without philosophical training. Whichever side it lands on, it should give the strongest argument for the losing side an honest and unpatronising hearing, rather than caricaturing it. If you think the question of whether the earlier self may bind the later one turns on a distinction the committee has so far missed, I would be glad to see that distinction drawn and put to work in the recommendation.
