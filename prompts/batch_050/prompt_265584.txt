Next month I'm giving a talk at a civic-tech conference defending a proposal drawn from Simone Weil's essay On the Abolition of All Political Parties: that liberal democracies would reason better if political parties were dissolved and replaced by fluid, issue-specific associations that form and disband around particular questions. My version of the argument, following Weil, is that a party operates as a machine for generating collective passion, that it exerts continuous pressure on the thinking of everyone inside it until the party line displaces individual judgment, and that it inevitably comes to treat its own growth as an end rather than a means — so a legislature organized by parties is structurally incapable of deliberating in good faith. I'm a software engineer with no formal training in political theory or political science; I built the talk mostly on the essay itself, plus an analogy I plan to draw between parties and distributed systems that have lost their error-correction mechanisms.

The audience will be comparative-politics scholars who study party systems, a few campaign professionals, and some political scientists who work on polarization — no philosophers.
