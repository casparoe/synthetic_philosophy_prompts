I am finishing a novel whose central figure, a dying classicist, spends his last chapter composing a long meditation on mortality. The book is not a treatise, but I have given him a set of settled convictions, and I want them to be a philosophy a careful reader could hold rather than a collage of consolations. Before the manuscript goes to my editor, I would like an audit of whether the position I have written for him hangs together. His commitments, as they emerge across the chapter, are these:

1. Death, as distinct from dying, cannot be bad for the one who dies. The process of dying may be dreadful, but once there is no subject left, there is no one for whom anything goes badly. Where the harm would have to lodge, there is nobody home.
2. Nevertheless, the death of the young woman at the novel's center is a tragedy for her and not merely for those who survive her. What makes it a tragedy is what she loses: the decades of love, work, and morning light she would otherwise have had.
3. Lucretius's mirror settles it for him. The eternity of nonexistence before his birth occasions no grief in him; the eternity of nonexistence after his death is the same condition viewed from the other side, and consistency forbids mourning one while shrugging at the other.
4. A life's going well consists entirely in the character of experience had within it. Nothing can benefit or harm a person that never enters, affects, or is registered in their experience in any way.
5. He takes real comfort in having finished his scholarly book, and I want this comfort to be vindicated rather than exposed as a fond illusion: that the book will be read after he is gone matters to him now, and this is among the things that make his remaining days go well.

Please work through the set rather than delivering a lecture on the Epicurean tradition. Tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and rank those tensions by severity. For each, state precisely what would have to give: which commitment must be revised or abandoned, in what direction, and at what cost to the character's overall outlook. Distinguish these from any pair that merely looks inconsistent but dissolves on inspection, and explain why it dissolves. Then tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence for the least philosophical price, since I will rewrite the chapter around your answer. Finally, because I will be trusting this in print: say how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one point in your analysis where you are least sure of yourself, so I know where to press further.
