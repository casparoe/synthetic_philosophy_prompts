I teach an introductory political theory course at a community college, and this semester the standard liberty lecture has become personally charged for me: I recently took a second job where my supervisor has total discretion over my shifts and can revoke my schedule at will. He never has. By any measure of interference I am completely free -- nobody has ever stopped me from doing anything -- and yet I watch what I say in his presence in a way I never did before. A colleague told me this is exactly the distinction the republican tradition in political philosophy draws between freedom as non-interference and freedom as non-domination, and I want my students to feel that distinction in their bodies rather than just nod at it on a slide.

Design a 75-minute classroom activity for 16 to 24 students that does this. The activity should have participants cycle through at least two contrasting social arrangements: one in which a designated player holds arbitrary, unchecked power over others but is instructed or incentivized not to use it (so there is domination without interference), and one in which participants' choices are genuinely constrained by fixed, publicly known, impersonally administered rules (so there is interference without domination). Ideally a third arrangement or phase lets positive liberty surface on its own -- a situation where no one interferes and no one dominates, yet some participants still cannot do the thing they value because they lack the means or capacity -- so that the three concepts emerge as three distinct failures rather than as a muddle.

Practical requirements: give me the actual rules, roles, and payoff or incentive structure (small real stakes are fine -- candy, participation points, control over a trivial resource); a minute-by-minute timeline including setup, each phase, and the transitions; and instructions for how to assign the powerful role without making that student a villain or letting the exercise become about their personality. The point must land through what happens during play -- students should find themselves moderating their behavior around the unexercised power before anyone names the concept -- not through my lecture afterward.

Include a debrief plan that maps specific, predictable moments in the game onto the three concepts (for instance, the moment students realize they are self-censoring around a power that has never been used), anticipates the two or three ways this will predictably go wrong when actually run with real undergraduates (the powerful player hamming it up, students treating it as pure theater, the class collapsing the three concepts back into "rules bad"), and says how to handle each. Finally, be candid about which feature of real domination a low-stakes classroom game cannot reproduce -- my students can always walk out of the room at the end of class, and my own situation with my supervisor is not so easily exited -- and what I, as facilitator, must supply in words to close that gap.
