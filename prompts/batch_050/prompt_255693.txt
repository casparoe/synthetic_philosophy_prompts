I was arguing with a labmate last week and realized we were both taking for granted something I can't actually justify. I work in machine learning, and in our world it's completely standard that you keep a test set locked away and only evaluate your model on it once, at the end -- a model that scores well on data it was trained on proves nothing, because it might just have memorized it. Fine. But then I noticed that physics seems to run on the same norm: general relativity's explanation of Mercury's weird orbit, which Einstein already knew about when he built the theory, gets treated as nice but somehow second-rate compared to the light-bending that Eddington measured in 1919, which nobody had seen before. Journal referees sneer at "post-hoc" explanations, grant panels want "falsifiable predictions," and fitting existing data is basically treated as a lesser achievement even when the fit is spectacular.

Here's what's bugging me. On the simple probabilistic picture of evidence I half-believe, support is just: does the observation come out more likely if the hypothesis is true than if it isn't? And that relation doesn't seem to care about timestamps -- Mercury was already wobbling in 1915, so its probability for Einstein was ~1, and yet it still seems like it should confirm the theory. So my question is: what theory of evidence does this whole practice (novel prediction > accommodation, held-out data > training data) actually presuppose? Does it secretly require some non-probabilistic account of confirmation where the order of events matters? Or can a probabilist reconstruct it -- maybe by reasoning counterfactually about what the theory "would have predicted," or by treating it as a purely pragmatic guard against researchers unconsciously tuning their models to known data rather than a deep fact about evidence?

I'd like you to separate what the practice strictly commits us to from what it merely sits comfortably with, because I suspect it's underdetermined -- maybe several rival epistemologies would license the same norm, and maybe some parts of the practice (like the prestige of the eclipse expedition over the Mercury result) are actually inconsistent with other parts (like the fact that Mercury is still cited as a triumph for GR). Also tell me concretely what would have to change in refereeing or in ML evaluation if we dropped the novelty preference entirely and went purely probabilistic. Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the overall picture you give, and single out the single claim in your answer you're least sure about.
