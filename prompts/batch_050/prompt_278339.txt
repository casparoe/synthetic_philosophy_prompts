I teach philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and for the past year I have also been advising a small robotics startup on a consulting basis. Their engineers keep running into a practical version of what my field calls the frame problem, though they do not know it by that name. Their warehouse robot maintains a world model -- the locations of shelves, the charge on its battery, which doors are open, what is on which pallet -- and every time it acts, the software team has to decide which entries in the model to re-verify. Re-checking everything after every action is computationally ruinous; re-checking nothing produces a robot that confidently drives through a door someone closed five minutes ago. The team's current fix is a hand-maintained list of "things that might have changed," which grows every week and still fails in surprising ways. I want to give them something better than folklore, so I would like you to translate what philosophers and AI researchers actually learned from the frame problem into a procedure an engineer without any philosophy background can follow.

Concretely, I would like a written protocol the team can run whenever they add a new action type to the robot's repertoire: a sequence of questions to ask about the action, in order, that settles which world-model entries must be re-checked after it executes, which can be safely assumed to persist, and which require a standing sensor check rather than a post-action update. The protocol should reflect the real conceptual distinctions the literature settled on -- the difference between an action's direct effects and its downstream ramifications, why you cannot solve the problem by listing everything an action does not change, and what the so-called common-sense law of inertia buys you and where it breaks down. Please be explicit about which steps of the protocol encode a principled answer and which steps are heuristics that will occasionally misfire, and mark the points where the team has to make a judgment call that no procedure can make for them. Where a genuinely satisfactory solution does not exist, say so rather than papering over it.

Then apply the protocol to three action types the team is currently implementing: moving a box from one pallet to another, opening a door to enter a new aisle, and handing a box to a human worker. I would like at least one of these applications to produce an answer the engineers will find inconvenient, since that is usually the sign a procedure is doing real work rather than confirming what people already assumed. Please write the whole thing in American English, and pitch it so that I can hand it directly to the team -- technical enough to be useful, but without assuming they have read Dennett or McCarthy.
