For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mathematics, I need a prediction exercise on Lakatosian themes — specifically, on what should count as a genuine counterexample in mathematics versus a "monster" to be excluded by redefinition. Below are the verdicts my seminar colleague (call her Dr. A) has given on five historical-style cases throughout the term. Each case is described briefly; no outside knowledge beyond the description should be needed.

Case 1 — The hollow cube. The conjecture V − E + F = 2 for polyhedra is threatened by a cube with a cubic tunnel through it, for which V − E + F = 4. Verdict: genuine counterexample. The proof must be reopened, the faulty step located, and the theorem restated so that the counterexample is absorbed (engaging it eventually yields the more general formula relating the Euler characteristic to the number of tunnels).

Case 2 — The picture frame. A frame-shaped object (two joined square rings) also violates V − E + F = 2. Verdict: a monster. It is legitimate to exclude it by tightening the definition of "polyhedron"; incorporating it teaches nothing.

Case 3 — The pathological function. Against the claim that every continuous function is differentiable at some point, a function is constructed that is everywhere continuous and nowhere differentiable, by an infinite recursive procedure. Verdict: genuine refutation. The claim is dead, and it would be cheating to save it by redefining "function" so as to exclude the construction.

Case 4 — The star polyhedra. Certain regular star-shaped solids violate V − E + F = 2. Verdict: not counterexamples at all. They belong to a different theory; restricting the theorem's stated domain is a legitimate adjustment, not an evasion.

Case 5 — The gapped proof. A celebrated proof of a famous conjecture is found to contain a gap: one lemma is established only for a restricted class of cases, though the argument needs it in full generality. The gap is later repaired and the conjecture stands. Verdict: the proof was genuinely refuted — the counterexample to the lemma was real — even though the conjecture itself was untouched.

Next term's case (Case 6): A recent proof of a theorem about a class of topological spaces relies on a lemma about all spaces of a certain kind. A critic constructs, using the axiom of choice, a highly non-explicit space — one that provably cannot be exhibited by any concrete description — in which the lemma fails. However, the main theorem survives intact: the spaces the theorem is actually about are all concretely describable, and an obvious one-line restriction of the lemma's scope repairs the proof. Repairing the proof this way appears to yield no new concepts, no deeper understanding, and no generalization — only a patch. Nobody would have encountered the choice-constructed space for any reason other than to break the lemma.

Please do the following:

(a) Identify at least two general rules, each consistent with all five of Dr. A's verdicts, that she might implicitly be running — for instance, a rule keyed to the counterexample's structural role in the proof (does it threaten a step the proof actually uses?) versus a rule keyed to fruitfulness (does engaging the counterexample improve the theorem or generate new concepts?). State each rule precisely enough that its verdicts on Cases 1–5 can be checked.

(b) State what each rule predicts for Case 6. If the rules diverge, say so explicitly.

(c) Give your best prediction of Dr. A's actual verdict on Case 6, with reasons drawn from the pattern of her earlier verdicts — noting, in particular, how Cases 3 and 4 pull in different directions.

(d) Describe the further case — not Case 6 itself — that would most cleanly discriminate between your candidate rules, and say what each rule would predict about it.

(e) Finally, state what would change your mind about (c): which feature of the track record your prediction leans on most heavily, and what additional verdict or consideration from Dr. A would lead you to predict the opposite verdict on Case 6.
