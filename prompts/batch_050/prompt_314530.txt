I was listening to an episode of a philosophy podcast (one of those big ones that does episodes on "great books") where the host summed up Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind like this, and I wrote it down because it bugged me:

"Ryle's target is the picture we get from Descartes, where every person is a ghostly inner self piloting a bodily machine. Ryle calls this the ghost in the machine and says it rests on a category mistake: treating the mind as the same kind of thing as the body, just made of stranger stuff. His famous example is the visitor who tours the colleges and libraries of Oxford and then asks to be shown the university -- he's looking for one more building when the university was there in the arrangement of what he'd already seen. Ryle's conclusion is that there is no inner realm at all. Words like 'belief,' 'pain,' and 'intention' don't name private inner events; they just describe patterns of actual and possible behavior, so saying you're in pain is really just shorthand for wincing, limping, complaining, and so on. In that sense Ryle is basically a behaviorist, and dualism isn't so much false as meaningless -- mental talk taken literally is nonsense, the way the visitor's question was nonsense. Once you see that, the traditional mind-body problem dissolves rather than gets solved."

Then a friend who's actually read the book told me the host has it half wrong, and she pointed me at this bit from Ryle's introduction:

"A myth is, of course, not a fairy story. It is the presentation of facts belonging to one category in the idioms appropriate to another. To explode a myth is accordingly not to deny the facts but to re-allocate them."
