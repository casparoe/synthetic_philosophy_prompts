Now that I'm retired (thirty-five years as a geologist, mostly petroleum, don't hold it against me) I've finally gotten around to all the philosophy I skipped in college. Problem is it's made me a menace at family dinners. Last week I got into it with my son-in-law, who did a philosophy minor and never lets anyone forget it, over whether "worse than" has to be transitive. I told him I could prove it isn't. He said my conclusion is absurd so I must have a bad premise, but honestly neither of us could find teh leak. Here it is, and I'd like to know where it goes wrong.

Imagine a long series of headaches. H1 is a full-blown migraine, the lie-in-a-dark-room kind. H2 is just barely milder than H1 — so close that nobody who experienced one and then the other could reliably say which was which. H3 is the same tiny amount milder than H2, and so on down to H10,000, which is the faintest little pressure behind one eye, gone the second you start thinking about lunch.

(1) If two experiences are indiscriminable to the person having them — meaning no amount of careful introspection could tell them apart — then neither one is worse than the other for that person. How things are for you is fixed by how they feel to you, and these feel identical.
(2) Each neighboring pair in the series is indiscriminable in that sense. This is just the scientist in me talking: there's a floor on discrimination, same as any instrument has a resolution limit, and below that floor two readings are the same reading.
(3) So from (1) and (2), no headache in the series is worse than its neighbor.
(4) "No worse than" is transitive: if A is no worse than B, and B is no worse than C, then A is no worse than C. I actually checked a logic book on this one.
(5) So H10,000 is no worse than H1 — the barely-there twinge is no worse than the full migraine.
(6) But that's crazy. Anyone would pay good money to trade the migraine for the twinge, and someone who chose the other way round would be making a plain mistake.
(7) Now, I find (6) undeniable, and (2) is just psychophysics, and (1) seems like the merest common sense. So the casualty has to be (4): "no worse than" — and with it "worse than" — is not transitive. You can have A worse than B, and B worse than C, without A being worse than C.

My son-in-law says I've proved too much right there, that transitivity of better and worse is bedrock and any argument against it refutes itself. So where does my argument actually go wrong, if it does? If I'm forced to give something up I'd rather drop (1) than (4), but I want the price spelled out: does denying (1) commit me to saying one pain can genuinely be worse than another even though no subject could ever tell the difference, even in principle? And if someone defends (4) by attacking (2) instead — insisting that in any real series there'd be some step you could actually detect — does that rescue transitivity or just change the subject?

He also mumbled something about "spectrum arguments" and a philosopher named Temkin, so if this is a known beast, tell me what the standard moves are and what each one costs. I don't need citations, just the shape of the options.
