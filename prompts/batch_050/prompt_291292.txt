Exam question: State Robert Nozick's tracking conditions for knowledge. Explain why his account entails the denial of epistemic closure under known entailment, using the example of knowing that you have hands versus knowing that you are not a brain in a vat. Is the denial of closure a defensible feature of the view? Briefly argue.

Student's answer:

Nozick holds that S knows that p if and only if (1) p is true, (2) S believes that p, (3) if S were to believe p, p would not be false, and (4) if p were true, S would believe p. Condition (3), the sensitivity condition, ensures that the belief tracks the truth across nearby possible worlds, while condition (4), the adherence condition, rules out cases where S would fail to believe p even though it is true.

Epistemic closure says that if S knows that p, and knows that p entails q, then S knows that q. Nozick's account denies this principle. Let p = I have hands, and q = I am not a brain in a vat. My belief that I have hands is sensitive: if I did not have hands (for instance, if I had lost them in an accident), I would not believe that I did. But my belief that I am not a brain in a vat fails the adherence condition: if I were a brain in a vat, my experiences would be subjectively identical to my actual experiences, so condition (4) is violated and I do not know q. Since I know p, and know that p entails q, but do not know q, closure fails.

This denial of closure is defensible. By securing knowledge of ordinary propositions like "I have hands," Nozick's account fully refutes Cartesian skepticism, so no further anti-skeptical argument is required. Moreover, closure is only an optional technical principle of epistemic logic, and abandoning it carries no real intuitive cost.
