In Jon Kleinberg, Sendhil Mullainathan, and Manish Raghavan’s Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores, the authors observe:

“First, the algorithmic estimates are often being used as “input” to a larger framework that makes the overall decision — a risk score provided to a human expert in the legal and medical instances, and the output of a machine-learning algorithm provided to a larger advertising platform in the case of Internet ads.”

A colleague argues that this observation undermines the practical importance of the paper’s impossibility theorem. The theorem concerns three properties of a score across two groups: calibration within each group, equal average scores among actual positive cases, and equal average scores among actual negative cases. His argument is:

1. In the relevant systems, a risk score functions only as an input to a larger decision framework.
2. An input can be unfair only by producing or transmitting unfairness to the framework’s final decisions; nobody is harmed merely by the numerical properties of an intermediate score.
3. Therefore, score-level fairness conditions matter only insofar as they constrain the final distribution of decisions and burdens.
4. The two balance conditions concern average scores among actual positives and negatives, not which people are ultimately approved, denied, detained, or released.
5. For any fixed decision policy, either balance condition can be abandoned or modified while leaving the policy’s final decisions unchanged.
6. If the final decisions remain unchanged, no affected person has been treated worse, so the abandoned balance condition cannot ground a fairness objection.
7. Thus the theorem shows only that one cannot exhibit all three score-level metrics simultaneously, not that any actual decision system is unfair.
8. Consequently, the impossibility result imposes no practical constraint on algorithmic decision-making; fairness regulation should concern itself exclusively with final decisions.

This conclusion seems much too strong, but I am unsure where the argument first goes wrong. I would like to retain the claims that final decisions matter most and that fairness criteria must be connected to institutional effects, though not at the price of making score-level properties normatively irrelevant.
