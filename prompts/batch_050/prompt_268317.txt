So I'm in grade 11 and I play a ton of Minecraft, like an embarrassing amount, and I also follow some AI safety stuff on youtube. I got into this rabbit hole about "low-impact AI" -- the idea that you could make an AI safer by penalizing it for changing the world too much, so it does its job but doesn't knock stuff over or grab tons of resources on the way. The obvious problem everyone raises is that "changing the world too much" is doing a LOT of work there, because literally everything the AI does changes the world, and the world changes on its own anyway, and if you penalize all change the AI just does nothing (or worse, it tries to freeze everything in place, which is somehow even worse). The people writing about this say defining "side effects" seems to require already knowing everything about what's good, which defeats the point.

Here's what I actually want. Translate this whole debate into Minecraft/videogame vocabulary, since that's the framework I think in natively. You've got an agent on a server, blocks, mobs, item durability, world ticks, other players, griefing, chests, redstone contraptions that do stuff when you're not looking. So: what does "impact" become -- number of blocks changed relative to spawn? What does the "the world changes anyway" problem become when the sun's going down and mobs spawn whether the agent does anything or not? What does "offsetting" become -- like the agent mining a mountain and then rebuilding it block-for-block so it looks untouched? Take each main idea from the debate (the penalty, the baseline of comparison, the difference between breaking blocks to build a house versus just smashing things, the doing-nothing failure mode) and give me the Minecraft version.

Then the part I care most about: a residue report. What doesn't survive the translation? My gut says Minecraft is TOO clean -- everything's made of discrete blocks so you can count changes exactly, there's no ambiguity about what got broken, and the agent can't be hurt by anything it does so there's no stakes. But maybe that means games are missing something real that the philosophy has, or maybe it means the philosophy problem partly comes from the real world being fuzzy and continuous and the actual puzzle would mostly dissolve in a world made of countable blocks. Lead with your verdict in the first sentence -- like, does the translation mostly work or does it fall apart -- then do the translation, then the residue, no preamble or restating my question back at me.
