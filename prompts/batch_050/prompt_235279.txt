You are assisting me in preparing material for a seminar on the paradoxes of divine attributes. Please compose a piece of sustained satire, roughly 500 words, in the form of an open letter addressed to the philosophical community by an omnipotent being who has grown exasperated with the paradox of the stone — that is, the challenge of whether an omnipotent being can create a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it.

The letter should be written in a tone of dignified grievance, as though from a party weary of centuries of what it regards as frivolous litigation. The humor must arise from the actual dialectic of the debate, not from generic jokes about deities or philosophers. In particular, the letter should engage, directly or obliquely, with the following moves: the traditional position that omnipotence extends only to what is logically possible, so that "a stone too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift" fails to describe any genuine task and inability with respect to it is no limitation; the rival suggestion that an omnipotent being could indeed create such a stone and then lift it anyway, on the grounds that logical impossibility is no bar to unlimited power; and the self-limitation variant, according to which the real puzzle is not stones but whether an omnipotent being could bring it about that it ceases to be omnipotent, for instance by making a binding and irrevocable promise.

Two constraints of internal consistency are essential. First, the author must remain deadpan throughout; the irony should be legible to a reader without any explicit acknowledgment that the letter is satire. Second, the author's complaints should repeatedly, and without its noticing, serve as demonstrations of its omnipotence — the grievances should keep requiring feats that only an omnipotent being could perform — while it never concedes a genuine limitation of its power except where the concession is itself the joke. A reader finishing the piece should feel that the letter has, in passing, reproduced the structure of the underlying philosophical problem rather than merely mocking it.

The letter should be in American English, formal in register, and approximately 500 words. Conclude with a final line beginning "Summary:" followed by a single sentence stating the central comic conceit of the piece.
