I'm drafting the standing criteria for a review board that audits statistical risk models (sentencing, lending, and the like). When a modeler's prior can't be estimated from data, the board has to decide whether the chosen default is defensible, and every camp of Bayesianism sends us a different memo about what "defensible" means. Before I read any more proposals, I want the specification itself. Write the desiderata that a theory of rational default priors must meet to serve this purpose -- adequacy here meaning: good enough that a regulator could invoke it to call one prior choice rational and another a fault, when both modelers had the same data. For each desideratum, give the case or result that motivates it -- the standard trouble cases are fine starting points (partition-dependence of flat priors, Bertrand-style paradoxes where "uniform" depends on the parameterization, van Fraassen's cube-factory problem, the dependence of maximum-entropy answers on the choice of reference measure and constraints, the tension between minimum-relative-entropy updating and ordinary conditionalization) -- but state each desideratum as a requirement, not as a history lesson. Among the desiderata, please include at least these three and say whether each is a genuine requirement or one camp's prejudice: (1) invariance of the warranted prior under reparameterization, (2) invariance under redescriptions that preserve all empirical content (a stronger demand than (1), since one can redescribe without reparameterizing), and (3) uniqueness -- same evidence, same rational prior. Then handle two cases I'm especially unsure about. First, the relabeling problem: a hostile party redescribes the very same physical situation in an equivalent vocabulary, the board's official prior applied to the redescription yields different verdicts on propositions both vocabularies express -- must an adequate theory forbid this, and can any theory actually deliver that? Second, committee parameterization: each auditor on a panel chooses her own parameterization in good faith, applies the same objective rule, and they get different priors -- must an adequate theory say the panel is rationally required to converge on a shared parameterization first, or may it call all the resulting priors rational, and what does each answer cost? Mark every desideratum as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (subjective Bayesian, Jaynesian maximum entropy, reference-prior, imprecise-credence), and identify any subset of desiderata you believe is jointly unsatisfiable given known results -- if the list itself is inconsistent, that's the most important finding and should be argued, not just flagged. Conclude by naming which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and give one paragraph per contender (classical indifference, Jaynesian maxent, reference priors, imprecise credences as a fallback position) on how it fares against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring is secondary.
