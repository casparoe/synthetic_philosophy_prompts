After selling my stake in our family business, I hold a lump sum that must fund my retirement, and my financial advisor keeps reaching for the same analogy: the stock market is a casino in which you, the patient investor, get to play the house. Any single year can go badly, he says, just as the house loses individual bets; but because each bet has a positive expected payoff, the law of large numbers guarantees that staying at the table long enough converts a small edge into near-certain gains. On this basis he recommends that I put essentially everything into a diversified equity portfolio and stop worrying about the order in which good and bad years arrive.

Recently, however, I came across a line of argument — associated with what is called ergodicity economics — that seems to cut against this picture. Consider a gamble in which a fair coin decides each round: heads increases your wealth by 60 percent, tails reduces it by 50 percent. The expected value per round is positive (+5 percent of current wealth), yet because gains and losses compound multiplicatively, a player who keeps flipping almost surely ends up poorer: one heads and one tails, in either order, multiply wealth by 0.8, so the typical trajectory trends downward even as the average across many parallel players grows. The suggestion, as I understand it, is that an investor's life is one such trajectory rather than an average across parallel selves, and that the casino analogy quietly substitutes the second for the first.

Please audit the analogy as an argument for the advice I am being given. I would like an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the house, the individual bets, the house edge, the long run — followed by the points where the mapping breaks down: the difference between additive and multiplicative dynamics, between the ensemble average and the time average, and the presence of absorbing states such as ruin or forced withdrawals during a downturn in retirement. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for my situation, and which are merely incidental? Does the advice to stay invested and let the law of large numbers do its work survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? Finally, if there is a better analogy for what long-run equity investing is like for a single investor with a finite horizon and ongoing withdrawals, please propose it and explain what it gets right that the casino picture does not.
