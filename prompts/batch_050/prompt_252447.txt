In public debate, famines are still routinely pictured through a Malthusian analogy: a famine is like a dinner table with more guests than plates, where drought or blight plays the role of a cook who made too little. Scholars influenced by Sen counter with a different image: a famine is like a market in which some shoppers suddenly find that their wages or assets no longer buy bread, while the shelves remain stocked. As a PhD student in development economics writing a dissertation chapter on the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward, I want to know how much argumentative weight either picture can bear. Please give the correspondence table for each analogy (guests, plates, cook; shoppers, wages, shelves, shopkeeper), identify where each mapping fails, and say what the Malthusian picture quietly imports -- that food availability per head is the operative variable, that starvation falls evenly across a population, that the cause lies in nature rather than in prices, entitlements, or policy. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the natural-disaster framing survive at all, or was the dinner-table image carrying it? And does the stocked-shelves picture overcorrect, given that the Great Leap involved genuine output collapse?
