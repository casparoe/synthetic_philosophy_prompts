Population ethics has been stuck for roughly forty years, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than one more proposed axiology. The passage I keep returning to is in Book IV, Chapter 1 of Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics (I am using the seventh edition, 1907). Asking how far utilitarianism would have us encourage population growth, Sidgwick says the answer is not the point at which average happiness is greatest — he notes that the political economists of Malthus's school assumed it was — but the point at which the product of the number of persons living and their average happiness is greatest. He gives the matter about a page and moves on, evidently treating the question as settled by the principle itself rather than as a hard case for it.

Contrast the field since Parfit's Reasons and Persons (1984). Every proposed axiology implies something its own defenders concede is terrible: the total view yields the Repugnant Conclusion, average and critical-level views have their own horrors, and Gustaf Arrhenius's impossibility theorems show that no axiology can jointly satisfy a list of adequacy conditions each of which looks weak and plausible on its own. The standard responses are by now stock moves: deny the transitivity of 'better than' (Temkin, Rachels), accept the Repugnant Conclusion as a bullet worth biting (Tännsjö, Huemer), or engineer a theory that fails a different condition and then defend that failure as the least costly one. Nobody has converged; the literature's main product is an ever more precise taxonomy of the bullets.

My hypothesis is that the two episodes are not one debate continuing but two different projects under a single name, and that this is why the modern one cannot end. For Sidgwick the population question was a theorem: the principle stood on independent grounds, the answer fell out by arithmetic, and a repugnant-seeming upshot would have counted against the principle, not as a datum about population value. Modern population ethics instead treats intuitions about hypothetical populations as data and axiologies as hypotheses answerable to them; the impossibility theorems then show the data to be jointly inconsistent, which no theory can accommodate and which each camp reads as vindicating its own method — reductio of the data for those who accept the conclusion, proof of indeterminacy or plurality in impersonal betterness for those who keep refining the conditions.

Tell me where this diagnosis is right and where it is wrong, engaging what the participants actually say and do rather than surveying their theories. Three things I want pinned down. First, the Sidgwick reading: am I right that he is deriving rather than consulting intuition, and does anything in his method predict how he would have received the Repugnant Conclusion? Second, what kind of disagreement the current impasse really is — substantive, verbal, methodological, or a collision of bedrock intuitions no side can argue for — and whether the question is ill-posed or merely hard. Third, given your answer, what would count as progress: a new theorem, a conceptual distinction, a change of method, or a principled argument that convergence should not be expected. If you think my reading of Sidgwick collapses the contrast, say so and rebuild the diagnosis without it.
