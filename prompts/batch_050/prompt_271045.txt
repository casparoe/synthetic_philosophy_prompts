In 1980 John Searle published the Chinese Room argument: a monolingual English speaker locked in a room, following a rulebook that tells her how to manipulate Chinese symbols in response to inputs, could produce flawless Chinese conversation without understanding a word of it; therefore, Searle concluded, running the right programme is not sufficient for understanding, and strong AI is false. More than four decades later the argument is still contested, and as far as I can tell nothing has ever moved anyone. The Systems Reply says the understanding belongs to the whole system, not the person in the room; Searle answers that the person could internalise the entire rulebook and still understand nothing. The Robot Reply says embodiment would fix it; Searle says he can imagine the robot's workings running inside him and still finds no understanding. Each side's rejoinders were all in print by the mid-1980s, and the debate has been repeating them ever since -- including now, in arguments about whether large language models understand anything, which seem to be the Chinese Room rerun with the intuitions simply reversed among those who talk to such models daily.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement at all but a clash between two conceptions of what the word 'understanding' must cover: one side takes understanding to be constituted by certain functional and behavioural capacities, so that a system passing every such test just does understand; the other takes understanding to involve something essentially first-personal, so that no third-person evidence -- behavioural, functional, or architectural -- could in principle establish its presence, and its absence in the room is simply obvious. On this hypothesis, Searle's argument is question-begging in one direction and the Systems Reply is question-begging in the other, and the LLM debate inherits the same standoff rather than resolving it, because both sides treat the new systems as fresh evidence for a conclusion they already held. But I am not confident in this diagnosis, and I would like it tested rather than accepted.

So: diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it genuinely verbal -- two senses of 'understanding' talking past each other -- or is there a real issue underneath that survives once the verbal part is stripped away? In particular: does the functionalist have a positive account that actually excludes the first-personal reading, or only a refusal to countenance it; and does Searle's appeal to original intentionality identify a real phenomenon the room lacks, or merely re-describe the intuition that it lacks it? If the impasse is a clash of unarguable starting intuitions, say so and say why neither side can do better. If it is not, say what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves it, an empirical finding about minds or machines that would genuinely bear on it, or a reformulation of the question both sides could accept. Engage with what each side actually claims rather than surveying the literature, and give your honest verdict on whether the debate is stalled because it is ill-posed or merely because it is hard.
