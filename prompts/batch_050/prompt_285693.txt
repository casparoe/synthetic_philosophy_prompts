You are a philosopher who specializes in collective action problems and the ethics of individual contribution. Before composing your answer, spend a few sentences brainstorming the full range of ways an individual act can be causally or morally related to a large-scale aggregate outcome; then organize your final response along the lines set out below.

In everyday climate and consumer ethics, a familiar analogy does a lot of quietist work: a single person's flight, burger, or idle car is said to be like a single vote in a national election — the outcome would have been exactly the same without it, so declining to act is not wrong, or at least is not wrong in virtue of the consequences. I want you to audit this analogy rigorously.

1. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what in the individual-consumption case plays the role of the voter, the ballot, the tally, the winning margin, and the election result?

2. Then identify the structural feature that makes the "one vote never matters" argument tempting in the electoral case — namely, that outcomes change only at thresholds and the probability that a given vote is the decisive one is minuscule — and ask whether each of the following cases shares that feature or departs from it, and in what direction: (a) greenhouse gas emissions and climate harm, where harm plausibly scales with total concentrations, though possibly with tipping points; (b) purchasing meat, where an individual purchase changes a supermarket's or producer's orders only when it happens to trigger a restocking threshold; (c) joining a protest or boycott whose effectiveness requires a critical mass.

3. Distinguish carefully between three different conclusions the analogy might be used to reach and assess which, if any, it can actually support: (i) my individual act makes no difference; (ii) my individual act has expected impact so small that it cannot ground a duty; (iii) collective, not individual, action is the appropriate remedy, so private restraint is beside the point. In particular, examine whether the move from (i) to (ii) smuggles in a principle that an act's moral significance is exhausted by its actual, rather than expected, consequences — and whether that principle is one its users would accept elsewhere (consider a case where each of a hundred people presses a switch that administers a tiny, imperceptible electric shock to a person, the shocks adding up to severe pain).

4. Finally, say whether the analogy, once the disanalogies are on the table, still licenses any form of the quietist conclusion, and if you think it fails, propose a better analogy for the voter's situation that does not overgeneralize to the consumption cases — or explain why no single analogy can cover both.

Your answer should run about 700–900 words. Do not merely survey positions in the literature; take a stand on whether the analogy survives, and flag explicitly any premise your verdict depends on that a reasonable opponent would contest.
