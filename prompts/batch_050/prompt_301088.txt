I am a philosophy professor preparing a sixty-minute seminar for second-year undergraduates in rational choice and formal epistemology. They are comfortable with expected value and elementary probability, but many are algebra-shy and tend to equate rationality with maximising average payoff on a single play. I want to teach the Kelly criterion not as a gambling trick but as a case study in geometric rationality: why, under repetition and compounding, maximising expected log wealth can diverge from maximising expected wealth, and why the usual one-shot decision frame is the wrong lens.

Act as an expert philosopher of probability with extensive experience teaching mathematically nervous humanities students. Think carefully before answering; plan silently, then give me a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. Do not provide citations, readings, or a bibliography. Write in British English.

The seminar should be built around one invented carrying example of your own design: a college Winter Fund that may be repeatedly allocated across a favourable but noisy scheme and a safe but near-flat scheme, with the crucial feature that losses compound multiplicatively and the committee keeps mistaking a positive arithmetic edge for a licence to bet heavily. The example must be vivid enough to carry the whole session, but must not depend on casino lore, real market history, or any niche result the students could not reconstruct from the prompt.

Plan the explanation with explicit dependency structure: what must be established before the words log and growth are allowed to appear; how to move from one-off expectation to trajectories of wealth; where to introduce the distinction between time-average growth and ensemble-average payoff; when the Kelly fraction may be named; and how to state its scope honestly, including estimation error, model uncertainty, ruin as an absorbing boundary, ergodicity worries, and the difference between risk aversion and a change in what is being optimised.

Include a misconception map: for each predictable error, say when in the sequence it will naturally arise and exactly where to head it off. I expect at least: Kelly is a method for winning more often; logarithmic utility is merely squeamishness; a positive-expectation policy cannot be rationally rejected; ruin only matters if wealth literally reaches zero; fractional Kelly is just cowardice; and maximising geometric mean contradicts expected-value reasoning rather than refining its domain. Add two further misconceptions you have actually seen students form.

Finish the plan with: one end-of-seminar check that would show the idea has landed rather than been memorised; one question that reliably exposes students who still think in one-shot terms; and a short list of topics I should openly defer, such as proofs, stochastic-process formalism, and portfolio theory, while explaining why deferring them does not make the session intellectually dishonest. Keep the proposal precise and teachable within the hour.
