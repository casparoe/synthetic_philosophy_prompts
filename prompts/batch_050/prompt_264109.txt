Six months ago I sold the small manufacturing business I had spent fifteen years building, and I now live off a pool of capital rather than a salary. That change has made the ergodicity-economics critique of expected value personally urgent for me: every allocation decision I make is one draw in a long sequence, and I keep encountering arguments about time averages versus ensemble averages that trade on different, usually unstated, standards of what a decision rule is even for. Before I read any further in this literature, I want the specification written down. Write the desiderata for a decision rule governing the allocation of capital across repeated, partially irreversible risks: what any adequate candidate rule must deliver to count as a serious answer. Each desideratum should come with the case or consideration that motivates it — what the rule must say about multiplicative rather than additive wealth dynamics, about absorbing states such as ruin, about when maximizing the ensemble average is nonetheless the correct prescription (pooled risks, insurance), about the difference between a one-off gamble and a sequence of them, about whether the rule may legitimately vary with the agent's circumstances and preferences or must yield a single objective answer, and about whether it must be operable with estimated probabilities rather than known ones — and you should say whether you take each desideratum to be genuinely required or merely a prejudice of one camp. Format the reply as a numbered list of exactly eight items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: items 1 through 6 give the desiderata with their motivating cases; item 7 identifies the single desideratum that excludes the most candidates and names any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, treating the incompatibility itself as a result if you find one; item 8 briefly scores expected-utility maximization, Kelly-style growth-rate optimization, and the time-average approach against the list.
