I teach a philosophy elective for third-year undergraduates, about 24 students, and I have one 75-minute session for a unit on the dark forest hypothesis as an answer to the Fermi paradox. Design an in-class game that makes the students feel why a cosmos full of rational, non-malicious actors could still end up silent and lethal. The anchor text is the passage in chapter 13 of Hobbes's Leviathan where he argues that, under conditions of mutual diffidence, the only reasonable way for anyone to secure himself is anticipation -- getting the upper hand by force or cunning before the other does. Students should have read the chapter, but the game must make them enact its logic rather than recite it: set up rules and a payoff structure (no real money) in which players control civilizations that can choose to broadcast, stay silent, or strike, where detection of another civilization is noisy, intentions cannot be verified, and survival is never jointly guaranteed -- so that the group watches itself slide from cautious silence toward pre-emptive elimination even though no player began hostile. Include: the rules and materials, how many rounds and what changes between them (I want at least one mid-game rule shift that mirrors sudden technological change), a scoring scheme that rewards survival without simply rewarding aggression, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the dark forest reasoning -- the chain of suspicion and the inability to communicate trustworthiness across distance -- and onto Hobbes's anticipation argument, asking students whether the game's outcome was produced by the players' dispositions or by the structure. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., students treating it as a bluffing party game, or one table coordinating its way out of the dilemma) and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which features of the actual hypothesis the game cannot reproduce -- I am thinking of real existential stakes and communication delays measured in centuries -- and what the facilitator must supply by narration instead.
