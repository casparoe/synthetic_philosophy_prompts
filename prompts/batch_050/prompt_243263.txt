I run a two-evening course at a lifelong-learning institute on philosophy for musicians. The second evening is on Schopenhauer's account of music in The World as Will and Representation. The class — about a dozen adults, most of them competent amateur players or choral singers, none with any philosophy beyond general reading — arrives knowing a great deal about harmony and nothing about Kant, and I have ninety minutes.

The centrepiece of the evening is a passage from the supplements to the Third Book, the chapter usually called On the Metaphysics of Music; I am working from the public-domain Haldane and Kemp translation, published as The World as Will and Idea. I paraphrase rather than quote it here: Schopenhauer takes the graduated voices of harmony — the ground bass, moving slowly and ponderously as the lowest grade, rising through the tenor and alto to the soprano that carries the melody — and maps them onto the grades in which the will objectifies itself in nature, from inorganic matter up through plants and animals to the conscious, deliberating, frequently thwarted striving of human life, so that melody's delays, digressions and eventual return to the tonic replay in pure tones the essential structure of willing itself, with all particular circumstance stripped away. The wider claim the passage serves is that music, alone among the arts, is not a copy of the Platonic Ideas through which the will appears in phenomena but a copy of the will as such.

Plan the evening rather than delivering it. Specifically:

First, the order of ideas. What must be in place before the passage can be heard as an argument rather than as a rhapsody — thing-in-itself versus representation, the will, the grades of objectification, the Platonic Ideas as what the other arts copy, aesthetic contemplation as a temporary release from willing — and in what sequence should these be introduced, given the dependencies between them? Which of them can responsibly be treated as a black box for one evening and asserted rather than explained, and which one, if left vague, will quietly ruin everything downstream?

Second, the carrying example. Should the passage's own bass-to-melody analogy carry the evening, or should I build it instead around a specific short piece I could play on a piano — and if the latter, what must that piece have and lack (words, programme, imitative passages, dance rhythm) given Schopenhauer's stated contempt for tone-painting and his insistence that where words and music meet, the music is primary? Say what goes wrong with each of the obvious candidates a room of musicians would suggest.

Third, the misconception map. I predict, in roughly this order: that 'will' means wanting or intending, so the claim becomes that music is about desire; that 'copy' means depiction, so the claim becomes the commonplace that music expresses sadness and joy; that the whole thing is a psychological observation about what listeners feel; and that a melody's return to the tonic makes Schopenhauer an optimist about striving. Tell me where in the sequence each of these takes root, and exactly at which point and with what move I should head each off — particularly the second, since the distance between expressing particular emotions and copying willing itself is the whole evening.

Fourth, the check for understanding: one question I can put to the room in the last ten minutes whose answers would distinguish someone who has grasped the metaphysical claim from someone who has merely been charmed by it.

Fifth, what to leave out and say so. I am minded to exclude Leibniz's remark about music as unconscious arithmetic, the numerological flourishes elsewhere in the chapter, the whole Wagner and Nietzsche reception, and — the hardest call — the apparent tension between this chapter and Schopenhauer's pessimism: if music copies the will, why does hearing it console rather than horrify? Tell me whether that tension should in fact be the evening's final move instead, and if not, how to handle it when a sharp listener raises it.

For each of the five parts, state how confident you are in the sequencing and in the predicted misconceptions, and single out the one point in the whole plan you are least sure of. Please write in British English.
