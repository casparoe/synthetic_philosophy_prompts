I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I sit on our editorial standards committee, which is revising the style guide's rules for contested classifications. Three recurring fights prompted the revision: whether to call the ouster of an elected government a coup when the military's role was ambiguous; whether to describe a synthetic organism as alive when it metabolizes but cannot reproduce unaided; and whether our science desk should keep following the International Astronomical Union's 2006 definition and deny planetary status to borderline bodies our readers intuitively regard as planets.

The committee has deadlocked between two proposed policies. The first would have us adopt, for each contested term, a single operational standard -- explicit criteria with definite cutoffs -- and apply it uniformly, accepting that some cases will fall a hair on the wrong side of a line that could have been drawn slightly differently. Its defenders argue that a sharp boundary is what readers are owed: consistency across desks, resistance to pressure from interested parties, and the honest admission that any usable rule produces hard cases. The second policy would treat genuinely borderline cases as indeterminate: where the facts fail to settle the term under any reasonable sharpening of it (or settle it under all of them), the magazine should refuse the predicate altogether and write descriptively instead -- reporting what happened, what the thing does, and letting contested labels appear only in attributed quotation. Its defenders argue that issuing a sharp verdict in a genuinely borderline case asserts something that is neither true nor false, and that a news organization should not pretend otherwise.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before next month's meeting. It should recommend one of the two policies, or a precisely specified hybrid, with criteria the copy desk could actually apply at deadline, and it should give an honest account of the strongest objection to whatever you recommend -- whether that is the arbitrariness of any cutoff, the regress problem of deciding where the borderline zone itself begins, or some difficulty you consider harder still.
