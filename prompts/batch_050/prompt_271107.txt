I spent forty years as an organic chemist and now that I'm retired I've gotten hooked on archaeology books, and I keep running into the same worry: how do you get from pots and bones to what people actually believed? I came across Hawkes' old ladder of inference somewhere, the idea that reconstructing technology is easy, subsistence and economy harder, social institutions harder still, and religious belief nearly impossible, and I'd like to know how the field actually sorts itself on this question. Write me a decision tree or taxonomy of the positions on how far beliefs and institutions can be inferred from material remains. Each fork should be phrased as a question someone could answer without knowing the jargon, along lines like: does any inference from objects to beliefs require analogy with a living or historically documented culture, is the gap between stuff and belief just insufficient evidence or a barrier in principle, and can we ever correct for the fact that only some practices leave traces at all. The leaves should land on real stances (optimistic processualist-style reconstruction, a pessimistic reading of Hawkes, hermeneutic or post-processualist approaches, and whatever else genuinely exists), plus at least one combination of answers nobody seems to defend, with a sentence on whether that seat is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Test the tree against these cases as you build it: grave goods in a culture with no written record (afterlife belief or wealth display?), a pit full of deliberately smashed figurines, and a building aligned to the winter solstice sunrise. Flag any pair of positions your tree can't actually seperate. And please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just walk me through the forks in order.
