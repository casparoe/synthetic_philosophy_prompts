I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, approaching my third year, and I need to settle on a dissertation agenda in the next few months. My intended topic is Brandom-style inferentialism: the programme that treats the meaning of a sentence as constituted by its inferential role — by what commitments it licenses and what commitments license it — rather than by its truth conditions or its representational links to the world. I have worked through the core apparatus (normative pragmatics, deontic scorekeeping, material inference, logical expressivism) and I am convinced the programme is worth taking seriously, but I am struggling to identify which of its open problems would repay three years of full-time work, and in which order I should attempt them.

The candidate problems I keep circling include: whether inferential role semantics can be made compositional at the subsentential level without collapsing back into truth-conditional semantics; how the holism threatened by role-individuation can be tamed enough to permit genuine disagreement between speakers with different inferential habits; the status of singular terms and anaphora, where Brandom's own treatment is intricate and arguably the least developed part of the edifice; how perceptual and non-inferential observational content is to be integrated without smuggling in the representationalism the programme opposes; whether the account generalises beyond assertion to imperatives, questions, and other speech acts; and whether recent work on large language models — systems trained on inference-like relations over text with no worldly embedding — gives inferentialism new empirical traction or, conversely, exposes its blind spots.

What I want from you is not a survey of these problems but a defended agenda. Identify which open problem (or problems, including any I have omitted) deserves to be upstream of the others: which questions must be answered before the answers to the rest even become well-posed, and which are downstream refinements. For each item in your ordering, give the reasoning explicitly: the criteria you are applying (foundational load, tractability for a single philosopher with three years and no laboratory, neglectedness relative to the existing literature, and how much of the programme stands or falls with it), the trade-offs between those criteria where they pull apart, and what specific result or development — a published objection, a formal result, an empirical finding from AI — would cause you to move an item up or down the list. Where two candidate problems are entangled, say precisely how, and whether the entanglement means they should be attacked jointly or sequenced.

Please also flag any item on my list that you think is overrated as a dissertation topic — either because the hard work has effectively already been done, or because it resists progress by a lone philosopher and would only yield to collaborative or interdisciplinary effort. I would rather be told this now than in my third-year review. The deliverable should read like advice from a sceptical but sympathetic external examiner, not like a literature overview.
