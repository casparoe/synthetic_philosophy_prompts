I teach a philosophy of law seminar on transitional justice, and a colleague has pressed an argument that appears to invalidate not only South Africa’s amnesty-for-disclosure mechanism, but almost any institutional practice in which leniency is exchanged for testimony. The argument concerns cases of grave wrongdoing for which the surviving evidence is fragmentary, records have been deliberately destroyed, and several participants have incentives to minimize their own responsibility. Assume that a commission may grant immunity only after concluding that an applicant has fully disclosed the relevant facts. My colleague reasons as follows: (1) As a truth-producing bargain, immunity is rational only if the applicant’s testimony has counterfactual epistemic value: it establishes facts that the state could not practicably establish without the testimony. (2) A commission is rational to rely on such testimony only if its material claims are corroborated by evidence independent of that testimony. (3) Evidence is genuinely independent only if the commission could have obtained it even if the testimony had never been given. (4) If the corroborating evidence could have been obtained without the testimony, then the facts it establishes could have been established without the testimony. (5) Consequently, the testimony either lacks independent corroboration and is unreliable, or has independent corroboration and lacks counterfactual epistemic value. (6) No testimony can satisfy both conditions for a rational truth-for-immunity bargain. (7) Conditional amnesty is therefore incoherent: the state should prosecute where independent evidence is available and refuse to bargain where it is not.

The conclusion seems to prove too much. It would apparently condemn accomplice immunity, plea bargaining, sentence reductions for confession, and many historical inquiries that begin with incomplete evidence. Yet each premise reflects a plausible concern: states should not purchase information they already possess, perpetrators’ testimony should not be accepted merely because it is detailed, and a commission cannot verify full disclosure by relying entirely on the disclosure itself. The comparison cases also pull in different directions. South Africa treated individualized disclosure as a condition of amnesty; Argentina’s later prosecutions suggest that truth need not permanently replace punishment; Rwanda’s confession-based proceedings treated admission less as the purchase of wholly new truth than as a basis for mitigation and communal acknowledgment.

Which premise or inference should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Please identify the exact point of failure rather than answering that context matters. Explain why the troublesome step is especially attractive in transitional justice, where corroboration is scarce and perpetrators may be the best witnesses. Then compare the costs of three repairs: retaining conditional amnesty while accepting partial or mutually reinforcing corroboration; replacing immunity with sentence mitigation; or abandoning evidentiary bargains in favor of ordinary investigation and later prosecution. Finally, say whether your diagnosis applies equally to ordinary plea bargaining, or whether mass violence differs because responsibility is collective, evidence has often been systematically destroyed, and public acknowledgment may have value beyond the discovery of previously unknown facts.
