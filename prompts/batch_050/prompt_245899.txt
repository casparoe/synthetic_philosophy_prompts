And here's the student's answer:

"Dennett says there are three strategies we can use to predict the behaviour of a system. The physical stance predicts from the system's actual physical makeup plus the laws of physics — if you knew the exact state of every molecule in a thermostat you could in principle work out what it will do next, but this is usually impractical. The design stance predicts by assuming the system will do what it was designed to do: I predict the thermostat will switch on when the room gets cold because that's its function, without knowing anything about its wiring. The intentional stance goes a step further and treats the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires, predicting that it will do what it rationally ought to do given those. So I predict the chess computer will move its queen because it 'wants' to win and 'believes' the queen move is best. Basically the intentional stance is just the design stance for cases where we don't yet know the design: once we fully understood the brain's wiring, we could drop talk of beliefs and desires and the intentional stance would have no further work to do.

A 'real pattern,' for Dennett, is a pattern that is genuinely there in the world and that we need for prediction or compression — his example is the stripes in a binary image, where some patterns can be described compactly and others can't be captured any more simply than by listing every pixel. Since patterns only count as real relative to someone who finds them useful, Dennett ends up an instrumentalist: beliefs are nothing more than what the intentional stance attributes to us, useful fictions like centers of gravity. So he is not a realist about beliefs — he's closer to the eliminativists, except he thinks the fiction is too handy to give up."
