I've spent enough time on rationalist blogs to have seen the declinist thesis -- that the world has grown less violent over the centuries -- both championed and torn apart, and I'm still not sure the two sides are even arguing about the same thing. Write a short dialogue between a Declinist and a Skeptic on this question. The Declinist defends the per-capita picture: homicide rates, battle deaths, and everyday cruelty have fallen over long stretches of history, so a randomly chosen person today faces less risk of violence than almost any ancestor. The Skeptic presses two points: first, that absolute numbers and tail risks matter too -- a single nuclear exchange could undo centuries of decline in an afternoon, and a rate-based metric hides that; second, that "violence" is partly a bookkeeping choice, since whether we count structural harms and coercion or only direct physical assault changes the answer, making the thesis look less like a discovery than a definition. Both characters should be equally competent and should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past each other. Close the dialogue with each character stating, in a sentence or two, which observation or argument would most change their mind -- the Skeptic naming what would convince them the decline is real and robust, the Declinist naming what would convince them the whole framing has been misleading.
