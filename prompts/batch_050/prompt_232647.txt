So I run a little philosophy-of-logic seminar series at my uni, and the next speaker is defending dialetheism -- the view that some contradictions are actually true. He's sharp, and the last time someone interviewed him he basically spent forty minutes re-explaining the liar paradox to a nodding host. I don't want that. Draft me 8-10 interview questions that assume the audience already gets the liar setup and push on the parts of the view that are actually load-bearing and weird. Specifically I want at least one question on each of these:

- how his paraconsistent logic blocks explosion without just refusing to ever use disjunctive syllogism, since we clearly reason with it fine all the time in math and daily life
- the "you can't even say what you reject" problem, i.e., if he says the liar sentence is both true and false, how does he express the difference between sentences that are just false and ones that are both, without the distinction collapsing
- Curry's paradox and whether the same fix he uses for the liar generalizes, or whether taming Curry costs him something the liar didn't
- what his view implies about rational belief -- should we believe true contradictions, and if so how he avoids trivialism, the view that everything is true, since that's the boogeyman he keeps getting lumped in with
- and one closing question about what observation or argument could actually convince him dialetheism is wrong, since people suspect the view is unfalsifiable

For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give (e.g., if he says disjunctive syllogism is only *classically* valid but fails in inconsistent contexts, press him on how we know which context we're in before we've settled which contradictions are true). Keep the tone probing but friendly -- he's a guest, not a defendant.
