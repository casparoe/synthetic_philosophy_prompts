I'm a PhD student in metaphysics and next month I'm giving a departmental colloquium defending my answer to Bradley's regress. My line, roughly: the regress of relations is only vicious if you think a relation's holding between things itself needs a further fact to bind the relation to its relata, and I argue that demand comes from treating "being related" as just another constituent that needs gluing. So my positive view is that relational facts are metaphysically fundamental and don't require any internal connection between the relation and what it relates -- the regress stops not because we found the right glue but because the request for glue was confused.

The audience is mostly analytic metaphysicians (a few grounding-and-fundamentality people, one deflationist about ontology who thinks most of these debates are verbal), plus two philosophers of language who are pretty orthodox Fregeans about the unity of the proposition, and a historian who works on Bradley himself and will definately push back on my reading. No one there works on truthmakers specifically.

Put together my Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections this particular crowd will actually raise, phrased the way they'd phrase them (the Fregeans will come at it as a unity-of-the-proposition problem, not as a regress, and the historian will say I've got Bradley wrong), ranked by how much trouble they'd cause me, each with a short reply I'd give and a note on where that reply is weakest. I also want to know what to concede up front to the deflationist so I don't lose the room early, and which question I'm hoping nobody asks.
