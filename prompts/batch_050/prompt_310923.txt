A colleague walked me through the following argument, and although each premise seems right as I read it, the conclusion strikes me as intolerable. Six strangers will die unless I act, and I can save either one of them (call her A) or the other five, but not all six.

(1) The only losses at stake are the losses of the six individuals; there is no further party — the group, the world — who stands to lose anything.
(2) Each of the six stands to lose the same thing, the remainder of her life, and a person's loss is not made greater or smaller for her by how many others share her fate: death is not worse for someone because it has company.
(3) One outcome can be worse than another only if it is worse for someone; betterness and worseness are always betterness or worseness from someone's point of view.
(4) The outcome in which the five die is worse for each of the five than the alternative, but only to the same degree that the outcome in which A dies is worse for A; no one in either outcome loses more than someone loses in the other.
(5) Losses borne by different people cannot be pooled into a single larger loss, because there is no one who bears the pool.
(6) So neither outcome is worse than the other, and I do nothing wrong if I choose between them arbitrarily — by coin toss, or simply by saving A because I happen to know her face.
(7) Nothing in this reasoning turns on the particular numbers; it applies equally when the second group is fifty, or five million, or everyone else alive.
(8) So it would be no worse, and no more wrong of me, to let everyone else on Earth die in order to save one stranger.

I would like to keep (2) and (5) if I can — the denial of a super-subject who suffers aggregated losses seems to me the deepest thing in the argument. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the main positions in this debate divide over which step to surrender, identify each position with its step and say what each must swallow.
