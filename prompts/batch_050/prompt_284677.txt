Act as a philosopher of mind with expertise in the nature of experience and consciousness. I want you to treat the following as an exercise in reflective equilibrium, not as a literature summary: start by privately brainstorming several candidate principles that could unify my verdicts, then present the strongest one (or two) in your answer.

Here are my considered verdicts on a set of cases about dreaming, and I want to know what principle about experience underlies them, if any does.

1. An ordinary dream, vividly recalled on waking and reported immediately: I am confident this was an experience had during sleep; there was something it was like for the dreamer while it happened.

2. A dream that is completely forgotten within minutes of waking, recoverable only because a diary prompt the next evening brings it back: I still think it was an experience during sleep, merely an unaccessed one. Forgetting does not retroactively annihilate experience.

3. A sleeper in a lab, sprayed with water during REM, wakes seconds later and reports a dream about a waterfall whose narrative seems to build toward the splash. I think the dream experience unfolded during sleep and incorporated the stimulus in something like real time; I am not tempted by the idea that the whole story was assembled at the moment of waking.

4. A sleeper woken from deep, non-REM sleep who confidently narrates an elaborate dream, though nothing in the preceding hour gives independent reason to think any mentation occurred: I think this report may well have been confabulated on waking, in which case there was no experience during sleep at all -- only a memory-like state produced at awakening.

5. A lucid dream in which the dreamer, aware that she is dreaming, deliberately performs planned eye movements to signal experimenters: the clearest case of all, I think -- an ongoing experience during sleep, complete with agency.

6. A person who wakes every morning with the bare conviction of having dreamt but never any content: I genuinely do not know whether there was experience there, and I suspect the question may have no answer.

Now, the tensions I can already feel: verdicts 2 and 4 sit awkwardly together -- in 2 I let experience float free of any waking report, but in 4 I let the absence of supporting evidence count against there having been an experience. And verdicts 3 and 4 pull against each other too: if a whole narrative can be assembled at waking in 4, why not in 3? Meanwhile verdict 6 suggests I am committed to facts about experience possibly outrunning all possible evidence, which Malcolm-style skeptics about dreams as experiences would pounce on.

Your task: state, precisely, the best principle (or small set of competing principles) about what makes a dream an experience that captures as many of these verdicts as possible -- for instance, one that leans on the functional role of sleep-stage mentation versus one that leans on the availability of the content to later systems. For each clause of your principle, say which case motivates it. Then tell me which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and must overturn, and defend that sacrifice. Finally, apply your principle to two cases I did not list: (a) a dream in a dreamless-sleep-stage whose report is later shown to be borrowed wholesale from a story the person read before bed, and (b) a non-human animal displaying REM and dream-typical behavior but incapable of any report.

Keep the whole response under 700 words. Do not summarize the historical debate between Malcolm and Dennett except where it directly sharpens the principle; I want the principle, the casualties, and the two new verdicts.
