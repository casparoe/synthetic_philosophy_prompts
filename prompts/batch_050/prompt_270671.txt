**Money as Idea: Was Berkeley the First Idealist Economist?**

Most people know Berkeley for claiming that the being of physical things consists in being perceived. Fewer people know that late in life he got deeply interested in monetary policy. In The Querist, written in the 1730s while he was back in Ireland, he argued (in a long series of questions rather than assertions) that money is fundamentally a ticket or counter: its value comes not from the metal it's stamped on but from the credit, convention, and general acceptance behind it. On this view, a nation's wealth isn't its pile of gold but the industry and labor that money *mobilizes*.

Here's my thesis: this isn't a quirky side interest. It's actually the purest application of Berkeley's idealism. If even trees and tables only exist as bundles of perception sustained by minds, then money is the idealist entity par excellence. A gold coin is matter pretending to be value; a paper note is honest about what money always was -- a shared idea, a token whose power comes from collective acceptance. Berkeley, I want to say, didn't just happen to hold a nominalist theory of money. His whole metaphysics *predicts* it. Money's esse really is percipi, more literally than any stone's.

Now, I should flag my own worry: as far as I know, Berkeley never explicitly draws this connection himself. He doesn't say "money proves my idealism." So there's a risk I'm retrofitting a tidy narrative onto two parts of his thought that he kept in separate drawers. Maybe his monetary views came from practical Irish economic misery, not from metaphysics at all.

Finally, the modern payoff. Fiat currency, and even more so the numbers in your bank's database, look a lot more like Berkeley's counters than like anything a metallist would recognize. When people freak out that modern money is "just made up," a Berkeleyan can shrug: it was always just made up, and gold only ever borrowed its authority from the same collective trust that paper now holds directly.
