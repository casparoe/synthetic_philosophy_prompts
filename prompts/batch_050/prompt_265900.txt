I am a PhD student working on the border of epistemology and philosophy of language, and my dissertation chapter on Moore's paradox has turned into a source of low-grade despair, because the literature appears to have converged on the data and diverged on nothing else. Everyone accepts the core observations: present-tense, first-person sentences of the form "p, but I do not believe that p" and "p, but I believe that not-p" are deeply defective to assert even though they can plainly be true; the defect vanishes when you shift to the past tense (it was raining, but I did not believe it), to the third person (it is raining, but she does not believe it), or into contexts of supposition and embedding. And yet the field is split between at least two explanatory strategies that have been restating themselves at each other for decades. Belief-first accounts locate the absurdity in the state of mind: a Moore-paradoxical belief is already self-undermining, and the defective utterance merely inherits that defect because assertion expresses belief. Assertion-first accounts locate it in the speech act: assertion is constitutively governed by a norm (assert only what you know, or only what you believe, or only what is true), the utterance violates the norm, and any oddness in the corresponding belief is derivative or nonexistent. Each side has by now answered the other's standard objections, and neither has gained ground.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about Moorean sentences at all but about what an explanation of a linguistic-cum-epistemic phenomenon is answerable to. The assertion-first camp treats the pattern of felicity judgments -- the tense, person, and embedding asymmetries -- as the fixed explanandum and any account that predicts it as adequate. The belief-first camp treats a constitutive thesis about the relation between assertion and belief as non-negotiable, and regards any account that severs them as having changed the subject, even if it fits the utterance data. If that is right, the two camps are not disagreeing about the paradox; they are operating with different success conditions, and each camp's victories are invisible to the other.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey. Is my hypothesis correct, or does the disagreement have some other structure: a genuine dispute about the data (for instance, whether the commissive form really is as defective as the omissive form, or whether embedded Moorean sentences are felicitous); a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work (say, about the semantics of belief attribution or the psychology of self-ascription); or a bedrock intuition about the priority of thought over speech that neither side can argue for without circularity? Please support the diagnosis with what the two camps actually say and concede. And tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress -- a new datum, a distinction between kinds of defect, a criterion for adjudicating constitutive claims about speech acts -- or argue honestly that nothing would, because the question as posed has no fact of the matter behind it.
