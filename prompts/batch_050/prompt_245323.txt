So I host a small econ-and-philosophy podcast (emphasis on small) and next month I'm interviewing a political economist whose new book revives Lange-style market socialism for the cloud-computing era. His pitch, roughly: Lange's 1930s proposal that a Central Planning Board could set prices by trial and error was laughed at as computationally absurd, but now that Walmart-scale logistics and machine learning exist, Hayek's knowledge objection has been overtaken by technology. Trial and error is what recommendation engines do all day.

I want to skip the greatest hits of the socialist calculation debate and press where the view is actually weakest. Draft me 8-10 interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. Requirements:

- One question has to be built around this passage from Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), which I plan to read aloud on air: "The various ways in which the knowledge on which people base their plans is communicated to them is the crucial problem for any theory explaining the economic process, and the problem of what is the best way of utilizing knowledge initially dispersed among all the people is at least one of the main problems of economic policy—or of designing an efficient economic system." Ask him whether that framing — the economic problem as a problem of *conveying* knowledge — actually plays into his hands, since moving information around is exactly what the internet is good at, or whether it smuggles in the assumption that the relevant knowledge is the conveyable kind. I need a follow-up ready for both directions he could jump.
- At least one question on tacit knowledge: the stuff a shop-floor worker or a small supplier knows but couldn't write down or upload even if they wanted to. Can sensors and ML really extract what the knower herself can't articulate? He'll probably say modern firms already run on exactly this extraction, so have a follow-up for that.
- At least one question on the difference between Lange's parametric prices and real entrepreneurship — in the model, firm managers just react to prices and accounting rules, but who proposes the new product, the weird new process, the bet against the current price structure, when the state owns the firms?
- At least one question separating the knowledge problem from the incentive problem (soft budget constraints, managers who can't really fail), since he'll definately want to run those together and I want them kept apart.
- And end with a question about what he would accept as evidence against the view — what would have to be true of an actual market-socialist or heavily planned experiment for him to concede the knowledge objection still bites.

No softballs, nothing about what drew him to the subject, and don't let him hide behind "that's an engineering problem" without the follow-up pinning down why not.

One more thing: at the end, tell me how confident you are overall that the replies you've scripted for him are what a sharp modern market socialist would actually say, and single out the one question where you're least sure you've anticipated his response correctly.
