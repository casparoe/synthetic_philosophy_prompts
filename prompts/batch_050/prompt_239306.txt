ok so my roommate is writing his MA thesis on the doing/allowing distinction and last night he cornered me with an argument that he says dissolves the whole thing. I couldn't find the hole and it's bugging me, because the conclusion is obviously crazy but every step looks fine when he's talking. Here it is roughly as he gave it:

(1) If the doing/allowing distinction matters morally, then there have to be pairs of cases that differ ONLY in whether the harm is done or allowed, but where what's permissible differs between the two.
(2) The famous candidate pairs don't deliver this. Smith sneaks into the bathroom and holds his cousin's head under the water; Jones sneaks in planning to do the same but finds the cousin has slipped and is drowning on his own, so Jones just stands there and watches. Same motive (the inheritance), same intention, same outcome. Both are monsters, and equally. So the distinction does no moral work even in its best-case scenario.
(3) And it gets worse: whether something counts as "doing" or "allowing" is just a matter of how you describe it. The guy who pulls the plug is "failing to keep the patient alive." The lifeguard who doesn't move is "actively keeping his body in the chair" while the kid drowns. If permissibility can't vary with our choice of words, and the words are all that's separating doing from allowing, then permissibility can't track doing vs allowing.
(4) What permissibility actually tracks is the agent's reasons and intentions. That's why Smith and Jones come out equal — not because they both "did" something, but because they both wanted the kid dead for the money.
(5) So the doing/allowing distinction is morally irrelevant, full stop.
(6) Which means the transplant surgeon case is easy: killing one healthy patient to save five is permissible, since with the distinction gone the only live consideration is the numbers, and five beats one.

I refuse to accept (6), and honestly I don't want to give up (1) either. If I have to lose something I'd rather lose (2) — the Smith/Jones verdict has always felt a little cooked to me — but I can't articulate why, and my roommate says I'm just squeamish. Which step actually goes wrong (or which premise is cheapest to drop if none of them "go wrong")? And when you diagnose it, can you also price the main positions people take here — like, what do the people who defend the distinction as tracking rights (the Foot/Thomson style view) have to say about (3)'s redescription move, what does someone like Bennett who wants a cleaner positive-vs-negative-relevance criterion gain and lose, and what does it cost the Rachels-style consequentialist to just bite the bullet and accept (5)? I want to be able to push back on him without getting caught agreeing that word choice can't change what's permissible.
