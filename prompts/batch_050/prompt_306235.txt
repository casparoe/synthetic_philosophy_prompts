My mother passed away eight months ago, and a few weeks ago a startup contacted our family offering to build what they call a "memorial companion" — a conversational model trained on her text messages, emails, voicemails, and video recordings from family gatherings. My siblings are split, and I've been trying to work out my own position with the help of some of the philosophical writing on so-called griefbots. But I keep running into what seems to me like a basic difficulty that nobody I read addresses head-on.

As I understand it, an ethical objection has to identify someone who is wronged or harmed. But my mother no longer exists — she cannot be distressed by the simulation, cannot feel misrepresented, cannot suffer indignity. Whatever the chatbot says, it says to us, the living, and we are all adults using the service voluntarily and with full knowledge that it is a statistical reconstruction rather than a channel to her. If the simulation turned out to be badly inaccurate, that would be a product defect, not a moral wrong; and if it is accurate, then talking to it seems morally on par with rereading her letters or watching old home videos, which nobody finds ethically troubling. Either way, I can't locate a victim.

So my question is this: given that the deceased cannot be harmed and the bereaved are consenting users, why do ethicists treat griefbots as raising genuine moral problems at all, rather than merely aesthetic or psychological ones about taste and healthy grieving? Is there some party or interest I'm failing to count, or is the philosophical concern really just disguised discomfort with an unfamiliar technology — and if the latter, shouldn't we expect it to fade the way unease about photography or recorded music faded?
