I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past year, rather late in life, working through the philosophy of science. In a popular book on the limits of machine learning I encountered the following summary of the no-free-lunch theorems (the results of Wolpert and Macready from the 1990s), and I would like an expert audit of it. The author writes:

"The no-free-lunch theorems prove that all learning algorithms perform equally well. A state-of-the-art neural network, linear regression, and a strategy of guessing completely at random are, mathematically speaking, exactly on a par; none can outperform any other. It follows that the spectacular success of induction in the actual sciences is a stroke of sheer luck -- we happen to live in a universe where our preferred methods work, and there was no reason whatsoever to expect this. In this way the theorems provide, at last, a rigorous mathematical proof of Hume's conclusion that induction is unjustifiable, and they further establish that our preference for simple theories, Occam's razor, is an arbitrary prejudice with no rational standing."

My instinct, formed over forty years of watching simple theories succeed in the laboratory, is that the book has somewhere overshot what the mathematics licenses -- but I cannot locate the precise point of slippage, and I am genuinely unsure how much of the pessimism survives a correct statement of the results.
