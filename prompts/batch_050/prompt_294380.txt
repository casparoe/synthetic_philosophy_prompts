I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind writing a dissertation on the nature of agency, and I have run into a puzzle about a senior colleague whose judgment I generally respect. She has a standing habit of issuing quick verdicts on whether particular systems are agents, and I have been keeping an informal tally because I suspect she is running a consistent principle that she has never articulated. Her verdicts so far: an ordinary household thermostat — not an agent; a robotic vacuum that bumps around a room — not an agent; a honeybee navigating back to its hive — an agent; a classical chess engine that searches a game tree — an agent; a reinforcement-learning system trained to play video games from raw pixels — an agent; and the global economy, despite its impressive adaptive complexity — not an agent, on the grounds that, as she puts it, nothing is at the helm and no end is being pursued. Last week, over coffee, I asked her about a current large language model — specifically a base model with no tools, no memory across sessions, no reinforcement fine-tuning, generating a single long chain-of-thought response to a hard problem, in the course of which it sets itself subgoals, backtracks when a line of reasoning fails, and reorganizes its approach. She hesitated, said she needed to think, and I have a bet with another student about what she will say.

Please answer as an expert in philosophy of mind and cognitive science who is familiar with the contemporary debate about whether language models are agents, simulators, or something else entirely. Think carefully rather than reaching for a reflexive answer, and begin by laying out the candidate principles before committing to one. What I want, in order: first, at least two principles that are each fully consistent with all six of her earlier verdicts, stated precisely enough that they could in principle disagree about something — for example, one might suspect she is running something like a flexible-goal-directed-pursuit criterion in the spirit of the intentional stance, or something like a selection-history criterion on which genuine goals must be the product of evolution, design, or reinforcement training rather than of training to imitate goal-directed behavior, but do not assume these are the only options if you can construct a better-fitting third. Second, show that these principles diverge on the base language model case I described, and explain what each one predicts and why — including whether either principle would classify the model as a simulator of agents rather than an agent, and what work that distinction is doing. Third, give me your best prediction of her actual verdict, with reasons grounded in the texture of her earlier calls (the beehive-like cases she accepted and rejected seem especially diagnostic to me, but tell me if I am wrong about which cases carry the evidential weight). Fourth, if the track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so honestly rather than forcing a prediction, and describe the one further case — a real or realistically near-future system — whose verdict would most efficiently discriminate between the surviving principles. I am less interested in whether the language model is really an agent than in whether my colleague has a stable implicit theory at all, so if your analysis suggests her verdicts are better explained by an unprincipled cluster of intuitions than by any criterion, that is a finding I want to hear argued rather than papered over.
