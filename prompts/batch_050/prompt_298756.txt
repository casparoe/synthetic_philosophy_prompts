I work in insurance -- I'm a claims adjuster at a mid-size property & casualty outfit, going on twelve years now, so my whole professional brain runs on indemnity, liability, and who owes what to whom. At a barbeque last month a friend of mine who does political philosophy got on the topic of reparations for slavery and colonialism, and when I pushed back with the usual stuff (everyone involved is dead, the payers never did anything, how would you even calculate it) she later emailed me a chunk of Locke's Second Treatise of Government, from the chapter Of Conquest, with the note "this is why your 'claims expire' instinct is wrong":

"Should a robber break into my house, and with a dagger at my throat make me seal deeds to convey my estate to him, would this give him any title? Just such a title, by his sword, has an unjust conqueror, who forces me into submission. ... What is my remedy against a robber, that so broke into my house? Appeal to the law for justice. But perhaps justice is denied, or I am crippled and cannot stir, robbed and have not the means to do it. ... But my son, when able, may seek the relief of the law, which I am denied: he or his son may renew his appeal, till he recover his right. But the conquered, or their children, have no court, no arbitrator on earth to appeal to."

Its been bugging me ever since, so here's what I actually want. Restate Locke's argument in this passage, and the modern case for reparations for historical injustice that she says grows out of it, in the vocabulary I use at work every day -- first-party vs. third-party claims, making the claimant whole, subrogation, wrongdoer-pays vs. spreading a loss across the pool, limitation periods and repose, successor liability when the defendant entity has been merged or dissolved, claims with no solvent defendant, moral hazard. Do it as faithfully as you can manage. Don't sand down Locke's edges so he fits my filing system; if he says something my field has no category for, I want to hear that.

Then give me the residue report, which is honestly the part I care about most. Which moves in the reparations debate translate cleanly into claims language -- my guess is the robber-with-a-deed bit maps onto something like voiding a conveyance procured under duress -- which translate only with distortion (claims lodged on behalf of claimants dead for a century; premiums effectively charged to people who never wronged anyone), and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. On that last bucket: my friend keeps saying reparations are partly about acknowledgment, not just compensation, and there's no line item for that anywhere in my world. Tell me straight whether that gap means my professional vocabulary is missing something real, or whether it's a sign that part of the debate can't be cashed out as any concrete demand at all. And one more thing: does Locke's version survive the translation better or worse than the modern one? He's at least talking about a specific deed, a specific robber, a specific son -- that reads like a well-pleaded complaint. The modern argument starts from aggregate historical wrongs and sounds more like a class action where half the class is dead and the defendant is a successor entity. Be blunt about which version you'd rather defend.
