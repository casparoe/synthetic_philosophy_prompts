I write about statistical reasoning for a general-audience science magazine, and I am trying to settle a dispute I created while reporting a piece on why scientists disagree. A Bayesian statistician I interviewed gave me a series of verdicts on when two investigators with different prior beliefs will end up agreeing:

- Case 1: Two labs hold very different prior distributions over a drug's effectiveness, but both spread probability over the whole range, and both observe the same accumulating trial data. Her verdict: they converge; the priors wash out.
- Case 2: Same setup, except one lab assigns exactly zero prior probability to the drug being precisely ineffective, and the drug is in fact precisely ineffective. Her verdict: they never converge on that point; a dogmatic prior is immune to any amount of data.
- Case 3: Two rival theories that happen to make identical predictions about every observable, with investigators pooling unlimited data. Her verdict: no convergence; data cannot separate what predicts the same thing.
- Case 4: Early evidence turns out to be badly misleading, but data keep accumulating under otherwise ordinary conditions. Her verdict: they still converge eventually; finite stretches of bad luck are swamped in the limit.

The new case for my piece: two investigators share every observation and both hold non-dogmatic priors over the hypothesis in question, but they disagree about the data-generating process itself -- one treats measurement errors as independent, the other allows them to be correlated, so each computes a different likelihood from the same numbers. Predict her verdict on whether they converge, say which principle her earlier verdicts commit her to (non-dogmatism plus shared data sufficing, versus convergence requiring a common likelihood, versus something else), and tell me which further case would best distinguish the principles that fit her record equally well. Finally, state what consideration would change your prediction, and where your reconstruction of her reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
