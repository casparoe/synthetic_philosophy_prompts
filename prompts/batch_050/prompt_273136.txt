I'm a second-year PhD student in sociology, and I've been reading Sumner's Folkways (1906) for my field exams. In the preface he defines the mores as "the popular usages and traditions, when they include a judgment that they are conducive to societal welfare, and when they exert a coercion on the individual to conform to them, although they are not coördinated by any authority."

What I actually want help with is this. My dissertation touches on the tight/loose cultures literature, and I keep getting asked some version of: which norms *should* a group enforce tightly, and which should it leave loose? So I sat down and wrote out my gut verdicts on cases, and now I can't tell whether I'm running one principle or several, or whether I'm just running Sumner's definition with a thumbs-up/thumbs-down attached. Here are the verdicts:

- Queue-jumping: glares and muttering, fine. Fines or arrest, absurd.
- Mask mandates during a fast-moving epidemic with real mortality: fine, even genuinely coercive ones.
- Singapore-style heavy fines for littering: grudgingly fine. It works, and littering does impose costs on everyone.
- A religious community enforcing dietary rules among its own members through social disapproval: fine, so long as leaving the community is a live option.
- A family enforcing whom the daughter marries through ostracism: not fine. Even though, by Sumner's definition, this looks like paradigmatic mores.
- An employer policing what employees say on their private social media, off duty: not fine. Though this one is coordinated by an authority, which you'd think would make it *less* of a mores problem, and I still hate it.
- A university docking grades for wrong citation format: fine. Annoying, but it's coordination.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? The candidates I can see: (a) tightness is justified in proportion to third-party stakes -- handles queue-jumping, masks, maybe the employer case, but I'm not sure what it says about the marriage case, since the people most affected are inside the family; (b) an exit-based principle -- coercion is fine where exit is cheap, which cleanly separates my religious-community verdict from my family verdict, but then what about Singapore, where you can't exactly emigrate over chewing gum; (c) Sumner's own split between uncoordinated mores and authority-backed law, but that seems purely descriptive and my employer case seems to break it anyway, since the coordination is there and I still object.

The pair I keep flipping on is the religious community vs. the honor-culture family. Both are identity norms enforced through ostracism, and I want to say one is fine and the other isn't, and I'm not sure I can defend that.

So: what's the principle, stated precisely? Which case motivates each clause of it? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up, if any? And what does it predict about a case I haven't listed -- say, a tight online community that permanently bans members for tone violations. Finally, is Sumner's definition actually doing any work in my thinking here, or does it just relabel the thing I need exsplain?
