You are a philosopher of science specializing in the scientific realism debate, and I am a graduate student preparing to defend this argument at my reading group. Think carefully before answering: first reconstruct the argument in its strongest form, then give your diagnosis.

The argument is meant to show that structural realism — the view, roughly, that we should be realist about the relational or mathematical structure of the world rather than about the natures of the objects that stand in those relations — is either vacuous or self-undermining. Each premise seems plausible to me, and the conclusion seems absurd, so I assume something goes wrong, but I cannot find the step.

(1) Across major theory changes, what is retained is the mathematical form of our theories, not the posited entities: Fresnel's equations for the propagation of light survive essentially intact in Maxwell's electromagnetism, while the luminiferous ether — the stuff those equations were supposed to describe — is simply gone.

(2) What is retained across theory change is precisely what we should be realist about; the rest is the part history teaches us to distrust.

(3) Therefore we should be realist about structure, not about objects or their intrinsic natures. This is the structural realist's core claim.

(4) A claim that the world instantiates a given structure S is true just in case there exists a collection of things, of the right cardinality, standing in relations that exhibit S.

(5) Given any collection of the right cardinality, pure set theory guarantees that relations exhibiting S exist over it: one can always define such a relation, by enumeration if necessary.

(6) Therefore, provided the world contains enough stuff, any structural claim about it is true almost automatically.

(7) Structural realism holds that structure is all we can know of the world — and on the stronger, ontic version, all there is to the world.

(8) Therefore structural realism's content is guaranteed a priori given the world's size: it says almost nothing about the world at all, or else must quietly smuggle in non-structural content to avoid saying nothing, which its own motivation forbids.

Some context: I recognize this is a descendant of the objection Maxwell Newman pressed against Russell's structuralism about perception, and my supervisor says the modern structural realist has replies. I would most like to keep (1) and (3), since they are the view's motivation; I am willing to sacrifice almost anything else. Your diagnosis should: identify the premise or step you reject; explain why that step looked fine — including the reading under which it is true; state the disambiguation the structural realist needs at the crucial point, in terms precise enough that it does not merely restate the dispute; price the rejection, in particular whether escaping the argument forces the realist to reintroduce intrinsic natures or non-structural facts that (1) and (3) were designed to do without; and say briefly whether your diagnosis differs for the epistemic and the ontic versions of the view, since the argument trades on both. If you think more than one premise must go, say so and rank the costs.
