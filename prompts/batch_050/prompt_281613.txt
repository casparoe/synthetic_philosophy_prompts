I'm prepping for my grad seminar on formal epistemology and I need a new exam question, so help me design a case. The problem of old evidence: if I already know E, then by the Bayesian's lights my credence in E is 1, so conditioning on E can't raise my credence in anything. But plainly scientists DO treat already-known facts as confirming new theories -- the standard example being the way the anomalous precession of Mercury's orbit was taken to confirm general relativity even though the precession was old news by 1915.

Here's the assignment. Construct a thought experiment, one paragraph, set in something ordinary like a hospital or a small biotech lab (no physics examples, everyone uses Mercury), with the following specifications:

1. An agent learns a new hypothesis H and realizes that some evidence E she's had in her files for years supports H. Intuitively, H gets confirmed.
2. The most common patch for the old evidence problem -- the counterfactual move, i.e. "ask what the agent's credences would have been in the hypothetical state where she'd never learned E" -- gives the clearly WRONG verdict in your case. It should either fail to deliver confirmation or deliver it for the wrong reason.
3. The reason the counterfactual patch fails has to be a structural feature of the situation, not just "the agent is irrational" or "we can't know the counterfactual." E.g., maybe what she would have believed absent E depends on E itself in some way, or the hypothesis wouldn't have been formulable, or something along those lines -- but make it concrete and your own, don't just gesture.

After the scenario: walk through (a) why the naive Bayesian treatment blocks confirmation, (b) what the counterfactual patch says and where it goes off the rails in your case, and (c) whether a different fix -- say the "logical learning" approach where what really confirms is the agent's discovery that H entails (or raises the probability of) E -- handles your case better. Then tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing all the work, and how a defender of the counterfactual approach would try to defuse it. If you think the case actually just shows the counterfactual patch is fine and I've overstated things, say that instead -- I want a case that survives grading, not a strawman.
