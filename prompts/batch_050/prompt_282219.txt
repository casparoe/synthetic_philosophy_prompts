I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of action, and I have found that when the unit on akrasia arrives, students reach for labels far too quickly: one week in, everyone calls themselves a "Davidsonian" or says the will is just weak, without having worked out what they actually think happens when someone acts against their better judgment. To force the issue, I want a classification instrument for the first session of the unit: a decision tree that takes a student from a shared starting point -- a case in which a person freely does what they sincerely judge, all things considered, to be the worse option -- to a named position, where each branch is a question phrased concretely enough to be answered by someone who has never heard the labels. I would like the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the diagnostic questions, then the combinations of answers, then the actual positions located among them.

The branching questions I have in mind should include at least the following fault lines, though you may restructure them if a more natural ordering emerges. First, whether clear-eyed synchronic akrasia is even possible, or whether apparent cases always involve a shift in judgment at the moment of action (this separates the Socratic position from the rest). Second, granting the phenomenon, whether its core mechanism is temporal: whether hyperbolic or otherwise present-biased discounting can render the preference reversal rational, so that the "akratic" agent is simply an agent with steep time preferences, or whether discounting merely describes the failure rather than excusing it. Third, for those who take the failure to be genuinely irrational, where it is located: in a lapse of evaluative judgment, or in the executive gap between judgment and intention (the will as a faculty of resolve, in Holton's sense, versus the view that there is no such gap to fail in). Fourth, what the rational response to predictable future temptation is: naive choice, sophisticated self-management, precommitment in the style of Ulysses and the Sirens, or resolute choice in McClennen's sense, where forming a plan itself changes what it is rational to do later. Finally, whether the temporally extended agent is best modeled as one self with stable values or as a succession of selves with conflicting interests, as on Ainslie's picoeconomic picture.

Two further requirements. First, please work the following case into the tree as one of the diagnostic questions, since I devised it for this purpose and I want to see whether the standard positions can even hear it: an agent is offered a pill that would permanently flatten her discount rate, eliminating her present bias; she declines, on the grounds that the person she would become would not endorse the projects her present-biased self cares about -- and the question is whether her refusal is itself akratic, and on which leaves of the tree that question is well-formed. Second, where two named positions land on the same leaf because the tree's questions cannot separate them, flag the pair explicitly rather than papering over it; and where a combination of answers is unoccupied, note briefly whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the literature. The intended audience is students who have read no primary sources for this unit yet, so every node must be answerable from the case descriptions alone, and the whole tree should be usable as a handout they fill in before the first discussion.
