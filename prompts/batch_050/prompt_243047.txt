And here's the student's answer:

(a) Heritability is the proportion of variation in a trait across a population that is attributable to genetic variation in that population, as opposed to environmental variation. It's a population-level statistic, not a property of an individual — so saying "height is 80% heritable" doesn't mean 80% of your height came from your genes, which is the mistake people always make. It's also not a fixed constant of the trait: it depends on the range of environments in the population studied, so the same trait can have different heritability in different populations or eras.

(b) High heritability doesn't mean a trait is set in stone. The heritability of myopia is fairly high, but glasses fix it, and its recent rise is clearly environmental since genes don't change that fast. Heritability tells you about the causes of existing variation, not about what would happen if you introduced an intervention that isn't currently varying much in the population.

(c) Once we know a trait is highly heritable within each of two groups, we can infer that a large portion of the average difference between the groups is probably genetic as well, because genes are doing most of the explanatory work for that trait in both populations. If the trait were mostly environmental we'd expect low within-group heritability in the first place, so high heritability rules that out for the between-group difference too.

(d) A heritability estimate of zero means genes are irrelevant to the trait. If none of the variation is due to genetic variation, then genes are not among the causes of the trait — the trait is entirely a product of the environment. For example, if some behavior had zero heritability, we could conclude biology has nothing to do with it and focus purely on social explanations.
