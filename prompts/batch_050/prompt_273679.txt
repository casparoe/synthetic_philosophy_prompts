A constitutional monarch's most striking feature, for my purposes, is that whatever reserve powers the crown possesses -- refusing assent to legislation, appointing or dismissing a head of government, granting or refusing a dissolution of the legislature -- exist only on condition of never being used. A single decisive exercise, even a legal and popular one, would likely end the institution that confers the power. I want my students to feel that paradox from the inside rather than hear it from a lectern.

The setting: an upper-division undergraduate seminar on comparative democratic institutions, 16 students, one 75-minute session, covering the British, Scandinavian, and Japanese cases. Design an in-class simulation meeting these requirements:

- Roles for roughly 16 players (a monarch, a prime minister, government and opposition factions, perhaps the press), with written rules, a turn structure that fits the time, and a scoring scheme requiring no real money. Any props should be preparable with index cards and an hour of my time.
- The equilibrium of the game must be restraint: a monarch who never touches the formal powers should reliably do well, and this should be something players discover through the incentive structure, not something the rules announce.
- Include at least one event or scenario card in which using a reserve power is locally rational -- it would resolve the immediate crisis and would be publicly popular -- so that some monarch, in some round, is genuinely tempted. I want the class to watch what happens to the game itself, not merely to that player, when someone finally pulls the lever: how every other role's expectations shift, and why the power is never quite the same afterward.
- The same formal rules should be playable twice with different outcomes, so that students see that what actually governs the monarch's conduct is not the written rule but shared expectations about how everyone else would respond to its breach -- in other words, convention rather than law.

Then supply the debrief: map specific moments of the game onto the real architecture -- the law/convention distinction, the idea that royal neutrality is an achievement of continual self-denial rather than a legal fact, and the contrast between systems that have written the monarch out of politics entirely and systems that leave everything to restraint. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I expect some students to play the monarch as a dictator with extra steps and others as a purely decorative rubber stamp -- and how to handle each failure. Finally, be candid about which feature of real constitutional monarchy a one-shot classroom game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by other means. My own guess is that it is the decades-long time horizon over which accumulated restraint compounds into legitimacy; tell me whether you agree or think the irreproducible feature is something else.

Please write the answer in American English.
