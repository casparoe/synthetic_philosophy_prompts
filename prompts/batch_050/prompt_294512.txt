A friend of mine and I have been arguing about collective guilt for years, and I've been keeping a private tally of her verdicts. She has read her Jaspers and likes the distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, but I don't think her actual judgments line up neatly with that taxonomy, and I want help figuring out what rule she is really running.

Her verdicts so far. A German civilian in 1943 who privately loathed the regime, voted against it when voting still mattered, paid his taxes, and kept his head down: she says he shares in his nation's guilt, though only in a thin, political sense, and is not personally blameworthy. A man whose grandfather participated in a land theft from an Indigenous nation, and whose present comfort is substantially funded by the inherited proceeds: she says he owes something real, but flatly denies that he is guilty of anything, and gets annoyed if you use the word. The ordinary members of a rowing club whose committee secretly embezzled the membership fees of a rival club to build a boathouse everyone now enjoys: not guilty, she says, not even a little, and the benefit is irrelevant. A woman who emigrated from her country a decade before its government committed atrocities and has never returned: no share in the guilt whatsoever. A young man today who publicly celebrates his great-grandfather's role in a massacre and wears the symbols: guilty, she insists, and she uses the word without flinching, even though he has never harmed anyone himself.

Here is the new case. A woman immigrates to a country decades after its founding crimes — dispossession, slavery, take your pick — becomes a citizen, and builds her life on institutions and infrastructure that were substantially paid for by the spoils. She neither celebrates nor denies the history; she simply regards it as something that happened before she arrived and therefore not hers. Will my friend say this woman takes on a share of the nation's guilt upon naturalization, or not?

Please do the following. First, identify at least three rules that each fit all five of her earlier verdicts — I can see at least that many myself, and that's the problem. Second, say what each rule predicts about the naturalized citizen, noting where they diverge. Third, give me your best prediction of her actual verdict and your reasons for weighting one rule over the others, drawing on the texture of her past judgments rather than on what you think the correct theory of collective guilt is. Fourth, describe one further case, as-yet unposed to her, whose verdict would do the most to discriminate among the remaining candidate rules, and explain what each rule would say about it.
