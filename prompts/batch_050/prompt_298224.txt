I'm a PhD student in machine learning, working on language model internals, and I'm at an impasse about whether a year's worth of my dissertation work shows what I thought it showed. I'd like advice on how to think about it and what to do next.

Here's the situation. I trained a linear probe on the residual stream of a mid-sized language model to classify whether the statement the model is currently processing is true or false (using a dataset of simple factual and counterfactual sentences). The probe generalizes remarkably well: it holds up across topics, phrasings, and even on statements in languages the probe never saw during training. The probe's direction in activation space also seems to do causal work: adding a multiple of it to the residual stream makes the model more likely to produce false continuations, and subtracting it makes the model more likely to produce true ones. I drafted a chapter claiming the model has an internal representation of truth — something like a belief-like state that tracks correspondence with the world rather than mere plausibility.

My advisor then ran a control I should have run myself. She fed the model statements that are false but that the model itself confidently "believes" (things it reliably asserts as true in zero-shot questioning), and true statements it reliably denies. The probe tracks the model's own verdicts, not the actual facts: it reads "true" for the model's consistent falsehoods and "false" for truths the model rejects. So at best, the probe has found a representation of what the model takes to be true, not of truth. My advisor went further in our last meeting and argued I've found even less than that. Her view: any sufficiently rich activation space contains countless directions a probe can lock onto, the fact that intervening on my direction changes outputs only shows I found a convenient control knob, and talk of the model "representing" anything adds nothing beyond what we could already say by describing its input-output behavior. On her picture, my probe is like a palm reader's crease: real pattern in the hand, zero insight into a future.

I don't fully buy this. The cross-lingual generalization and the dose-response behavior of the interventions feel like evidence that I latched onto structure the model itself uses, not structure I imposed. But I can't currently say what observation would distinguish "the model computes using this feature" from "this feature is a shadow that the model's computations happen to cast." And my committee needs a decision: do I reframe the chapter as being about the model's self-consistency rather than truth, do I design new experiments to settle whether the direction plays a genuine computational role, or do I concede my advisor's deflationary reading and cut the chapter?

My questions: What would count as evidence that an internal direction or pattern of activations deserves to be called a representation of some property, as opposed to an artifact of the probing method? Is there a principled line between a model having a state that means something and merely behaving as if it did, or does that distinction collapse under scrutiny? And given your answer, what should I do with the chapter?
