I am a novelist by trade, and a recent accusation of plagiarism against a colleague — since withdrawn, on evidence that she had never seen the book in question — has left me unsure what I actually think credit is. So let me give you my verdicts on a run of cases, and ask you to find the principle, if there is one, that underlies them. Newton and Leibniz each deserve full credit for the calculus; independence, I feel, earns as much as priority. Darwin and Wallace were rightly announced together, and the fact that posterity remembers Darwin bothers me less than I believe it should, because the decades of development were his. Yet when I imagine a writer who produces a novel nearly identical to one of mine, wholly independently, a month after mine appears, I find I want to give her sympathy but not credit — which sits badly with my first verdict. A graduate student who, working in isolation, rediscovers a theorem that is known but obscure deserves more than a student who merely learned it, though less than its first prover. A poet who read my poem years ago, honestly forgot it, and reproduces its central image believing it his own has, I feel, no claim to the image at all — even though, from the inside, his case and the graduate student's feel identical. And I have a general instinct that discoveries which were, as people say, in the air earn their makers somewhat less credit per person than ones no one else was near. Is there a principle here — perhaps built from priority, independence, and how soon the thing would have come about anyway — or must I surrender one of these verdicts, and if so which, and at what cost? Finally, tell me what your principle says about my own situation: my new novel shares an elaborate premise with one published earlier, in a language I do not read, by a writer I had never heard of.
