I work on backend services and recently fell down a rabbit hole about intransitive preferences, which struck me as a familiar class of bug. I'd like a piece that mines that.

Write a mock incident postmortem, around 700 words, for a fictional music-playlist ranking service called QueueWise. Use the standard blameless-postmortem format: Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause, and Action Items. The incident: to stop playlists from "jittering" under tiny rating changes, an engineer gave the pairwise comparator a tolerance -- track A only counts as better than track B if its score is at least 2% higher. Under this tolerance the comparator violates the strict weak ordering contract the sorting library requires, and production symptoms follow: nondeterministic playlist orderings, one sort that ran for 41 hours, and -- the worst part, buried in the Impact section -- a premium "auto-upgrade" feature that repeatedly swapped a subscriber's tracks for ones the comparator judged slightly better, charging a small fee per swap, until she had paid $38 to arrive back at exactly the playlist she started with.

The satire should work on two levels. On the engineering level, the humor should come from getting the technical details right: comparator contracts, sort stability, undefined behavior, a timeline of increasingly confident wrong diagnoses, and action items that keep escalating (one action item should propose "fixing the users' preferences" via a schema migration; a junior engineer in an appended comment should object that this is not a database problem). On the philosophical level, the humor should come from the reader slowly realizing that the "bug" is just the classical structure of a spectrum argument -- a chain of barely-noticeable differences that each look too small to matter but collectively loop back on themselves -- and that the money-pumped subscriber is a real argument walking around in the logs. A reader who knows the literature on the transitivity of "better than" should feel the satire is fair; a reader who doesn't should still find it funny as a story about a team treating a deep puzzle as a Jira ticket.

Constraints: stay completely deadpan -- no winking, no asides. Do not name any philosopher, paper, or named philosophical theory anywhere in the document; the staff must rediscover everything from first principles, in engineering vocabulary. Distinguish, at least once, between "A is better than B" and "A is at least as good as B," since the whole incident turns on conflating them. By the final Action Item, the reader should be genuinely unsure whether the subscriber was irrational, whether the comparator was fine and the sort library was wrong, or whether the concept of "better than" is the thing with the bug -- and the postmortem should close with the incident being marked "resolved: working as intended."
