Next month I'll be presenting a conference paper at an early modern philosophy workshop defending a deflationary reading of the cogito: that what Descartes actually establishes, at the moment of the cogito, is no more than that thinking is occurring, and that the move from there to an existing I -- let alone to a thinking substance that persists through time -- requires premises Descartes smuggles in rather than earns. My paper tries to show that the deflationary reading isn't a modern imposition but is forced on us by Descartes' own insistence that he has not yet proved the existence of anything corporeal or any other mind, and I sketch how the rest of the Meditations can be read as an attempt to repair the gap rather than as building on a foundation that was already solid. The audience will be about fifteen people: two senior Descartes scholars who have written on the cogito and are committed to the view that it does establish the meditator as a thinking substance, a couple of Kant scholars who tend to side with the deflationists for their own reasons, a historian of scholasticism who will care about what Descartes owed to Augustine, and several graduate students. No one in the room will be hostile to scholarship, but the two senior people have heard versions of this reading before and found them thin.

What I need is a question-period prep sheet, not a rearguing of my thesis. Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered roughly by how much damage each would do if I handle it badly, each phrased the way that particular audience member would phrase it -- the senior Cartesian talks in terms of what the text forces, the Kantian in terms of what the unity of consciousness requires, and so on. For each objection, tell me what I should concede immediately (I'd rather concede ground than look evasive), what I must hold, and a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft. Where my paper's reading depends on a contested interpretive choice -- for instance, how much weight to put on the second Meditation's reworking of the first's formulations -- flag it as such rather than presenting it as settled.

I want the prep sheet to be opinionated, not a survey: tell me which objection you judge to be the genuinely dangerous one for a deflationary reading and why the others are survivable, and give me your verdict on whether my strongest reply to it actually works or merely changes the subject. Finally, include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks -- the place where my position is weakest or where I'd have to say something the room would find implausible -- and your best advice on what to say if it comes anyway.
