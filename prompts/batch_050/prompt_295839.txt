So I'm giving a talk in our department's number theory seminar next month on my preprint, and I want to survive the Q&A. Quick sketch of the thesis so you know what they'd be attacking: everyone in analytic number theory uses the informal heuristic that arithmetic events are independent unless there's a specific reason they can't be -- the probability a random n is divisible by p is about 1/p, these events get multiplied together, and out pop conjectures like twin primes with the right constant. My paper tries to upgrade this from folklore to an actual default rule in a formal system: you presume independence, and known obstruction types (congruence conditions, the local-to-global corrections that show up in the singular series, sieve-theoretic parity barriers) get encoded as a checklist of named "defeaters" that can override the default. My bolder claim is that every historical case where the independence heuristic gave a wrong answer is a case where a defeater was missed, not evidence against the rule itself -- so the rule, once axiomatized, is basically unfalsifiable-by-counterexample in a way I argue is a feature, not a bug.

The audience: about fifteen analytic number theorists who use these heuristics daily but are instinctively allergic to anyone "formalizing" them, one probabilist who works on random multiplicative functions and I suspect thinks the whole project is philosophy not math, and a handful of grad students. No one hostile, but no one who needs the formalism either.
