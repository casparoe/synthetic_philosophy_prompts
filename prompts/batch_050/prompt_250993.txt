Here is an argument I put together for a decision-theory reading group, and I now suspect it proves too much. (1) A rational agent chooses among the options currently open to her by comparing what those options will bring about from this point forward. (2) Resources already spent on a project are spent regardless of what she does next; no available choice recovers them, so they figure identically on every side of the ledger and cancel out. (3) Therefore the size of one's past investment in a project can never by itself be a reason to continue it. (4) Suppose instead she runs what I'll call the fresh-start test: ignoring everything already invested, would it be worth beginning this project today, given its remaining costs and expected payoff? If not, she should abandon it — for if the remaining costs exceed the remaining benefits, continuing is strictly worse than stopping, whatever the history. (5) By iterating the fresh-start test, any long-term undertaking — a doctorate, a marriage, a novel, a career — is rationally retained only so long as it would be rational to adopt it from scratch today, and must be dropped the moment it would not. (6) But a person who actually lived this way — who abandoned every commitment the instant the test failed, unmoved by the years already poured in — would strike most of us as defective rather than ideally rational; some undertakings seem worth finishing partly because of what they have already cost. (7) So the argument proves too much, and one of its steps must go. Tell me which step you reject and why it looked innocent. If it helps: I would rather keep (6) than any of (1)–(4), and I have a nagging feeling that the trouble lies somewhere between (2) and (4), in what exactly gets "cancelled," but I cannot pin it down.
