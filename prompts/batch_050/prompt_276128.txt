A popular science book I was reading summarizes the cognitive science of religion roughly as follows:

"Religion, we now know, is a byproduct of ordinary mental machinery rather than an adaptation in its own right. The human mind comes equipped with a hyperactive agency-detection device that evolved to err on the side of seeing predators in the rustling grass, plus a theory-of-mind system that automatically attributes beliefs and desires to anything that behaves like an agent. Running together, these systems lead people to see invisible persons behind coincidences, storms, and illnesses. Once such ideas exist, they spread because they are maximally counterintuitive — the more a concept violates our intuitive expectations about persons, places, and things, the more memorable it is, which is why bizarre beliefs about gods outcompete sober ones. The conclusion for the philosophy of religion is straightforward: since we have a complete scientific explanation of why people believe in gods, one that makes no reference to any god, belief in gods has been debunked, and continuing to hold it is irrational."

The book is by a reputable scientist and much of this matches what I've seen elsewhere, but something about the move from the cognitive story to the philosophical conclusion feels too quick, and I'm not sure the details are all being reported accurately. Is this a fair summary of the cognitive science of religion? Please separate out: (a) which claims are accurate descriptions of the field's mainstream findings; (b) which claims present a contested position — for instance, on whether religion is a byproduct or an adaptation, or on the memorability of counterintuitive concepts — as settled consensus, and how the actual debate runs; and (c) whether the final debunking move is licensed by the empirical work at all, or whether it conflates explaining the origin of a belief with undermining its justification. If the errors are corrected, does any defensible residue of the book's anti-religious conclusion survive — for example, via some more carefully constructed evolutionary debunking argument — and what additional premises would such an argument need that the cognitive science alone does not supply?
