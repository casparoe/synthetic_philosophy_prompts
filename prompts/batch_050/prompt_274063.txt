I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and there's a pattern in human and institutional behavior that I keep noticing but can't find a clean name for. An agent performs all the visible motions of deliberation — soliciting arguments, weighing alternatives, sometimes even announcing a reconsideration — but the output is insensitive to the inputs in a specific way: present a decisive objection and you get the same decision with a new justification attached; remove the objection and the old justification returns. Two instances I have in mind: a grant committee that runs interviews, scores candidates carefully, and reliably funds the chair's favorites whatever the scores say; and a friend who asks for advice, debates it seriously and in apparent good faith, then does what they were always going to do, afterward describing the debate as having "helped them see why." This doesn't seem to be mere habit, since the deliberation is real cognitive work; it isn't ordinary self-deception, since the deliberator would honestly report having considered the matter; and it isn't weakness of will, since there's no conflict between judgment and action. Does this phenomenon have an established name? If so, how exactly does it differ from nearby concepts like rationalization, cached patterns of behavior, and mere inflexibility — where are the boundaries? And if it has no name, what would you call it, and would an agent whose deliberation worked this way count as free in any sense worth wanting?
