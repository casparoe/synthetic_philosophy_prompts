I'm a museum educator designing an interactive exhibit station: a decision tree that sorts visitors into their actual position on why (or whether) an original artwork is more valuable than a perfect copy. Build the tree from questions rather than labels: eight to ten concrete cases (a perceptually indistinguishable forgery hung in the original's frame; a copy made by the artist's own hand a year later; a painting once praised as a master's work and revalued when exposed as a forgery; a sculpture cast posthumously from the artist's mold; a digital image file that is literally identical in every copy), each phrased so a visitor can answer without knowing any philosophical vocabulary. Map answer patterns onto at least these leaf positions: perceptualism (only what you can see matters, so a perfect fake loses nothing), history-of-production views (the object's causal link to the artist is part of its aesthetic character), aura or authenticity views (the original's value is non-aesthetic but real), and the deflationary position that the premium on originals is snobbery or market behavior. Flag any leaves the tree cannot distinguish, note any coherent answer combinations that no named position seems to occupy, and say briefly whether that gap reflects incoherence or mere neglect. No question may ask visitors which theory they endorse.
