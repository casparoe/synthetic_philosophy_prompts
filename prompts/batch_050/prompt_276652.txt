Last week a friend and I fell into an argument that ran until two in the morning: setting aside whatever suffering the dying itself involves, would the extinction of humanity be a further, distinct bad thing — and if so, where exactly does the badness sit? She thought it was obvious that it would be; I kept pressing her on whose loss it could possibly be once no one is left to suffer it, and we discovered that each of our apparently clear intuitions dissolved under the other's counterexamples. I now suspect the two of us were never disagreeing about one question but trading places among several positions we never distinguished.

Please write a decision tree for a philosophy student in our situation. The tree should begin from the one premise we both accepted without dispute — that the deaths of currently living people matter morally — and lead, through a sequence of branch nodes, to a named position on the further badness of extinction. Requirements:

1. Include as leaves at least the following: the strictly person-affecting view on which extinction adds nothing beyond the deaths; an impersonal welfarist view on which the badness lies in the well-being that future generations would have had; a view in the spirit of Samuel Scheffler's conjecture that much of what we value depends for its significance on the belief that humanity will continue after us (so that the badness is a present loss of meaning for the living, not a loss to the unborn); a view locating the badness in the destruction of humanity as a continuing project, culture, or enterprise with value of its own; and any further position you think the logical space demands, even if rarely defended.

2. Build the tree from the questions, not from the names. Each branch node should be a single diagnostic question — e.g., whether it would be a tragedy if every living person died painlessly and simultaneously tonight; whether it matters that humanity would have ended naturally in a billion years anyway; whether a last generation that simply has no children is worse than a catastrophe that kills the same people; whether being replaced by superior successors would remove the loss. Phrase every question so that someone can answer it without knowing which position the answer commits them to. The point is to sort by intuition about cases, not by preferred label.

3. Where a set of answers corresponds to a coherent position nobody seems to defend, mark it as unoccupied and say briefly why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored.

4. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what additional case or distinction might.

Keep the tone suitable for a seminar handout, and aim for no more than seven or eight branching questions on the longest path.
