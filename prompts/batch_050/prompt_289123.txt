I have no formal education in politics or philosophy, but over the past few years I have been reading on my own about how countries deal with atrocities committed by a previous regime or during a civil war -- the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Argentina's trials and amnesty laws after the dictatorship, and Rwanda's gacaca courts. Lately I have been trying to organize what still seems genuinely unsettled in this area, because my country (I would rather not say which) is likely to face these questions within a decade, and I volunteer with a small civic group that may be asked to submit recommendations on transitional justice to a future government. We will realistically be able to research and write about only two or three issues in depth, so I want help deciding which ones deserve our limited effort.

In my own notes I have been working with two distinctions I came up with myself, and I would like you to treat them as hypotheses to assess rather than established concepts. The first is what I call amnesty drift: my suspicion that amnesties, once granted, tend to expand in practical scope over time -- through pardons, sentence reductions, administrative quiet, or simple non-enforcement -- until the original bargain looks very different from what the public agreed to. The second is what I call the truth-punishment exchange rate: the idea that a society is implicitly trading a quantity of accountability for a quantity of truth (confessions, records, names), and that every institutional design -- truth commissions, conditional amnesties, prosecutions, lustration, reparations -- sets a different rate, whether it admits this or not. If these framings are any good, the most important open questions should be the ones where getting the rate wrong, or ignoring drift, does the most damage over a fifty-year horizon rather than a five-year one.

So here is what I am asking. First, identify what you take to be the main genuinely open questions in the design of transitional justice -- for example, whether conditional amnesty can be made durable, how much weight the preferences of direct victims should carry against negotiated political stability, whether truth commissions without prosecutorial follow-through do more harm than good to later rule of law, how to handle mid-level perpetrators who are too numerous to try, or questions I am not seeing at all. Second, pick the two or three that a small, non-expert civic group like ours should prioritize, and defend the ordering. I want to see your criteria made explicit: which questions are upstream of others in the sense that a position on them determines what you can even coherently say about the rest; which are tractable for outsiders without legal training; which would most change a commission's actual design if answered well; and which are neglected relative to how much is at stake. Third, assess my two coined notions as part of this: if amnesty drift is a real and recurring pattern, does it reorder the priorities, and is the exchange-rate framing illuminating or does it smuggle in the false assumption that truth and punishment are fungible?

Finally, for each of your prioritized questions, tell me what development over the next few years -- an outcome in some country undergoing transition now, a court decision, a pattern in how older transitions have aged -- would move it up or down your list. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or recommended readings; I want a defended agenda for where three amateurs with a few hundred hours should place their effort, and the reasoning that would let me argue back.
