I'm a novelist pretending to be an essayist for a weekend, and I need savage comments on this sketch before I embarass myself. Don't give me citations or a reading list, just argue with it.

Thesis: the late Roman Republic did not fall because one bad man wanted a crown. It fell because informal rules got laundered into precedents. From Tiberius Gracchus using the tribunate and getting clubbed near the Capitol, through Sulla marching on Rome and making proscription lists, to Pompey's stacked commands, Caesar's Rubicon, and Octavian's polite restoration-that-wasnt, the machinery mostly kept working: elections, senate meetings, courts, triumphs, laws with nice names. What drained out was the expectation that a rival would still be alive and legitimate after losing. Each side broke some mos maiorum to save the constitution from the other side, then pointed at the breach as proof the next breach was necessary. Norm violation became a receipt you could reuse.

My working chain is: debt and land pressure make veterans and poor citizens available as clients; provincial commands turn troops loyal to commanders rather than to a fuzzy res publica; tribunician veto and senatorial obstruction turn politics into mutually assured gridlock; violence enters as a one-time emergency move and never leaves; citizenship and Italian grievances after the Social War make the winners club too narrow; finally everyone prefers order under a patron to legitimacy under procedure. Optimates and populares were not parties in our sense, more like donor brands plus family vendettas with pamphlets.

Modern analogy I want to test: not Rome had Caesar so we will get a Caesar, which feels lazy. More like procedural hardball where norms are treated as cheat codes: emergency powers that never sunset, debt-ceiling hostage logic, court packing talk, pardons as coalition maintenance, militia-ish street theater normalized before it is named, primaries rewarding people for promising to break the thing voters say they love. The scary bit is not tyranny arriving, its legality developing a limp.

Comment on whatever is weakest, but especially: 1) is norm-laundering an actual mechanism or just a fancy name for escalation; 2) where does the modern analogy break because Rome had slavery, conquest economics, no mass electorate, and a very different army; 3) what obvious piece am I missing that a small model could still know if it stopped pattern-matching to Caesar; 4) which modern parallel is the cheapest and should be cut; 5) for the novel, what single domestic scene could show a republic dying while all the forms are still correctly filled in. Be honest if the essay is basically a horoscope with togas.
