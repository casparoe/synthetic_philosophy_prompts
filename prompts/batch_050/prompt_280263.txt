Some background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired atmospheric chemist, spent about thirty years doing air quality modeling for regulators, and since retiring I've fallen headfirst into moral philosophy, which my old labmates find hilarious. Here's the thing that keeps me up at night. In my field we worked with a linear no-threshold model for stuff like fine particulates: there is no safe dose, every extra microgram of PM2.5 shaves a little bit of life off somebody, somewhere, statistically speaking. Which means that every time a regulator set a "permissable" emission standard — and every time I personally drove to the office — we were knowingly imposing a small but very real risk of death on millions of people who never agreed to any of it. At the time we called this "residual risk" and got on with our day. Now, after a year of reading philosophy, I'm no longer convinced that phrase did any work except helping us sleep.

I've come up with what I think is a fix, borrowed straight from radiation safety culture: the ALARA principle, exposures kept "as low as reasonably achievable," with costs and benefits of reduction factored in. My claim is that imposing a risk on someone without their consent is fine exactly when the risk sits below what an honest ALARA-style analysis of that activity would allow, and wrongful when it exceeds it. A moral dose limit, basically. Below the limit you've done your duty; above it you've wronged someone even if the totals look good. I want to stress-test this against a genuine opponent, not a strawman.
