So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I got into it with my coworker over something that started as a joke about our monitoring setup and now I genuinely can't see where the hole is.

We have this temperature sensor in our server room. I wanted to verify it was accurate, so I wrote a little script: every minute, log the sensor's reading, and log the true temperature at that moment. After a week I had 10,000 rows and every single one showed the reading matching the true temperature. Sensor verified, right?

Here's the thing though -- the "true temperature" column was populated from the same sensor. And my coworker said that makes the whole exercise worthless, and I said okay but walk me through it formally, and we ended up with this argument that I can't break:

(1) For each of the 10,000 logged timepoints, the sensor's reading matched the true temperature.
(2) The readings covered a decent range of conditions (day/night, AC on/off, etc).
(3) A sensor whose readings match the true temperature across a wide range of conditions is reliable.
(4) So the sensor is reliable.
(5) The only instrument used anywhere in the verification was the sensor itself.
(6) So a sensor can establish its own reliability using nothing but its own outputs.

(6) can't be right. The same procedure would "verify" a sensor that's frozen at 72°F forever -- its logs would also show reading = true value on every row, because both columns come from the same place. A broken sensor certifies itself just as easily as a working one. So the argument proves way too much.

But here's why I'm stuck instead of just saying "it's circular, duh":

First, every step looks locally fine. (1) is just what's in the logs. (3) is literally what "reliable" means for a sensor. The inference to (4) looks like ordinary induction, the same shape as any calibration.

Second, self-reference isn't always bad in my world. Compilers are routinely compiled by themselves (that's how Go and rustc work), and nobody thinks the resulting compiler is untrustworthy. My test suite has tests for the test helpers. So "it used itself" can't be the whole story of what's wrong.

Third -- this is the part that actually worries me -- if the answer is "you need an *independent* instrument," then that instrument needs verifying too, and so on forever, and it starts to look like no instrument can ever be verified, including my own eyes, which I also only ever check against themselves. My coworker happily bit that bullet and said yeah, maybe nothing is ever really verified. I do not want to bite that bullet.

So: which step do I reject? I want to keep (3) if at all possible because it seems definitionally true, and I'd like to keep ordinary induction too. Please don't just say the word "circularity" at me -- tell me exactly where in the chain the poison enters, why that step *looked* innocent, why the compiler case is different (if it is), and what rejecting the step costs me -- i.e. does the fix secretly force the regress/skepticism thing, or is there a way out that doesn't end with "and therefore you can't trust your senses"?
