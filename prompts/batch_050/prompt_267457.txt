You are a political philosopher specialising in the ethics of partiality: the debate over whether compatriots have special claims on one another that foreigners lack. I want you to think carefully and work methodically. Begin by briefly brainstorming two or three candidate scenarios (a few sentences each), then commit to the strongest one and say why you set the others aside. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background preparing an internal talk for colleagues, so define any technical terms (for example, "associative duties" or "cosmopolitanism") as you use them, in plain English.

Your task is to construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

The scenario must present two decisions, not one. In the first decision, an agent favours a compatriot over a foreigner — a choice that a moderate liberal nationalist, who grounds special obligations in co-membership of a shared political community, would clearly endorse. Crucially, an impartial cosmopolitan, who denies that nationality carries any intrinsic moral weight, must endorse the very same choice, but for a different reason (for instance, because some nationality-independent feature of the situation does the justificatory work). The second decision must be structurally analogous to the first in whatever respect the nationalist's principle deems morally relevant, such that applying her own principle consistently to it yields a verdict she herself would find repugnant. The case should therefore function as an internal stress test of the nationalist position, not merely as an external cosmopolitan critique of it.

Constraints: the setting must be realistic and ordinary — a local council's procurement process, a hiring decision, the allocation of a community budget, or something of that kind; no trolleys, no life-or-death stakes, no science-fictional devices; the scenario itself must fit in a single paragraph; and the repugnant implication must not arise because the nationalist in the story holds an obviously implausible principle — it should fall out of a mainstream associative-duties view that a reasonable person could actually hold.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following, in order: (1) state what the nationalist and the cosmopolitan each say about the first decision, and show that they converge on the verdict while diverging on the justification; (2) show step by step how the nationalist's principle generates the unwanted verdict in the second decision; (3) identify the single structural feature of the case that produces this result, and say whether removing it collapses the example back into a routine stand-off where the two views simply disagree; (4) give the nationalist's strongest available reply — whether that is biting the bullet, drawing a principled distinction between the two decisions, or restricting the scope of the principle — and assess whether that reply succeeds. Close by telling me, in two or three sentences, what you think the case ultimately shows and what its most serious weakness is.
