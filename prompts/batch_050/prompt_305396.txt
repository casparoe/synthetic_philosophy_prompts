I am trying to work out what principle, if any, lies behind my judgments in the reference class problem. My tentative principle is: use the narrowest reference class that is both adequately populated and causally homogeneous, and shrink toward broader classes only as sampling noise threatens to dominate. Please test that against the following verdicts, repair it if needed, and tell me whether any verdict has to be abandoned.

The drug trial: a new treatment succeeds in 80 percent of a large trial, but in a prespecified genetic subgroup of 120 patients it succeeds only 45 percent. For a patient in that subgroup, I think the 45 percent should govern, despite the smaller sample.

The coin run: a minting machine is calibrated over millions of tosses at 60 percent heads. A particular coin from it lands heads 7 times in its first 10 tosses. For toss eleven, I still think 60 percent, not 70 percent, should govern.

The insurer: young drivers as a class have many accidents; Mara is young but has three years of telematics showing unusually calm driving. I think the insurer may use Mara’s telematics to lower her premium, but should not be allowed to raise it because of her age once the telematics are extensive and stable.

The sentencing board: people with a certain prior record reoffend at a high rate; this defendant has that record but also has completed treatment, has steady work, and shows low scores on several validated risk factors. I think the board may consider the narrower lower-risk profile, but not present the bare group rate as if it were this person’s probability.

The election district: for forty years the district has voted for one party, but three independent recent polls show a durable shift among the actual current electorate. I think the polls beat the long historical class for this cycle.

The unique catastrophe: for an unprecedented event, such as a novel technology causing civilizational collapse in the next decade, I do not think there is a correct frequency waiting to be found; at most there are model-based credences with explicit humility.

The hard pair for me is the drug subgroup and the coin run: both pit a small local sample against a large stable class, yet I want to trust the small sample in one case and dismiss it in the other. I suspect the difference has something to do with prespecification, mechanism, and whether the local variation is itself evidence of heterogeneity rather than noise, but I cannot state the rule cleanly.

Give me one principle, or the smallest set of principles you think is needed, stated precisely enough to decide new cases rather than merely redescribe these. Do not answer with a bare injunction to use all relevant evidence; specify how relevance, sample size, causal homogeneity, selection effects, and the difference between frequencies and credences are supposed to interact, and keep epistemic admissibility separate from legal or moral constraints on use. Then apply the repaired principle to cases I did not list: an oncology model using a rare multi-marker signature; a hiring model that has rich interview data for one applicant but only demographic base rates for another; a court asked to admit group recidivism statistics as evidence about an individual; and a one-off forecast about whether an untested governance proposal will cause unrest. For each, say what the principle recommends and what further information would most change the recommendation.

Finally, say which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate if it remains simple, which verdict you would overturn first if forced, and what would change your mind: the kind of case, theorem, or argument that would show the repaired principle is wrong, and the step in your reasoning where you think failure is most likely.
