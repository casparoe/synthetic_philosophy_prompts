**Morgan’s Canon as a Test for Borrowed Intelligence**

Morgan’s canon is usually taken to say that we should not explain an animal’s behavior by appeal to a higher mental faculty if a lower one can explain it. This sounds like good scientific restraint, but I think it hides two different questions. The first is how much cognition we should attribute to the animal. The second is where the cognitive work is happening.

Animals often borrow intelligence from their surroundings. A wasp may appear to know where its nest is, while much of the solution is stored in a stable pattern of nearby landmarks. A squirrel may seem to remember its buried nuts, while smell, habit, and ordinary spatial orientation do much of the work. Calling this borrowed intelligence need not be dismissive. Human cognition also leans heavily on notebooks, road signs, routines, and other people. The danger is not environmental support itself, but mistaking a successful animal-environment system for an internally represented plan.

I propose that the canon should encourage a search for the **minimal sufficient mind**: the least cognitively demanding account that explains the behavior across changes, not merely in the experimental setup where it was first observed. Lower should not simply mean reflexive or neurological. An associative process can be lower than reasoning while still being genuinely cognitive. Conversely, a neural description is not automatically lower than a psychological one; it may answer a different question.

I then suggest three stress tests for borrowed intelligence:

1. **Portability:** Does the competence survive a moderate change of context, or does it disappear when familiar environmental scaffolding is removed?
2. **Recombination:** Can the animal put old information together in a configuration it has not directly experienced?
3. **Interruption:** If a cue or routine is disrupted midway, can the animal revise its behavior, or does it simply restart the same sequence?

These tests would not prove the presence of belief, awareness, or some other disputed mental state. They would instead limit what can responsibly be excluded. An animal that repeatedly passes them may still lack abstract thought, but its success can no longer be explained merely by the environment carrying the solution.

My conclusion is that Morgan’s canon should be interpreted less as a command to prefer stupid explanations and more as a command to distribute explanations honestly among the animal, its body, and its surroundings. Parsimony applies to the whole system, not just to the brain. Sometimes the simplest explanation attributes less cognition to the animal; sometimes it attributes more cognition to the animal precisely because that explains why the behavior survives when the environment changes.
