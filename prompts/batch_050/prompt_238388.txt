Got into an argument in my local ACX meetup group chat about mechanistic interpretability, and my friend hit me with this chain that seems to prove mech interp is basically astrology, and I can't find the exit:

(1) A network contains a representation of X only if there's a fact of the matter that some internal state means X, independent of how anyone chooses to look at it.
(2) For pretty much any activation vector, you can train linear probes that read out many different properties — including properties the network never behaviorally uses, and (the embarrassing part) you can often decode stuff out of randomly initialized networks too.
(3) Which decoding counts as "the right one" depends on what the probe was trained to find, i.e. on choices made by the researcher, not on anything in the network itself.
(4) If which content a state carries depends on an external choice of readout, then the content isn't in the state; it's projected onto it by the observer.
(5) So transformer "features" aren't representations in the network, they're in the eye of the interpretor. Interpretability doesn't discover things, it invents them.
(6) And this generalizes horribly: by the same argument biological neurons don't represent anything either, and for that matter the mercury in a thermometer only "represents" temperature relative to a scale some human picked.

The conclusion is obviously unhinged — thermometers do show the temperature, and presumably something in Claude really did represent the Golden Gate Bridge when that feature got clamped. But every step feels locally fine. My gut says the slide is somewhere in (3)-(4), but I can't articulate why in a way that doesn't also accidentally vindicate reading tea leaves. So: which premise do you kill? And what does each way out cost — going functional-role about content, arguing the network's downstream causal structure privileges a basis, or biting the bullet and saying some indeterminacy about content doesn't imply nonexistence? If it matters, I'd rather give up (1) than accept that the Golden Gate feature isn't real, but I want to know what I'm paying.
