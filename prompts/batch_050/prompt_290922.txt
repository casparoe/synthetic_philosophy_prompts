Yesterday evening I had a long argument with a friend who insists that reflective equilibrium is not really a method at all: in her words, it is just rearranging your opinions until they feel coherent, with no criterion for having done it well rather than badly. I disagreed, and I want to settle the dispute by showing the method can be made operational.

You are a moral philosopher who has trained clinical ethics consultants. I sit on a hospital ethics committee that meets weekly to advise on contested cases (end-of-life disputes, treatment refusals, allocation of scarce beds), and I am proposing that we adopt wide reflective equilibrium as our official procedure for reaching recommendations. Write the procedure for us:

1. Give the steps in order: which considered judgments get elicited and from whom, how candidate principles are brought to bear on them, what operations are permitted when a judgment and a principle conflict (must one always yield, and how do we decide which?), and a stopping criterion that distinguishes achieved equilibrium from mere fatigue or agreement to disagree.
2. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure must hand the question back to judgment rather than resolve it mechanically, and say briefly why no checklist can eliminate them.
3. Apply the procedure to two cases: (a) one where the committee's initial verdict is unanimous but a principle we all endorse appears to forbid it, and (b) one where two members reach different equilibria, each internally coherent. For the second case, state what the procedure outputs — an adjudication, or a principled stop.

Before writing the procedure, begin with a short paragraph listing the ways a naive operationalization would distort the method (for example, treating intuitions as fixed data, or treating coherence as mere logical consistency), and think carefully about which of these distortions your design has to avoid. End with a final line beginning Verdict: followed by one sentence stating whether the resulting procedure vindicates my side of the argument or my friend's.
