I'm working on a podcast series (4 episodes, maybe a short book down the line) about national myths built on defeats -- the stories nations tell about the times they lost beautifully. My four: the Confederate Lost Cause after 1865, Masada, the Kosovo myth (the 1389 battle, Prince Lazar, all that), and Dunkirk in 1940.

Here's the passage that got me onto this whole thing. Josephus, The Jewish War, Book VII (Whiston translation), describing the morning the Romans finally got inside Masada:

"At length they made a shout, as if it had been at a blow given by the battering ram, to try whether they could bring any one out that was within; the women heard this noise, and came out of their under-ground cavern, and informed the Romans what had been done, as it was done; and the second of them clearly described all both what was said and what was done, and this manner of it; yet did they not easily give their attention to such a desperate undertaking, and did not believe it could be as they said;"

Sit with that for a second. The entire Masada story -- Eleazar's speech, the lots, the 960 dead, the whole thing -- comes down to us through exactly one author (Josephus, a Roman client writing for a Roman audience), who got it from two women who had spent the decisive night hiding underground with a stash of water. A few paragraphs earlier he mentions those were the only survivors. And this story became a foundational national slogan. Wild.

So here's what I want: rank my four myths by source fragility, which I'm defining as how much of the canonical version depends on a thin chain of tellers -- one author, the losing side's own boosters, material produced generations after the fact -- versus how much is checkable against records produced by people with no stake in the myth, ideally including the winning side's records. Give each myth a score out of 10 (10 = the canonical story basicly evaporates if you lose its myth-friendly sources), put them in order, and defend each placement in a short paragraph. While you're at it, grapple with these rather than just asserting around them:

- Does Masada's single-source problem make it MORE fragile than Kosovo, where the battle itself is well attested but the mythic payload (Lazar choosing a heavenly kingdom over an earthly one, the whole covenant framing) was layered on in poems and sermons long after 1389? Or is "event real, meaning invented later" actually a sturdier position than "the event itself reaches us through double hearsay from a cistern"?
- The Lost Cause has the opposite pathology: the documentary record (secession declarations, the Confederate constitution, contemporaneous speeches) is embarrassingly rich and mostly says exactly what the myth needed forgotten. Under my definition, does a myth contradicted by abundant sources score as low fragility or high fragility? Take a position, don't dodge.
- Dunkirk is exhaustively documented and within reach of living memory, yet "Dunkirk spirit" does real myth-work that the evacuation's actual numbers don't fully support. Where does it land, and does the recency of the record change the kind of fragility rather than the amount?

Final call: if I can only keep three episodes, which myth is doing the most cultural work on the thinnest evidence -- i.e. which one is the purest case of myth-making and therefore the one I definately have to keep? Defend your pick against the strongest alternative.
