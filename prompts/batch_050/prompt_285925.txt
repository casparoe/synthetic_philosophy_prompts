I work as a backend engineer, and for the past year I've also volunteered on the exhibition committee of a small nonprofit art space in my city. We run open-call shows, which means we constantly have to decide whether borderline submissions count as art at all. In code review I rely on precedent: a new change should be handled the way our prior decisions imply we'd handle it, and when two readings of the record diverge, we argue about which rule we've actually been running. I've been trying to apply the same discipline to our committee votes, and a case has now come up where I think our record points in two directions at once.

Here is our track record on borderline cases. We accepted an unaltered, commercially manufactured snow shovel that the artist hung on the wall with a title; no craft was involved, but visitors genuinely lingered over it and argued about it. We accepted a suite of images produced entirely by a text-to-image model from prompts the artist wrote; again the artist made nothing by hand, but the work drew sustained attention. We accepted a functional quilt sewn decades ago by the submitting artist's grandmother purely for household warmth, presented now with the artist's wall text; the maker never intended it as art, but visitors treat it as a highlight of the show. We declined a lucky but blurry camera-trap photograph of a deer: the engineer who runs the traps explicitly described it as a monitoring byproduct rather than art, nobody involved was willing to present it as art, and frankly it held no one's attention. And we declined a technically flawless copy of a well-known painting, presented openly by the painter as a training exercise; visitors admired it as a feat of skill, but nobody engaged with it the way they engage with art, and the painter never claimed otherwise. I take it these five verdicts already kill any rule based purely on craftsmanship (the shovel has none) or purely on visual appeal (the copy had admirers), but I can see at least two more sophisticated rules that fit all five and pull apart on what happened this week.

Last night, contractors repainting the gallery left their materials in the main room: paint-spattered drop cloths, a ladder, a coil of cable, and a half-unrolled canvas, arranged purely by accident. This morning's member preview opened before anyone realized, and visitors spent the better part of an hour interpreting the arrangement; a local critic called it the strongest thing in the show, and several members want it kept and given a wall label. No one intended it as art, no one is claiming authorship, and the contractors are bemused. Our curator wants a committee vote by Friday. So: which way should we vote if we want to be consistent with our own record? Please lay out the two or three decision rules that are each compatible with all five prior verdicts, state what each predicts about the contractors' materials and where exactly they diverge, and identify which earlier case is the closest analog, including whether it actually decides the matter or only seems to. Give me your recommended vote with the reasoning. And if the record genuinely underdetermines this one, tell me honestly which principle we'd be adopting with each possible vote, and describe the further fact or hypothetical variant that would reveal which rule we are really running — for instance, whether our answer should change if it emerged that an artist had secretly staged the whole scene overnight.
