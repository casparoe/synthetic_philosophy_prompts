For years now I have been circling a thought I arrived at on my own, and I want it pressed into verse. The thought is this: there seem to be things one simply must not do — deliberately kill an innocent person, say — and yet the prohibition appears to hold even in cases where violating it just once would prevent several violations of the very same kind by someone else. If what makes the act wrong is the death it brings about, then forbidding the one death at the cost of permitting five seems to protect the rule by multiplying the thing the rule exists to prevent. I have never found a comfortable way out of this, and I no longer think I want one.

Write me a villanelle that lives inside that difficulty. The two refrains should be the two voices I cannot silence: one speaking for the prohibition — that I must not make myself the author of an innocent death — and one speaking for the arithmetic — that standing on the prohibition leaves five graves where there could have been one. The tercets should run the refrains through a small sequence of cases of increasing pressure, something like: refusing to divert a runaway cart from five strangers onto one; refusing to kill one innocent person to stop a killer who will otherwise murder five; and a final case of my own invention's logical extreme, where the only way to prevent many deliberate killings is to perform one. The form itself should do part of the work: I want the reader to feel the paradox reasserting itself each time a refrain returns unchanged after the cases have grown worse, and when the two refrains finally stand side by side in the closing quatrain, I want them to meet without reconciling — both lines still true, neither defeated.

Two constraints. First, do not let the poem take a side: no stanza should wink at the reader that one refrain is obviously right, and the ending must leave the tension exactly where it began rather than dissolving it into consolation. Second, the reasoning in the lines should be accurate enough that someone who has thought seriously about ethics would recognize the structure of the problem — the distinction between what I do and what I merely allow, and the strange fact that a rule grounded in the badness of killing can command me to permit more of it — rather than finding the poem merely gestures at a dilemma. Nineteen lines, the refrains repeating in the usual pattern, and no title that gives away an answer.
