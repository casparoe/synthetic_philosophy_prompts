I'm part of a small metaphysics reading group, and for the past month we have been stuck on the material constitution puzzle. Our group has split into two camps that keep talking past each other. The coincidence theorists hold that when a sculptor takes a lump of clay and shapes it into a statue in the morning, and then squashes it flat in the evening, the statue and the lump were genuinely two distinct objects that happened to share all their matter and occupy exactly the same region during the day -- after all, the lump existed before and after, while the statue did not, and distinctness follows from differing properties. The one-thingers hold that this multiplies objects beyond necessity: there is only one object on the pedestal, and "statue" and "lump of clay" are different sortals under which that single object falls, different ways of counting or describing it, with persistence conditions attaching to descriptions rather than to things. Both camps agree the squashing case is a problem for the other side and both accuse the other of an unpalatable ontology, but they have never once produced a shared account of what they actually agree on.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could produce after a serious adversarial collaboration. Open with exactly five numbered agreed claims, each a sentence or two -- these should be substantive points both sides can genuinely sign, about the case itself, about what Leibniz's Law does and does not settle here, about what a satisfactory theory owes us -- not vague platitudes. Follow this with a disagreements table with four columns: the exact point of disagreement (stated neutrally), the coincidence theorist's position (phrased so a coincidence theorist would accept the wording), the one-thinger's position (phrased so a one-thinger would accept the wording), and a concrete case, argument, or consideration that both sides would accept as bearing on the disagreement, with each side's prediction about it recorded. Close by identifying which parts of the dispute, if any, you judge to be verbal rather than genuine, and say why. If you think the standard framing of this debate rests on a confused premise, or that one of the two positions as I have sketched it cannot be coherently stated, say so plainly rather than politely writing the document around the problem.
