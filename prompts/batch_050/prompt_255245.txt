A colleague of mine teaches a seminar on laws of nature and runs the same exercise every year: she describes a possible world and a generalization that holds without exception in it, and asks whether the generalization is a law of nature in that world. Over the years I have collected her own verdicts on four of the cases, and this year she has set a fifth. I want to predict what she will say.

Her past verdicts:

1. A world containing plenty of gold, where, purely as a matter of contingent history, no lump of gold anywhere ever exceeds one mile across. There is no physical reason for this; it just never happens. Is "no gold lump exceeds one mile" a law there? Her verdict: no, it is an accidental regularity.

2. A world physically like ours, where no lump of uranium-235 ever exceeds one mile across, because any accumulation approaching that size would undergo runaway fission first. Is "no uranium lump exceeds one mile" a law there? Her verdict: yes, even though it is not one of the fundamental dynamical equations but a derivative consequence of them.

3. A world like ours in which a curator keeps a display case and, by policy, admits only green emeralds to it, so that "every emerald in the case is green" holds without exception throughout the world's history. Her verdict: not a law, despite being exceptionless and even though the curator's practice makes it extremely reliable.

4. A world containing exactly one particle, which drifts at constant velocity for all eternity in otherwise empty space. Is "the particle moves inertially" a law there? Her verdict: yes. Her stated reason was that it supports counterfactuals: had a second particle entered the world, the two would have interacted in definite ways, and the lone particle's motion is no mere coincidence.

This year's case: a world containing two kinds of fundamental particle, call them X and Y, which as a matter of actual history are never located within interaction range of one another and never will be — the X-particles are all in one region, the Y-particles in another, forever. Consider the generalization "whenever an X-particle and a Y-particle meet, they annihilate." Is this a law of that world, is its negation a law, or is there simply no fact of the matter?

Here is what I would like from you. First, articulate at least two general accounts of lawhood that are each consistent with all four of her past verdicts — I take it that both broadly Humean systematizing accounts and anti-Humean governing or necessitation accounts can be made to fit, but show that they fit rather than asserting it, especially for verdict 4, which seems to me the hardest one for the Humean side. Second, state what each account predicts about this year's case, since I suspect they diverge: on one kind of view, I gather, the world's total history underdetermines which candidate systematization is best, so there may be no fact of the matter; on another, there is a determinate fact, albeit one the world's inhabitants could never discover. Third, give your best prediction of her actual verdict, with your reasons — does the counterfactual talk in verdict 4 tip her hand toward governing laws, or can a sophisticated Humean say everything she said? Finally, since the two main candidate accounts may agree with each other on this year's case (a determinate but unknowable fact), describe a further hypothetical case that would discriminate between them — I have in mind something about whether two worlds sharing all the same fundamental properties could nonetheless differ in their laws — and say what each account would have to say about it.
