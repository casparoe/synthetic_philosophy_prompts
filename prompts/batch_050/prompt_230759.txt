Two friends of mine fell into a long argument at my dinner table last night over intellectual property, and it ended up turning on one passage from Thomas Jefferson's 1813 letter to Isaac McPherson, where Jefferson writes:

"Inventions then cannot, in nature, be a subject of property. Society may give an exclusive right to the profits arising from them, as an encouragement to men to pursue ideas which may produce utility, but this may or may not be done, according to the will and convenience of the society, without claim or complaint from anybody."

Dana read this as settling the matter: if patents are a pure policy instrument that society may grant or withhold "without claim or complaint from anybody," then no inventor is ever wronged by a patent's denial, refusal, or abolition — at most, society acts imprudently. Marcus replied that "as an encouragement" does real work in the passage: the entire point of granting the right is to induce invention, and a society that revokes granted patents, or signals its grants are unreliable, defeats the very purpose Jefferson assigns the institution, so "without claim or complaint" can only mean there is no natural-right complaint, not that there is no legitimate complaint at all. Dana answered that Marcus was smuggling in exactly the quasi-property thinking Jefferson rejected, and that Jefferson himself observed elsewhere in the letter that nations refusing such monopolies seemed to him no less inventive. Marcus countered that an observation about 1813 England cannot bear the weight of a general abolitionist conclusion, and that Jefferson's closing admission — that as a patent board member he never found good general rules for which inventions merit a patent — shows Jefferson took the policy question to be genuinely hard, not a formality.

Please adjudicate their exchange.
