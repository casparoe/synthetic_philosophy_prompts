I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, in the last year, has been working my way through the philosophy of logic, and I keep finding that the literature on the Liar paradox consists largely of two camps talking past one another: the gap theorists, who hold that sentences like the Liar are neither true nor false, and the glut theorists (the dialetheists), who hold that the Liar is both true and false and that the law of non-contradiction must accordingly be given up. To sharpen my own grasp of what actually divides them, I set myself a small exercise, and I would now like you to complete it for me. The exercise rests on a concrete case I invented rather than on any text: imagine I keep an old laboratory notebook, every page of which is blank except for pages 100 and 101. Page 100 contains exactly one sentence: "The single sentence on page 101 of this notebook is true." Page 101 contains exactly one sentence: "The single sentence on page 100 of this notebook is false." Both sentences are grammatically impeccable, their terms refer, and nothing about the physical notebook is in doubt.

Please draft the joint statement that a committed gap theorist and a committed dialetheist could both sign at the conclusion of a good-faith adversarial collaboration about this case and about the Liar generally. I would like it in two parts: first, a numbered list of agreed claims -- for instance, claims about what makes the notebook case paradoxical at all, about what classical logic commits one to, about what each theory actually says (as opposed to the caricatures each side rehearses of the other), and about whether either side's treatment of the notebook sentences escapes difficulty without remainder. Second, a disagreements table with three columns: the precise point of disagreement, phrased so that each party would accept the wording of its own position; the crux -- the sub-question such that settling it would force at least one side to revise; and, where both sides can manage it, a case or argument that each agrees in advance would bear on that crux, with each side's prediction about it recorded. Please make sure the statement addresses the revenge problem directly: if the gap theorist declares the sentence on page 101 "not true," page 101's sentence appears to say exactly that, and I want the joint statement to record, in each side's own words, how each proposes to handle this without either smuggling in the very classical notions it has renounced or stipulating a hierarchy it cannot state.

Finally, since I am new to this field and suspicious of disputes that dissolve on inspection, I would like the closing section of the joint statement to identify which parts of the gap-versus-glut dispute the collaborators conclude are genuinely substantive and which, if any, they conclude are verbal -- that is, cases where the two sides attach different meanings to "true" and so assert compatible propositions in different vocabularies. If the honest verdict is that some disagreement is verbal, the statement should say which disagreement and why; if the verdict is that none of it is, it should say what, concretely, the world or our inferential practice would have to be like for one side to be right and the other wrong, since I confess that after months of reading I cannot yet tell whether the two camps disagree about logic itself or only about how to use the word "true."
