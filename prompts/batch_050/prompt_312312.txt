I coordinate a philosophy reading group for professionals from other fields—engineers, a couple of physicians, a lawyer—who meet monthly and work through one argument per session. Next month's topic is the causal exclusion argument against non-reductive mental causation: roughly, the worry that if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and mental properties are distinct from physical properties, then mental properties are causally idle. The group has no prior exposure to supervenience, closure principles, or the type/token distinction, and forty-five minutes of the session is mine to use before open discussion.

Plan the explanation rather than deliver it. Specifically:

1. The order of ideas. Lay out the sequence in which the components must be introduced so that the argument lands as a genuine trilemma rather than as a sleight of hand, and explain what each step presupposes about the ones before it. I am particularly unsure whether supervenience should come before or after the causal closure of the physical: the argument seems to need both, but each is independently hard to motivate for this audience, and the order changes which one bears the justificatory weight.

2. The carrying example. Propose the one concrete case the whole session should be built around—say, a twinge of pain causing me to pull my hand from the stove, or something better—and state what the example must contain (an intuitively undeniable mental cause, a complete neural story alongside it) and what it must not smuggle in. Warn me if the obvious folk examples are already rigged toward one resolution, and suggest how to construct one that is neutral.

3. The misconception map. Anticipate the misunderstandings this audience will form, in the order they will form them, and mark where in the sequence each should be headed off. I expect at least these, but please correct my list: that the argument is an argument against dualism (when its sharpest target is the non-reductive physicalist who accepts closure); that mental epiphenomenalism means my pain doesn't really hurt; that accepting a physical and a mental cause together is ordinary harmless overdetermination, like two assassins firing at once; and that closure of the physical is just a physicist's dogma rather than a premise with its own motivations and costs.

4. The resolutions on offer. Indicate how much of the menu—identity theory, epiphenomenalism, denial of closure, the exclusion-of-exclusion responses—I should sketch, and where the teaching value lies in letting the group feel the pull of the trilemma before any exit is shown.

5. A check for understanding. Give me a question or short exercise whose successful completion would show the group has grasped the structure of the argument rather than memorized a slogan—ideally something that would catch a member who thinks the argument simply proves dualism false.

6. What to omit. Name the machinery a philosopher would consider essential—functional realization, the causal inheritance principle, interventionist accounts of causation, the exclusion problem's generalization to all special-science properties—that I should deliberately leave out at this level, with a sentence each on why omitting it does not distort what remains.

Throughout, be candid about your confidence in the pedagogical choices: flag where the ordering is genuinely contestable among people who teach this material, and state which consideration—about the argument's logic or about how non-philosophers actually misunderstand it—would most likely lead you to restructure the plan if it turned out you were wrong.
