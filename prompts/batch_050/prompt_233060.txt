I am an intensivist who has just been put in charge of rewriting my hospital's policy on the determination of death. Our clinicians make these determinations constantly, in two very different settings: at the bedside before withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, and in the operating room before organ retrieval under the dead donor rule, where the stakes of getting it wrong are as high as they get in medicine. The current policy is a patchwork of checklists borrowed from other institutions, and nobody can tell me what it is actually committed to. I want a single bedside procedure that a competent physician who has never read the philosophy or bioethics literature can run at three in the morning, and I want the procedure to be philosophically honest about what it is doing.

Please write that procedure. Specifically, I need:

1. The ordered steps for declaring death under the neurologic criterion and under the circulatory criterion: what each step is examining, what finding is sufficient to proceed to the next step, what invalidates the whole sequence (sedatives, hypothermia, an uninterpretable exam), the observation or no-touch intervals, and who must countersign. Where the two criteria share steps, say so; where they diverge, make the divergence explicit rather than papering it over.

2. For each criterion, a plain statement of which underlying conception of death it presupposes — loss of the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole, or irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness — and a flag on any step where the procedure quietly imports one conception into the other's territory. I do not want the document to pretend the two criteria are obviously two windows onto one event if they are not.

3. Careful handling of the distinction between irreversible and permanent cessation. As I understand it, circulatory determinations for donation rest on permanence — circulation will not return because we will not attempt to restore it — not on irreversibility, since in some of these patients circulation could in principle be restored by intervention. The procedure must mark this point as a decision about what we will do rather than a finding about the patient, because families and lawyers will read it as the latter, and it must say what follows for the length of the no-touch interval.

4. Coverage of the hard cases: a patient on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation whose circulation is mechanically maintained, so that the circulatory criterion is unavailable and parts of the neurologic exam may be impossible; a donor family asking to shorten the no-touch interval because the wait is distressing; a patient with catastrophic brain injury who retains one ambiguous reflex; and a family that does not accept death by neurologic criteria at all.

5. Worked applications. Run the procedure on three cases: an uncomplicated brain death after traumatic head injury; a controlled donation after circulatory death following withdrawal of treatment; and the ECMO case, where I suspect the procedure will return an answer I will not like — namely, that whether this patient can be declared dead depends partly on what the team decides to do next, not solely on the patient's condition. If that is where the procedure lands, say what the clinician at the bedside should do with that result.

Close with a section titled, in effect, what this procedure cannot do: which of its parameters — the length of observation, the legitimacy of permanence-as-decision before retrieval, the status of the dissident family — are choices the institution must own and defend rather than facts the procedure can discover.
