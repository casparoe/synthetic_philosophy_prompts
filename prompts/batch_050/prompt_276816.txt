I’m the token philosopher on our med school’s Translational Models Review Board, which sounds made up but definately emails me at 11 pm. In three weeks we vote on LYMPH-0, a two-year pilot that wants serious money to build a multiscale digital twin of T-cell exhaustion in solid tumors. It stitches together single-cell RNA seq, ODEs for signaling pathways, an agent-based spatial model of the tumor microenvironment, and a neural surrogate that fills the gaps when the mechanistic parts get too slow or too unknown. The uncomfortable part: it already predicts held-out mouse results pretty well, and it flagged a weird timing order for two existing drugs that a small organoid test says might be real.

The fight is not really about accuracy. The PI says LYMPH-0 is an explanation, not just a forecast, because it has entities and activities organized so the phenomenon falls out: exhausted cells, inhibitory receptors, spatial crowding, cytokine sinks, feedback loops, the usual machinery. The basic-science people say a bunch of arrows in a model is not productive continuity unless the arrows are the actual biochemical pushing-and-pulling, and the neural surrogate is exactly where right-for-the-wrong-reasons can hide. The clinicians on the board are openly fine with a black box if it picks combos; the mechanists say that is how you fund a very expensive horoscope. Our charge says we can fund projects likely to produce either intervention-ready targets or an explanation that changes what researchers bother to measure next. We cannot demand a new wet-lab program before the vote, but we can attach conditions.

Draft the memo I’ll circulate: 900-1200 words, bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, written for smart non-philosophers, no bibliography or name-dropping beyond maybe a casual nod to the mechanism crowd. Recommend fund / reject / fund-with-conditions, with a timeframe and conditions the board could actually enforce. Make the memo do the conceptual work without turning into a seminar: separate prediction, explanation, and control; say what would make an in-silico part constitutively relevant rather than merely correlated; explain the difference between a how-possibly story, a mechanism sketch, and something closer to how-actually; and take seriously levels, since some components are molecular, some cellular, some tissue-scale. Include an honest section on the strongest losing consideration: if I recommend funding, give the best anti-black-box objection; if I recommend rejecting or conditions, give the best pragmatist objection that patients do not care about metaphysics. Also propose 3-5 cheap tests of explanatory worth that are not just more prediction: perturbation, ablation, cross-level consistency, negative controls, surrogate-removal, failure-mode analysis, or whatever you think is better, and say which one is weakest. End with the one sentence I should say out loud if the room starts treating fit-to-data as understanding.
