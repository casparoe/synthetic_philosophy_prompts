I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about the Euthyphro dilemma -- the old question of whether something is good because God commands it, or whether God commands it because it's good. In my reporting I stumbled into the fact that medieval Islamic theologians fought exactly this fight, centuries ago and at a very high level: the Mu'tazilites apparently held that good and bad are objective features of acts that unaided human reason can in principle detect, with God's commands tracking those features, while the Ash'arites bit down on the other horn and held that an act becomes right or wrong only because God addresses us about it, so nothing -- not justice, not truthfulness, not gratitude -- is good or bad in itself, and asking whether God could be unjust is just confused, since whatever God does sets the standard. My editor wants the piece to actually adjudicate rather than just survey, and honestly I don't trust my instincts about what fair adjudication would even look like here.

So before I evaluate anyone, I want the spec. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of the relation between God's will and moral value -- what any such theory must deliver, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it has to respect, with the case or consideration behind each one. Some cases I've been using to stress-test positions (feel free to add your own): a father receives what really is a divine command to kill his innocent son, and every moral bone in his body screams no -- what does the theory say he should trust, and can it explain why this is an extraordinary one-off test rather than making child-killing good, full stop? If God tomorrow commanded cruelty for its own sake, would it definately become good, and if a theory says yes, is that a result or a reductio? A child dies at five, an honest man dies at seventy, a cruel man dies at seventy -- if God knew how each life would go, does justice require compensating the shortchanged in the afterlife, and can God owe anyone anything at all? Also: can God lie, or command lying? And does the theory give a reader like me -- no revelation in hand -- any way to tell the difference between a genuine command, a schizophrenic episode, and a cleric's power grab?

Beyond the cases, frame the desiderata around a purpose: a theory good enough that a philosopher can't topple it with a two-line counterexample AND that a thoughtful believer could actually live inside -- meaning it has to leave room for worshipping God out of love and gratitude rather than raw fear of power, and it can't let the word "just" in the claim that God is just mean something totally different from what we mean when we call a person just, or all the praise of God's justice becomes empty noise. Mark which desiderata you take to be genuinely non-negotiable versus which are Mu'tazilite or Ash'arite tribal preferences dressed up as common sense (I suspect "nothing can be obligatory for God" and "reason can reach moral truth unaided" might both be the latter, but convince me or correct me). Most of all, flag any subset of the desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable -- if the list itself is a no-go result, that's the most valuable part of the exercise and I'll lead the piece with it -- and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Then, briefly, a few sentences each: score the two positions above against the list, plus any third position you think does better (a middle view where reason gets you part but not all of morality, or the modern move where God's unchanging nature rather than His will is the standard), and don't let the scoring crowd out the criteria.
