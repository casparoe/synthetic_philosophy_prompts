I run the interview feature for a graduate-student philosophy journal, and our spring issue profiles a metaphysician who has spent her career defending the Humean best-system account of laws of nature — roughly, the view that the laws are nothing over and above the axioms of that true systematization of the world's particular matters of fact which best balances simplicity and informativeness, with no necessary connections, governing relations, or powers underneath.

Midway through the interview I intend to read her this passage from Section VII, "Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion," of Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding:

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

Draft eight interview questions for her, meeting these requirements:

- At least one question must put the quoted passage to her directly: does the best-system account simply accept Hume's observation as the entire truth about the world, with lawhood a matter of how well we summarize what follows what, or does it covertly reintroduce the "binding" it disavows — through the notion of axiomatization itself, or through the idea that the best system's theorems constrain the rest of the mosaic? The follow-up should anticipate her likely reply that systematization plus perfectly natural properties does all the explanatory work governance was ever meant to do, and press on whether that really satisfies someone who hears "best" as an honorific we confer rather than a constraint the world imposes.
- At least one question on the simplicity–strength trade-off: whose standards of simplicity and informativeness fix which system is best, and whether that makes lawhood objectionably anthropocentric; the follow-up should be keyed to the likely appeal to privileged natural kinds or a language carved at nature's joints, pressing on ties between equally good systematizations and on what becomes of the laws if fundamental physics' preferred vocabulary turns out partly gerrymandered.
- At least one question on objective chance: how a Humean can admit irreducible chances when everything supervenes on the categorical mosaic, what the undermining problem shows about the coherence of her own credences in future chance-events, and whether the Principal Principle quietly reinstates exactly the kind of constraint on the future that anti-Humeans build into governance; with a follow-up anticipating her answer.
- At least one question on the brute-regularity objection: the anti-Humean charge that, on her view, the mosaic's spectacular orderliness is an unexplained cosmic coincidence, since there is no law standing behind it to make it non-accidental; the follow-up should anticipate the retort that explanation must terminate somewhere and ask why termination at the mosaic is better motivated than termination at governing laws.
- At least one question that engages a rival view by name — Armstrong's nomic necessitation or dispositional essentialism — at the point where the best-system account is most exposed, not where it wins easily.
- Every question must include a one-sentence follow-up calibrated to the answer she is most likely to give.
- No biographical questions, no invitations to summarize the view for a general audience, and no questions she could answer by reciting the standard textbook presentation.

Keep the full set to at most 450 words; the Hume passage is already written out above and does not count toward that limit.
