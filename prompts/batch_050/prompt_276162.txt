Our undergrad philosophy society runs a "dead thinkers" night where a prof roleplays a historical author and has to answer in character, and this term they've landed on Bertrand Russell for Marriage and Morals (1929). The prof playing him is a genuine Russell nut and will definately have done his homework, so I need questions that can't be answered by just summarizing the book back at me.

I want the whole thing anchored in the opening chapter, "Why a Sexual Ethic Is Necessary," where he writes:

"The sexual morals of the community will be found to consist of several layers. There are first the positive institutions embodied in law; such, for example, as monogamy in some countries and polygamy in others. Next there is a layer where law does not intervene but public opinion is emphatic. And lastly there is a layer which is left to individual discretion, in practice if not in theory. There is no country in the world and there has been no age in the world's history where sexual ethics and sexual institutions have been determined by rational considerations, with the exception of Soviet Russia."

Draft me 8 or 9 interview questions. A few things I want covered, roughly in an order that builds:

- The layer picture itself: if the middle layer (public opinion) is the one doing most of the actual policing, and law by his own admission can't reach it, what does a "rational" reformer actually do about that layer? He'll probably say something about education and the long run, so have a follow-up ready pointing out that education is run by the very people whose opinions constitute the problem (he basically concedes this himself in the chapter on the taboo on sex knowledge).
- The Soviet Russia exception. By the mid-1930s the USSR had recriminalized abortion, tightened divorce, and generally reversed the experiments he was praising in 1929. Russell lived until 1970, so make him answer for that: does the reversal show the exception was never really "determined by rational considerations," or that rational ethics just can't survive certain politics? Follow-up keyed to whichever escape he takes.
- His functional story that female chastity taboos exist to guarantee paternity certainty, tested against his own second chapter on societies where fatherhood is unknown. Is the correlation between knowing about paternity and policing women's sexuality actually clean enough to carry the causal weight he puts on it?
- Whether "love" is doing instrumental or intrinsic work in his test for a good sexual ethic: he judges morality by consequences for happiness and children, but elsewhere in the book he treats love as valuable in itself. If it's intrinsic, hasn't he smuggled a non-rational axiom into a framework that claims to be purely about rational considerations?
- Trial marriage: once you strip out children as the condition, what is left that makes it marriage rather than courtship with paperwork?
- The eugenics chapter sitting next to his distrust of the state in the family-and-state chapter: who decides, and why does the state get trusted there but not elsewhere?

For each question, include the follow-up aimed at the answer Russell is most likely to give, not just the question itself. And no softballs — nothing about what inspired him or his "legacy," the audience will have read the first three chapters and I want them squirming a bit.
