I'm a TA for a philosophy of biology course, and I'm putting together a handout on the tautology objection to natural selection -- the charge that "the survival of the fittest" reduces to "the survival of those who survive," since fitness is allegedly definable only by actual reproductive success, leaving the principle empty and unfalsifiable. I'd like the handout to be anchored in a short passage from Chapter IV of the sixth edition (1872) of Darwin's On the Origin of Species, where Darwin writes that "individuals having any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and procreating their kind," and then, one sentence later, glosses his principle as follows: "This preservation of favourable individual differences and variations, and the destruction of those which are injurious, I have called Natural Selection, or the Survival of the Fittest." What strikes me is that the first formulation sounds probabilistic -- advantageous individuals have the best chance, no guarantee -- while the second sounds like a bare definition that the tautology objector could quote against him. Please keep that tension in mind when answering.

Here are the replies to the objection I already have on the handout; please don't repeat or repackage any of them. (1) The expected-success reply: fitness is defined as expected, not actual, reproductive success -- an objective magnitude that actual offspring counts are merely fallible evidence for -- so "fitter organisms leave more offspring" is a statistical claim that can fail in any given case, not a tautology. (2) The propensity interpretation (Mills and Beatty): fitness is a probabilistic propensity grounded in an organism's traits together with its environment, analogous to the fragility of glass -- a disposition that exists whether or not it is ever manifested, so the fittest individual can be killed by a falling branch without refuting anything. (3) Sober's reply in The Nature of Selection: the theory never claimed the fittest must survive; within population-genetic models, fitness values are assigned on the basis of trait-environment relations, and the empirical work is done by those assignments, not by the slogan. (4) The slogan-versus-theory divorce: the empirical content of Darwinism is the conjunction of heritable variation, overproduction, and causal influence of heritable traits on reproductive success, all independently testable; "survival of the fittest" is a popular gloss, and even if that gloss were tautologous, the tautology would infect the gloss rather than the theory.

What I need is one more reply, genuinely different from these four -- not a restatement of the propensity view, not merely the observation that biologists in fact measure fitness, and not a historical point about what Darwin or Spencer meant. It should engage the objection's logic directly. After stating it, tell me how it relates to the items on my list: does it presuppose one of them, compete with one of them, or undercut the objection at an earlier point than any of them do? Then give me your honest assessment of whether it works, and if you think the four listed replies already exhaust the space of good answers, say so and tell me which combination you would put on the handout instead. Finally, return to the Darwin passage: does your new reply help make sense of the coexistence of the "best chance" formulation with the definitional gloss, and on balance does the passage give the objector or the defender the better material?
