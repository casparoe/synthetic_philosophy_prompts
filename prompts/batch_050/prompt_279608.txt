After reading around in the literature on the paradox of blackmail, I've settled on a resolution of my own, and I'd like it stress-tested. The paradox, as I understand it: it's generally lawful to threaten to reveal truthful but damaging information about someone, and lawful to ask someone for money, yet the combination of the two -- pay me, or I'll reveal what I know -- is criminal nearly everywhere, and most people find this conjunction intuitively worse than either component alone. My proposed resolution, which I'll call the "jurisdictional displacement" account, runs as follows. Threats to reveal information are protected, when they are, because they channel genuine grievances into public fora -- the threatened disclosure either happens (in which case the public benefits from the truth) or is forgone because the underlying grievance is redressed (in which case justice is done). Blackmail short-circuits both outcomes: it replaces the two publicly valuable resolutions of a dispute with a private transaction whose entire function is that neither resolution occurs. On my view, blackmail isn't wrong because it combines a permissible threat with a permissible request, nor because information is being "sold," but because it converts a speech-act whose justification depends on its public upshot into a bargaining chip whose value depends on that upshot never materializing. The blackmailer, in effect, privatizes a public remedy.

What I'd like from you is a rigorous map of what would count against this position. Consider at least the following kinds of challenge, and tell me which ones actually land: cases where the underlying disclosure would not be protected speech at all (threats to report a crime unless compensated, threats to expose an affair), and whether my account handles them better or worse than the rival "two wrongs" and "unjust enrichment" approaches; counterexamples in which a transaction structurally identical to blackmail seems perfectly acceptable (settlement agreements with non-disclosure clauses, an employee offering silence about a defect in exchange for severance), and whether my account is forced to condemn them or can distinguish them without special pleading; and the objection that my account explains why a state might *regulate* blackmail but not why blackmail is *morally* wrong independent of its effects on public fora. For each objection, distinguish what a committed defender of my view would have to concede as a genuine cost from what can be absorbed or answered, and rank the defeaters from potentially fatal to merely awkward.

Finally, I want an honest verdict on the position's exposure overall. Does the jurisdictional displacement account actually risk anything -- does it make predictions that could fail, or have I built in enough flexibility ("public upshot" is doing a lot of work) that it can absorb any case by redescribing it? If the latter, say so plainly, and tell me whether that unfalsifiability is a defect for a view of this kind or just the normal shape of a normative theory. If you think some other resolution of the paradox survives the same stress test better, say which and why, but only after giving my account its best run.
