A slogan I keep meeting in my corner of the internet -- the rationalist blogs, their comment sections, and the alignment writing that grew out of them -- is that evolution is cleverer than you. I have seen it do two quite different jobs. In discussions of genetic engineering and gain-of-function work, it is invoked as a standing caution: the designs selection has produced embody tricks no human engineer has thought of, so confident intervention is hubris. More recently I saw it pressed into service in an alignment argument of the following shape: evolution was, so to speak, optimizing for inclusive genetic fitness, yet what it produced was agents who pursue status, sweet food, stories, and contraception -- so optimization over a target routinely yields systems whose goals are proxies rather than the target, and we should expect the same of gradient descent by default.

What unsettles me is that the slogan's plausibility seems to vary enormously with what it is taken to mean, and I suspect the alignment use quietly needs the strongest reading. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this one line could be expressing? Candidates I can dimly see include: a claim about search efficiency (blind variation plus selection explores regions of design space that directed, foresightful design does not); a claim about embodied information (existing organisms encode more functional knowledge than any engineering discipline currently possesses); a claim about predictability (selection pressures will find solutions that surprise us, so our interventions will have effects we failed to foresee); a claim about the relation between optimization targets and resulting goals (what is selected for and what the resulting system wants come apart systematically, not just occasionally); and perhaps something almost deflationary, namely that cleverness was never the right predicate for a process without comprehension in the first place, so the slogan is a category error that gestures at Dennett's strange inversion without cashing it out. I do not insist that your menu contain exactly these -- add readings I have missed and merge ones that collapse into each other.

For each reading, I would like a verdict: is it a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or actually false? Then, the question I care about most: which reading does the alignment argument above actually require in order to go through, and does that reading survive scrutiny? In particular, I would like you to address whether the move from inclusive fitness to human motivations is really evidence about optimization in general, or whether it depends on contingent features of how evolution instantiates its optimization -- frozen genomes, learning agents as the unit of adaptation, the gap between the environment of selection and the present environment -- that need not carry over to training a model.

Finally, please be explicit about your own epistemic position. State which argument, evidence, or consideration would change your verdict on whether the slogan supports the alignment conclusion, and identify where in your disambiguation you think a reading is most likely to have been missed or misjudged.
