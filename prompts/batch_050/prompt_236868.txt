I've been thinking, on my own and without any formal training in philosophy or political theory, about the moral difference between nudging people and simply telling them what to do. By a nudge I mean an intervention that steers people toward some behavior by changing the context in which they choose — default enrollment in a pension plan, putting the fruit at eye level in the cafeteria, making organ donation opt-out rather than opt-in — while leaving every option genuinely available and easy to take. By a rule I mean an open requirement backed by a penalty: a mandate, a ban, a fine.

Here is the puzzle I can't get past. The standard case for nudges is that they are gentler than rules because they leave your freedom intact. But I have a persistent suspicion that there are situations in which I would rather be subject to an open, coercive rule than be nudged — situations where the gentleness is precisely the problem, because the intervention works on me without ever addressing me. I can't tell whether that suspicion tracks something real or is just squeamishness, and I'd like a thought experiment built to order to test it.

Please construct a matched pair of scenarios meeting these specifications:

1. In each scenario, the same kind of authority pursues a legitimate public goal by two means — a nudge and a rule — and the two means produce the same behavioral outcome to the same degree. The comparison must not turn on effectiveness, cost, or side effects; those have to be held equal.
2. In the first scenario, the nudge is clearly morally preferable to the rule: the intuition that nudges respect freedom should come out at full strength.
3. In the second scenario, the rule is clearly morally preferable to the nudge: a reasonable reader should feel they would rather be openly forbidden than quietly steered.
4. The two scenarios should differ from each other as little as possible — ideally in one identifiable feature, with everything else held fixed.
5. Both scenarios must be realistic: things an actual government, hospital, or large employer could plausibly do this year. No dystopias, no mind-reading technology, no subliminal messaging.
6. Keep each scenario to a paragraph at most, and let the verdicts arise from the features of the cases rather than being asserted — I should be made to feel which is worse, not told.

After the scenarios, please do the following:

- Name the single feature you think flips the verdict, and test it against the obvious rival candidates: publicity versus covertness, ease of avoidance, whether the intervention engages my reasons or bypasses them, and whether the chooser or a third party bears the costs.
- Say what happens if that feature is removed from the second scenario — does it collapse back into a standard case where the nudge is preferable, or does some residue of the objection survive?
- Give the strongest response available to a defender of nudging who insists the second scenario still favors the nudge, and assess whether that response succeeds.
- State what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would convince you that the real moral work is being done by a different feature than the one you named, and where your analysis is most likely to fail.

Lead with your bottom line — the single feature you believe flips the verdict — in the first sentence, then present the scenarios and the analysis. No preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
