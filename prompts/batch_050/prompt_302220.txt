I'm setting the final exam for my upper-level medieval philosophy seminar (juniors and seniors, 3 hours, they write two essays out of four options, each essay worth 50 marks). One essay has to be on Abelard's ethics of intention, and I want a question that can't be answered by regurgitating "sin is in the consent, not the deed."

Here's the setup I want the question built around. Students have already seen Abelard's two stock examples: (a) two men hang the same convicted criminal, same rope, same day, same outcome -- one does it out of genuine commitment to justice, the other out of personal spite, and Abelard says the deeds are identical and only the consent differs; (b) the servant who kills his own master because the master is attacking him with a sword and he can't otherwise escape, where Abelard bites the bullet that the servant still sins, because he consented to a killing he should have endured death rather than commit. What students usually haven't worked through is the follow-through: Abelard also holds that sin requires consenting against your own conscience, i.e. doing what you yourself judge you shouldn't do, which is why he says the people who persecuted Christ and the martyrs, sincerely believing they were pleasing God, did something bad but did not strictly sin.

So the exam question should make students do three things at once: (1) use the twin-execution case to explain exactly why Abelard thinks the deed itself is morally indifferent and can't carry the sin; (2) confront the uncomfortable consequence that a sincere persecutor comes out sinless, and say whether that's a reductio of the view or a feature of it; and (3) answer the objection that if sin is wholly in consent, Abelard can't condemn any action as such -- does anything remain wrong with the *deed*, and can Abelard even say the servant in case (b) ought not have done what he did, without smuggling the moral weight back into the act? A top answer has to keep apart "the act is bad" and "the agent sins," which is where everyone gets tangled up.

Please give me: the question itself, worded the way it would actually appear on the paper; a model answer of around 600 words written at the level of a genuinely good undergrad (not a published article); and a marking scheme out of 50 that seperates A from B from C by naming concrete moves -- e.g., what exactly an A answer does with the sincere-persecutor consequence that a B answer doesn't, and which specific confusions (like treating Abelardian consent as ordinary "intention" or importing double effect anachronistically) should cost how many marks. Thanks!
