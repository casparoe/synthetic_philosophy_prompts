I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and for two semesters now our reading group has been host to a running argument between two of its members. One defends the etiological, selected-effects account of function: roughly, that the function of a trait is whatever effect of it explains why it was historically selected for, so that the heart's function is to pump blood (rather than to make noise) because pumping is what hearts were selected for. The other defends a causal-role account: roughly, that a function is just a causal contribution a component makes to some capacity of the containing system, so that anything playing the pumping role in an organism has the function of pumping, regardless of its history. They agree about most of the empirical facts but cannot agree about what a theory of function is even trying to do, and the rest of us suspect that part of their dispute is verbal — they may be using "function" to name different projects — while part of it is a genuine clash about which puzzling cases a theory must accommodate. For instance, the etiological theorist keeps pressing cases of malfunction (a diseased heart that pumps nothing still has the function of pumping, which pure causal contribution seems unable to deliver), while the causal-role theorist keeps pressing cases where selection history seems absent or irrelevant (a brand-new beneficial mutation in its very first generation, functions attributed in molecular biology papers without any evolutionary reconstruction, functions ascribed to components of ecosystems or of novel synthetic organisms).

Write the document these two should have produced if they had run a proper adversarial collaboration instead of arguing. Specifically: (1) a numbered list of claims about function ascriptions in biology that both sides can sign, stated precisely enough to be non-vacuous (for example, agreement that a theory must somehow distinguish the heart's pumping from its noise-making, or that biologists' everyday function attributions are mostly legitimate); (2) a list of their remaining disagreements, each phrased in neutral wording that both parties would accept as a fair statement of their own view; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side — and for each crux, say whether it is a genuine disagreement about biological phenomena or a disagreement about which concept deserves the word "function"; and (4) for each crux, at least one concrete case or line of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance (for example, how each theory should classify a one-off organ that fails on its first and only use, or what each side expects a close reading of function-attributions in a typical molecular biology paper to reveal).

End the document with a short section each party could sign individually, stating what finding would make them abandon their position entirely. The goal is a text both could put their names to: no verdict, no adjudication, and formulations that neither side would call slanted.
