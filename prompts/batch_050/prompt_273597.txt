In two weeks I'm presenting a paper at my department's philosophy of mind colloquium defending Dennett against Searle on original versus derived intentionality, and I want to survive the question period. My argument runs through a variant of Dennett's two-bitser thought experiment that I hope is stronger than the original. In the original, a vending machine built to detect US quarters ends up in Panama happily accepting quarter-balboas, and Dennett asks what its internal state really means. My version removes the designer entirely: imagine a lineage of coin-accepting devices whose detectors were shaped over generations by whatever actually arrived at their slots, where the actual stream consisted overwhelmingly of quarter-balboas even though the detectors would also have accepted US quarters had any shown up. I argue there is no fact of the matter about whether the lineage's detectors mean quarter or quarter-balboa, and that once we concede this, the same conclusion follows for us: our representational states were selected for tracking whatever our ancestors actually encountered, so Searle's allegedly intrinsic, original intentionality is just derived intentionality whose derivation we have forgotten.

The audience is about twenty people, and I know the dangerous ones. One wrote her dissertation on the Chinese Room, knows Searle's published responses to Dennett chapter and verse, and will certainly argue that Searle never denied that human intentionality has causal origins — only that causal origins don't constitute aboutness the way an agent's use does. A Wittgensteinian colleague thinks both Searle and Dennett get meaning wrong by ignoring use and rule-following, and he tends to ask long questions that are really comments. Several others are naturalists broadly on my side but steeped in Millikan and the teleosemantics literature, and they will press hard on whether my drift case dissolves the distinction or merely relocates it — after all, teleosemantics already builds selection history into content, so they may say my example shows that content is fixed by what the detectors were actually selected for responding to, which would hand Searle a disanalogy between us and artifacts. There's also a biologist in the room who will pounce if my evolutionary story is loose about drift versus selection.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense of my view: lead in the first sentence with the single most dangerous objection I'm likely to face, then give the rest in roughly the order they'll come, each phrased the way that particular audience member would actually phrase it, and for each one say what I should concede outright, what I should hold, where my best reply is weakest, and how to answer in the two minutes of floor time I'll realistically have. Finish with the objection I'm most hoping nobody asks, and why. No preamble, and don't restate my argument back to me.
