So Switzerland runs its defense on a militia model: basically all able-bodied men get called up around 18-19, do basic training, then go home and keep doing refresher courses for years, and the country pairs this with armed neutrality and constant referendums on army stuff. Fine. But here's what bugs me. If you refuse the army on conscience grounds you can do civilian service instead -- except civilian service is deliberately made substantially longer than military service (about half again as long). And if you're excused entirely, say for medical reasons, you owe the state an extra tax on your income, year after year, well into your thirties. Meanwhile Sweden scrapped conscription, then brought it back in a gender-neutral, selective form a few years ago, and Finland runs near-universal male conscription with women allowed to volunteer, and all three countries are armed neutral or were until recently, and they seem to mean very different things by "the citizen defends the country."

What I'm wondering is what theory of citizenship or political obligation the Swiss setup actually presuposes. Like, the militia model seems to say that defense isn't a service you purchase with taxes but something that partly constitutes membership -- closer to a republican "freedom requires participation in your own protection" idea than to the liberal view where you consent to the state by paying for it and the soldier-citizen line is sharp. But then the exemption tax says the opposite: the duty is fungible, you can discharge it in cash, like a debt. And the extra-long civilian service seems to presuppose that conscientious objection is fine but must cost you more, as if conscience were a kind of free-riding to be compensated. Are those three features even consistent with each other, or do they each smuggle in a different theory? And which parts are actually load-bearing -- could you keep a militia on a purely contractualist picture where the exemption tax is just insurance pricing, or does something essential get lost? Last thing: Sweden's selective lottery-style model and Finland's men-must-women-may model -- what does each of *those* presuppose about fairness and civic equality, and which of the three systems has the most coherent implicit theory? tbh I suspect the Swiss one is running two theories at once but I can't pin down whether that's a bug or just what a compromise looks like.
