I'm writing the final exam for my third-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility and I need one essay question on Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment' that students can't answer by trotting out the standard summary. You know the one: reactive attitudes are part of human nature, we couldn't give them up even if determinism were true, so the sceptic's demand for justification doesn't really arise. Every year at least half the cohort writes exactly that and it does my head in.

Here's the angle I want to force them onto. Strawson says questions about the rationality of our blaming and praising practices can only arise from inside those practices -- there's no external vantage point from which determinism could threaten the whole show. But our actual moral history sits awkwardly with this. People used to genuinely resent others for things -- homosexuality, blasphemy, marrying the wrong sort of person -- that we now think were never fitting objects of resentment at all, and we came to think that partly through moral argument, not just by discovering new excusing or exempting conditions. So it looks like the reactive attitudes themselves can be criticised from *somewhere*, and it's not obvious that somewhere is internal to the practice in Strawson's sense.

So I want a question that makes students take a stand on whether Strawson's argument survives this observation: is moral revision of the reactive attitudes the kind of thing his framework can accomodate, or does it reopen exactly the external-justification question he thought he'd shut down? Should be answerable in about 45 minutes under exam conditions.

Please give me:

1. The question itself, worded so a student can't just recite the participant/objective attitude distinction and leave it there -- they have to say what work that distinction is actually doing in answering it.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 20: five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how weaker students typically botch each one. For instance, I'd expect weaker answers to treat moral revision as just more excusing conditions, or to assume Strawson thinks the attitudes are immune to all criticism -- which he plainly doesn't, since he allows plenty of criticism *within* the practice. I'd also like the scheme to say what seperates a first-class answer from a 2:1 -- name the specific move a top answer makes that a decent one misses.

Level: upper-level undergrads who've read the paper closely plus a couple of the standard critical responses.
