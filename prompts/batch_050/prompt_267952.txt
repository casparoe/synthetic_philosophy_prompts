I run the behavioral evaluation group at an AI lab, and about once a month someone -- a regulator, an enterprise customer, our own marketing department -- asks whether a given system "understands language," "has beliefs," or "is conscious," and expects us to answer on the basis of testing rather than philosophy seminars. I want a standing procedure my team can run on any new system to decide which mental-state vocabulary our behavioral evidence actually licenses, built out of what the Turing test debate genuinely established and no more: what the imitation game was and was not meant to prove, the difference between treating behavior as evidence for a mental state and treating it as the definition of one, and the standard pressure points (the Chinese Room objection, the charge that the test rewards mimicry, the charge that it is unfairly demanding of anything not human-shaped). The procedure should give the tests in order, state what counts as passing each, say which attributions it is permitted to issue and which it must refuse, and mark the points where it hands the question back to judgment because behavior cannot settle it (phenomenal consciousness being the obvious candidate). Then run it on three systems: a chatbot that sustains extended casual conversation but collapses on any novel task; a narrow system that fails conversation entirely yet solves problems of kinds it was never shown; and one case where the procedure issues an attribution I will not like, with an explanation of why I have to live with it or else change the procedure rather than the verdict. Close with a statement of what the procedure cannot do. Please write the answer in American English.
