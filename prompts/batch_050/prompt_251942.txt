So here's the situation: I'm giving the eulogy at my aunt Mara's funeral this saturday and I've got a problem. Mara was by far the most generous person in our family -- drove neighbors to chemo for years, quietly paid off a coworker's dental debt, organized the church meal trains, all of it. Everyone adored her. Then last month we were cleaning out her house and found what she called her "good book": a notebook going back thirty years where she logged every kind thing she did, and next to each entry a column for what she hoped to get out of it. Stuff like "drove Linda to her appointment — she'll finally owe me one" or "brought the Hendersons dinner — need them on my side in the fence dispute." Sometimes the expected return was just "felt good all week, people think well of me." The family is split. My cousin says the notebook proves none of it was real generosity, that she was basically running a tab on everyone. My mom says who cares what was in her head, the meals got delivered, the rides happened, people were helped — that's what counts.

The funeral will have family, church friends, and also three or four of her old philosophy department colleagues (she dropped out of a PhD in the seventies but kept up with them). I want to write a eulogy that takes the notebook seriously rather than pretending we never found it, and actually wrestles with the question out loud: if someone's kindness was always aimed at some payoff — even a payoff as small as liking herself more — does that drain the kindness of its worth? I don't want it to come out as a philosophy lecture though. It should still be a funeral speech that comforts people and honors her, and it should be honest enough that her philosopher friends nod along instead of wincing. Somewhere it should probably land on something like: maybe demanding pure motives is asking humans to be something they aren't, or maybe the motives matter but her motives were more tangled and human than the ledger makes them look. Figure out the right move and write it — around five minutes spoken.
