My thesis: Lewis's triviality results, especially the original 1976 argument plus the later strengthenings and generalizations (Hajek, Hall, and co. showing it's not just a fluke of one particular setup), really do establish that indicative conditionals can't have propositional truth conditions whose probability tracks the corresponding conditional probability, and that the honest response is a non-propositionalist or suppositional semantics along the lines of Adams and Edgington. I explicitly want to argue against the move where you keep truth conditions but restrict the triviality result's assumptions, and against just shrugging and keeping Stalnaker's conditional because it "mostly works."

The audience: mostly philosophers of language brought up on possible-worlds semantics, Stalnaker-style and Kratzer-style, i.e. people whose whole toolkit assumes indicatives have truth conditions; plus two or three formal epistemologists who know Adams's Thesis and Dutch books cold and will catch any sloppy probability talk instantly (I am quietly terrified of one of them); plus one semanticist from linguistics. Rough sketch of my argument so you know what they're shooting at: (1) Adams's Thesis is about as well confirmed as anything in semantics gets, the judgments are overwhelming; (2) triviality shows no truth-conditional content can vindicate it across all probability functions; (3) non-propositionalism isn't a bug but a feature, since it predicts the funny embedding behavior, e.g. why conditionals sound off inside "It might be that..." or under "probably."
