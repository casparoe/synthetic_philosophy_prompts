I hold that Molinism is the best account of divine providence: God knows, prior to and independently of any creative decree, what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible circumstance, and orders the world accordingly. What would count against this view? Please take the grounding objection head-on rather than merely gesturing at it — must counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have truthmakers, what could they be when the creature never exists in the circumstance in question, and is the demand for truthmakers a substantive metaphysical constraint or a question-begging move against the Molinist? I'd also like you to assess two internal worries: first, whether middle knowledge collapses into either simple foreknowledge or divine determinism once you press on what fixes the truth of the counterfactuals (if God fixes them, freedom is threatened; if nothing fixes them, the knowledge seems to lack an object); second, whether the view depends on an intelligible sense of explanatory, as opposed to temporal, priority that can actually bear the weight placed on it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish the objections a sophisticated Molinist would concede as genuine vulnerabilities from those they'd dismiss as misreadings, and close with an honest verdict on whether Molinism has been qualified into something immune to refutation — and whether unfalsifiability is even the right charge to bring against a doctrine of this kind.
