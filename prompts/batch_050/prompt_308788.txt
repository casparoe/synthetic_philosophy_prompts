I'm currently taking an undergraduate seminar in epistemology and the philosophy of language, and we've just finished a unit on the norms of assertion — whether the felicity condition on flat-out assertion is knowledge, truth, justified belief, or something else entirely. Our readings covered the classic battery of test cases: Gettierized believers who speak truly but somehow improperly, lottery propositions that everyone is reluctant to assert despite their near-certainty, and Moore-paradoxical utterances like "it's raining, but I don't believe it is," which may be true yet sound like a confession of disorder. For the seminar's creative assignment, we're asked to explore a position by inhabiting it rather than arguing for it, and I'd like to take the knowledge norm as literally as possible.

Write me an official licensing handbook (roughly 600–700 words) issued by the "Office of Assertion Standards" of an imaginary country in which the knowledge norm of assertion has been enacted as law: citizens must hold a valid Assertor's Licence, and unlicensed or unwarranted assertion is a finable offense. The handbook should be written in the plodding, euphemistic register of real bureaucratic guidance — the tone of a driver's manual or a tax authority's FAQ — and should cover such matters as: the examination (including a notorious section in which candidates must explain why they may not assert that their lottery ticket will lose, given that they are almost certainly right); licence suspensions for citizens who asserted propositions that turned out to be Gettier cases, and the Board's awkward acknowledgment that these speakers believed truly and justifiably, yet must still be sanctioned; a special enforcement division for Moore-paradoxical utterances, treated as a category of offense distinct from lying; and the appeals process, in which political factions within the country — the Truth Party, which holds that speakers should be excused whenever what they said was in fact true, and the Belief Party, which holds that sincere conviction should suffice — file amicus briefs that the Office dismisses with strained patience.

The humor should arise entirely from the actual philosophical content: the satire works by showing what it would take to enforce the knowledge norm consistently, so that someone who knows the literature recognizes Gettier cases, lottery propositions, and Moorean absurdity being handled with perfect bureaucratic seriousness, and finds the treatment fair rather than a caricature. Keep the irony deadpan throughout — the Office should never acknowledge that anything is absurd — and do not have any character state the standard objections to the knowledge norm directly; the reader should be able to reconstruct the whole debate from the handbook's procedures alone.
