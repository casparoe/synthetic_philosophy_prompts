I'm taking a graduate seminar on twentieth-century continental ethics, and for our session on Levinas I asked a friend who did her undergraduate degree in philosophy to give me a head start. She read Totality and Infinity years ago and summarized the position for me over coffee as follows, and I'd like to know how much of it I can take into the seminar. Her summary: Levinas holds that ethics is first philosophy because the encounter with the other person's face comes before everything else -- before reasoning, before argument, before any theoretical framework. When I actually look into another person's face, she said, the face issues a command not to kill them, and this command doesn't rest on any principle or justification; it just grips me immediately, prior to any decision on my part. Because this encounter precedes reason, rational justification and universal principles are at best secondary, at worst a form of violence: Western philosophy since the Greeks has tried to subsume the Other under concepts and categories, reducing what is irreducibly other to the Same, and Levinas thinks this totalizing tendency is the deep philosophical root of the atrocities of the twentieth century. So, she concluded, Levinas's position is that systematic moral theory -- Kantian universalizability, utilitarian calculation, contractual reasoning, all of it -- is a betrayal of the ethical relation, and that the responsible response is to abandon the project of justifying morality by argument and instead practice an unconditional, asymmetrical responsibility for the Other in which I owe them everything and can demand nothing in return.

Something about this rubs me the wrong way, though I can't articulate what. It makes Levinas sound like an irrationalist who thinks moral philosophy as such is complicit in violence, and while I gather he does use very strong language about totality and the Same, I find it hard to believe a careful philosopher spent hundreds of pages of dense argumentation to establish that argumentation is violent. I also vaguely remember from the seminar readings that something called the third party complicates the picture -- that once a second Other arrives, I have to compare and weigh, which sounds like it reintroduces exactly the kind of impersonal reasoning my friend says Levinas rejects.

So, please audit her summary. Which parts would a careful reader of Levinas accept as accurate, including the parts that are genuinely as radical as she says? Which parts are common simplifications that flatten something more careful -- for instance, is the face literally something seen, and is first philosophy a claim about what comes first in time or experience, or something else? And where is she misrepresenting him outright, for example by presenting him as opposing reason, justice, or comparison per se rather than something more specific? Finally, does her concluding recommendation -- that Levinas's view requires abandoning systematic moral theory and principled justification -- follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or is it an overextension that Levinas's own treatment of justice and the third party rules out?
