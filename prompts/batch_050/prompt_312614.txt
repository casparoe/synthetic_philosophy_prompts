I’m in an upper-year decision theory seminar and we’ve been arguing about Kavka’s toxin puzzle, but with this tweaked version:

At 11 pm, an infallible intention-scanner checks whether you genuinely intend to drink a toxin at noon tomorrow. If you do, you get £1 million immediately. At noon, drinking gives you two days of horrible illness and no further benefit. You keep the money either way, nobody punishes you for backing out, and no weird self-binding device or hypnotist is allowed. Assume you know all this in advance and are cognitively perfect: no self-deception, no false beliefs, no weakness of will. The scanner only cares whether the intention is genuine at 11 pm, not whether you later act on it.

Here’s the proposition I want your credence on:

Such a cognitively perfect agent could rationally form the intention at 11 pm, pass the scanner, and then rationally abandon that intention at noon.

My credence is only around 0.25, while someone else in class says 0.8, but I suspect we’re disagreeing about different things and just talking past eachother.
