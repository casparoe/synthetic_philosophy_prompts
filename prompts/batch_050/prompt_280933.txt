In his 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Turing takes extrasensory perception seriously enough to suggest, apparently only half in jest, that the interrogator and the hidden players should be placed in a room shielded against telepathy, so that genuinely psychic evidence cannot contaminate the imitation game. Please explain what this caveat reveals about what the test is actually designed to measure: does treating ESP as a confound to be excluded show that the imitation game operationalizes thinking as indistinguishability through a deliberately restricted communication channel, and is that exclusion a principled component of the operational definition or an ad hoc patch protecting a desired conclusion about machines? Then take definite positions on the follow-ups: if a machine passed an imitation game in a setting where telepathic leakage could not be ruled out, would that genuinely undermine the test's credentials as a test of thinking, and does Turing's own apparent credence in the statistical evidence for ESP bear on how much weight the objection deserves, or is his personal belief philosophically irrelevant to the argument? Commit to answers and give your reasons, rather than surveying the options and leaving the choice to me.
