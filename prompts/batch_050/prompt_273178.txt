I work as a software engineer, and over the years I've noticed that my profession reaches for randomness at exactly the moments a philosopher would find most interesting. When a load balancer has several identical servers that could handle a request, it picks one at random. When two database nodes hold the same data, a client hashes its request and lets the hash decide. When two candidate designs for a page perform indistinguishably in testing, teams genuinely do flip a coin or run an A/B split and let traffic decide. Nobody regards any of this as irrational; if anything, deliberating longer over genuinely equivalent options is seen as a failure mode, the engineering equivalent of the donkey starving between two bales of hay. I use these techniques constantly without thinking about them, but a conversation with a friend who studied philosophy left me wondering what I'm actually committed to when I do.

So my question is: what does this widespread practice of randomised tie-breaking presuppose about the nature of choice? It seems to assume that a rational agent can select between options without having any preference or reason that discriminates between them, and that handing the selection over to a coin (or a hash function) is a legitimate way of doing so rather than an abdication of choice altogether. But is that coherent? Does the practice quietly presuppose a distinction between choosing and merely picking, where picking needs no justifying reason? Does it assume that reasons, if they existed, would have to favour one option over the other, so that a tie in reasons licenses any resolution at all, including an arbitrary one? And does it matter that in my examples the "chooser" is often not a person at all but a deterministic machine whose randomness is simulated?

I'd also like to know where the presuppositions of this practice strain or break, because they clearly do somewhere: most people accept a coin flip between two equally qualified job candidates far less readily than between two identical servers, and a coin flip between two patients needing the last ventilator feels different again from either. If the underlying logic is the same (no discriminating reason, so resolve arbitrarily), what explains the difference in reaction, and does it show that randomised tie-breaking presupposes something more than the mere absence of preference, perhaps that the options must be equivalent in a stronger sense, or that nothing morally significant rides on which one is selected? Finally, what would have to change about the practice if the opposing view were right, that all genuine selection requires a discriminating reason? Would we have to conclude that load balancers and coin flips don't actually choose anything, and if so, does that conclusion describe engineering practice or indict it?
