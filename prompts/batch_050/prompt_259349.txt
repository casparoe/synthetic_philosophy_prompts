I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on classical Chinese political thought, and I am setting this year's take-home final. I want a single essay question on Han Feizi's case for rule by incentives over rule by virtue, built around the following scenario, which should be stated in full within the question itself: a regional hospital network is failing on two fronts at once -- nurses chronically skip mandated hand-hygiene protocols, and mid-level administrators routinely file compliance reports they know to be inaccurate. The hospital's director, an admirer of Confucius, proposes to solve both problems by hiring for moral character, instituting rituals of professional dedication, and modeling scrupulous conduct from the top. The question should require students to (i) reconstruct, as charitably as possible, how Han Feizi would diagnose why this virtue-first plan fails even in the cases where it appears to work -- drawing on his claims about the rarity of genuine worthies, the middling moral constitution of most people, and the unreliability of moral suasion compared with clearly published standards backed by reward and punishment; (ii) design the rival incentive-based scheme Han Feizi would actually recommend, making genuine use of his notions of fa (public standards), shu (the ruler's techniques for controlling officials), and shi (positional authority), rather than merely gesturing at bonuses and fines; and (iii) confront the hardest objection to that scheme -- that measurable incentives get gamed, so that administrators rewarded for accurate reports will simply become better at fabricating accuracy -- and say whether Han Feizi's doctrine of matching performance to professed role or title gives him real resources against the objection, or whether gaming shows that some element of virtue is indispensable after all. Students may argue either side of (iii), but must take a position.

The question must be answerable in about ninety minutes and must not be answerable by reciting the fa/shu/shi triad or the slogan that Legalists prefer law to morality; the scenario and the gaming objection should make summary-recitation visibly inadequate.

Alongside the question, supply a model answer of roughly 700 words that takes the harder line on (iii) -- namely, that performance-and-title matching does not fully solve gaming, and that Han Feizi must either concede a residual dependence on the integrity of whoever audits the auditors or bite the bullet -- while showing why this concession is less damaging to his overall position than a Confucian would hope.

Finally, supply a marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit points with the marks each carries, where the highest-weighted point is the treatment of the regress problem in (iii), and append to each point a one-line note on the specific way a weak candidate typically fumbles it (for instance, treating shu as merely a synonym for surveillance, or attributing to Han Feizi the view that people are naturally wicked rather than merely responsive to incentives).
