I'm TAing a second-year undergrad course on rational choice and the midterm had an essay question on sunk costs. I've got a huge pile to mark and one answer I keep going back and forth on. The question I set:

"Standard decision theory tells us to ignore sunk costs. Yet people routinely take them into account, and some philosophers and economists argue this is not always irrational. (a) State precisely the principle behind the 'ignore sunk costs' rule and where it comes from. (b) Describe two distinct mechanisms under which attending to sunk costs could be instrumentally rational, and for each say whether it vindicates the folk behaviour or instead redescribes the situation so that the costs are no longer, strictly speaking, sunk. (c) Does the possibility of rational sunk-cost-honoring undermine the standard experimental finding that the sunk cost effect is a bias?"

Rubric: (a) 5 marks, (b) 8 marks, (c) 7 marks.

The student's answer:

"The principle behind the rule is expected utility maximization. The axioms of expected utility theory (completeness, transitivity, independence, continuity) force a rational agent to evaluate options by the probability-weighted utility of their outcomes. Since a sunk cost is by definition already incurred, it appears in every possible outcome equally and so cancels out of the comparison; attending to it would violate the independence axiom. Hence the rule: compare only prospective costs and benefits.

There are, however, cases where honoring sunk costs is instrumentally rational. First, commitment and reputation. If others can observe whether I finish what I start, then abandoning projects whenever immediate prospects turn bad damages my reputation as someone who follows through, and that reputational cost is genuinely prospective. A firm that has sunk billions into a project may rationally continue partly to signal resolve to competitors. Second, information. The money already spent is evidence: my earlier self chose to invest, and I may rationally trust that earlier judgement even if I've forgotten the reasons. Ignoring the investment entirely means throwing away information, so weighing it is good Bayesian practice rather than a fallacy.

Whether these mechanisms vindicate the folk behaviour is a seperate question. In the reputation case, what does the work is the future reputational cost of quitting, so the past expenditure is not really sunk in the relevant sense -- the situation has been redescribed so that abandonment carries new prospective costs. The information case is different: here the expenditure genuinely is sunk and attending to it is still rational, so this mechanism vindicates the folk behaviour rather than redescribing it.

At bottom, the sunk cost effect is just the name economists give to loss aversion: abandoning an investment means accepting a sure loss relative to the purchase price as a reference point, and losses loom larger than gains. And since reference-dependent preferences are not in themselves irrational, the standard experimental findings that subjects honor sunk costs do not actually demonstrate a bias. Subjects in those experiments may simply be rational agents with reference-dependent preferences, so the experiments are inconclusive as evidence of irrationality."
