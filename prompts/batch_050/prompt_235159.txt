So I'm writing a novel set in a late-antique monastery (long story) and one of my characters, this old abbot, keeps sorting the other characters' lies into what he swears is Augustine's system from On Lying -- the famous eight kinds, ranked from worst to least bad. My notes on this are a disaster and what I need is the actual sorting mechanism, not another lecture.

Could you build me a decision tree that takes any lie a character tells in a scene and lands it in one of Augustine's eight categories? Some requirements:

- Every branch should be a question about the lie itself, phrased concretely enough that my abbot could answer it about an actual scene: does it hurt anyone, does it help anyone, is it about matters of faith, was it told just for the pleasure of deceiving or to be agreeable in company, that kind of thing. Order the questions the way Augustine's severity ranking implies -- i.e. the stuff he thinks is worst should get sorted out early, not as an afterthought.
- Each leaf should name the category, say roughly where Augustine ranks it, and give one sentence on the kind of scene where a modern reader would push back on that ranking (because some of his priorities are, let's say, not my readers' priorities).
- There are combinations a tree like this probably can't cleanly reach or can't separate, and I want those flagged, not fudged. For example: a lie about religious teaching that's told to save someone's life -- does subject matter trump consequences for him, or is that combination just not on his map? And a lie told to be polite in conversation versus a lie told for the sheer love of lying, when the liar honestly feels both motives. For each stuck case, tell me how you'd break the tie for dramatic purposes.
- One more thing. Augustine notoriously won't permit any lie at all, not even to save an innocent life, and my plot definately needs the abbot to wrestle with exactly that. So end with a note on where the classic case lands in the tree -- the soldiers at the gate asking whether the fugitive is hidden inside -- and why its landing spot is the dramatic problem of the whole book.
