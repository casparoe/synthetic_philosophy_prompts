I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts about whether you can learn that something is aesthetically good from being told so, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A friend whose cooking judgment I trust tells me the ramen at a new place is outstanding. I believe her and go. Unproblematic — testimony works fine here, and I don't even feel a category difference from being told the restaurant is open late.
2. A film critic whose taste has matched mine for years calls a new film a masterpiece. I haven't seen it. If a colleague asks me whether the film is any good, I feel I can honestly say "my critic calls it a masterpiece" but not "it's a masterpiece." Asserting it flat-out feels like a kind of fraud, even though I fully believe it and would bet on it.
3. Same film, same critic. I buy opening-weekend tickets for four friends on the strength of her review and tell them they'll love it. Acting on the testimony, and even spending others' time on it, feels completely fine. This sits oddly with case 2, and I know it: if the testimony is good enough to stake an evening and my friends' goodwill on, why isn't it good enough to assert?
4. I say, comfortably and without any felt dishonesty, that Hamlet is a great play. I've read it, but I suspect my confidence is doing a lot of work that was loaded in by teachers, critics, and the general weight of received opinion — the same route by which I know that Jupiter has moons. If testimony alone can't transmit aesthetic warrant, I may have to conclude I don't properly know the verdict I feel most sure of, and that seems like a reductio.
5. A critic tells me the painting's final room uses a shift from cool to warm tones to devastating effect. I fully accept the descriptive part (the shift exists, it comes in the final room) on her say-so. The evaluative part (devastating) is the part that won't transmit. So within a single sentence, testimony works for one conjunct and fails for the other.
6. A trusted friend sees a concert and reports that the orchestra played the slow movement too fast and it fell flat. I repeat this to a third party: "apparently the slow movement fell flat." Second-hand transmission of a negative verdict feels mildly off but mostly acceptable — noticeably more acceptable than flatly asserting a positive verdict second-hand. I can't justify the asymmetry between this and case 2, but I feel it.
7. A person blind from birth has studied a famous painting exhaustively: its composition, pigments, provenance, critical reception, and the testimony of hundreds of viewers. I want to say their judgment that the painting is great can be just as warranted as a sighted viewer's — denying it seems to confuse the having of an experience with the having of knowledge. But this verdict is in obvious tension with cases 2 and 5, and I suspect it's the one I'll have to give up.

My tentative principle is that testimony transmits warrant for aesthetic belief but not aesthetic standing — you may believe on another's word, but you may not judge, assert, or appreciate except from acquaintance with the work itself. Test it against these cases, repair it where it fails, and state the repaired version precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (I'd suggest: whether an AI system that has processed every image and review ever published could have aesthetic knowledge superior to any human's), and whether the asymmetry in case 6 survives or must be explained away.

One constraint on the answer: please be explicit about what is genuinely contested here. Aesthetic testimony is a live dispute, and some of my verdicts align with positions that serious people reject. Distinguish what is broadly agreed (for instance, that most people feel something like the anomaly in cases 2 and 5) from what is a substantive theoretical choice (for instance, whether that felt anomaly tracks a real epistemic boundary or is a social convention about conversational propriety). Don't present either the acquaintance-based view or its optimistic rival as the settled consensus, and where the literature's leading repair strategies each sacrifice one of my intuitions, say which intuition each one sacrifices.
