I am a philosophy professor whose home turf is legal epistemology — burdens and standards of proof, the presumption of innocence, admissibility rules, adversarial versus inquisitorial procedure — and I have only a working knowledge of statistics. I want to understand the methodological debate behind the replication crisis in psychology and the charges leveled against null-hypothesis significance testing: p-hacking, optional stopping, the file-drawer problem, publication bias, HARKing, and the Bayesian proposal to replace p-values with Bayes factors.

Restate this debate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of the criminal trial: the null hypothesis as presumption of innocence, the significance threshold as the standard of proof, rules of evidence and exclusion, disclosure obligations, prosecutorial discretion, and the relative disvalue of false convictions versus false acquittals. In particular: what is the legal counterpart of p-hacking — is it closer to retrying a defendant before successive juries until one convicts, to withholding exculpatory evidence, or to something subtler with no clean procedural analogue? What is the counterpart of the file drawer, given that courts see only the cases prosecutors bring? Does preregistration have a legal expression, perhaps in disclosure obligations fixed before trial or in exclusionary rules adopted in advance of seeing the evidence? And when Bayesians accuse frequentists of conflating the probability of the data given the null with the probability of the null given the data, is that strictly the prosecutor's fallacy, or only a relative of it?

Then give me the residue report: which elements of the statistical dispute translate cleanly into trial vocabulary, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed there at all. My own suspicion is that the Neyman–Pearson tradeoff between Type I and Type II error maps well onto the calibration of a standard of proof, but that the prior has no home in an adversarial system that deliberately blinds itself to base rates — tell me whether I am right, and whether that residue is a defect of the trial framework or a sign that the statistical dispute is about something a trial is structurally incapable of seeing. Conclude with a verdict: which side of the frequentist–Bayesian dispute, if either, does the actual design of adversarial criminal procedure vindicate?
