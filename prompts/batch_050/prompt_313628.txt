Last weekend a friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether the internet — algorithmic social media in particular — is trapping people in echo chambers. She defended what I will call the insulation thesis: feeds and self-selection seal people inside homogeneous information environments and, worse, teach them to distrust any source outside those environments, so counterevidence bounces off not because it is never encountered but because its bearers have been discredited in advance. I defended the exposure thesis: people online encounter opposing views constantly, plausibly more often than before social media, and polarization is driven not by information deprivation but by identity, geographic and social sorting, and precisely that contact — feeds disproportionately surface the most outrageous representatives of the other side, so the disease is too much hostile exposure, not too little exposure of any kind.

We got nowhere because we kept trading impressions and sliding between mechanisms. So we agreed to rerun the argument as a proper adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement such a collaboration should produce. Two requirements of form. First, early in the argument we found it essential to distinguish an epistemic bubble, in which contrary voices are simply absent, from an echo chamber proper, in which contrary voices are present but systematically discredited; every claim in the statement must say which mechanism it is about, and claims that only work by conflating the two must be flagged as such. Second, the statement must not rest on named studies or on any authority one of us accepts and the other does not; it may appeal only to kinds of evidence we could in principle gather or recognize together.

Structure the document as follows. Begin with a numbered section of claims both of us can sign — about what feeds plausibly optimize for, what ordinary selective-exposure psychology actually establishes, and what neither of our positions requires. Follow with a disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated in one or two sentences, phrased so that the side it describes would accept the wording as fair. Then list the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. I suspect these include whether heavy consumers of partisan media have narrower or wider information diets than everyone else, whether exposure to opposing arguments on net depolarizes or entrenches, and whether distrust of outside sources is a cause of polarization or a downstream rationalization of it; correct or extend that list as needed. For each crux, specify the observation or evidence we would both accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Finally, if any of our disagreement dissolves into a verbal dispute about what counts as an echo chamber, say which part and why. Nothing vacuous that both sides always believed, and nothing slanted toward my side just because I am the one asking.
