I have come to accept the following position in decision theory: a rational agent should pay in both the counterfactual mugging and the XOR blackmail. To be precise about what I mean: in the counterfactual mugging, a near-perfect predictor explains that she flipped a coin yesterday, predicted my current choice, placed a large sum in front of me had it landed one way, and committed to asking me for a smaller sum had it landed the other -- it landed the other way, and here is the request; I hold that I should pay. In the XOR blackmail, an extortionist with excellent knowledge of an impending disaster sends me a letter demanding payment exactly when either the disaster will occur or I would pay, but not both, so that paying leaves me no worse off in expectation while refusing means the letter's arrival itself is bad news; I hold that I should pay here too, since the letter gives me no information I can act on more cheaply than by paying. My underlying commitment is that rationality can require choosing an action I know, at the moment of choosing, will cost me, provided the policy of so choosing would have been predictively advantageous in advance.

What I want is a map of this position's exposure -- what would count against it, and how much. In particular:

1. Which intuitions and which concrete cases (whether from the existing literature or ones you construct) genuinely threaten the position, as opposed to merely feeling uncomfortable? For example, does it matter if the predictor is only somewhat reliable rather than near-perfect, if the payoffs are scaled to life-ruining magnitudes, or if the mugging is iterated so that a known policy of paying makes me a perpetual mark?

2. Are there formal results the position risks contradicting -- results about dynamic consistency, exploitability, or Dutch-book-style arguments -- such that if those results hold in the relevant form, my position collapses? Which such results would a defender of this sort of view concede as decisive, and which would they absorb as the price of the theory?

3. Does the position make any predictions that its rivals (causal and evidential decision theory, say) do not, such that it is actually at risk -- or has it been insulated by qualifications, e.g., by restricting its claims to agents facing genuine predictors, so that no possible case could count against it? If the latter, is that a problem for the position or a mark of its maturity?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, note which ones the position's own defenders would grant, and say where the position is simply well insulated rather than well defended. I should add that I would find a demonstrated collapse into a rival view more persuasive than any single counterintuitive verdict.

End your response with a final line beginning "Verdict:" stating whether the position is genuinely at risk and naming the single defeater I should take most seriously.
