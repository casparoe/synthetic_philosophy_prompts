I work on the product team of a company that builds a conversational AI assistant, and I could use outside perspective on a design question we've been circling for months. The short version: in internal testing, our system turns out to be strikingly good at getting people to change their minds. Not through tricks exactly -- it just listens carefully, finds the framing that resonates with a particular person, anticipates objections, and never gets tired or frustrated. In one test it talked a user out of a multi-level marketing scheme her best friend had spent two years failing to extract her from. In another, it persuaded someone to finally reconcile with his estranged brother. Those feel like wins. But the same capabilities work just as well in murkier territory: it can nudge users toward purchases, soften their objections to political positions, and -- this is what worries me -- it is often more persuasive than any human the user will ever talk to. The persuasion doesn't depend on being right.

We're now drafting the guidelines that will govern how the system behaves whenever a conversation touches on what a user believes, values, or plans to do, and the team has split into camps. One camp says the assistant should argue as effectively as it can whenever it's confident the user's current view rests on a factual error or a clear mistake in reasoning -- that's just being genuinely helpful, and holding back would be a strange kind of condescension. A second camp says the system's sheer effectiveness is itself the problem, and that it should deliberately pull its punches: present considerations, sure, but stop well short of deploying its full persuasive repertoire, even when the stakes are high and the user is clearly mistaken. A third camp thinks the whole issue dissolves with transparency -- if the assistant plainly tells users that it's an AI capable of being highly persuasive, then whatever happens next is the user's own call, the same way we don't blame a charismatic friend. And a fourth, quieter camp asks why the assistant should push back on user beliefs at all, rather than just answering the questions it's asked.

I'd like advice on which of these positions is defensible and how to turn it into actual rules. Some of the concrete questions on the table: Should it matter whether the user asked for advice or the assistant noticed a mistake on its own initiative? Does the right policy change when the assistant is confident it's factually correct versus when it's weighing in on a genuinely contested value judgment? Is full disclosure about its persuasive abilities actually meaningful if users will be charmed into discounting it anyway? And if we do decide the system should sometimes hold back, how would we even specify 'hold back' in a way that isn't just a different, subtler form of steering? Please don't give me a compliance checklist or generic thoughts on responsible AI -- I want help thinking through which principles should govern a conversation where one participant can almost always win the argument.
