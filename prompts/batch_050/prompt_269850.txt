So I took an ethics seminar this term and afterwards got into a late-night argument with my roommate (he's an engineering guy who thinks philosphy is basically easy). We'd just covered Parfit's teletransporter case in class — the scanner that reads your whole body, destroys it, and rebuilds you on Mars out of new atoms, and the idea that this is basically as good as ordinary survival since you wake up there with all your memories, personality, and plans. My roommate went away and came back with this argument, which he says blows the whole framework up, and I've been stuck on it for a week:

(1) What matters in survival is not numerical identity but psychological continuity — being followed by someone who has your memories, character, intentions, and projects. That's the lesson of the teletransporter: if the Mars person has everything psychological, nothing that matters has been lost in transit.
(2) Now take the branch-line version, where the scanner malfunctions: it builds the Mars copy but doesn't destroy the Earth you. The Mars copy is fully psychologically continuous with you, but it's obviously not *you* — you're still standing on Earth watching it wave. So identity and what-matters come apart, and identity is the dispensable one.
(3) But if identity isn't what matters, then what's actually bad about killing someone while simultaneously producing a psychologically continuous replica — same memories, same loves, same unfinished projects? By (1), everything that matters continues. Nothing valuable is lost; the loss column is empty.
(4) So a practice of routinely replacing people with psychologically continuous copies — say, replacing the elderly with healthy young replicas carrying their complete memories — would lose nothing of value, and plausibly be a net gain.
(5) But (4) is monstrous. It licenses something that sure looks like murder as long as you keep a good backup. So something above has to go.

He says (5) shows the whole "what matters" picture collapses back into plain old identity mattering after all. My gut says the slip happens between (2) and (3) — like "identity isn't what matters" gets quietly inflated into "nothing is lost" — but when I try to say what's actually wrong with that move, (3) does seem to just follow from (1) and (2), so maybe the flaw is earlier. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs. If I have to choose, I'd most like to keep the intuition that teletransporting is a fine way to travel, since that's the case that got me into this stuff in the first place — but I want to know what I'm paying for it, and what the alternatives (keep identity, keep the intuition but draw some line between teleportation and replacement, bite (4)) would each cost me instead.
