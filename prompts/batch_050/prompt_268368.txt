I coordinate volunteers for a small mountain-rescue nonprofit, and last month we faced a decision that I still can't stop turning over in my head. A storm system moved in faster than forecast, and we received two distress calls almost simultaneously: one from a party of five hikers stranded on a ridge, and one from a solo climber stuck on a ledge on a different peak, about forty minutes away in the other direction. We had exactly one helicopter crew available, enough fuel and daylight for exactly one trip, and no realistic prospect of outside air support arriving in time. Everyone involved is fine now -- we went to the ridge and got the five, and the climber was mercifully picked up by a private operator two hours later, hypothermic but alive. But the debrief got heated. One of our senior volunteers argued that in a genuine either/or like this, we should give each stranded person an equal chance -- say, a coin flip between the two calls -- because the climber's life counts for just as much as any one hiker's, and sending the helicopter to the bigger group every single time means the person who happens to be alone never even gets a chance. He said that if he were the one on the ledge, a policy of "we always fly toward the larger group" would feel like being told his death was simply arithmetic. Our board chair was appalled and said that deliberately risking five certain deaths on a coin flip, for the sake of one person's chance, is something no responsible organization could ever defend, and that the duty to save the greater number in a forced choice is about as close to obvious as ethics gets.

Here's what I want your help with. My instinct sides with the chair -- it seems perverse to risk five lives for one -- but I can't dismiss the volunteer's point either, because he's right that the solo climber's situation isn't any worse than each hiker's situation, and it's not his fault he was alone. When I try to articulate why the five should win automatically, I find myself saying things like "more people is more good," which treats the hikers as units to be summed, and I'm no longer sure that's how we should think about persons. At the same time, the coin flip strikes me as going too far in the other direction, as if the presence of the other four hikers counted for literally nothing. Is there a principled middle position -- for instance, some kind of weighted lottery where each person adds a chance of rescue rather than a full vote, so the five get five-sixths and the climber gets one-sixth? Or is that just the coin flip in disguise? And more fundamentally: when I must choose between groups of strangers and can only save one side, is there any sense in which the unsaved person has a legitimate complaint against me that the number of people on the other side could answer -- or is a complaint of that kind necessarily about how I chose, not how many I chose? Please don't just tell me to defer to standard triage protocols; I want to understand whether the protocols rest on a defensible foundation.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order: Bottom line (one short paragraph stating your overall advice); What my dilemma is really about (two to three paragraphs identifying the underlying philosophical structure, in your own words); The case for always flying to the larger group (two to three paragraphs); The case for giving the lone person a real chance (two to three paragraphs, presenting the strongest version of the coin-flip or weighted-lottery intuition, not a strawman); Your recommendation (two to three paragraphs reaching a definite conclusion about what our policy should be and why, including how you would respond to the strongest argument on the losing side).
