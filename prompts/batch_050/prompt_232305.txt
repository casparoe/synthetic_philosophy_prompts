I am currently deciding whether to pursue IVF, and the moral questions raised by creating a possible person have stopped being abstract for me. I have provisionally adopted a qualified version of the procreation asymmetry:

Before a person exists, expected suffering can provide a strong moral reason not to create them, but expected happiness does not by itself provide a symmetrical reason to create them. No one is wronged or deprived merely because a happy life that could have existed is not created. However, once a person exists, their happiness and suffering count symmetrically: the happiness of an existing child is a genuine benefit, just as their suffering is a harm. Thus, the asymmetry I accept concerns reasons to bring people into existence, not the value of lives after they exist.

I am not treating this as a license for extreme anti-natalism. My view permits creating a child when there is good reason to expect a worthwhile life, even if the happiness involved was not independently a duty-creating reason. But it may make it wrong to create a child when there is a sufficiently high risk of severe, intractable suffering.

What would count against this position? I am looking for a map of its genuine exposure rather than a routine survey of objections. In particular:

1. Distinguish what a defender of the asymmetry should concede as potentially decisive from what is merely counterintuitive, rhetorically effective, or costly.
2. Test whether the distinction between reasons to create a person and the value of an existing person can be made coherent without relying on an equivocation about harm, benefit, or the identity of the possible person.
3. Examine whether the view can explain the apparent badness of bringing a severely suffering person into existence without also implying that bringing a happy person into existence confers a comparable benefit.
4. Consider whether the view depends on a mistaken account of deprivation: why is an uncreated person not deprived of happiness if an actual person can be harmed by suffering?
5. Assess the most serious population-ethical consequences, including whether the view leads to unwelcome conclusions about creating any life containing suffering, whether it unfairly treats prospective disabled lives as less worth creating, and whether it can avoid implying duties to create exceptionally happy people.
6. Compare its performance against its strongest alternatives, such as full symmetry between procreative harms and benefits, a person-affecting threshold view, and a view on which procreative wrongs are fundamentally about parental obligations rather than the comparative value of existence and nonexistence.
7. Where useful, construct cases that would isolate the disputed intuition. Please indicate whether each case would defeat the view, force it to adopt an implausible qualification, or only reveal an emotionally uncomfortable implication.
8. Consider how my personal IVF decision changes, if at all, once uncertainty is included: the issue is not usually certainty of misery or happiness, but a distribution of possible lives. Should a small probability of severe suffering outweigh a high probability of ordinary happiness? If so, by what non-arbitrary standard?

Please rank the objections or potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, state what would have to be true for it to succeed and what the most plausible response would be. I would also like an explicit judgment about whether the qualified asymmetry is a stable position in its own right or whether it collapses under pressure into either full symmetry or a more uncompromising anti-natalist view.
