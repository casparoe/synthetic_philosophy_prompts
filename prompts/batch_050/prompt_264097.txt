Two friends of mine, both philosophy grad students, got into an argument over drinks about the St. Petersburg paradox (fair coin, pot doubles on each heads, game ends at the first tails; expected monetary payoff is infinite, yet almost nobody would pay more than a modest sum to play). It ran roughly like this:

(1) Anna said the paradox was solved centuries ago: money has diminishing marginal utility, so with a logarithmic utility function the expected *utility* of the game is small and finite, which correctly predicts that a rational person pays only a modest price. The paradox is just what happens when you confuse dollars with value.

(2) Ben replied that this is cosmetic. Whatever concave-but-unbounded utility function you choose, you can redesign the prize schedule so the prizes grow fast enough in utility terms to make expected utility infinite again. So the "solution" dissolves one game and immediately generates another; the real problem is the combination of unbounded payoffs with expected-value maximization, and Anna has touched neither.

(3) Anna conceded the regress but said it cuts the other way: it shows the correct moral is that rational utility must be *bounded*. There are only finitely many discriminable grades of human experience anyway, so boundedness is independently plausible, and once utility is bounded no prize schedule can resurrect the paradox.

(4) Ben said boundedness is a patch, not a diagnosis, and a costly one: a bounded utility function must eventually go flat, which means treating two outcomes differing by astronomically large stakes as exactly equally good. Swapping one counterintuitive verdict for another isn't progress. Moreover, the paradox was always a theoretical challenge to a decision rule, not a practical puzzle about gambling.

(5) Anna then shifted ground: in any actually offerable game the casino's bankroll is finite, which truncates the prize schedule and makes the expectation finite (and modest). The "paradox" therefore never arises; it trades on idealized payoffs nobody could ever owe you.

(6) Ben's last word: that concedes everything. If the resolution is that payoffs must be capped, you need a principled reason the cap belongs inside the decision theory rather than being an empirical accident about casino balance sheets -- and she hasn't given one.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it's sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Then say who, if anyone, comes out ahead overall, and whether there's a position neither of them reaches that would settle what they're actually disagreeing about -- in particular, whether their dispute is really about the shape of utility functions or about the status of expected-value maximization itself.
