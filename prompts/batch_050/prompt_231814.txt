I'm a philosophy student taking a cognitive science seminar on predictive processing, and I'm hoping you can act as a philosopher of mind who knows this literature well and help me prepare for the next session. In last week's discussion I asked what I thought was an innocent question: if the brain is constantly generating a top-down model of the world, what on this picture distinguishes ordinary waking perception from dreaming or vivid imagination, which are also top-down constructions? The lecturer waved it off with the slogan "perception is controlled hallucination" — as if that settled the matter — and moved on. I've since noticed the phrase everywhere: in talks, in pop-science articles, in other students' essays, usually invoked to end exactly this kind of conversation. But I genuinely can't tell what precise claim it's supposed to be making, and I suspect the people using it couldn't tell me either.

So here is what I'd like. Think carefully about the different precise theses that could be hiding under this one slogan, and lay them out as a menu, ordered roughly from the most trivial to the most radical. For each reading: state the thesis precisely enough that one could say what evidence or argument would count for or against it; say whether it's a platitude that no one ever denied, a substantive empirical claim, a contentious philosophical thesis, or something close to false; and note what work the word "controlled" is doing on that reading, since on some readings it seems to carry the entire weight of the answer to my question. Some candidate directions I can dimly see, which you should feel free to refine, reject, or subdivide: the slogan might just mean that expectations and prior knowledge shape perceptual experience (which surely nobody disputes); it might be the stronger empirical claim that what we consciously perceive is the content of the brain's best guess, with sensory signals serving only to correct errors in that guess; it might be a metaphysical claim that we never perceive external objects directly but only the brain's internal model of them, in which case it starts to sound rather Kantian; or it might be something more deflationary about how the word "hallucination" is being redefined.

Finally, and most importantly for my purposes: for each reading, tell me whether it actually answers my original question. Which readings genuinely explain how perception differs from dreaming and imagination, and on those readings is the difference merely a matter of degree (how tightly the construction is constrained by incoming sensory signals) or of kind? And which readings are compatible with the slogan being true while my question remains completely unanswered — so that the lecturer was, in effect, using a true platitude to dodge a hard problem? I'd like the answer in a form I could bring to office hours: numbered readings, clear verdicts, and a closing paragraph on what a genuinely satisfying answer to my question would have to establish that the slogan does not.
