I TA for an intro ethics course, around 30 undergrads, and I get one 80-minute discussion section per week. Later this semester we hit moral luck -- the Nagel/Williams material about how we judge people for stuff that isn't really under their control. Last year I mostly lectured it, everyone nodded, everyone agreed it's irrational to blame people for outcomes they couldn't control, and then on the exam half of them wrote that the drunk driver who hits someone is obviously worse than the drunk driver who gets home fine. So clearly nobody actually felt the pull of the problem, they just parroted the "correct" answer back at me.

Design me an in-class activity that makes them feel it before they ever hear the term. What I'm picturing: something where the students themselves have to make a reckless-ish choice, chance decides whether it goes badly, and then the class ends up punishing or blaming some students way more than others for what was literally the same decision -- and only afterwards do they realize what they did. Real (small) stakes are fine, like points that count for a participation grade or candy or whatever, it just can't be pure hypotheticals because hypotheticals are exactly what didn't work last year.

Give me the actual rules and materials, a rough timeline for 80 minutes, and how to structure it so the students who got lucky vs unlucky can't see it coming. Then a debrief plan that maps what happened in the room onto the different kinds of luck (like, the difference between "my decision turned out badly" and "I was never even in the situation where my bad character would be exposed"). Also tell me what's going to predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- e.g. my guess is some students will deliberately refuse to punish differently because they smell the trick, and I don't know what to do about that. And be honest about which part of moral luck the game can't capture, since obviously nobody's actually going to jail in my classroom, which might be load-bearing for the real thing.
