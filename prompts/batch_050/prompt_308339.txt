**Draft: Judy Benjamin Was Told More Than a Constraint**

*1. The problem.* Van Fraassen's Private Benjamin is dropped somewhere in a jungle divided into four equal regions: Red Company territory, first battalion (R1) and second battalion (R2), and Blue Company territory, first battalion (B1) and second battalion (B2). Her prior is uniform: 1/4 each. Headquarters radios a single piece of information: conditional on her not being in B2, the probability that she is in R1 is 3/4. What should her posterior be? The message constrains her posterior via P(R1 | not-B2) = 3/4, but infinitely many distributions satisfy that constraint, and she has learned no event on which she could simply conditionalize. Jeffrey conditionalization requires her to fix probabilities on some partition, but the message is silent about P(B2), the natural partition's remaining cell.

*2. Three answers.* The literature's three candidate update rules, as I understand them:

- Rule A: hold P(B2) fixed at 1/4, impose odds of 3:1 between R1 and the disjunction R2-or-B1, and preserve the prior ratio between R2 and B1. This gives (R1, R2, B1, B2) = (9/16, 3/32, 3/32, 1/4).
- Rule B: hold P(B2) fixed and impose the 3:1 odds homogeneously, so that R1 ends up three times as likely as each of R2 and B1 taken separately. This gives (9/20, 3/20, 3/20, 1/4).
- Rule C: update by minimum relative entropy subject to the constraint. Symmetry keeps R2 = B1; solving gives approximately (0.507, 0.085, 0.085, 0.324).

The striking feature of Rule C is that P(B2) rises from 1/4 to roughly 0.32, even though the message concerned only what holds on the assumption that Judy is not in B2. Probability mass flows into the one cell the message was not about. Van Fraassen reportedly regarded this as a reductio of information-minimizing updates; defenders of Rule C bite the bullet.

*3. My thesis.* The non-uniqueness is an artifact of treating the radio message as a bare constraint rather than as an event. Judy did not receive a constraint handed down from nowhere; she received a transmission, produced by a process, in a world where that process might have produced something else. The correct formal object is her probability conditional on the event "HQ transmits this message," and that depends on HQ's sending policy -- on what HQ would have said, or whether HQ would have said anything, in each of the four cells. This is the same lesson as the sequences' point about filtered evidence: being told X is not the same event as X, and the likelihood ratio of the telling depends on the teller's selection procedure, just as in anthropic reasoning the answer depends on the sampling procedure and not only on the sample.

Conjecture: each of Rules A, B, and C is exactly the conditionalization posterior under some simple, natural sending policy, and the rules differ because they encode different tacit assumptions about the channel. I have constructed policies that yield Rule A and Rule B (roughly: policies under which HQ's transmission would have gone out in the same form regardless of Judy's cell, versus policies under which the message format tracks the cell-by-cell odds). I have not been able to construct a policy that yields the maximum-entropy posterior. If none exists, then Rule C's strange B2-bump is not a defensible response to any actual channel but an artifact of an optimization criterion, and van Fraassen's complaint is vindicated on grounds he didn't state. If one does exist, it will tell us what the entropy minimizers were implicitly assuming all along, and the B2-bump will turn out to encode a substantive claim about the circumstances under which Judy would have heard nothing.

*4. The upshot.* The standard moral of Judy Benjamin -- that updating on a conditional is radically underdetermined -- is formally correct but practically misleading. Real conditional testimony always arrives through a channel, and specifying the channel completes the problem. The residual underdetermination does not disappear; it moves up one level, to uncertainty about the sending policy. But that is a place where agents have actual evidence: experience with how informants choose what to report. The paradox is a special case of the general rationalist lesson that you cannot update on content while ignoring the process that delivered the content.
