I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the self-improvement industry -- apps that help you "become a morning person," acquire expensive tastes, train yourself out of wanting things. At a workshop last month I mentioned this to a decision theorist, and she grinned and walked me into an argument that seems to prove the entire premise of my article is incoherent, which can't be right, but I can't find the hole. Here's her argument as best I reconstructed it on the train home. (1) If a pattern of choices lets a counterparty extract money from you while leaving you exactly where you started -- strictly worse off by your own lights at every step -- that pattern is irrational; that's just the standard money-pump thought that everyone accepts for intransitive preferences. (2) Suppose on Monday I strictly prefer career A to career B, but by Friday, after reading a memoir or whatever, I strictly prefer B to A. (3) Then a broker who can predict this flip can pump me: on Monday he sells me A for B plus a small fee (worth it, since I prefer A), and on Friday he sells me B back for A plus another small fee (worth it, since by then I prefer B), and now I have B again and he has both fees. (4) So by (1), changing your preferences is irrational in exactly the way intransitive preferences are. (5) But surely changing your mind about what to value is sometimes the most rational thing you can do -- that's what growing up, recovering from addiction, or learning to like opera is. (6) So the whole self-transformation industry either sells irrationality or (1) has to go, and she said she'd bet on the former.

The piece practically writes itself if she's right, but I don't want to publish something that condemns every ex-smoker and new parent as a money pump waiting to happen. Which step do I reject? If forced, I'd most like to keep (1), since it really does seem like the same principle that does all the work in the classic synchronic case, and I'd like to keep (5) for obvious reasons -- so I suspect the problem lives somewhere in (3), but when I pressed her she just said "predictable exploitability is predictable exploitability, the calender doesn't matter." Can you tell me exactly what (3) needs to be true in order for the pump to run -- what the broker has to know, what I have to not know or not be able to do -- and whether the synchronic pump against the intransitive agent needs those same things or something weaker? And if I do have to give up one of (1) or (5), what does each option cost me: does weakening (1) let the classic intransitive-preference victim off the hook too, and does biting the bullet on (4) have any defenders I should definately interview for the piece?
