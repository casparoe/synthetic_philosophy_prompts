I am a novelist drafting a campus novel set in a philosophy of science department, and I want an examination scene to feel intellectually authentic. Please write one final-exam essay question for an upper-level undergraduate course on idealization in scientific modeling, together with a model answer and marking scheme.

The question should be based on the following fictional case:

A research group models the spread of rumors through a city by assuming perfect mixing: every resident is equally likely to encounter every other resident each day. It also assumes uniform forgetting: everyone stops repeating a rumor exactly seven days after first hearing it. Both assumptions are known to be false. Older residents encounter relatively few people but repeat rumors for a long time; younger residents encounter many people but quickly lose interest. Nevertheless, across three rumor waves, the model predicts the timing of the citywide peak within one day. When the researchers replace perfect mixing with a realistic contact network while retaining uniform forgetting, the predicted peak arrives too late. When they retain perfect mixing but introduce realistic variation in forgetting, the predicted peak arrives too early. A journalist concludes that the original model has been falsified twice over. One of the researchers replies that the model’s predictive success is what matters and that criticism of its unrealistic assumptions commits a crude misunderstanding of modeling.

The exam question should require students to assess both the journalist’s inference and the researcher’s defense in light of Milton Friedman’s claim that the realism of a theory’s assumptions is less important than the accuracy of its predictions. It should not be answerable merely by summarizing that position. It should force students to explain whether predictive success can license knowingly false assumptions without showing those assumptions to be true, and whether the cancellation of two errors supports or complicates that license.

Have the question introduce the term load-bearing unreality for an idealization whose correction worsens the model unless other parts of the model are also changed, and ask students to determine whether perfect mixing, uniform forgetting, both, or neither qualifies. Require them to consider how the answer may depend on the model’s purpose: predicting citywide peaks, predicting neighborhood-level differences, explaining why the peaks occur, or designing an intervention to slow a rumor. The strongest answers should also propose one further observation or experiment that would discriminate between a genuinely robust model and one whose apparent success depends on offsetting errors.

The completed exam question should be suitable for a 75-minute answer and worth 20 marks. Please provide:

1. The exact wording of the question.
2. A model answer of roughly 650–800 words.
3. A marking scheme organized around five must-hit points worth four marks each.
4. For each point, a one-sentence description of the characteristic mistake a weaker candidate will make.
5. A brief explanation of the specific moves that should distinguish an A answer from a B answer.

Do not include citations, quotations, a bibliography, or a reading list.
