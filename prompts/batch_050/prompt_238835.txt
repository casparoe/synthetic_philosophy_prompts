Earlier this year I read Peter Singer's drowning child essay for my school's philosophy club, and it led to an argument that has now consumed four meetings without moving anyone. The essay's reasoning seems simple: if you would ruin expensive shoes to save a drowning child in front of you, and you could instead save a child's life abroad for roughly the cost of the shoes, consistency pushes you toward giving far more to charity than almost anyone does. Half our club thinks this is basically correct and that the rest of us are just uncomfortable with it. The other half says a morality that treats me as a walking donation pump -- one where every coffee, every hobby, every hour not spent earning to give is a moral failure -- has gone wrong somewhere, even if they cannot say exactly where.

What strikes me is that neither side disputes the facts. We all agree global poverty kills people, that donations can help, that most of us could give much more than we do. And this is not just our club: as far as I can tell from what I've read, philosophers have been having exactly this fight for over fifty years, and the literature mostly seems to restate the same two moves -- the consequentialists press the consistency argument, and the critics reply that morality must leave room for personal projects, relationships, and an ordinary life.

So my question is not which side is right but what the disagreement actually is. Here are the hypotheses I've been turning over, though you should feel free to reject all of them: (a) it's really a factual dispute in disguise about how much good an individual's donations actually do; (b) it's a dispute about what morality is for -- whether it's a system for making the world go best or a set of rules that has to be livable for the beings governed by it; (c) the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, one about what is right and one about what we may blame people for not doing; or (d) it's a collision of bedrock intuitions -- impartiality versus the separateness of persons -- that no argument can settle, only a choice.

Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually argues, tell me which hypothesis (or which combination) best fits, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction, an empirical finding, a change of question -- or argue honestly that none should be expected. Finally, be self-critical about your own diagnosis: tell me which consideration or argument would most likely change your mind about where the disagreement lives, and where your analysis is most likely to be wrong.
