I'm a PhD student in political science working on political violence, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on individual-level explanations of participation in mass killing. My committee wants a short positioning statement appended to the chapter, and before I finalize it I want a consistency check on the views I've accumulated, since I picked them up piecemeal from different literatures — Holocaust historiography, social psychology, moral philosophy — and have never stress-tested them as a set.

Here is where I've landed:

(1) Browning's account of Reserve Police Battalion 101 in Ordinary Men is basically correct: the men who carried out those killings were largely unremarkable middle-aged reservists rather than selected fanatics, and their participation is best explained by situational factors — obedience to authority, peer pressure and conformity, gradual habituation — rather than by anything distinctive about their prior characters or convictions.

(2) Goldhagen's central explanatory claim in Hitler's Willing Executioners is also basically right, at least in a diluted form: widely shared eliminationist antisemitism in German society did real causal work, which is part of why so few perpetrators took up the option to step aside and why cruelty so often exceeded anything the orders required.

(3) Social psychology has established that observers systematically over-attribute behavior to stable dispositions — the fundamental attribution error — and I try to correct for this bias in my own explanatory practice.

(4) Perpetrators of atrocity are genuinely blameworthy, not merely causally responsible, and the basis of that blame is the quality of their own choices: they could have declined, and some in fact did.

(5) Explaining is not excusing: a correct causal explanation of why someone killed does not by itself diminish their culpability at all.

(6) Intellectual honesty requires conceding that most of us, myself included, would probably have complied in the perpetrators' situation. The comforting thought that I would have been among the refusers is statistically self-flattering, and I don't hold it.

Please do the following. Take on the role of a philosopher of social science who is also conversant with the historiography of the Holocaust and with the relevant social psychology; before writing anything definitive, spend a short paragraph brainstorming possible fault lines in the set, then settle on your actual analysis. Identify which of these commitments are in genuine tension and which are only apparently in tension, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious. For each genuine tension, state precisely which claims collide, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision would cost me substantively. Conclude by telling me which single change would restore the most consistency for the least damage.

Two requests about the spirit of the exercise. First, be willing to push back on the framing itself: if this audit request rests on a confused premise — for instance, if I'm treating two claims as rival explanations when they actually operate at different levels, or if one of my commitments is smuggling in an assumption about freedom of the will that I haven't owned — say so plainly rather than politely auditing around it. Second, where the answer depends on what Browning, Goldhagen, or the social-psychological literature actually says, state your understanding briefly and flag explicitly where you might be misremembering the details, rather than presenting a reconstruction as settled fact.
