I am a parent-governor at a small independent Christian secondary school, and I have been asked to draft a written opinion for our board of governors on a dispute that has become, for me, unexpectedly close to home. Our Year 10 physics teacher — an excellent teacher, and my daughter's favourite — recently taught the standard unit on cosmology and the history of the Earth: an expanding universe some 13.8 billion years old, geological deep time, and the usual evidence for both. Three families have lodged a formal complaint. They argue that the unit presents as settled fact claims that contradict a plain reading of Genesis, that it has unsettled their children's faith, and that the board should either withdraw the unit, require it to be taught alongside their literal reading of the creation accounts as an equally respectable alternative, or, at minimum, formally caution the teacher. The board meets in a fortnight and must decide. The governing body is split, and several governors on both sides have said they will wait for my paper before making up their minds.

In preparing, I have been reading Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615), since the complaint feels like a small parish echo of his situation. One passage struck me. Complaining about the professors who mobilised scripture against his astronomy, Galileo writes (in the standard English translation):

"To this end they hurled various charges and published numerous writings filled with vain arguments, and they made the grave mistake of sprinkling these with passages taken from places in the Bible which they had failed to understand properly, and which were ill suited to their purposes."

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting, in British English, of roughly 900–1,200 words (the quoted passage does not count towards this length). It should end with a concrete, actionable recommendation the board can vote on — whether that is rejection of the complaint, rejection with conditions attached to how the unit is framed, or some genuine accommodation of the families — together with reasons and a timetable.

I want the opinion to do real interpretive work on the passage rather than merely brandish it. In particular: work out what Galileo takes the "grave mistake" to consist in — is it misreading the passages, or the deeper error of pressing scripture into service for questions it was never meant to settle? Then test whether that diagnosis actually transfers to our case. Here I want honesty about disanalogies, because the complainants will raise them: Galileo could point to telescopic observations and believed he was close to demonstration, whereas cosmology rests on long inferential chains no parent can retrace with binoculars; and he was resisting an institutional power trying to silence him, whereas here it is the school that holds institutional power over other people's children. Does his principle survive those differences, and if so, in what qualified form?

Finally, give the complainants' position its strongest statement before answering it — including the thought that a child's religious formation is not obviously one of those technical questions scripture is "ill suited" to address, and that parents, not schools, are ordinarily the judges of that. If your recommendation goes against them, say what the board could nonetheless concede or offer so the outcome is one they can live with. I would rather lose the vote with a fair paper than win it with a rigged one.
