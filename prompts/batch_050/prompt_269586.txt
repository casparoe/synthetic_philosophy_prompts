'What if everyone did that?' I first heard it from my mother when I cut across a neighbor's lawn, and I heard it again last week in a game theory reading group, where someone deployed it to argue that defecting in a one-shot, anonymous prisoner's dilemma is irrational: since the other player is presumably reasoning the same way you are, your choice and theirs will match, so you should pick the outcome you would want both of you to reach. Lay out, as a menu, the different precise claims that this one question can be making. I can already see a few candidates and want them developed and compared rather than merely listed: the empirical prediction that your behavior will be copied, or that enough people already behave this way for the practice to collapse under one more participant; the Kantian thought that a principle of action you cannot consistently will as a universal law is thereby ruled out, consequences aside; the fairness objection that you are exempting yourself from a rule whose benefits you continue to enjoy; and the stranger claim, which I gather has defenders in the decision theory literature, that in symmetric situations your decision is somehow bound up with the decisions of agents built like you, so that you ought to choose as if you were choosing for all of them at once. For each reading: state the claim precisely enough that it has truth conditions, say whether it is trivial, substantive, or false, and say whether it actually supports the use the slogan was being put to in that instance. My mother's use seems to need only the empirical reading (one lawn-crosser does no harm, many ruin the lawn). The reading group's use seems to require the fourth, since anonymity and one-shotness rule out any causal channel of copying. Does the fourth reading collapse into ordinary reasoning about agents whose behavior you have independent evidence about, or is it a genuinely separate principle -- and if separate, is it defensible in the strictly one-shot case, or does it smuggle in cooperation under the guise of rationality? Finally, address a problem I suspect undermines all the readings at once: what 'everyone doing that' amounts to depends on how the action is described -- crossing the lawn, crossing the lawn when late for class, crossing the lawn once per person per lifetime -- and a sufficiently clever description can make universalizing anything look harmless or anything look ruinous. Is there a principled way to fix the relevant description, or does the slogan only ever function as a device for forcing your interlocutor to supply one?
