I recently finished a course on risk regulation, and I've been arguing about it ever since with a friend who works in pharmaceutical policy, because the more I look at the actual rules, the less coherent the underlying philosophy seems. Consider the landscape. In the European Union, the precautionary principle is an official principle of environmental and public-health policy: where there are credible grounds for concern about serious or irreversible harm, regulators may restrict a substance or activity without waiting for scientific certainty, and under the REACH regime chemical producers must register their products and demonstrate that their intended uses are safe, rather than regulators having to prove them dangerous. Genetically modified crops require pre-market authorisation. The United States splits the difference in a curious way: a new medicine cannot be sold until its manufacturer has shown it to be safe and effective, whereas a dietary supplement can be sold freely until the regulator builds a case that it is dangerous. And then there is criminal law, where the very same societies run the default the other way round entirely -- the accused is presumed innocent, the state bears the whole burden of proof, and we officially accept the maxim that it is better for the guilty to go free than for the innocent to be convicted. So the state presumes chemicals guilty until proven innocent, supplements innocent until proven guilty, and citizens innocent beyond a reasonable doubt.

What I want to know is what theory of rational decision under uncertainty this patchwork presupposes, if anything. Does precautionary regulation presuppose that expected-utility maximisation is simply the wrong standard when probabilities are contested and harms irreversible -- a genuinely non-Bayesian stance -- or could a Bayesian reach the same prescriptions just by plugging in an asymmetric loss function, in which case the practice presupposes something about the relative costs of false positives and false negatives rather than anything deep about uncertainty? Does the asymmetry between pre-market and post-market regimes presuppose a moral difference between harms of commission and harms of omission -- that a death caused by an approved product counts for more than a statistically predictable death caused by a delayed one -- and is that a defensible commitment or just a bias the institutions happen to embody? Critics also raise a well-known objection that a strong precautionary principle paralyses itself, since acting carries risks as well as refraining; does the actual practice presuppose some answer to that objection, and if so, what?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, but then do three further things. First, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: I suspect several rival theories (maximin under deep uncertainty, asymmetric expected utility, a deontological constraint on imposing risks on non-consenting parties) could each license much of the same behaviour, so tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Second, tell me what determines which way the default points: is there a single coherent principle under which the burden of proof lands on chemical producers, supplement regulators, and prosecutors respectively -- does it track who has the power to generate the evidence, who stands to profit, whose liberty is at stake, or the irreversibility of the worst outcome -- and if no such principle exists, say so plainly. Third, tell me what would concretely have to change if regulators abandoned the precautionary stance and instead ran a symmetric cost-benefit analysis that priced delay and blocked benefits on the same scale as harms: which familiar decisions would come out differently, and would anything we currently take for granted survive the switch?
