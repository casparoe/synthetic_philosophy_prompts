I've become fairly convinced of the futarchy position: that representative democracies would make better policy if they retained elected institutions for choosing the goals (a wellbeing metric, say, or a set of targets) but delegated the choice of means to conditional prediction markets, adopting whichever policy the markets imply will best realise the chosen goals. The rough thought is that markets aggregate dispersed information and punish confident error in a way that elections, committees, and expert panels structurally cannot.

What would count against this view? I'd like the defeaters organised from decisive to merely awkward, and in each case a statement of what the world would have to look like for the objection to succeed empirically rather than merely in principle. Please cover at least: manipulation and the conditions under which a wealthy actor could profitably move a policy market; the thin-markets problem for obscure but consequential policy questions; the objection that markets price expected outcomes conditional on adoption in ways that break down when the market itself determines adoption; the worry that the values half of the division inherits every problem of democratic politics, so that the proposal relocates rather than resolves the difficulty; and the objection that some policy domains have outcome horizons too long or too entangled for any market signal to be legible. Also tell me which objections a committed futarchist would themselves concede as damaging, which they would dismiss and on what grounds, and whether the view has been protected by qualifications (markets as one input among several, only for low-stakes decisions, and so on) to the point where it no longer risks anything. Please answer in British English.
