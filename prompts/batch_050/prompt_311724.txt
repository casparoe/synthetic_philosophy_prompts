You are a professional philosopher with expertise in population ethics and procreative ethics, and you have served for years on the examination committee of a rigorous undergraduate philosophy program. I am setting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on population ethics, and I need your help designing one essay question on the procreation asymmetry -- the view, standardly traced to discussions in Parfit and since widely debated, that we have a moral reason not to bring into existence people whose lives would be miserable, but no comparably strong moral reason to bring into existence people merely because their lives would be happy.

Please work carefully and in stages: first, brainstorm (briefly, in a few bullet points) which tensions and cruxes in the asymmetry literature are rich enough to distinguish students who understand the issue from students who have memorized the label; then settle on one crux and write the deliverables below.

The key constraint: the question must not be answerable by reciting the asymmetry and its standard motivations (the non-identity problem, person-affecting intuitions). I want it to force students to do something harder. Specifically, I would like the question to center on the tension between the asymmetry and the common judgment that human extinction -- or, less dramatically, a policy that sharply reduces the number of future happy people -- would be bad beyond its effects on currently existing people. If there is no reason to create happy people, why would it matter, impersonally, if they never exist? A good question should make students decide whether this tension is real, and what has to give.

Deliverables:

1. One essay question answerable in 90 minutes, incorporating a brief concrete scenario (e.g., a fertility-subsidy policy or a last-generation scenario) so students cannot answer purely in the abstract. The question should have no more than three explicitly numbered tasks.

2. A model answer of roughly 600-700 words. It must at minimum: state the asymmetry precisely and distinguish its reasons-reading from its value-reading; diagnose exactly where the extinction intuition conflicts with it; and assess at least three ways out (for instance: biting the bullet and denying the impersonal badness of extinction; distinguishing reasons to create from reasons to preserve or continue; abandoning or weakening the asymmetry by admitting weak pro tanto reasons to create happy lives). It should take a defensible position, not merely survey.

3. A marking scheme out of 20 marks, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth. For each point, add a one-line note on how candidates typically fumble it (e.g., conflating the asymmetry with the non-identity problem, or answering a question about reasons with an observation about value). Conclude the scheme with two or three sentences stating what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of the moves each contains.

Format the question as it would appear on the exam paper, then the model answer, then the marking scheme.
