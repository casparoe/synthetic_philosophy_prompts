Okay so I'm a novelist, fifteen years in the trade, and I've stumbled into a philosophical argument I only half understand, and I want it translated into my home turf. Here's the situation. Last year I wrote an alternate ending to one of my novels for an anniversary edition -- in the original, my protagonist leaves her husband; in the new ending she stays. And my readers got genuinely angry, not "I preferred the old ending" angry but "that isn't HER, the woman who stays is a different person wearing her name" angry. Then I watch fan fiction writers cheerfully take characters and put them in coffee shops in modern-day Chicago and everyone agrees it's somehow still the same character. So which is it? What makes a character in another version of the story the same person, and when have you changed so much that you've just written someone else?

I gather the philosophers have a fight about exactly this. From what I've pieced together (correct me if I've got it wrong): David Lewis says each person only ever exists in one possible world, and when we talk about what "could have happened" to someone, we're really talking about a counterpart of them in another world -- somebody similar enough in the right respects. And Saul Kripke says no, the actual person herself shows up in the other possible worlds; when we ask what could've happened to her we mean HER, not some lookalike. That's counterpart theory vs. transworld identity, I think.

Here's what I want. Restate this whole debate in the vocabulary I actually live in: drafts, revisions, alternate endings, canon, sequels, spin-offs, fan fiction, "out of character" complaints, the works. Be as faithful as you can. I suspect my angry readers were being accidental Kripkeans and fanfic culture is secretly Lewisian, but I want to see whether that hunch survives a careful translation rather than just being a cute line for interviews.

Then -- and this is the part I actually care about most -- give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' disagreement map cleanly onto things novelists already argue about (say, whether a sequel with a radically rewritten backstory still counts as being about the same character), which parts you had to fudge to fit into fiction-talk, and which parts just have no counterpart at all in how writers and readers treat characters. And for whatever doesn't survive: is that because my novelist's vocabulary is missing something the philosophers are tracking, or because part of their disagreement is the kind of thing that evaporates when you stop talking about "possible worlds" and start talking about actual made-up people? I honestly can't tell, and it matters to me -- if the deep answer is that "same character" is just similarity in respects we happen to care about, that changes what I owe readers who loved the original ending.

One more thing: don't just tell me which philosopher is right. I want to know what my own practice -- alternate endings, fanfic tolerance, the "that's not her" reaction -- commits me to, and whether those commitments are even consistent with each other.
