So I'm a backend engineer and lately I've gotten sucked into the governance side of crypto stuff, mostly because my team had to write upgrade rules for a smart contract system and we kept arguing about whether the contract itself should be able to change its own upgrade rules, or whether there has to be some "outer layer" that can't be touched from inside. Someone in the thread mentioned Nomic, this game Peter Suber invented where changing the rules is itself a move in the game, and how it supposedly exposes weird paradoxes about self-amending systems -- like, what happens if the players amend away the rule that lets them amend rules, or pass an amendment that contradicts the amendment procedure itself.

Anyway that got me thinking about the whole DAO hack thing on Ethereum back in 2016. My rough understanding: the DAO was this big investment fund running entirely as a smart contract, someone found a reentrancy-ish bug and drained a huge chunk of the ether, and the Ethereum community eventually hard-forked the chain to effectively undo the theft. A minority refused the fork on "code is law" grounds and kept the old chain going as Ethereum Classic. I might have some details off but that's the gist as I remember it.

Here's what I want: analyze that episode through the lens of the Nomic stuff -- self-amending rules and the paradoxes of self-reference in legal systems. Where exactly was the "rules of the game" in this case? Was the code the constitution, or was the social layer (devs, miners, exchanges) the real sovereign that could amend anything, with the code just being ordinary legislation? Did the fork basically reveal that "immutable" systems always have an implicit extra rule -- "we can change everything if enough of us agree" -- and if so, is that a solution to the self-amendment problem or just a way of hiding it? Also tell me where the analogy breaks: Nomic players all know they're playing a game, DAO investors maybe didn't sign up for a constitutional crisis. And run it both directions -- does the episode vindicate the idea that self-amending systems need an unamendable core, or does it show the opposite, that pretending any layer is unamendable is a fiction? Please be concrete about which parts of the episode the theory explains well and which it just can't say anything about, rather than forcing the fit.
