I teach an undergraduate course in epistemology and the philosophy of logic, and next week we reach Carroll's tortoise — the puzzle in which a patient interlocutor accepts the premises of a modus ponens but refuses to infer the conclusion until the inference rule itself is written in as a further premise, at which point the same refusal simply repeats one level down. My students have just completed a unit on formal validity, so they arrive confident that a valid argument is one whose conclusion follows from its premises; my aim is to unsettle exactly that confidence without leaving them thinking logic rests on nothing. Plan the session for me: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what each depends on (I suspect they need a felt sense of the difference between following a rule and stating a rule before the regress bites, but tell me if I have the dependencies wrong); the one carrying example to build the whole session around — whether it should be modus ponens itself or something more mundane, and what it must contain and must lack to do the job; the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface (that the puzzle shows deduction is ultimately unjustified, that adding the rule as a premise resolves it if we just insist firmly enough, that the tortoise is merely being obstinate and can be ignored), and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than left to harden; the check that would tell me they have grasped the distinction between justifying an inference and justifying an inference rule, rather than merely reciting it; and what I should openly decline to cover at this level — the literature on rule-circularity, meaning-constituting inferences, and the like. One more request: if my framing of the puzzle, or my sense of what the students need, rests on a confusion, say so plainly rather than building the plan around it. Please answer in American English.
