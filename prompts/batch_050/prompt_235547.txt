Two of my grad students, Maya and Tomasz, got into it in the hallway after my ancient philosophy seminar and they're still emailing me about it, so I want a referee. We had been reading Aristotle's *Physics*, Book VI, in the Hardie and Gaye translation, and the fight is over what exactly Aristotle is doing in this bit from VI.1, where he's arguing that a continuum can't be built out of indivisible parts:

"the motion will consist not of motions but of starts, and will take place by a thing's having completed a motion without being in motion: for on this assumption it has completed its passage through A without passing through it. So it will be possible for a thing to have completed a walk without ever walking"

Here's the disagreement as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Maya's opening move: this passage is Aristotle's real answer to Zeno's dichotomy paradox, and it's a good one. Zeno's runner only looks doomed if you picture the run as a series of discrete little tasks -- get to the halfway point, then the next halfway point, etc. Aristotle's reductio shows that motion doesn't decompose into "starts" like that. There's no first sub-motion to complete, so there is no infinite to-do list. The runner just... walks.

(2) Tomasz replied that she's got it exactly backwards. The passage only rules out *atomic* motions, motions with no parts. But Zeno doesn't need atoms at all -- he needs infinite divisibility, and Aristotle hands it to him in the same chapter when he says everything continuous is divisible into divisibles that are infinitely divisible. So the passage doesn't answer Zeno, it loads his gun: if the interval really divides without end, the runner really does pass through infinitely many segments.

(3) Maya's comeback: Tomasz is conflating actual and potential division. For Aristotle the halves exist only potentially until something actualizes them -- a stop, a mark, a count. The runner never stops at the midpoints, so no infinite series of completions ever becomes actual. The absurdity in the quoted sentence ("completed a walk without ever walking") is precisely what you get if you treat the potential divisions as actual tasks, and that absurdity is Zeno's problem, not Aristotle's.

(4) Tomasz said the potential/actual move is a promissory note. Passing a point seems like just as much of an actualization as stopping at one -- the runner really is at the halfway mark at some time, whether or not he pauses there. And anyway, he said, we now know from the supertask literature that completing infinitely many operations in finite time isn't obviously incoherent (the lamp, the bouncing ball, all that), so even if Aristotle's distinction were coherent it might be unnecessary. Zeno loses, if he loses, for reasons Aristotle couldn't have given.

(5) Maya's last email said Tomasz's supertask point concedes Zeno's framing -- it accepts that there IS an infinite sequence of things to get through and then argues you can get through it -- whereas the whole force of the quoted passage is to deny there's a sequence in the first place. A walk isn't the sum of its sub-walks the way a pile is the sum of its stones. She also accused him of grading Aristotle by the standards of the calculus, which she called "a category error, bascially."

Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the five moves, tell me whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-incomplete, and say why. Two things I especially want settled: first, what work is the quoted sentence actually doing in Aristotle's argument -- is it aimed at Zeno's sort of reasoning or only at atomists about motion? Second, whose mistake is worse: Maya's (if she's reading modern measure-theoretic ideas back into 4th century BC), or Tomasz's (if he's missing what the potential/actual distinction was supposed to buy)? Feel free to say they're both missing something if they are.
