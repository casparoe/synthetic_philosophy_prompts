For an upper-level political theory seminar, construct a decision-tree taxonomy of normative positions on constitutional monarchy in a democracy. Use a fictional, stable parliamentary state in which the hereditary monarch formally appoints the prime minister, grants royal assent to legislation, dissolves the legislature, receives confidential ministerial briefings, and is conventionally expected to remain politically neutral. The constitution does not specify when, if ever, the monarch may refuse a ministerial request.

Begin the tree with the following challenge: monarchy cannot be justified merely by its longevity, popularity, ceremonial appeal, or economic benefits; either it must perform a democratically valuable function that an elected or appointed office could not perform at least as well, or hereditary continuity must be defended as valuable in itself.

Build the taxonomy from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a ready-made list of schools. Use 8–11 branch nodes and 8–10 leaves. Each branch node should be a concrete question that a respondent could answer without knowing the name of any position, and each branch should identify the principle that divides there. The tree must distinguish, at minimum:

- political power from informal influence
- procedural judgment from judgment about the substantive merits of policy
- neutrality from silence, passivity, or deference to ministers
- a narrowly bounded reserve power from a general constitutional veto
- legitimacy grounded in heredity, in demonstrated performance, or in periodic democratic ratification
- opposition to monarchy on democratic principle from opposition because monarchy fails its supposed practical purpose

At least five of the following situations must be used as diagnostic cases:

1. A prime minister who has evidently lost parliamentary confidence requests a dissolution in order to prevent an alternative majority from forming.
2. An inconclusive election leaves two plausible coalition governments, and the monarch’s choice between them may determine which one takes office.
3. A democratically enacted bill is legally valid but seriously restricts a fundamental right.
4. During an emergency, the government asks the monarch to approve a postponement of the next election.
5. The monarch privately warns ministers against a policy, threatens to withhold assent, and allows the warning to become public.
6. The monarch wishes to speak publicly on a morally urgent but politically contested issue.
7. A monarch’s personal misconduct damages the office’s credibility without violating the constitution.
8. Voters are offered a referendum replacing the monarch with an elected president possessing identical formal powers.

The leaves should include descriptively named versions of pure ceremonial retentionism, procedural umpire, constitutional guardian, civic educator or soft-power defender, hereditary trustee, conditional democratic retentionism, principled republicanism, and instrumental abolitionism. Add further leaves where the branching questions generate important possibilities. For every leaf, provide a one- or two-sentence statement of the position and its most awkward commitment.

If a coherent combination of answers has no obvious defender, mark it as unoccupied and explain briefly whether it seems incoherent, politically unattractive, or merely underexplored. Flag any leaves that the tree cannot reliably separate, especially where different theories recommend the same conduct in ordinary times but diverge only during a crisis. Do not organize the answer around the United Kingdom, Scandinavia, Japan, particular royal families, historical anecdotes, citations, or public-opinion claims.
