I work in a philosophy of language reading group where two-dimensional semantics is our common currency, and last week a colleague pressed an argument on me that I have been unable to shake. It runs as follows. (1) If a complete microphysical description of the world, call it P, is ideally coherently conceivable while some further truth Q is denied, then P does not a priori entail Q. (2) If P does not a priori entail Q, then there is a metaphysically possible world in which P holds and Q fails; his gloss was that any counterexample would have to be a necessary a posteriori truth, and on the two-dimensional framework every such truth has a contingent primary intension, so ideal reflection at the level of primary intensions cannot miss genuine metaphysical impossibilities. (3) A world physically identical to ours but lacking phenomenal consciousness is ideally coherently conceivable -- not merely conceivable in the loose way that water-without-H2O was conceivable to the pre-chemist, but conceivable even after ideal reflection, since no amount of a priori reasoning from P seems to yield facts about what it is like. (4) Therefore P does not necessitate the phenomenal truths, and materialism about consciousness is false. Each step looked defensible in isolation, and the argument is, as far as I can tell, the standard two-dimensional route to the conclusion, which I had thought I understood.

What unsettles me is that the schema seems to prove too much. A thoroughgoing mechanist in 1750 could have run the identical argument with life in place of consciousness: living behaviour is not a priori deducible from the then-available physics of matter in motion; a world of mere mechanism without vital spirit is conceivable to him even on reflection; so mechanism about life is false. The vitalist was wrong, and not because his physics was incomplete in a way that blocks the inference -- for if the inference only goes through when the reasoner's P happens to be the true completed physics, the argument seems to beg the question against any future completed neuroscience that would let us see, on ideal reflection, why P must give rise to experience. My colleague insists the cases are disanalogous: with full physical knowledge, life is a priori recoverable from P, whereas consciousness, he says, is not recoverable from any P however complete. But I cannot see how to establish that asymmetry without already assuming the falsity of materialism, which is the conclusion under dispute.

I would like to keep the two-dimensional apparatus itself -- I rely on the primary/secondary distinction constantly and do not want an answer that amounts to scrapping the framework wholesale. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look acceptable? If you reject (2), explain what becomes of the framework's bridge between ideal conceivability and metaphysical possibility; if you reject (3), explain what grounds we could have for confidence that our zombie conception is ideal rather than merely ignorant; and in either case say what the rejection costs elsewhere. Please end with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing the number of the step you reject and, in a few words, the reason.
