So I'm a novelist, mid-career, and I need your brain on something that's been bugging me for weeks. Pretend you're a philosopher who specializes in aesthetics and questions about art and authenticity, and actually think carefully about this instead of just giving me fortune-cookie advice.

Here's the situation. About a year ago I spent a genuinely stupid amount of money -- like, skip-a-vacation money -- on what was sold to me as a first edition of a famous 1920s novel. This particular book is a big deal to me personally. I read it at nineteen, it's the reason I started writing, and I liked the idea of having one of the copies that actually rolled off the presses back then sitting on my desk while I work. Silly, maybe, but it meant something.

Last month an expert tells me it's a fake. A really good one. It was made by photographing a genuine first edition page by page and reproducing everything -- paper, binding, even the foxing spots. She told me most dealers would never catch it. The seller (who seems genuinely mortified, I think he got duped too) offered me a full refund and said I can keep the facsimile if I want.

So now the object on my desk is the exact same object it was last month. Same look, same feel, same smell even. Nothing about it changed -- only what I know about it changed. My buddy Dave, who took one philosophy class in college and never lets anyone forget it, says I'd be irrational to feel any differently about it, since all my experiences of it are identical either way. And honestly I can't fully answer him, because here's the part that really twists me up: it's a printed book, not a painting. Even a REAL first edition is just one of thousands of identical copies mass-produced by a machine. The author never touched my copy. So if I'm honest, what exactly was I even valuing in the first place? It's not like owning the Mona Lisa where there's one physical thing the artist labored over.

But at the same time... if Dave's right, then it seems like nothing about an object's history could ever matter, and that seems nuts to me. People care whether the flag on the moon is the real flag. I care.

Please structure your answer like this, in this order:

1. The Gut Check -- one short paragraph, your honest first reaction to whether I'm being silly.
2. Naming the Problem -- one paragraph, maybe two, identifying the actual philosophical issue underneath my situation. Don't just describe my story back at me; tell me what general question this is an instance of and whether philosophers have argued about it.
3. Dave's Case -- one or two paragraphs making the strongest possible version of the argument that I shouldn't care, steelmanned, including the point about printed books being copies all the way down.
4. The Other Side -- two or three paragraphs on why it might be perfectly reasonable to value the genuine copy over a perfect facsimile. Engage seriously with the printed-book wrinkle, because I think it's the hardest part -- don't pretend it's a painting.
5. The Verdict -- one or two paragraphs where you actually take a side on whether my attachment survives scrutiny, and then tell me concretely what I should do with the refund offer and the fake. Don't wimp out with a both-sides shrug.
