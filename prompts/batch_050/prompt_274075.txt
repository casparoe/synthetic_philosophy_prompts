At a philosophy society social, two members fell to arguing about a mutual friend's alarm-clock habit. The friend sets his alarm for 6 a.m. each night, sincerely intending to get up and work, and each morning switches it off and sleeps until nine. Daniel called this textbook akrasia: the friend acts against his own better judgement, and the nightly alarm-setting shows what that judgement is. Mara disagreed. At 6 a.m., she said, his desire for sleep is simply stronger, so he acts on his current best judgement on both occasions; there is no single better judgement being defied, only a preference that shifts as the options draw near, exactly as hyperbolic discounting predicts, and a shift in preference is not a failure of will. Daniel replied that the predictable shift is precisely the problem: a rational agent who knows his 6 a.m. self will defect ought to bind himself in advance, and the friend's (admittedly failed) experiments with precommitment apps show that he himself endorses the evening self over the morning one. Mara answered that precommitment is merely one self coercing another and proves nothing about which judgement is better; the evening self has no special authority, and calling the morning choice 'weak' quietly assumes that the far-sighted self is the real one. Daniel's final move was that Mara's picture makes regret unintelligible, yet the friend bitterly regrets snoozing by mid-morning; Mara countered that regret shows only that the preferences have reversed once more, not that any of them was mistaken.

Neither would concede, and I stayed out of it.
