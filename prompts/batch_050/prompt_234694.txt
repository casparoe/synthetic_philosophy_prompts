So I've been chewing on this question of whether you can coherently assign probabilities to your own actions, and I notice my gut gives different answers in different cases. I want to know what principle, if any, is behind my gut. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

- Odds I'm still a vegetarian in five years: no problem, I'd say maybe 70%. Feels like a normal empirical question.
- Odds I take the Boston job over staying put, asked while I'm literally sitting here weighing the two offers: this one feels impossible to answer. Not just hard -- like, giving a number would end the deliberation, and I'm not done deliberating, so any number I gave would be a lie about where things stand.
- Odds I have a third glass of wine tonight: I can estimate it, around 80% honestly, but saying the number out loud feels like giving myself permission. Like the prediction does some of the drinking's work for it.
- Odds that in the chess position in front of me I end up pushing the bishop: I cannot do this while I'm still figuring out which move is best. The question "which will I play" just collapses into "which should I play." But I have zero trouble predicting what my opponent will play.
- Odds I actually get up when the alarm goes off at 6: about 50-50, and I've set a backup alarm at 6:20. But I also fully intend to get up at 6 tonight. These two attitudes feel like they're fighting each other.
- Odds I'd keep my friend's secret under really serious social pressure: I refuse to put a number on this below like 95%. Saying 60% feels disloyal somehow, like I've already half-betrayed her by admitting it.
- Odds I'll vote for the incumbent in November, asked before I've watched the debates: fine, I'll give a number based on my past voting and the polls. But I expect that same question will feel unanswerable in the voting booth.

So the pattern seems to be: predictions about distant-future me are fine, predictions about what I'm currently deliberating are not. Except the wine case and the alarm case are both about tonight, and one feels fine while the other feels like it's corrupting something. And the secret case breaks the pattern entirely -- there the problem isn't that prediction is impossible, it's that honest prediction feels morally wrong.

Is there a single principle that delivers all these verdicts? Something about when a prediction of your own behavior can play a role in your deliberation versus when it has to stay outside it? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon if it can't save them all, and tell me what it says about two cases I haven't listed: a surgeon asked mid-operation to estimate the odds she'll choose to amputate, and whether it was coherent of me to buy the good running shoes *because* I predicted the expensive purchase would make future-me more likely to actually run.
