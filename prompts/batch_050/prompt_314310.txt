I’m a PhD student in linguistics, and in a semantics seminar last week we got stuck on generics like “ducks lay eggs” and “mosquitoes carry malaria.” Someone ended the discussion by saying “the exception proves the rule” — apparently meaning that male ducks, infertile ducks, and the many mosquitoes that don’t carry malaria don’t really count against the generic. This annoyed me because I’ve also heard the slogan used to mean almost the opposite: that an exception tests a rule, exposes its limits, or is only an exception because a rule already exists.

Can you unpack what precise claims that proverb could be making? Lay out the main readings as a menu, from trivial to philosophically substantive, and give each one a verdict: platitude, defensible thesis, plausible only in special cases, or just false. Please connect the readings to different kinds of generics — statistical ones, normative ones, stereotypical ones, and cases where the relevant property belongs only to a salient subclass. I’m especially interested in whether exceptions can ever provide positive evidence for a generic rather than merely failing to falsify it.

Also, here’s a case from a dataset I’m building: suppose a tiny island community says “harbor dogs guard the fishing boats.” Only about a third of the dogs actually do this, but nearly all the dogs who sleep by the harbor do, and the practice exists because people expect those dogs to guard the boats. Is “the exception proves the rule” doing any coherent work there, or does it just blur together prevalence, social roles, and defeasible expectations? Feel free to distinguish a useful technical precisification of the slogan from the conversational move people are usually making with it.

Please say how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and end by singling out the one point you’re least sure of. I want to know where the argument is most likely to be shaky, not just the clean taxonomy.
