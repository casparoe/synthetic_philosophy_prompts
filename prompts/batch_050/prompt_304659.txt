I'm a software engineer working adjacent to ML, and the whole alignment problem thing — how do you get an extremely powerful AI to reliably do what you want when you can't write the goal down perfectly, it'll game whatever proxy you hand it, and even if it behaves flawlessly in training it might have internalized some other goal that only shows up after deployment (the mesa-optimizer worry) — keeps reminding me of political philosophy I only half remember. Hobbes basically tried to solve an alignment problem for the most powerful agent in his world, right? The sovereign gets near-absolute power, and the setup is supposed to deliver peace and stability anyway.

So if Hobbes could see the modern AI alignment debate, what would he make of it? A few things I'm especilly curious about:

- Would he think approaches like constitutional AI, where you give the model written principles to follow, are hopeless — since he argued that covenants without the sword are just words, i.e. rules mean nothing to an agent nothing has power over?
- Would his actual advice be "control, not values" — don't bother making the AI good, just keep it weak, sandboxed, dependent, with the sword held over it forever? And is that even coherent for something we'd want to be generally useful?
- Does his framework just break at superintelligence? His state-of-nature argument leaned on humans being roughly equal in ability, which is what makes conflict rational in the first place. If the AI is vastly smarter than any sovereign who could hold the sword, does Hobbes have anything left to say besides "then don't build it"?

Feel free to tell me where I've mangled Hobbes — my knowledge of him is basically one podcast and half a wikipedia article.
