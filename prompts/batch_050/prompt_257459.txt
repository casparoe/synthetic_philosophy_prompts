Last month I had a tooth pulled and on the sedation combo they gave me I could feel everything -- the pressure, the drilling, something I'd probably call pain -- but it just didn't bother me at all, like watching it happen to a stranger. That sent me down a rabbit hole on pain asymbolia, and now I've been reading this old fight about what makes pain bad and I honestly cannot figure out why it hasn't been settled in like fifty years.

As I understand the two sides: one camp says pain's badness is just in how it feels -- the unpleasantness is built into the sensation itself. So if a patient with asymbolia (or someone on morphine) says they feel pain but it doesn't bother them, then either they aren't really feeling pain, or the badness is still there in the experience and they're just failing to register it, maybe because of the brain damage or the drugs. The other camp says the case shows the sensation and the badness come apart: the sensory part is one thing, and the suffering comes from a seperate attitude -- disliking it, wanting it gone -- and asymbolics have the first without the second. And then both sides just redescribe the same clinical cases in their preferred vocabulary, over and over, and nobody budges.

My tentative diagnosis, which you should feel free to shred: it's partly a dispute about which cases count as data. The sensation-side mostly leans on introspecting ordinary pain -- attend to your headache, the badness is right there in the feeling, you don't find a neutral sensation plus a separate layer of dislike. The attitude-side leans on weird clinical edge cases that are murky as hell -- brain-injured patients, drug reports, people who might be confabulating or who have flattened affect about everything, not just pain (asymbolics often still flinch and guard the limb, which cuts in confusing directions). And I suspect "unpleasant" is doing double duty the whole time: sometimes it means a felt quality of the sensation, sometimes it just means "disliked," and each side slides between these to make the other look wrong.

So what's the real structure of this impasse? Verbal dispute, disagreement about admissible evidence, or a genuinely substantive one? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress -- a sharper distinction, better phenomenology of the edge cases, some empirical result -- or should we conclude the question "what makes pain bad" is ill-posed as stated and split it into questions that can each be answered?
