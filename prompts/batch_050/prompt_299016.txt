I am a PhD student in philosophy (working on formal epistemology), and I have been asked to contribute one question to this year's qualifying exam in epistemic game theory, aimed at students who have completed a graduate course covering Aumann's agreement theorem, the muddy children puzzle, and the coordinated attack problem. The exam committee wants a question that cannot be answered by reciting the definition of common knowledge or rehearsing the standard puzzles.

To that end, I have devised a scenario I call the Relay Puzzle, and I would like the exam question to be built around it. Here is the scenario: three players sit in a line, A–B–C. A referee privately flips a fair coin. If heads, she shows a red card to A only; if tails, she shows nothing to anyone. A then has the option to tap B on the shoulder (a public act that C also observes), and B may then tap C. A player who has observed the red card, or who has been tapped, knows the coin came up heads; everyone is rational, this is common knowledge, and players may stay silent even when they know the coin came up heads. The twist is that tapping is costly in a way that matters: each player receives a payoff only if the last player in the line, C, correctly announces the coin's state, and any player who taps without having certain knowledge that the coin is heads forfeits their own payoff. Taps are irrevocable and instantaneous; there is one round only.

Draft the exam question around this scenario, with the following requirements. First, the question should ask candidates to determine whether C can ever come to know the coin is heads, and to state precisely which levels of knowledge (mutual knowledge of order n versus genuine common knowledge) the relay does and does not generate — the trap I want caught is the assumption that a public, universally observed tap creates common knowledge of what prompted it. Second, the question should ask candidates to explain why the one-round, costly-signal structure changes the answer relative to the standard muddy children setup, where repeated public announcements suffice. Third, it should require candidates to connect the puzzle to the coordinated attack problem: specifically, whether any finite protocol of public acts can substitute for common knowledge when payoffs depend on terminal certainty, and what this implies about the relationship between common knowledge and "approximately common" knowledge for coordination.

Please provide: (1) the question text itself, written in exam register and answerable in 90 minutes; (2) a model answer of roughly 700–900 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 points, broken into five must-hit components, where for each component you indicate the marks available, what a full-marks answer must contain, and the specific error a borderline answer will typically make (for example, conflating common belief with common knowledge, or treating the tap's publicity as settling what motivated it). The scheme should be usable by a grader who has not seen my intended solution.
