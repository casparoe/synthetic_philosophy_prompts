I'm in my fifties, with no formal education in philosophy, but I've been turning over the question of what adult children owe their parents ever since my own father moved into care. I've arrived at a set of views I want stress-tested before I trust them further. Here they are:

1. The ledger model of filial duty is false. Parents choose to have a child and owe that child decent care; a good childhood therefore creates no debt that the child later repays. Any talk of children "owing" their parents for what was done for them is a category error.

2. What grown children do owe their parents is essentially what friends owe one another: time, care, and loyalty grounded in an ongoing relationship. And as with friendship, the duties lapse when the relationship genuinely ends — a parent can, through sustained cruelty or indifference, come to be owed nothing at all.

3. I myself owe my father real care in his decline — visiting, overseeing his arrangements, being reachable — even though he was cold and withholding throughout my childhood and we haven't had anything resembling a relationship in fifteen years. This is not merely a feeling I have. I think I would be doing wrong if I walked away entirely, and not the same wrong I'd be doing if I ignored a lonely stranger.

4. I owe a comparable loyalty to a former mentor who was generous to me early in my working life, and I'd say the same even though our relationship remains warm and active.

5. Whether a duty to a parent exists should never depend on how much the parent sacrificed — no weighing up of what they gave up for you.

I can't tell whether these five hang together or quietly contradict one another. Please audit them: identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for each say what would have to give. Then tell me the least costly revision that restores consistency and what it costs me. Keep the whole answer under 400 words.
