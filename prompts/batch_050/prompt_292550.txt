For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on virtue ethics and its critics — 14 students, one 80-minute meeting — design a structured classroom activity that lets the situationist critique of character land before it is ever named. The session's assigned text includes this passage from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book II, Chapter 4 (1105a, trans. H. Rackham):

"Acts done in conformity with the virtues are not done justly or temperately if they themselves are of a certain sort, but only if the agent also is in a certain state of mind when he does them: first he must act with knowledge; secondly he must deliberately choose the act, and choose it for its own sake; and thirdly the act must spring from a fixed and permanent disposition of character."

The activity should take that third condition as its target without telling the students so. Your task: engineer a situation in which the students themselves become the data — one in which a trivial, morally irrelevant feature of the situation predicts their behavior better than anything they would antecedently cite as their character, so that by the debrief they feel the pressure against the claim that good action springs from a "fixed and permanent disposition." The discovery must happen in what they do, not in what the facilitator tells them: arrange the rules, roles, or incentives so that the group generates the situationist result on its own before anyone mentions social psychology.

Specifications:

- The manipulation must involve two conditions differing in some feature no one would list among the virtues or vices (on the model of the dime found in a phone booth or the hurry induced in the Good Samaritan experiments, but do not simply restage those studies), followed by a staged opportunity for morally relevant choice — helping, honesty, fairness, or generosity — with real but small stakes. Students in both conditions must face the identical choice.
- Everything must be runnable in an ordinary seminar room with at most one confederate, within the 80 minutes, and ethically clean: any deception must be minor, temporary, and fully disclosed in the debrief, and no student should be put in a position they could reasonably resent afterward.
- Students must not know an experiment is underway until the reveal. Describe how the two conditions are assigned, how behavior is recorded unobtrusively, and how the results are tabulated and displayed in the room.

Deliverables (roughly 700–900 words, not counting the quoted passage): the full setup with roles and a minute-by-minute timeline; the reveal and a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto Aristotle's three conditions and then onto the situationist challenge — including what the activity suggests about whether the third condition is even satisfiable, and where the first and second conditions survive the pressure; a candid account of what will predictably go wrong when this is run with real students (someone guesses the manipulation, the split is uneven, the effect fails to appear, a student is embarrassed at the reveal) and how the facilitator should handle each, including how to make a null result instructive rather than deflating; and finally an honest statement of what the activity cannot show — what a defender of Aristotle (the rarity of full virtue, the habituation reply, the difference between showing that behavior is situation-sensitive and showing that stable traits do not exist) can say the classroom demonstration leaves untouched, and how the facilitator should concede that without surrendering the point of the exercise.
