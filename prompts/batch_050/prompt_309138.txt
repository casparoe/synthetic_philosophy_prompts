My aunt spent her whole career as a philosophy professor working on Schopenhauer — her one big book was on the metaphysics of the will — and now, in a twist she's half-horrified by, her grandson's school has asked her to give the commencement address at his high school graduation. She tried to decline on the grounds that a Schopenhauer scholar has no business standing at a podium that contractually requires optimism, but they wont take no for an answer. So she's roped me into drafting it, and I'm roping you in.

Act as an expert speechwriter who also knows 19th century German philosophy properly, not just the greeting-card version. Before writing, think carefully and jot down some brainstorming about the tension here; then write the speech.

The address has to be built around this passage from Schopenhauer's essay "On the Sufferings of the World" (Saunders translation), which she'd read aloud:

"But misfortune has its uses; for, as our bodily frame would burst asunder if the pressure of the atmosphere was removed, so, if the lives of men were relieved of all need, hardship and adversity; if everything they took in hand were successful, they would be so swollen with arrogance that, though they might not burst, they would present the spectacle of unbridled folly—nay, they would go mad. And I may say, further, that a certain amount of care or pain or trouble is necessary for every man at all times. A ship without ballast is unstable and will not go straight."

The passage has to do real work, not just get quoted and abandoned. The standard commencement move is to wish the graduates a future where everything they take in hand succeeds — and Schopenhauer's point is that this wish, if granted, would be a curse. So the speech needs to find something genuinely worth wishing a room of eighteen-year-olds that doesn't collapse into "may you suffer plenty," which would just sound bitter. The ballast image is the key: what's the difference between good ballast and dead weight at that age, and how much of it does anyone get to choose? No quietly swapping his pessimism out for ordinary uplift either — she'll notice and so will I.

Audience: about 400 graduates and their families who have never heard of Schopenhauer, plus three of her old colleagues in the front row who very much have and will clock any misreading. It needs to land for both at once. Roughly five minutes. Warm but not sappy, and she may mention Schopenhauer by name at most twice. No "the world is your oyster," no "the future belongs to you" — unless the speech is explicitly turning one of those lines over.
