A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about the human genome, and I would like an outside audit of the analogy our dispute turned on. His position: DNA is literally a code -- triplets specifying amino acids, the genome transcribed and then translated by the cell's machinery -- so sequencing a genome really is like obtaining the complete instruction manual for the organism, and all the talk about epigenetics, environment, and development is commentary on a text whose content is fully fixed by the sequence. Mine: code is a metaphor that has been overworked since the 1950s, because codes require conventions and interpreters with purposes, the route from sequence to organism runs through development rather than around it, and the same genome issues in different outcomes in different cellular and environmental contexts. Rather than adjudicate between us directly, please audit the analogy itself. Lay out what maps onto what -- codons and cipher symbols, transcription and copying, the ribosome as reader, mutation as typo -- and identify where the mapping breaks down. Which disanalogies actually bear on his conclusion that sequence fixes organism, and which are incidental? When biologists speak of genetic information, is it anything more than correlation in the information-theoretic sense, and if not, does any part of his position survive once that is on the table? Finally, if code is the wrong source domain, what better analogy would capture what a genome actually does, and what work could that analogy legitimately do?
