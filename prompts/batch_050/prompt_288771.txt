I teach a small undergrad seminar on rational choice (about 15 students, one 80-minute meeting) and I want to run the Allais paradox on them without them knowing what it is until after they've incriminated themselves. Design the activity for me.

Here's what I have in mind, feel free to change details: they should face two separate pairs of choices, far enough apart in the session that nobody does the math connecting them, and the choices should be real -- not hypothetical millions. I'm thinking actual lotteries run in class with a deck of numbered cards or percentile dice, with points toward their participation grade or maybe a raffle for a real small prize at the end, so that a 1% chance of walking away with nothing actually stings. Give me the exact choice structure (probabilities and payoffs) so that the modal pattern of answers violates the independence axiom, a protocol for collecting and committing to answers anonymously so people can't revise after discussion starts, and a script for the reveal where the class tallies its own votes and sees the inconsistency emerge from their own ballots rather than from me.

Then the debrief: I want a mapping from what just happened to the sure-thing principle and the independence axiom -- specifically how the two pairs share a common consequence that should be irrelevant and empirically isn't -- plus how to handle the students whose choices were actually consistent (there's always a few expected-value maximizers who spoil the gotcha, and I don't want them feeling smug or dismissed). Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time: students who already know the paradox and sandbag it, people misreading the probabilities, whatever else. And end with an honest note about which feature of the real thing my toy stakes can't reproduce -- presumably the regret structure of turning down a certain life-changing sum -- and what I should say about that gap instead of pretending the chocolate bar version is the same thing.
