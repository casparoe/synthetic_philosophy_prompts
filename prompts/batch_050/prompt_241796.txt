For my senior English class, I'm writing an essay on Shakespeare's Hamlet, and I want to read the play through a philosophical problem about self-knowledge that I've been thinking about, instead of the usual treatment of it as a tragedy of hesitation or revenge. Here's the problem as I understand it. Suppose we can't always be trusted to know why we do what we do. This failure could work in two very different ways. In the first way, the "hidden motive" way, there is a real reason for your action fixed in advance, but introspection can't reach it, so the reason you give yourself is simply wrong. In the second way, the "made-up motive" way, there is no settled reason until you produce one: explaining yourself is less like reading a diary and more like writing one after the fact, and the story you tell helps create the motive it claims to report. Psychologists have found hints of the second pattern in experiments where people confidently explain choices that were actually manipulated by the experimenters, inventing plausible reasons without noticing anything odd.

My essay's question is: which of these two failure modes does Hamlet actually dramatize? The clearest test case is the scene where Hamlet finds Claudius at prayer and decides not to kill him, giving the reason that killing a praying man would send his soul to heaven rather than hell. Read the first way, Hamlet's stated scruple conceals a real but inaccessible motive (fear, doubt, reluctance to become a killer). Read the second way, there is no motive at all until Hamlet supplies one, and his explanation doesn't just hide his hesitation, it is his hesitation, in rationalized form. I'd also like the reading to take in the "to be or not to be" soliloquy, where Hamlet claims that thinking dissolves the will to act (is that genuine self-insight or a story he tells himself?), and the play-within-a-play, where Hamlet demands external, publicly checkable evidence about Claudius precisely because private impressions can't be trusted — does he apply the same skeptical standard to his own inner reports, or does he exempt himself?

Two more things. First, please be honest about where the reading strains: Shakespeare leaves Hamlet's interiority genuinely ambiguous, so if the play refuses to settle which failure mode is at work, say so and explain what that ambiguity does, rather than forcing a verdict. Second, end with what the play contributes to the philosophical question that an abstract argument can't — for instance, whether watching a mind narrate itself in real time shows something about self-deception that a thought experiment only states. I'm not asking for quotations from the text, just a careful reading of well-known scenes and an essay I can argue with.
