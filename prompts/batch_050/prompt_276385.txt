So my roommate and I were reading about Mohism for a class on early Chinese thought, and he came up with this argument that he thinks basically sinks Mozi's doctrine of impartial care (jian ai, roughly the idea that I should be concerned for other people's families and states the same way I'm concerned for my own) by showing it proves way too much. Neither of us can find the hole in it, but I refuse to believe the conclusion. Please answer as a professional philosopher who knows the classical Chinese material well, and think carefully through each step before you commit -- maybe spend a few sentences brainstorming what the candidate weak points even are before you settle on one.

Here's the argument. (1) Impartial care says the welfare of a stranger's parent matters morally just as much as the welfare of my own parent -- that's the whole point of the doctrine, it's what distinguishes it from Confucian graded love. (2) If two people's welfare matters exactly equally to me, then in a situation where I can only benefit one of them, I have no moral reason to benefit one rather than the other. (3) So if my father and a stranger are both drowning and I can only save one, I have no reason whatsoever to save my father -- flipping a coin is the morally appropriate response. (4) But if I have no reason to prioritize my father even there, then filial duty, understood as a standing reason to give my parents special weight, simply doesn't exist. (5) Mozi himself clearly doesn't want (4) -- his own text praises filial sons and even argues that practicing universal love is the best way to benefit your own parents, since others will repay your care. (6) So Mozi's own doctrine commits him to a conclusion he himself rejects, and impartial care either collapses into the Confucian position it was supposed to replace or is just incoherent.

This has to go wrong somewhere, because Mencius made essentially this move against the Mohists -- he accused them of being unable to acknowledge their fathers as fathers -- and it always felt to me like a cheap shot rather than a refutation. But when my roommate lays it out like this I honestly can't say where. I'd most like to keep (1), since denying it feels like giving up on impartial care entirely, and I'd also like to keep the idea that there IS something right about filial priority, which puts pressure on me from both ends.

Tell me which step you think should be rejected, explain why that step looked perfectly fine on first reading (because it did, to both of us), and then price the alternatives: what does it cost a Mohist to reject your chosen step, what would it cost to reject (1) instead like a Confucian would, and what would it cost to just accept (3) and bite the bullet the way a modern utilitarian presumably would? Also say whether the step you're rejecting is the same step Mencius-style critics are exploiting, or whether his attack and my roommate's argument actually fail in different places.
