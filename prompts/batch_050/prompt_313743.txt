Some background first: I'm a retired physicist (solid state, mostly superconductors), and since retiring I've been slowly working my way through political philosophy with a reading group at the library. One idea that stuck with me is that populism might not be a full ideology at all but a "thin" one -- basically the claim that society splits into a pure people and a corrupt elite, and that politics should just be the direct expression of the people's will. Which would explain why the label gets stuck on basically everything.

Here's the practical problem. I volunteer at our town's little newspaper, and twice a week I help the editor pre-sort the letters to the editor before they go to her desk (she makes every final call; I just sort and flag). The word "populist" shows up constantly -- people accuse the mayor, the opposition, a local climate group, each other. She's planning a column on local political language and asked me to prep examples where the accusation actually tracks something real versus where it's just an insult. Trouble is I have no procedure, I go by gut feel, and my gut is inconsistent -- I caught myself calling the same style of rhetoric "populist" when I dislike the cause and "grassroots" when I like it. As a former experimentalist this embarasses me.

So: turn the thin-ideology idea (plus whatever else is needed -- the difference between populism and just being popular, between populism and demagoguery, the problem of what "the people" even refers to in a given speech) into a test I can run on a letter or a speech excerpt in about ten minutes. I want the questions in order; what counts as a yes or no at each step, with actual linguistic tells (claims of sole representation, moral framing instead of policy disagreement, what happens to pluralism); where the test stops with "not populist, just X"; and which steps are judgment calls I shouldn't pretend are mechanical. And be honest about where a ten-minute test will misfire -- e.g. can something be genuinely grassroots AND populist, does it matter whether the speaker believes what they say.

Then run the procedure on three cases I'm making up right now: (1) A mayoral candidate who says the town council is a closed shop that ignores "the real residents" and promises to "give the town back to the people," no policy details anywhere. (2) A neighborhood association fighting a new bypass road with petitions and angry flyers calling the planning department incompetent and corrupt. (3) A trade union leader who says workers are the backbone of the country, the government serves finance, and parliament should follow "what ordinary people actually want" as measured by referendums. I'm fairly confident (2) is not populist and I'd be annoyed if your procedure says it is, so please don't sandbag the thing to spare my feelings.

Format, roughly in this order: "The principle in one paragraph" (keep it short), "The procedure" (the core, numbered steps, maybe 60% of the whole answer), "Where judgment can't be removed" (a few paragraphs), "Worked cases" (run all three, a paragraph or two each), "Failure modes" (short list of how this test goes wrong in the wild). No citations please, I work offline occassionally and couldn't check them anyway.
