I volunteer as a lay chaplain at a regional hospital — no seminary training, just the two weekend courses the program requires — and I am typically called in three or four times a month, mostly to sit with families after a death or a devastating diagnosis. Over and over, sooner or later, someone asks me some version of why God allows this. I have read a fair amount on my own about the problem of evil, and I have noticed a tension I cannot resolve on my own: the philosophical material I have read is almost entirely about whether belief in God remains rational given suffering, while my situation is about what one human being should actually say to another human being who is in pain. I have also noticed that some of the standard moves — it was part of a plan, God needed another angel, suffering builds character, this will be redeemed somehow — can land as cruel even when they are offered sincerely, and I have read people arguing that offering reasons for someone else's suffering is itself a kind of moral failure, regardless of whether the reasons are sound. I take that worry seriously, but I cannot go as far as saying one should never offer any of this material, because some people genuinely ask for it and seem helped by it.

What I want is a written procedure I can actually follow in the room: a short sequence of steps, with questions I silently work through, that takes me from the moment the topic comes up to a decision about what, if anything, to say along theodicy lines. It should be built from whatever the philosophical and theological literature on the problem of evil contains that can survive translation to a bedside — the free will defense, soul-making, greater-good reasoning, the view that we are simply not in a position to grasp God's reasons, and the protest tradition that refuses justifications altogether. I want you to decide which distinctions among those have to survive the translation because they change what one may say, and which are fine to collapse because they only matter to philosophers. The procedure needs to handle at least these hard cases: the person who asks the question abstractly and intellectually versus the person who says the same words as a cry of pain; the person whose own faith tradition commits them to an answer I personally find harmful; the person who is angry at God and wants me to agree with the anger; the parent who has lost a child, where I need explicit guidance on which moves are simply off the table; and the person who asks me what I believe. Wherever the procedure cannot eliminate a judgment call, I want that marked plainly as a judgment call rather than disguised as a rule.

Please apply the finished procedure to three worked cases: a recently widowed man who says, calmly and curiously, that he has never understood how a good God permits cancer; a mother, hours after her teenage son's death in a car accident, who demands to know why God took him; and a woman with a terminal diagnosis who tells me she has stopped believing and asks whether I think she is wrong to have done so. At least one of these applications should produce an instruction I will dislike — my instinct in the moment is usually to offer some consoling reason, and I suspect the procedure will often tell me to keep quiet. Finally, tell me honestly what a procedure like this cannot do: where it will misfire, what no checklist can protect me from getting wrong, and how I should treat it differently from an actual rulebook given that the stakes are a grieving person trusting me.
