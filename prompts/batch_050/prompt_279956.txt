Heres something thats been bugging me. As I understand it, perjury law basically requires you to say something false. If a witness gives an answer thats literally true but carefully engineered to send the jury in the wrong direction — like answering "the money went into a business account" when technically it did, for one day, before being moved to a personal account — the classic position is that this isnt perjury, even if everyone in the room knows exactly what impression the witness was trying to create. But fraud law works differently: a literally true statement can still be fraud if it misleads someone out of their money. And then in ordinary life the line moves again. If I ask my flatmate where he was last night and he says "at the library" because he stopped there for five minutes on his way home from a party, nobody accepts "but it was technically true" as a defense — we treat it as straight-up lying, full stop.

So you have three practices that draw the line in three different places: the courtroom punishes only literal falsity, the marketplace punishes misleading impressions, and conversation punishes misleading pretty much whenever its deliberate. My question is what each of these practices presupposes about what a speaker is responsible for. Does perjury doctrine rest on a picture where speakers commit only to the literal content of their words, and hearers bear the risk of whatever they infer beyond that? Does the fraud rule presuppose a different picture where youre responsible for the total message your audience predictably takes away, or is it just that money changes the stakes without any change in the underlying theory? And which picture does the everyday norm assume?

Also: are these presuppositions even compatible, or is at least one of the three practices philosophically confused? And what would concretely have to change if we forced them onto one shared standard — e.g., what would trials look like if courts adopted the conversational rule that deliberate misleading counts as lying, or what would conversation look like if we adopted the perjury rule that only literal content is yours to answer for?
