In our forecasting group, whenever we disagree about priors, one senior colleague says that accumulating data washes out the prior, like a drop of dye in a clear stream: whatever color the water has initially, it becomes negligible far enough downstream. She uses the analogy to argue that prior choice is only a temporary nuisance rather than a genuine epistemic commitment. On that basis, she recommends that we stop spending meetings reconciling our priors and simply collect more observations.

Audit the analogy using the following setup as the target. Two analysts forecast which of several bias regimes describes a new industrial sensor. They observe the same readings and update by conditionalization. Analyst A places substantial prior probability on three regimes and none on a fourth physically possible one. Analyst B spreads prior mass across all four but uses a simpler likelihood that treats measurement noise as independent. The readings arrive in an order chosen through an internal forecasting contest: whichever analyst is currently more confident selects the next test condition. Some test conditions make two regimes generate identical readings. The group must make an expensive deployment decision after only forty readings, although observations could in principle continue indefinitely.

Treat the analogy as an argument rather than as a loose metaphor. Give an explicit correspondence table identifying what plays the role of the dye concentration, the water initially present, newly added water, downstream transparency, and agreement among observers. Then distinguish the disanalogies that matter for my colleague’s recommendation from those that are merely literal. In particular, assess how the analogy handles likelihoods that are not shared, zero or extremely small prior support for the truth, regimes indistinguishable under the tests actually selected, adaptive selection of observations, finite decision horizons, and the effect of priors on decisions made while evidence is still accumulating. You may use standard Bayesian distinctions, but define any technical term you rely on rather than merely invoking named convergence theorems.

Give a committed answer in American English: does the washing-out claim justify my colleague’s recommendation in this setup, in general, or only under explicitly stated conditions? If it applies only conditionally, identify the weakest condition and explain which feature of the scenario is most likely to violate it. If the dye analogy can be repaired, revise it; if not, propose a better analogy and state what practical conclusion it would support. Do not merely present both sides and leave the verdict to me.
