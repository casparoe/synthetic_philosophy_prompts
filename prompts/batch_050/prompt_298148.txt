I am a science journalist at a British current-affairs magazine, and I have been asked to draft the first cosmology entry in our style guide. The recurring problem is what our writers should be permitted to say about the expansion of the universe. Our copy is full of sentences like "space itself is stretching", "distant galaxies are being carried away from us by the expansion of space", claims that galaxies beyond a certain distance recede "faster than light", and accounts of inflation as a moment when "space blew up" in the universe's first fraction of a second.

The philosophical dispute I want brought to bear is the one between substantivalists, who take spacetime to be a thing in its own right, and relationists, who take talk of space to be shorthand for relations among bodies. That dispute is usually prosecuted through Newton's rotating bucket: the water's concave surface seems to demand explanation by rotation relative to something, and the question is whether that something must be space itself. I want to know what this dispute, and its modern aftermath in general relativity, imply for the mundane editorial question of which sentences about expanding space we should print, and with what caveats.

Please give me the most important implications of the substantivalism–relationism debate for how I should write and edit such sentences, and then rule on four specific conventions:

1. Stating, without further qualification, that galaxies beyond a certain distance recede from us "faster than light".
2. Attributing causal agency to space — writing that expanding space drives galaxies apart, as opposed to writing that galaxies move apart through space.
3. Presenting the cosmological redshift as caused by the stretching of space during the light's journey.
4. Describing the observable universe as "the region of space we can see", with the implication that space simply continues beyond it.

As you reason, keep two things distinct: whether a sentence is legitimate shorthand within general relativity, and whether it misleads a lay reader about what the theory says. Consider at least three stances — substantivalism, relationism, and the view that the choice between them is a matter of descriptive convention rather than a fact of the matter — and say which stance, if adopted, most changes what I should print. Note also where the physics community's own habits of speech might license a sentence that the philosophy tells against, or vice versa.

On epistemic posture: do not present a tidy consensus where there is none. State plainly what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would produce different verdicts on any of the four conventions, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong or to rest on a contested reading of the physics.

Format: a prose discussion first (roughly 600–800 words), followed by a table with these columns, one row per convention: Convention (the numbered item) | Verdict (one of: print as-is, print with caveat, rewrite) | Suggested rewrite (a sentence we could actually publish) | Which philosophical stance drives the verdict | What would change the verdict. Finish with one further row, Overall house rule, in which the Convention column contains your single-sentence rule of thumb for all cosmological language and the remaining columns are filled in as applicable.
