I'm on the governing council of a national chess federation (wont say which) and next month we vote on whether to scrap the women's titles and the women's championship and run everything as open events instead. The motion comes from a few of our younger grandmasters, who argue the separate track condescends to women, props up a lower standard, and that keeping it basically tells every girl shes not expected to compete with the boys. The other side, which includes most of the active women players we actually consulted, says the women's circuit is the main thing keeping girls in the game past about fifteen, that junior girls' events are the one space where they don't get patronised, and that binning the whole structure would halve female participation within a decade even if in principle open events treat everyone identically.

Someone on council keeps citing Mill, The Subjection of Women, specifically the bit where he argues that nothing can be inferred about women's nature from how women currently are, because we've never observed them outside conditions already shaped by men's arrangements. Applied to us: the rating gap at the top tells us nothing about aptitude because it's downstream of a participation ratio of something like twenty to one, plus everything that follows from being a tiny minority in a culture built by and for men. One of the grandmasters shot back that this is unfalsifiable — you can always claim the conditions weren't yet equal, for any gap, forever — and that at some point the size and stubbornness of a statistical difference just is evidence, even if not conclusive evidence.

Draft me the council's decision memo. Recommend keep, scrap, or keep-with-changes (we could for instance keep the titles but fold the championship into the open, or sunset the whole thing with a review in five years). I want the memo to genuinely do the philosophy rather than gesture at it: what would actually count as evidence about natural differences here, given that Mill's move cuts both ways — the participation ratio itself might reflect differences in interest that are partly natural, or might be wholly socialised, and it's not obvious what observation could settle that. Be honest about whether Mill's argument, taken seriously, means the gap question is undecidable in practice or whether it only means our current data is uninformative. And give the strongest version of the losing side properly, because whoever loses this vote will be livid and I want the minutes to show we engaged with their best argument rather than a strawman of it.
