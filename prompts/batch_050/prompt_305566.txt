I'm helping run a small reading group that's spent the term on work — Weber and the Protestant ethic, France's 35-hour week, one genuinely grim session on karoshi — and for the final meeting, on leisure, I want to open with a poem rather than the usual discussion question. Early in the term we read Book X of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, where he argues that happiness hangs on leisure, on the grounds that we busy ourselves precisely in order to be at leisure, the way we wage war only in order to live in peace. We nodded at it and moved on, but it's been nagging at me, because the modern order seems to run his sentence backwards. The weekend exists so you can work again on Monday; the holiday gets sold as 'recharging'; even sleep apps frame rest as an input to productivity. We're at leisure in order to be busy, and karoshi is where that logic ends if nothing interrupts it.

So: could you write me a poem of about 18–22 lines built as a mirror? The first half should be in the register of the modern order — rest as recovery, downtime as refuelling, the body as equipment to be maintained for the office. Then a hinge line at the midpoint, and then the second half should reuse the first half's lines (or near-lines) in reverse order, so the poem literally turns itself round — the way Aristotle turns the means–ends ordering round. Same words, flipped direction.

One wrinkle the poem shouldn't dodge, though: Aristotle's leisure was for citizens, and somebody else was doing the work that bought it. So as the reversed lines come back down the far side of the mirror, I'd like them to start meaning something different even where the wording is identical — leisure coming to sound less like the universal end of all our busyness and more like a question about whose busyness pays for whose schole. Don't mention Aristotle or quote him in the poem itself; let the structure carry him. If the mirror has to be imperfect in places to stay a good poem, fine, be imperfect — but the turn has to land, because I'm going to read it aloud and then just ask the group which half they live in.
