I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on the timing of conscious experience, and I'm putting together a seminar session pairing Dennett's Orwellian-vs-Stalinesque distinction (the color phi stuff) with Ambrose Bierce's "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge." The story seems almost too perfect for it: Farquhar lives through an entire escape -- swimming the creek under gunfire, the endless walk home, reaching for his wife -- in what turns out to be the fraction of a second while the rope drops him. Read it seriously through that lens. Does the story commit to a Stalinesque picture, where the brain constructs the whole narrative before it ever becomes conscious, like the brain pre-filling the color switch in the phi phenomenon? Or can you run an Orwellian reading -- real fragmentary experiences during the fall, instantly papered over by fabricated memory -- and is there anything in the text that actually decides between them? My suspicion is that the twist only lands if you assume there's a fact of the matter about exactly when the experience occured, i.e. a residual bit of Cartesian theater, and I want to know whether that's right or whether the story survives Dennett's deflation. And where does the fiction strain the theory? Filling in a couple hundred milliseconds of apparent motion is one thing, but the escape has continuous narrative content over experienced hours, which seems like a much bigger ask.
