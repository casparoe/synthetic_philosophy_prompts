I am a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics working on the epistemology of mathematical practice, and I would like your help stress-testing the central framework of my next dissertation chapter. Please write a self-contained analytical essay of roughly 1,200–1,500 words in response to the following.

The chapter turns on a hypothetical episode I have constructed, modeled on the structure of Imre Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations but transplanted to the present day:

In 2031, two combinatorial geometers prove what becomes known as the Halvorsen–Ito theorem: every 'admissible tiling' (a class they define by three explicit axioms) possesses a certain rigidity property. The proof is long, but within a few years it is fully formalized and machine-checked, and the result enters the textbooks. In 2037, a graduate student constructs an object that satisfies the three axioms exactly as written yet lacks the rigidity property. The object exploits a degenerate limiting configuration that Halvorsen and Ito insist was 'obviously never intended' to count as admissible. The community splits. One faction holds that the theorem was straightforwardly refuted: it was a false universal claim about the axiom-defined class, and the repaired version (with a fourth axiom excluding the degenerate case) is a strictly weaker and genuinely different theorem. The opposing faction holds that nothing mathematical was refuted at all: the intended informal concept of an admissible tiling always implicitly excluded such degenerate objects, so the episode merely exposed a faulty formalization of an intact mathematical truth.

Compare two diagnoses of this episode. First, the Euclidean-deductivist picture of mathematical methodology that Lakatos polemicizes against in Proofs and Refutations, on which genuine mathematical knowledge flows from precise axioms by proof and is therefore immune to refutation by counterexample. Second, Lakatos's own method of proofs and refutations, with its machinery of counterexample-driven concept revision — monster-barring, exception-barring, lemma-incorporation — and its notion of proof-generated concepts. Which of the two methodologies gives the better account of what actually happened in the episode, and of what the community should do next?

In answering, address at least the following: (a) which faction in the story each methodology would side with, and whether either methodology has difficulty accommodating the fact that the original proof was machine-checked rather than merely human-vetted; (b) whether the 'intended concept' faction can avoid collapsing into what Lakatos calls monster-barring, and whether that collapse, if it occurs, is a defect or instead a respectable move; (c) whether the episode supports Lakatos's broader thesis that mathematics is quasi-empirical — that its development follows something like the logic of conjecture and refutation found in natural science — or whether it instead highlights a disanalogy, namely that a fully formalized proof places mathematical knowledge beyond the kind of revision empirical theories undergo.

Do not end on a non-committal both-sides-have-a-point note. State which diagnosis you take to be correct for the episode as described, say whether you take the episode to vindicate or undermine the quasi-empiricist thesis, and justify both verdicts.
