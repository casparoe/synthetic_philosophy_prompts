My friend wrote up notes for our philosophy of language reading group, and here's his summary of two-dimensional semantics:

'Two-dimensional semantics, developed mainly by David Chalmers, says that a term like "water" has two intensions. The primary intension is roughly the reference-fixing description the speaker associates with the term, the criterion in their head for picking the stuff out, so for "water" something like the clear drinkable liquid that fills the lakes and comes out of the taps around here. The secondary intension is what you get once the actual world has done its work: since water turned out to be H2O, the secondary intension of "water" maps every possible world to H2O. This is how the framework explains the necessary a posteriori: "water is H2O" is necessary on the secondary dimension but contingent on the primary one, because the reference-fixer could easily have latched onto XYZ instead. The upshot is that the view vindicates Frege against Kripke: primary intensions play the role of senses, and since they are fixed internally, by what's in the speaker's head, it turns out one whole dimension of meaning is in the head after all, and Putnam's externalism only ever captured half the story.'

Something in those last couple sentences feels off to me. I was under the impression the whole point of the two-dimensional apparatus was to make peace with Kripke and with Putnam's Twin Earth cases rather than overturn them, and I also half-remember that Chalmers is pretty explicit that he's not a descriptivist. But my friend is generally careful and most of the standard stuff in there looks right, so I can't tell wether I'm misremembering or he quietly overreached.
