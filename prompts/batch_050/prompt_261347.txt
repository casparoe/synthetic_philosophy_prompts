I'm a novelist at work on a comic novel, and in trying to understand why some of my own scenes land and others die on the page I've been reading the philosophical and psychological literature on humour. Two camps keep talking past each other, and I'd like you to draft the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The first camp is the incongruity theorists: they hold that humour is the perception of a clash between what we expect and what we get — a violation of our conceptual schemes, scripts, or categories — often followed by some playful fit or resolution. The second camp is the benign violation theorists: they hold that humour requires two simultaneous conditions, a violation of something (a norm, a dignity, a person's well-being, the way things ought to go) and an appraisal that this violation is benign — acceptable, harmless, or not to be taken seriously. Treat the older superiority and relief theories as legacy positions whose phenomena (mockery of the disliked, laughter at the forbidden, nervous laughter) the joint statement should explicitly say whether and how each camp absorbs.

The statement should open with a numbered section of claims both sides accept. I suspect these include: that a mere departure from expectation is not funny on its own (an unexpected bill, a stranger at the door); that jokes fail in two opposite directions, by being too tame and by going too far, and any serious theory must explain both failure modes rather than only one; that humour at one's own past misfortunes becomes possible only after some distance, which both sides need to account for; and that whatever humour is, it is not identical with laughter, since people laugh from nervousness, tickling, and social lubrication. Then give the disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would sign: for instance, whether incongruity without any norm violation (a penguin in the living room, surreal absurdism, pure nonsense wordplay) is ever genuinely funny, with the incongruity theorist saying yes and the benign violation theorist claiming that on inspection a norm is always there; and whether "resolution" is an independent ingredient of humour or merely one device among several for rendering a violation benign.

Close with the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and for each crux, a concrete case or observation both camps accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Some cruxes you might consider: whether a joke ruined by adding distance, or saved by adding distance, cuts for one theory over the other; whether the funniness of tickling requires a social violator, and what each side predicts about self-tickling; whether jokes that offend their own audience but amuse outsiders show that benignness is in the perceiver rather than the stimulus, and whether that concession damages either view; and whether failed humour that elicits only awkwardness, rather than offence or boredom, is evidence for a third category neither theory currently handles. If, in drafting, you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal — that "incongruity" and "violation" are sometimes two names for one thing — say exactly which part, and which residue is genuinely empirical.
