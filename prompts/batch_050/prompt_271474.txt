Six weeks ago my father's ventilator was withdrawn in a hospice, and in the family meeting beforehand the palliative consultant was at pains to reassure us that stopping treatment was morally quite different from actively ending his life, even though the timing and manner of death would be much the same either way. Since then I have read a fair amount of the philosophical literature on the doing/allowing distinction, and I am struck by how completely stalled it seems: the same handful of paired cases has been circulating for fifty years, each side concedes the other's cases are hard, and nobody appears to have moved.

Here is the pair I keep coming back to, which I devised for myself. Patient A and Patient B have identical conditions and prognoses. A's ventilator is never connected, because the family could not be reached in time and the team's policy is not to intubate without consultation. B's ventilator is connected, then disconnected an hour later once the family is consulted. The teams' reasoning, the patients' experiences, and the outcomes are identical; the only difference is a switch being thrown and then unthrown. Defenders of the distinction say B's doctor did something A's doctor merely allowed. Critics say the cases show the distinction tracks nothing of moral weight. Both seem right, which is exactly my problem.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict or a literature survey. My working hypothesis is that this is not really a moral disagreement at all but a disguised disagreement about causation: the two camps deploy different background pictures of what it is to cause a death, and the moral language is decorative. Am I right? Or is it a verbal dispute about the word kill, or a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading (one asking what a clinician may do, the other asking who is to blame), or a clash of bedrock intuitions that argument cannot touch? Please test my hypothesis against what each side actually argues, using my ventilator pair as the test case, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, findings from moral psychology, a stipulative taxonomy for clinical ethics, or an honest admission that the impasse is permanent.

Please write in British English.
