I am preparing a handout for an undergraduate epistemology seminar on the surprise exam paradox, and before I present my own position as settled, I would like a consistency audit. Here is where I currently stand:

(1) Backward induction is a sound pattern of reasoning about finite sequential structures. I accept it in the centipede game, in iterated dominance reasoning, and in general I see nothing inherently suspicious about reasoning backward from the end of a finite sequence.

(2) The surprise exam paradox is a puzzle, not a proof of anything impossible. The teacher's announcement — there will be exactly one exam next week, and on the morning of the exam the students will not have known the evening before that it would fall on that day — can turn out to be true, and the students can reasonably believe it on Monday morning on the basis of the teacher's say-so.

(3) Knowledge is closed under competent deduction: if I know that p, and I competently deduce q from p, then I am in a position to know that q.

(4) The KK principle fails. Knowing that p does not in general imply knowing that one knows that p, and requiring arbitrarily many iterations of knowledge is typically where epistemic arguments overreach.

(5) "Surprise" should be given a deductive definition: the exam counts as a surprise if and only if on no evening prior to the exam can the students deduce, from the announcement together with what has happened so far, that the exam will fall on the following day.

My question is whether this set of commitments hangs together. Specifically: which pairs (or triples) of these theses genuinely conflict, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on inspection, and for each genuine conflict, what is the least costly revision and what does it cost me elsewhere in my wider views? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and note any place where a commitment is doing less work than it appears to be doing — for instance, where a thesis is consistent with the rest of the set only on a reading so weak that I would not actually want to defend it. Conclude by telling me which single revision would resolve the most tension at the lowest overall cost.
