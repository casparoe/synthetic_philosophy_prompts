I work as a nurse on a psychiatric ward, and one of my long-term patients has me genuinely puzzled. He is a man in his fifties who, for the past several months, has insisted that his wife of thirty years has been replaced by a physically identical impostor. No evidence or argument touches this conviction. What confuses me is how he lives with it. He says he wants the impostor exposed and his real wife found, yet he does nothing toward either: he eats the meals she cooks him, thanks her warmly for visiting, asks her to fetch things from home, and when she fell ill last month he seemed sincerely worried about her. When I gently pointed out that he does not act like a man living with a stranger wearing his wife's face, he shrugged and said the impostor presumably has her reasons for keeping him comfortable.

Our senior clinician describes him as holding a fixed false belief and wants his care plan built around patiently confronting that belief with counterevidence. A psychologist on the team pushed back in a case meeting, arguing that the man does not relate to what he says the way we relate to the things we believe -- that it is more like a story he is trapped inside than an opinion he has formed, and that piling up counterevidence misunderstands what we are dealing with. I cannot tell who is right, or whether the disagreement is substantive rather than terminological, and I am honestly not sure what rides on it practically, since either way he needs care.

So: does he believe what he says, in the sense that matters? Should the answer change how we approach him, or is the dispute clinically empty? And please be careful to distinguish what is genuinely established about this kind of condition from what remains contested among the people who study it -- I do not want one side of a live theoretical debate presented to me as settled consensus.
