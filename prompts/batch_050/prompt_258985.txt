I am preparing a short statement of my views for an epistemology reading group, and I would like a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here is my position set, stated as plainly as I can manage:

1. I accept a sensitivity condition on knowledge, in roughly Nozick's tracking style: for S to know that p, it must be the case that if p were false, S would not believe p (holding fixed the method by which S formed the belief).

2. I accept epistemic closure: if S knows that p, and knows that p entails q, then S knows, or is at least in a position to know, that q. Letting people know the obvious consequences of what they know seems to me non-negotiable for any usable notion of knowledge.

3. I take myself to know plenty of ordinary empirical propositions: that I have hands, that my car is parked where I left it, that my colleagues are not animatronic replicas.

4. I deny that I know I am not a brain in a vat. Any epistemology that lets me come to know that by trivial armchair inference from (3) strikes me as having gone wrong somewhere, since my evidence is exactly what I would have if I were one.

5. I think purely statistical evidence can suffice for knowledge. If I hold one ticket in a fair million-ticket lottery and check the published odds, I can thereby come to know that my ticket lost, without waiting to read the results in the paper.

6. I am a fallibilist: knowing that p does not require evidence that rules out every possibility of error, and knowledge is compatible with the bare metaphysical possibility that one is mistaken.

My questions, in order of priority: First, which of these commitments are actually in tension with one another, and which pairs merely appear to be? Please identify the specific derivation or case that generates each conflict rather than gesturing at a vague atmosphere of unease. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity, where severity is measured by how much of the rest of the package has to be sacrificed to resolve each one. Third, for each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision available and what it costs -- in particular, whether the cost falls on the sensitivity condition, on closure, or on one of my verdicts about cases. Fourth, if a single revision would resolve the greatest number of tensions at once, identify it, and say whether the resulting position is one a tracking theorist could still recognize as her own, or whether it collapses into a Moorean or relevant-alternatives view in disguise.

Please write the answer in American English, and do not pad it with a general survey of the literature: I want the audit, not the textbook chapter.
