I am a doctoral candidate in moral philosophy, and I help run our department's graduate colloquium interview series. Next month we are hosting a philosopher known for defending what she calls integrity-respecting consequentialism. Her position, briefly, is this: she concedes Williams's point that an agent's deepest commitments are partly constitutive of who the agent is, but argues that the costs to integrity of abandoning those commitments can themselves be registered inside the consequentialist calculus, so that the right theory can deliver Williams-friendly verdicts without abandoning impartial maximization. She further holds that the one-thought-too-many objection rests on conflating a theory's criterion of right action with the deliberative procedure it recommends for everyday moral thinking.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for me. The questions should press where the actual pressure points are, rather than inviting her to restate the view, and I would like them sequenced so that the pressure builds. Specifically:

1. Include at least one question on the Jim case, in which a traveler can save nineteen captives only by shooting one himself. The question should press on whether her calculus move genuinely dissolves Williams's objection or merely reproduces the standard consequentialist verdict with what one might call a conscience surcharge, and on whether the integrity cost she adds is doing explanatory work or simply tracking intuitions the theory was brought in to correct.

2. Include at least one question on the criterion-versus-decision-procedure distinction. If the ideally calibrated agent on her view does not in fact deliberate impartially at the moment of action, I want to ask what normative work the impartial criterion is still doing, and whether a theory whose criterion must remain psychologically invisible to well-functioning agents has become self-effacing in a way she should find uncomfortable rather than reassuring.

3. Include at least one question on negative responsibility, asking whether Williams's objection was ever really about what morality demands, as opposed to what an agent can live with having done, and whether treating the latter as a cost internal to the theory misses that it was meant as an objection to the theory's authority.

4. Include at least one question on the residual worry that even an integrity-weighted calculus still relates the agent to her own projects as one claimant among many, so that the concession to Williams is extensional but not structural.

For each question, supply a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, and make the follow-ups progressively harder to deflect with the observation that the theory was never intended as a manual for deliberation. End the set with one question about what case or result would count against her view in her own eyes. No biographical or warm-up questions, please; our audience is graduate students and faculty.
