Next month I am giving an evening talk about my newly published novel to an audience hosted by a university's philosophy-of-mind reading group, and I would like your help preparing for the question period. The novel follows a behavioral biologist who studies the digger wasp Sphex — the one that drags its paralyzed cricket to the burrow threshold, goes in to inspect, and, if the cricket is nudged a few centimeters away, retrieves it and repeats the inspection ritual from the beginning, indefinitely, never noticing the loop. Over the course of the book my protagonist gradually comes to see her own habits, loyalties, and even her love affairs as variations on the same pattern, and the novel's implicit thesis — which I intend to state openly in the talk — is that human freedom is not the opposite of sphexishness but sphexishness with deeper recursive self-monitoring: our flexibility is more machinery, not an escape from machinery, and recognizing this should be consoling rather than devastating. The audience, as I understand it, will be roughly a dozen philosophy graduate students and faculty who know Dennett's treatment of the wasp and the standard compatibilist landscape cold, two cognitive scientists who work on animal cognition and will object if I get the empirical details wrong, and, I am told, one entomologist. I am a novelist, not a philosopher, so some of them will also suspect I am laundering a deflationary thesis through fiction because it could not survive argument.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually come from this audience — not the ones a literary festival crowd would ask — phrased in the vocabulary they would use (reasons-responsiveness, manipulation arguments, levels of description, whether the wasp case even generalizes, whether consoling is doing any philosophical work or is just the novelist's exit hatch). For each, tell me what I should concede immediately and without visible pain, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in two minutes, with a note on where that reply is softest. Identify which objections will come from the philosophers, which from the scientists, and which single objection is most likely to come from the entomologist and catch me off guard. Flag the weakest point in my thesis — the place where I should prepare a real defense rather than hope the room moves on — and tell me the question I should be quietly hoping nobody asks, along with what I will say if someone does.

Finally, I would like to know how much to trust the preparation itself. For each objection you predict, indicate how confident you are that this audience will actually raise it, given that they are philosophers first and my credentials are literary; and at the end, single out the one prediction or recommended reply you are least sure of, and tell me whether that uncertainty is about the philosophy, about my sketch of the audience, or about whether the wasp example really does what Dennett and Hofstadter made it do.
