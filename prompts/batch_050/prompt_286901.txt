So I help run the grants committee at a small foundation that funds wild animal welfare stuff, and we're stuck on our latest application. Here's our track record so far, and I want to know what consistency actually demands of us:

1. We funded a deer contraception program in a suburban forest -- fewer deer born, fewer deer starving in winter, the pitch was basically "fewer wild lives, less total suffering."
2. We rejected funding for a campaign against a planned highway through a grassland, even though the highway would obviously kill some animals -- our reasoning at the time (I dug up the minutes) was that opposing development wasn't really an animal welfare intervention, it was just environmentalism with extra steps.
3. We funded a vaccination program against a disease that causes a slow painful death in a rabbit population, no effect on population size expected.
4. We rejected a proposal to feed supplemental food to an overpopulated, chronically starving rodent colony, on the grounds that it would just mean more rodents being born into short miserable lives.
5. We funded, somewhat reluctantly, a controlled burn program that kills a bunch of small animals outright but prevents much larger wildfires and, per the applicants' modeling, reduces the total number of animals the habitat can support long-term.

The new application: a land trust wants us to fund the legal purchase of a coastal wetland that's about to be drained for agriculture. Draining it kills most of the current inhabitants over a few brutal months (drying, predation of displaced animals, etc.) but leaves the land supporting far fewer wild animals afterward, mostly some rodents and insects in the margins. Keeping it as wetland preserves a dense, high-turnover ecosystem -- tons of small fish, frogs, insects, the classic r-selected crowd, most of whom die young and horribly as per usual. The applicants are pitching it as preventing the acute suffering of the drainage event.

Here's the thing: I can read our past decisions as at least two different rules, maybe three. One reading is we fund whatever minimizes total wild-animal-years-lived, on the theory that most wild lives are net negative -- that rule says fund the drainage (or at least don't fund the wetland purchase). Another is we fund interventions that reduce suffering-hours per dollar and we don't count population effects either way -- that rule probably says buy the wetland. And someone on the committee claims the real pattern is just that we fund interventions *on* wild animals and reject habitat-level stuff entirely, which would sort decisions 1-5 fine but I'm not sure it survives decision 5.

Please tell us what to actually do. I want a definite recommendation, not a menu: pick which precedent is the closest analog to this one and say why, state what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each possible verdict, and give your best call on which principle is really ours. If you think our precedents genuinely underdetermine this case, then say what further hypothetical past decision would have settled it, and recommend the verdict anyway based on which principle is more defensible. Also flag where the empirical assumptions matter (e.g., whether wetland fish-and-frog lives are actually net negative) versus where the disagreement is purely about values.
