A colleague in the philosophy of history has pressed the following argument on me, and while I cannot accept where it ends, I have been unable to locate a step I am genuinely willing to pay for. The argument begins with explanation in history but arrives somewhere absurd.

(1) To explain an event is to show that, given the circumstances preceding it, the event was to be expected; an account that leaves the event looking as though it could just as easily not have happened has described the event, not explained it.

(2) The only way to show that an event was to be expected given prior circumstances is to subsume the case under a generalization to the effect that circumstances of that kind are, always or with high probability, followed by events of that kind.

(3) Historians supply no such generalizations. When a historian explains the outbreak of a war by tracing the assassination, the alliance system, and the mobilization timetables, the only general claims in the vicinity are of the form "powers that believe themselves encircled tend to strike first," and these are riddled with exceptions — which is precisely why the historian never states them.

(4) Therefore historical narratives do not explain their events.

(5) Nothing in the reasoning turns on anything distinctive to professional history. When I explain the bridge's collapse by pointing to the fatigued bolt, or explain a friend's resignation by recounting the accumulating humiliations of her final year, I stand exactly where the historian stands: I cite conditions, not laws, and any generalization I could actually state would be just as exception-laden as the historian's.

(6) But these everyday accounts plainly do explain, and a theory on which nothing has ever been explained outside a physics classroom is for that reason alone false.

(7) Since (6) forbids accepting (4) generalized to everyday life, and (5) shows the historical and everyday cases stand or fall together, the argument proves too much somewhere — yet (1) and (2) seem merely to make explicit what the word "explain" means.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically: if I reject (2), am I committed to saying an event can be explained even when nothing settles that it was likely to happen — and is that still explanation, or something else wearing its clothes? If instead I keep (2) but distinguish stating a generalization from there being one, with the cited conditions gesturing at laws nobody needs to produce, does that rescue collapse into a promissory note, given that the required laws of human affairs never seem to arrive? And if I reject (1), what replaces the expectability condition? I have seen two candidates: that an explanation can instead show the event was the sensible thing to do given the agent's situation and reasons, and that a story explains by making each stage follow naturally from the one before it. Do these proposals genuinely avoid subsumption under generalizations, or do they smuggle generalizations back in through the judgments about what is sensible and what follows naturally?

I would most like to preserve (6) and the historian's claim to explain; I am least attached to (1). But I want the full price list for each exit, not just the cheapest one, and I want to know why the rejected step looked like a mere definition in the first place.
