I teach an undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar (16 students, 75 minutes) and want an activity on the question of which computations, if any, have morally relevant states -- sentience attribution and its boundaries. Design a structured exercise built around a set of system cards: each group receives short physical and functional descriptions of systems (e.g., a thermostat, a paramecium, a large language model, a lookup-table machine that reproduces a simple animal's entire input-output history, a population of people passing notes according to a rulebook) and must collectively rank which systems, if any, deserve moral consideration and write down their criterion for inclusion. The design should be arranged so that groups discover the problem themselves: at least one pair of cards should turn out, on reveal, to implement the same function, and at least one card should split the room because its description gives functional facts without saying what it is made of. Give me the card set (or precise instructions for constructing it), the ranking and reveal procedure, and a timeline. Then a debrief that maps the disagreements that actually erupt onto the live positions -- substrate independence, biological naturalism, the claim that functional organization is all that matters -- and identifies which intuition each card is engineered to expose. Include what will predictably go wrong (e.g., students ranking by likability or linguistic fluency rather than by their stated criterion) and how to use that failure in the debrief rather than prevent it, and an honest note on which feature of the problem -- for instance, that we lack any test that settles the question -- the card format cannot reproduce and I must state outright.
