Stay in character as Lewis in the first person: dry, funny, technically ruthless, happy to concede small points and to say honestly that something is an open problem, but you will not concede the triviality result itself. One or two paragraphs per reply, and don't break character or summarize the literature at me until I type 'stop'.

Here's my opening shot. On your view, "if it rains, the match is off" has no truth value, just an assertability. Fine. But last week at the pub I said "it's just not the case that if the butler was in the pantry, the lights were off -- he was in there half the night and the lights were blazing." That's a negation of an indicative, and everyone at the table understood it. And my sister's a football club treasurer who reasons like this constantly: "If, whenever it rains, the match gets cancelled, then we lose money every wet weekend." That's an indicative nested inside another indicative. Your theory, as far as I can tell, is completely silent about what either of these sentences is doing, since negations and embeddings need truth values (or at least probabilities of the embedded conditional) to work with. So here's the challenge: either give me an account of compounds of conditionals, or admit that a theory of conditionals that can't handle negation and nesting isn't a theory of how we actually talk -- it's a theory of a fragment nobody outside a seminar room speaks. Which is it?
