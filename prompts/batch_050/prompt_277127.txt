Two philosophers in my department have been bickering about material constitution for ages and I've volunteered (foolishly) to moderate. One is a one-object theorist: the statue and the lump of clay it's made of are literally identical, and apparent differences between them (the lump could survive being squashed, the statue couldn't) are just differences in which descriptions or sortals we're applying, not in how many things are sitting on the plinth. The other is a constitution theorist: the statue and the lump are numerically distinct, coinciding objects -- the lump constitutes the statue without being it -- and the different persistence conditions are genuine properties that show there are two things there, sharing their matter.

Draft the joint statement they should be able to produce after a proper adversarial collaboration. To keep it concrete, anchor the whole thing in this case (which I made up, so don't go hunting for it in the literature): a sculptor exhibits a plain lump of clay for a week as "raw material", then sculpts it into a statue without adding or removing a single particle, displays it for a month, then -- as the final act of the artwork -- squashes it back into a lump before a live audience. Call the phases A (lump only), B (statue), C (lump again).

I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both can actually sign -- e.g. about what the persistence-conditions data are, about the fact that the statue and lump share all their parts and mass at any moment, about Leibniz's law and what the one-objec side has to say about modal properties, about what "counting" even requires. Don't include anything vacuous, and don't smuggle in either side's vocabulary as if it were neutral.
2. A disagreements section, phrased precisely enough that each side would accept the wording of its own position. At least some of these should turn out, on inspection, to be partly verbal -- if you think the dispute over "how many things are on the plinth?" hinges on different uses of "thing" or "object", say so explicitly and say what non-verbal disagreement (if any) survives.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one side to give ground. I'd guess candidates include whether modal properties like "could survive squashing" can belong to one thing relative to two sortals without contradiction, whether constitution-without-identity is a coherent relation or just relabelled identity, and whether phase A and phase C contain the same lump (and whether that question is even independent of the main dispute).
4. For each crux, something both sides agree would bear on it -- an argument, an analogy with cases both accept (e.g. ordinary persistence through part-replacement), or a further thought experiment -- plus what each side predicts the right verdict on it is, written down in advance so neither can retrofit.

Please keep each side's position genuinely strongest-form, no strawmen, and write it in British English. A short intro paragraph framing the collaboration is fine but the meat should be in the four sections above.
