I run a little reading group at my company — we're all software engineers, about 10 people, no philosophy background, and we meet monthly for 90 minutes to chew on some Big Idea. This month the theme is the rule of law, and I want to push back on the group's default take, which is basically "the rule of law just means everyone has to follow the law, even the president." A friend of mine who's in law school told me about Lon Fuller's thing where he lists eight ways a legal system can fail — laws being secret, retroactive, contradictory, impossible to follow, constantly changing, and so on — and argues that a system can fail at being law at all even if the people running it mean well. That framing definately clicked with me as an engineer (it sounds like a spec review for a legal system), and I'd love the group to feel that, not just nod at it.

So: design a structured activity or game we can run in the 90 minutes where the group actually experiences what it's like to live under rules that violate these desiderata — not because the ruler is a cartoon villain, but precisely when the person in charge is benevolent and trying their best. I want them to construct or discover the failure modes themselves (the retroactive "that's against the rules now" moment, the rule nobody could actually comply with, the two rules that conflict) before I ever mention Fuller's name. Ideally there's some small real stake so the frustration is genuine — we usually do a points system where the winner picks next month's topic. Give me the full setup: roles, rules, materials, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, and then a debrief plan that maps specific moments from the game onto Fuller's list. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g. my guess is the person playing the ruler will ham it up as a tyrant instead of staying benevolent, and that ruins the point), and be honest about which part of the rule-of-law ideal a game like this just can't capture and I'd have to supply through discussion.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words, and lead with a one-sentence summary of the activity's core mechanic.
