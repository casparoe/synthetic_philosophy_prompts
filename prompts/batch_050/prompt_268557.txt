I facilitate a monthly ethics discussion circle at my local library. We are all laypeople -- no one has any philosophy training, myself included -- and I prepare each session by reading on my own. Next month the topic is whether it can ever be right to lie, and I want to devote the first half of the evening to Kant's notorious position, because most members will have heard the "murderer at the door" scenario as a punchline and will arrive assuming Kant was simply a rigid fool. I think his view deserves a fairer hearing than that, but I don't trust myself to give it one on the fly.

So: don't explain Kant's position to me. Plan how I should explain it, in roughly thirty minutes, to intelligent adults who have never read a page of him. Specifically, I want to know:

First, the order in which the pieces must be introduced, and why each has to precede the next. My instinct is to open with the murderer at the door, since that's what everyone knows, but I suspect that starting with the most extreme case guarantees a room full of hostility before anyone understands what kind of claim Kant is even making -- that his test is about what one can consistently will, not a rulebook handed down from nowhere. Should I begin with his own less dramatic example, like the lying promise to borrow money, and only later introduce the murderer case and Kant's late essay replying to his critic Benjamin Constant, where Kant refuses to back down? Or is there a better sequence?

Second, the one carrying example. What must it contain (a duty owed to a specific person, a conflict where lying looks obviously justified, stakes high enough to matter) and what must it avoid? I worry the murderer scenario is simultaneously too lurid and too easy to dodge with "I'd just stay silent or slam the door," which lets the group escape before meeting the actual argument.

Third, the misconception map. I expect at least these: that the categorical imperative is just the golden rule dressed up; that Kant thinks consequences never matter for morality at all, rather than that they can't be the ground of a duty; that his position forces you to volunteer the truth rather than merely forbidding you to assert a falsehood (the silence-and-evasion loophole, and whether it actually is a loophole for him); and that the whole view rests on worshipping rules for their own sake. For each one, tell me where in the sequence it will predictably surface and how to head it off there rather than after it has hardened.

Fourth, one question I can pose at the end whose answers will tell me whether the group genuinely grasped the position -- as opposed to merely having memorized "Kant says never lie" -- and one or two things I should openly declare out of scope for the evening (Kant's distinction between duties of right and duties of virtue? his own casuistical asides about white lies in company?) rather than improvise on.

Please give the plan as a sequence of moves with the reasoning attached to each, not as a lecture script, and please write it in American English.
