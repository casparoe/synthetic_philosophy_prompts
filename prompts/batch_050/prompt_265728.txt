Next month I'm presenting a paper at my department's colloquium defending reflective equilibrium as more than a housekeeping method for ethics. My thesis: the mutual adjustment between considered judgments and general principles is epistemically productive, not merely cosmetic. Considered judgments, I argue, are the deliverances of a capacity trained on actual moral experience, roughly the way perception is trained on the environment, so revising them against principles functions like calibrating an instrument — and wide reflective equilibrium is that calibration procedure, which is why its outputs deserve more trust than the raw intuitions going in. The audience is a mixed philosophy department, but the questioners who matter are two Kantians suspicious of anything intuition-based, a moral realist of an intuitionist bent who will push me to go further than I do, an experimental philosopher whose own published work shows how easily moral intuitions shift under framing and order effects, and a formal epistemologist who holds that coherence can never manufacture justification out of nothing.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience — not the generic ones from a textbook treatment of the method — ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that particular questioner would put it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credibility with the experimental philosopher without handing her the whole argument, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. End with a final line beginning "Most dangerous objection:" followed by the single objection most likely to sink the talk.
