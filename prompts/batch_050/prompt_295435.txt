In 1978, David Premack and Guy Woodruff asked whether the chimpanzee has a theory of mind, reporting that their chimpanzee Sarah, shown videotapes of a human actor unable to reach some bananas, tended to select photographs depicting the solution to the actor's problem — as if she attributed intentions to him. The controversy that followed remains instructive: in the 1990s Daniel Povinelli found that chimpanzees begged just as readily from experimenters whose eyes were covered as from those who could see, and concluded that apes read behavior, not mental states; then in 2000 Brian Hare, Josep Call, and Michael Tomasello showed that subordinate chimpanzees in a competitive setting preferentially approached food that a dominant rival could not see, which many took as evidence that chimpanzees do track what others perceive. Critics such as Cecilia Heyes have argued that for any mindreading explanation of such results, an associative or behavior-reading one can be constructed, and vice versa — leaving the dispute, especially over belief attribution, unresolved in outline even where particular results are accepted.

Analyze this episode through Morgan's canon — Conwy Lloyd Morgan's injunction, formulated in the 1890s, that one should not interpret an animal's behavior as the outcome of a higher psychological faculty when it can be interpreted as the outcome of a lower one. I am familiar with the textbook slogan; what I want is a disciplined application. Note that the canon as Morgan stated it is about a hierarchy of faculties, not simplicity in general, and that it was meant as a rule of evidential inference rather than a blanket denial of animal mentality. Give me your analysis as exactly five numbered points, each of two or three sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, covering: what the canon, carefully stated, actually forbids and permits, as against the popular rendering of it as prefer the simplest explanation; what it says about Premack and Woodruff's original interpretation, given the lower-faculty explanations available for Sarah's choices; whether it discriminates between Povinelli's behavior-reading and Hare, Call, and Tomasello's mindreading accounts, or instead goes indeterminate once the associative alternative is allowed to become arbitrarily elaborate; what the episode reveals about the canon's presupposition of a fixed scale of higher and lower faculties, and about the asymmetric costs of over- versus under-attributing mind; and a verdict on whether the controversy vindicates the canon as methodology, shows it to be rhetoric both sides can wield, or demonstrates the need for some replacement principle.
