I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized company with a strong blameless postmortem culture: after any serious incident we produce a document with a Summary, an Impact section, a Timeline in UTC, a Root Cause Analysis done via five whys, Contributing Factors, What Went Well, and Action Items with named owners and deadlines. At our holiday charity auction last month I watched two executives bid a $100 restaurant voucher up past $900, both visibly miserable by the end, and a colleague explained the dollar auction to me afterward: a trap in which the rules require the second-highest bidder to pay their bid too, so that whoever is in second place always finds it locally rational to bid once more, and the only true winner is the auctioneer. I haven't been able to stop seeing the pattern in our engineering projects since.

Write a parody postmortem in exactly that corporate genre — deadpan, procedural, entirely humorless in tone — for a fictional incident: Project NIGHTJAR, an internal platform rewrite that consumed three teams for eighteen months before being cancelled. The escalation mechanics should map faithfully onto the dollar auction: at every quarterly checkpoint, abandoning the project would have meant writing off everything spent and 'losing' to the rival team building a competing internal system (the second-highest bidder pays as well), so funding one more quarter was always the cheaper-looking option; the auctioneer role should be quietly filled by the external consultancy billing hours throughout. About 700 words, using the section structure described above. The jokes should come from getting the game theory right while misapplying engineering methodology to it: a five-whys chain that terminates in the payoff structure itself, an action item to install a 'circuit breaker' on human motivation with an owner and a Q3 deadline, a What Went Well section praising the teams' bid discipline. Per our blameless culture, the document must scrupulously avoid blaming any individual while describing a process in which everyone behaved reasonably and the outcome was catastrophic — the blamelessness should be part of the joke. Include at least one line from the incident review in which an engineer earnestly restates the trap without noticing it (along the lines of 'we couldn't stop, we'd already invested so much'). The satire should stay legible without ever winking: a reader familiar with the escalation-of-commitment literature should feel the author understands it, and a reader who isn't should still find it funny simply as a document about a doomed migration.
