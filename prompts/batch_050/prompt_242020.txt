**Examination question**

Using the proposition that Ada is taller than Bia, explain Bradley’s regress as a problem about the unity of a relational proposition or fact, rather than merely as an argument that there are infinitely many entities. Distinguish the inventory version, the grounding or explanatory version, and the psychological version of the regress. Then assess two proposed solutions: first, treating problematic relations as internal relations grounded in the intrinsic properties of their relata; second, taking states of affairs or facts as primitive, nonmereological unities. Conclude by explaining whether the regress gives good reason to deny that there are genuinely external relational facts.

**Student’s answer**

A list containing Ada, Bia, and the relation taller-than is not yet the proposition that Ada is taller than Bia. The list does not tell us that the relation applies to those objects, nor does it distinguish Ada’s being taller than Bia from Bia’s being taller than Ada. If we add a further relation of instantiation or exemplification to connect the relation to the objects, the new collection still appears to need something that connects the instantiation relation to its relata. The same problem can then be repeated indefinitely.

There are nevertheless different regresses here. The inventory regress concerns how many connecting relations must be included in the relational fact. This version is not vicious merely because it is infinite. An actually infinite collection is not logically impossible, and if all the higher-order connectors come with the original relation automatically, the main objection may just be that the ontology is extravagant. The explanatory regress is more serious. If the unity at each level has to be explained by another constituent whose own unity requires the same kind of explanation, the explanation of the original fact is continually deferred. The problem is not infinity by itself but the recurrence of the very problem that was supposed to be solved. The psychological regress is much weaker: understanding the proposition does not require consciously representing an infinite series of connectors, so human cognition is not directly threatened.

Internal relations offer a partial solution. Ada’s being taller than Bia may be grounded entirely in Ada’s height and Bia’s height. If so, there need not be an additional external tie between Ada and Bia over and above those monadic facts. This works naturally for comparative relations and perhaps for any relation that supervenes wholly on intrinsic properties. It is less plausible for paradigmatically external relations such as being two meters apart, touching, or causing. Moreover, reducing a two-place relation to several one-place facts does not by itself explain how those monadic facts jointly ground a fact with two subjects.

The primitive-fact response denies that the fact is assembled from independently existing parts by a further connector. The fundamental item is the state of affairs of Ada’s being taller than Bia. Ada, Bia, and taller-than can be abstracted from that state of affairs, but its unity is nonmereological and therefore does not require another relation. This stops the regress at its first step, although the solution is incomplete if nonmereological unity is only named rather than explained. It also shows that the unity of the proposition is just its grammatical ordering: once the words or concepts are arranged in the correct structure, no further metaphysical unifier is required.

Bradley’s regress therefore does not establish that there are no external relational facts. What it rules out is the conception of a relational fact as a mere aggregate of objects and relations. Either some apparently external relations must be grounded in monadic properties, or facts must possess a primitive form of unity not reducible to another constituent. The second option is more general, but it carries the burden of explaining why its stopping point is principled rather than stipulative.
