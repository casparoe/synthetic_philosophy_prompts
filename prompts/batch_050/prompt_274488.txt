I work as a policy analyst for a mid-sized city government, and for the past two years we have been piloting a market-based decision procedure loosely modeled on futarchy: a proposal is adopted if conditional markets forecast that a published welfare index will come out higher under adoption than under rejection, with the losing branch's trades voided. I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for, and before I write it up for our internal review I'd like to know whether it already has one. Two instances. First, a congestion-pricing proposal: the markets estimated a small negative effect on the index, the proposal was rejected, and a year later a comparable city across the river adopted the same scheme with visibly good results. What struck me is that our own market-based estimate barely moved in response, because the rejection branch never resolves, so the procedure itself generated no outcome data about the policy it declined — the only correction available was foreign evidence that participants felt free to discount as not applicable here. Second, a building-retrofit program that was rejected twice on the strength of weak forecasted returns; a small pilot was then proposed precisely in order to generate the missing evidence, but the pilot itself had to pass the same market test and was rejected on the grounds that existing estimates did not justify it. The mechanism refused to fund the very experiment that could have corrected its own estimates.

The structure I see is this: the procedure only ever produces outcome data for the options it selects, so a pessimistic estimate about an unselected option is never put to the test, and if that estimate is wrong the error is self-sealing — in an ordinary forecasting contest a bad forecast is exposed at resolution, but here the forecast's being believed is exactly what prevents the event whose observation would expose it. I want to be careful about what it is not: liquidity was fine, nobody was trading in bad faith, so this isn't the standard manipulation worry; the welfare index was not being gamed, so it isn't Goodhart's law either, at least not in any form I recognize; and while it resembles the statistician's problem of learning only from the treatment arm you actually ran, that is usually discussed as a difficulty for outside evaluators, whereas here it is a structural feature of a decision rule that chooses among options using its own estimates. So: does this exact configuration have an established name in the literature on decision markets, experimental governance, or causal inference, and if several concepts are nearby, which fits best and how does the phenomenon differ from each of the others? If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it precisely, and give boundary cases — does the phenomenon require markets at all, or does it afflict any evidence-gated rule (say, an agency that funds only interventions with prior evidence of effectiveness); does it afflict one-shot decisions or only iterated ones; and what modification to the decision rule would break it, if any?

One request about calibration: please clearly separate what is settled from what is contested. If you draw on established concepts, flag them as such; if you coin a term of your own, mark it explicitly as a coinage rather than presenting it as standard terminology; and where people who know this area genuinely disagree — for instance, about whether a mechanism of this kind could price in or subsidize its own experimentation, and so whether the phenomenon is a fatal flaw or a manageable design detail — present it as an open dispute rather than as a resolved consensus in either direction. I'd rather get a nameless but precisely carved phenomenon than a confident label that papers over the edges.
