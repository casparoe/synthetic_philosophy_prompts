I organize a monthly discussion evening at our public library — about sixteen adults, no academic background, ninety minutes per session. Our last few meetings kept circling a question nobody could settle: when a harm comes about only because millions of people each do something small and seemingly harmless — driving everywhere, buying cheap factory-farmed meat, staying home on election day — and no one person's choice would have changed the outcome, is each person still on the hook for it? I'd like you to design an in-person game or activity for us, not a lecture. Two requirements. First, one phase must be structured so that each individual choice genuinely cannot affect the outcome, with the group discovering this only afterward — I want them to feel the pull of "my bit made no difference," not just hear the phrase. Second, a later phase should secretly alter the setup so that any single participant might turn out to have been the decisive one, so the group can fight over whether a small chance of mattering creates real responsibility. Give me concrete rules, materials, a scoring system that doesn't involve money, a debrief plan that connects what happened back to our original question, an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and a candid note on which feature of the real-world problem an activity like this simply cannot reproduce.
