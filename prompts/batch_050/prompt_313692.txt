I help run an interdisciplinary seminar at my university — mostly political theorists and a few computer scientists — and next month I've been assigned the session on how to build a well-behaved AI system. The group's one prior session covered the basic worry that optimising hard for a goal produces systems that game, resist correction, or pursue proxies, so they know what a goal-directed agent is in rough outline. My job is to introduce the alternative that's sometimes proposed in alignment discussions: instead of making a system that wants outcomes, make one that wants its actions to be approved of by us — an approval-directed rather than goal-directed design. Last time I attempted this informally I reached for a thermostat as my opening example, and it backfired badly: half the room decided approval-direction was just obedience with extra steps, and the other half decided it was obviously safer and the session was over. I clearly need a plan rather than another improvisation.

So: plan the explanation for me, don't give me the explanation itself. Lay out the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what — for instance, whether the room first needs a crisp distinction between an agent's objective and its behaviour, or between evaluating actions and evaluating outcomes, before the proposal will even parse for them. Tell me what single example should carry the whole session, what properties it must have to work (and what it must conspicuously lack), and why a household servant or a committee-vetting consultant might do the job better than my thermostat. Map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they'll surface — I anticipate 'so it just does whatever we say', 'approvers can't be manipulated or fooled', and 'an approval-seeker would never take initiative' — and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than patched up afterwards. Include what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (the mechanics of training systems on human feedback, inner misalignment, what happens when approvers disagree) and how to say so without losing the room, and finish with one question I can pose at the end whose answer would show the distinction has actually landed rather than merely been memorised.
