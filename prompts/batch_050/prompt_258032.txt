Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day animal ethics workshop, and I need to be ready for the question period rather than merely ready for the talk. Some background on why this matters to me: four months ago I adopted a retired racing greyhound with a history of neglect, and trying to articulate — to myself, then on paper — what exactly was wrong with how she was treated pushed me from the welfarist vocabulary I had always assumed toward Kant. The paper that resulted argues that the passage commonly numbered §17 of the Doctrine of Virtue in Kant's The Metaphysics of Morals (1797) — where he holds that cruel treatment of animals is wrong because it deadens a natural capacity for sympathetic feeling that morality relies upon in our dealings with persons, so that the wrong is, strictly, a violation of a duty owed to oneself — should not be read as a grudging footnote to real ethics but as capturing something welfarism structurally misses: that cruelty is wrong partly because of what it does to the character of the person who practices it, and that this explains why gratuitous cruelty horrifies us even when painless killing does not. I also draw on the old-dog example from the student lecture notes published as the Lectures on Ethics, paraphrased rather than quoted in the paper, since I know the notes' reliability is contested.

Here is the audience. Roughly eight consequentialists of a Singer-inflected welfarist kind, for whom moral considerability tracks sentience and nothing else; two Regan-style rights theorists; and one Kant scholar whose presence is what actually worries me, because my reading of §17 leans hard on the analogy Kant draws between animal and human feeling, and that analogy looks empirical and therefore hostage to psychology in a way a Kantian should find embarrassing.

I want a committed prep sheet, not a menu. Rank the objections by how likely they are and how much damage they do; phrase each one the way that particular audience member would actually put it, in their own vocabulary (moral considerability, wrong kind of reasons, extensional adequacy — whatever fits); and for each, tell me flatly whether to concede it, and what the concession costs, or to hold, and what the best single reply is, including where that reply is weakest. Identify the one reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session, and say why. And name the objection I am privately hoping nobody raises — the one I have no good answer to yet — rather than letting me leave the room unready for it. Where you think my reading of Kant is itself the soft underbelly, say so and say whether I should defend the reading or retreat to a weaker one before someone forces the choice on me.
