I got into an argument with friends after a museum exhibit about reparations, and I realized my intuitions don’t obviously hang together. Say a government confiscated a named community’s forest in 1910 and still owns it as a public park; I think it should return the land or its current value to the community, even if most of the value now comes from later public maintenance. Say instead that the state organized forced labor against a dispersed ethnic group but destroyed the payroll records, so neither particular victims nor their descendants can now be identified; I still think reparations are owed to the surviving community as a community, not merely to society generally through neutral welfare spending. A third case: a private railway knowingly bought equipment and land from a company whose profits came from coerced workers, paying a bargain price because the legal claims were inconvenient; I think the buyer inherits some liability, but if it merely later happens to operate over the same route without buying those tainted assets, it doesn’t.

Two more verdicts are where things get messy. If descendants of victims are now wealthy and secure, I don’t think the claim disappears, though maybe the remedy should become commemorative or institutional rather than a giant transfer. But if the wrong is ancient, descendants of victims and perpetrators are thoroughly intermarried, and nearly everyone now belongs partly to both groups, I don’t think there is a material debt that can sensibly be transferred between present people. Also, if the confiscated forest would certainly have been destroyed by an unrelated dam project five years later, I think the state owes only the extra loss it caused, not the forest’s whole modern value, though it may still owe an apology for the rights violation. Is there one principle behind all this—perhaps separating the persistence of a claim from the counterfactual amount of loss, and tracing from beneficiary liability? Please state the best principle precisely, say which case motivates each part, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Also test it on a case I haven’t listed: an unidentified worker’s stolen wages eventually seeded a university endowment whose records prove the source but none of the worker’s descendants can be found.
