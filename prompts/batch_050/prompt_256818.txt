I am a PhD candidate in moral philosophy writing a dissertation on the ethics of abortion, and I have run into a problem I would like help reasoning through. My dissertation advisor has a well-known set of intuitions about Thomson-style cases involving bodily autonomy, and I need to predict how she will judge a new hybrid case, because an argument in my third chapter depends on it. Here is her track record, as I have compiled it from seminars and conversations over two years:

1. The canonical unconscious violinist (you are kidnapped and connected for nine months): disconnecting is permissible; staying connected is generous but not owed.
2. Same setup, but the required connection is for the rest of your life: still permissible to disconnect, though she calls refusal at that scale monstrous rather than merely ungenerous -- indecent, but not unjust.
3. The trivial-cost variant (a famous person needs only the touch of your hand on their brow to survive): refusal is the height of indecency, but she insists it violates no right.
4. The expanding-child case (you are trapped in a tiny house with a rapidly growing child who will crush you; the child is innocent): she holds you may kill the child in self-defense.
5. The people-seed case where you installed a fine-mesh screen over your open window and a seed drifts in anyway: you have no obligation to house the resulting person; removal is permissible.
6. The people-seed case where you knowingly left the window wide open, aware that seeds drift on the wind, because you enjoy the breeze: here she switches -- once the seed has taken root, she says, you may not expel it, because you courted the risk.
7. A variant of her own: the dependent party is a patient in an irreversible coma with no prospect of future experience, and disconnection costs you nothing. She says disconnecting is not merely permissible but involves no indecency at all, on roughly future-like-ours grounds: the patient has no future of value to lose.

The new case: through your negligent driving you cause an accident, and the victim -- who will fully recover to an ordinary life -- can survive only by being connected to your circulatory system for nine months. You consented to nothing; you simply drove carelessly.

Please do the following. First, lay out the candidate principles that jointly fit verdicts 1 through 7 -- I can see at least three: a responsibility-sensitive rights principle, a pure bodily-rights principle on which fault never transfers a right to one's body, and a consent-or-assumption-of-risk principle that distinguishes negligence from voluntary risk-taking. Check whether each really fits all seven verdicts, including the decency verdicts in 2 and 3, and say where the fit is strained. Second, state what each principle predicts about the new case, and where at least two of them diverge. Third, give your best prediction of my advisor's actual verdict, identify which earlier case is the closest analog, and explain why. Fourth, name the further hypothetical that would most efficiently discriminate among the surviving principles. Finally, tell me whether your prediction changes if the victim, upon disconnection, would survive only in a state with a radically diminished future -- and whether verdict 7 commits my advisor to an answer there that she might not want.
