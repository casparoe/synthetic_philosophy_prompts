A friend of mine who audited an ethics class last semester sent me her notes on Scanlon's contractualism because she knows I'm trying to get a handle on it for a reading group. Her summary goes like this: 'Scanlon's view is that an act is wrong if it would be forbidden by any principle that nobody could reasonably reject as a basis for general agreement. So morality works like a contract negotiation: picture everyone getting a veto over the rules they don't like, and whatever survives the vetoes is what's morally required. Since each individual holds a veto, you can never justify imposing a burden on someone by pointing to gains for lots of other people -- that's utilitarian aggregation, which is exactly what the theory was built to block. So take a rescue case where you can either save one person from dying or save a million people from painful headaches: the contractualist has to save the one, because that person's complaint is the strongest single complaint, and adding up headaches would treat people as mere receptacles of welfare. Scanlon therefore ends up where Taurek does -- the numbers don't count -- and the honest upshot is that in tradeoff cases you should just flip a coin between saving the one and saving the many. And since every moral question is really about which principles people could reject, the theory gives you a complete account of wrongness, including what we owe to animals and future people.'

Something about this smells off to me, especially the coin-flip bit, but I read half of What We Owe to Each Other years ago and can't pin down what's wrong versus what's just surprising. Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a careful contractualist would actually accept, which parts are misrepresentations (subtle or otherwise), and whether the coin-flip conclusion follows from the parts that are accurate? Also: my friend thinks the 'veto' framing is a totally innocent paraphrase of reasonable rejectability. Is it?
