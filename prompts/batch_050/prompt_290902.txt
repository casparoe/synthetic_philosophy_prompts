I am a novelist at work on a dinner-party scene, and I have drafted an argument between two of my characters that I can no longer adjudicate myself -- both keep winning when I write from their side. I would like a verdict on the substance, not on the dialogue.

The dispute is about whether what I ought to do is fixed by what an idealized version of me would choose. (1) Anna claims that the right answer to any "what should I do?" question is whatever I would endorse if I were fully informed, vividly aware of all the relevant facts, and reasoning without error -- my actual judgment is authoritative only insofar as it approximates that ideal deliberator's. (2) Ben replies that this smuggles in a stranger: once you have corrected all my confusions and supplied me with experiences I have never had, the resulting person's values may diverge from mine in ways that are not mere repairs -- the sheltered man, fully informed, may cease to be the sheltered man -- so the idealized verdicts bind me no more than the well-reasoned advice of a wiser friend who happens to share my memories. (3) Anna answers that idealization is like error-correction in arithmetic: the procedure does not replace the agent, it removes distortions, and the values being refined are mine and no one else's; if I reject the ideal self's authority, I am treating my own mistakes as constitutive of my identity. (4) Ben's final move: even granting that, the normativity is unexplained -- pointing at a counterfactual person and saying "you would agree with her" describes a prediction, not an obligation, and a prediction about what I would want cannot by itself generate a reason for the actual, unidealized me, who will in fact never deliberate that way.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say which of the four moves succeed and which fail, whether Anna's error-correction analogy actually answers Ben's worry about constitutive ignorance, and what, if anything, both are missing about how an idealized deliberator's verdicts could acquire authority over the actual agent.
