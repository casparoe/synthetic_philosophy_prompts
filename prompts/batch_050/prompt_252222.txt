I chair the five-member bioethics and planetary protection panel of a national space agency, and we must issue a written opinion on the following proposal.

A commercial consortium wants to fly what it calls a microbial precursor mission to Mars: a sealed bioreactor, landed inside a lava tube adjacent to a site where subsurface brines are suspected, containing cyanobacteria engineered for two purposes — to test in-situ oxygen production over a ten-year run, and to be incapable of surviving outside the reactor because they metabolically require a synthetic nutrient not found in nature (a built-in kill switch, in the consortium's phrase). The consortium's argument is that containment plus the kill switch makes the risk of releasing Earth life into a possibly habitable Martian environment negligible, and that the oxygen-production data are needed for the crewed program planned for the following decade. It adds that even in the unlikely event of a containment breach, the escapees would either die or be outcompeted by any native microbes already adapted to Martian conditions.

Two considerations have divided the panel.

First, our chief scientist argues that even a small probability of release is unacceptable because of a peculiar asymmetry. If life arose independently on Mars, discovering it would be the strongest obtainable evidence that life is common in the universe — two independent origins in a single solar system would imply that life arises readily — and Earth microbes introduced into Martian brines could either destroy native life outright or so scramble the biochemical record that we could never again know whether what we later found was genuinely Martian. The loss, she argues, is irreversible in a way the consortium's benefits are not: the oxygen experiment can be run in thirty years, after far better reconnaissance, while a contaminated aquifer cannot be un-contaminated.

Second, a dissenting panel member responds that the pristine-Mars premise is likely false. Meteorite impacts have been exchanging material between Earth and Mars for billions of years, so terrestrial microbes have probably been delivered there naturally all along, and any surviving native life would already have encountered Earth biology. On his view, the panel would be imposing real costs — delaying a crewed program that carries its own risks and benefits — to protect a state of nature that never existed.

The consortium has also offered two concessions: it will fund a dedicated life-detection survey of the landing region before launch, and it proposes that half of the most promising candidate habitats on Mars be designated permanently off-limits as preserves — a planetary set-aside — as compensation for any residual risk. Our chief scientist replies that a pre-mission survey cannot prove a negative about the deep subsurface, and that the set-aside would be unenforceable the moment commercial incentives shift.

Please draft the panel's written opinion — approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It should (a) state a clear bottom line, with any conditions specified concretely enough that the consortium could either comply or object on the record; (b) engage the strongest argument on the losing side of your recommendation rather than a weakened version of it; (c) take a position on how the panel should reason under this kind of asymmetry — irreversible loss against deferrable benefit — given that both the probability of a release and the probability of indigenous life are unknown, and on how much the meteorite-exchange objection should blunt that reasoning; and (d) say whether the kill switch and the set-aside genuinely do the moral work their proponents claim, or are better understood as devices for making an unavoidable judgment call look like a technical one.
