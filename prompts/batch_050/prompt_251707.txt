I facilitate a monthly evening philosophy course for adults at a community education center -- about fourteen regulars, mostly professionals with no formal philosophy training: a nurse, an accountant, a retired schoolteacher, a software engineer, and so on. Over the autumn we worked through Kant and the utilitarians, so they have a decent feel for what a moral theory looks like and a strong expectation that ethics means principles you could state and apply. Next month I have one ninety-minute session on Levinas, and I want to teach a single idea rather than survey him: the face of the Other as the origin of ethical obligation -- the thought that responsibility for another person is not derived from any principle, contract, or feeling but is something that seizes me before I've chosen anything, so that ethics precedes rather than follows from theory. My draft plan starts with "imagine a stranger approaches you on the street and looks at you," but I'm worried that opening invites exactly the readings I want to avoid, and I'd like a better plan rather than my plan polished.

Please give me a teaching plan for the session, not the explanation itself. Use these sections, in this order: "Sequence and dependencies" (roughly 250 words: the order in which the pieces must be introduced, and for each step what it presupposes about the ones before it -- I suspect the notion of obligation-without-reciprocity has to be in place before anything about the face can mean anything, but tell me if I have the dependencies wrong); "The carrying example" (roughly 200 words: the one example the whole session should be built around, what it must contain and what it must not contain -- and whether my stranger-on-the-street opening can be salvaged or should be discarded, and why); "Misconception map" (roughly 300 words: the misreadings this audience will predictably form -- I'm expecting at least that the face means someone's literal, expressive face, that Levinas is recommending empathy or kindness as a virtue, and that this is just the golden rule in phenomenological dress -- in the order they'll surface during the session, and exactly where in the sequence I should intercept each one rather than letting it harden); "Check for understanding" (roughly 100 words: one question or short exercise for the last ten minutes whose answers would show whether the idea landed, and what the tell-tale wrong answers would sound like); "What to leave out" (roughly 100 words: which adjacent Levinasian material -- the trace, the third party, his relationship to phenomenology, his later vocabulary -- I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level, and how to say so without making the session feel truncated).

Finally, a short section titled "Confidence": say how sure you are that the dependency structure and the misconception map reflect how Levinas's idea actually fails to land for non-specialists, and single out the one point in your plan you are least certain about.
