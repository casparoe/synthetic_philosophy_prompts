Last weekend a friend of mine -- a decision theorist by training -- and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether rational credences have to be sharp. He defended strict probabilism: an ideally rational agent's doxastic state is always representable by a single probability function, and anything vaguer is either indecision or a confused way of encoding ordinary higher-order uncertainty. I defended the imprecise camp: in cases of genuinely thin or conflicting evidence -- think Ellsberg-type urns, or a question where your total evidence is one anecdote -- it is not just excusable but rationally required to hold a credal state representable only by a set of probability functions. We traded the usual moves. He pressed the sequential-decision objections: agents with imprecise credences seem to make dominated choices across time, pay to avoid free information, and dilate -- becoming less committal when given evidence that is, by their own lights, irrelevant. I replied that those pathologies are artifacts of particular decision rules for imprecise agents rather than of imprecision itself, and that the classic arguments for probabilism (Dutch books, accuracy-dominance) only establish coherence given precision, never precision itself. We ended at a stalemate, and I'd like an outside verdict of a specific kind.

So: what's your actual credence in the following proposition -- that there exist epistemic situations in which an ideal agent (logically omniscient, unlimited time, no computational bounds) is rationally permitted to hold credences not representable by any single probability function? I don't want "it depends on which decision rule you pair imprecision with," and I don't want a survey of the literature. I want a number, and I want the derivation. Decompose your credence into the sub-questions it rides on and give a rough probability for each: first, whether the standard justifications of probabilism actually establish precision, or only coherence among whatever credences one happens to have; second, whether imprecise states can always be collapsed, without loss, into sharp higher-order uncertainty over probability functions, so that "sets of priors" is mere notation; third, whether dilation and the sequential-choice pathologies are genuine marks against imprecision or artifacts of rules like Gamma-maximin that no one is forced to adopt; fourth, whether there's even a determinate fact of the matter about what ideal rationality requires here, as opposed to a cluster of precisifications of "rational" on which the question gets different answers.

Then show me how the sub-probabilities combine into your headline number -- even roughly, even hand-wavingly -- and tell me which sub-question the final figure is most sensitive to: if you could have one of these sub-questions settled for you by an omniscient being, which answer would move your credence the most, and in which direction? For calibration, my friend puts the proposition at about 0.15 and I put it at about 0.8. Given your decomposition, where does our disagreement most likely live -- which sub-question is he most plausibly getting wrong (or am I), and what argument or result, if it exists, would do the most to close the gap?
