Exam question:

(i) Define luminosity for a condition C, taking care to distinguish knowing that C obtains from merely being in a position to know that C obtains. (ii) State the KK principle and explain its relation to the luminosity of states of knowledge. (iii) Set out the margin-for-error argument against luminosity, including the roles of a gradual series of cases, the relevant safety or margin premise, and the assumption that C obtains in some relevant cases but not others. (iv) Does this argument by itself establish that no mental condition is luminous? Explain.

Student’s answer:

A condition C is luminous just in case, whenever C obtains for a subject, the subject knows that C obtains. This is stronger than merely having enough evidence to know: the knowledge has to be actual.

KK says that if a subject knows p, then the subject knows that they know p. Some philosophers formulate it more weakly: if one knows p, one is in a position to know that one knows p. KK is therefore a luminosity thesis for conditions of the form “the subject knows p”: whenever that condition obtains, the subject knows, or at least is in a position to know, that it obtains. On the stronger formulation, KK is slightly stronger than the bare luminosity of knowledge states.

For the anti-luminosity argument, imagine a gradual series from a case in which someone definitely feels cold to a case in which they definitely do not. Suppose feeling cold is luminous. Then at every point where the person feels cold, they know that they feel cold. Knowledge also requires a margin for error: if one knows p in a particular case, p cannot be false in an immediately neighboring case that one’s evidence does not discriminate from the actual case. So if the person knows at one point that they feel cold, they must still feel cold at the next point. Luminosity then supplies knowledge at that point too, and repeating the process pushes the boundary all the way to a case in which the person does not feel cold, which is a contradiction. The argument therefore needs variation in C, sufficiently similar neighboring cases, and the margin premise.

The conclusion is that no non-trivial condition can be luminous, and consequently no mental condition is luminous. Even conditions such as being in pain can arise across imperceptible transitions, so there will always be cases in which one is in pain without being in a position to know that one is.
