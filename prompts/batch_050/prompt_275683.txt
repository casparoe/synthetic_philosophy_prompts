So this year in english class we read 1984 and also Orwell's essay Politics and the English Language, where he argues that sloppy, vague, euphemistic language and sloppy political thinking feed into each other — bad writing is both a symptom of muddled or dishonest thought and a cause of more of it. I want to write an essay testing that theory against a real case, and I need help thinking it through.

The case: after 9/11, the US government referred to certain interrogation methods as "enhanced interrogation techniques" rather than torture, and during the debate that followed, government officials, journalists, and the public all had to decide which words to use. Same thing happens with phrases like "collateral damage" for civilian deaths in war. News outlets openly debated whether to say "torture" or use the government's term.

Analyze this episode through Orwell's theory, and be disciplined about it:

1. As I understand it, Orwell actually makes TWO claims: (a) bad language is a symptom of thought that has already gone bad, and (b) bad language then causes worse thinking in the future, so it loops. Apply each claim separately. Was the euphemism just covering up a decision already made, or did calling it "enhanced interrogation" actually change what people were willing to accept or do? Which direction does the evidence from this episode mostly point?

2. Who exactly is the thinker whose thought is supposedly being corrupted? The officials who coined the term, the journalists who repeated it, or the public hearing it? Does Orwell's theory even distinguish between someone using euphemism strategically (knowing it's a cover) versus someone who comes to believe their own words? If the theory is silent on this, say so instead of forcing an answer.

3. Here's a complication I read about somewhere: the "euphemism treadmill" — the idea that any replacement word eventually picks up the negative associations of the original, so swapping words accomplishes nothing long term (people now hear "enhanced interrogation" as just meaning torture). Does this episode support Orwell or the treadmill view, and can both be true at once?

4. Run the analysis the other way too: Orwell's examples of bad writing were mostly lazy, dying metaphors and pretentious diction — but "enhanced interrogation" wasn't lazy, it was carefully engineered by people who knew exactly what they were doing. Is modern, deliberately manufactured political language a case his theory anticipated, or does it expose a gap in it? And what would Orwell's advice have been to a newspaper deciding whether to print the word "torture"?

Reach an actual verdict on whether this episode confirms, complicates, or undermines Orwell's theory — don't just say "it's a bit of both" without saying which side the balance tips toward and why.
