I'm preparing a seminar unit on epistemic game theory, and my students have already worked through the textbook material: the result that in finite games of perfect information, common knowledge of rationality leads to the backward induction outcome, plus the standard worry attached to it — if play ever reaches a node that the backward induction reasoning says should never be reached, the hypothesis that everyone is rational and knows everyone is rational starts to undermine itself, since the deviator's move gives later players reason to doubt something the reasoning required them to believe. They've seen the usual illustrations (the centipede game, the chain-store paradox), and I don't want another rehash of those. I'd like you to construct a fresh case to order.

Construct a sequential-move scenario involving three ordinary people in an everyday setting — something like negotiating the renewal of a shared lease, dividing the tasks and rewards of a family business, or planning how to split a finder's fee — with at least three decision nodes and stakes a normal person could face (money, time, favors; no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no abstract payoff matrix handed to the players). The case must satisfy three constraints: (i) the backward induction solution must be stark enough to be mildly counterintuitive — for instance, the game effectively ends immediately, or the first mover captures nearly everything — so that a deviation by the first mover is a psychologically plausible act rather than a stipulated blunder; (ii) at some intermediate node, the player to move must be able to rationalize the earlier deviation in at least two genuinely different ways — as a one-off mistake that carries no information about future play, versus as evidence that the deviator will continue to act against their apparent interests (or as a deliberate signal) — and these two readings must recommend different continuations; (iii) the scenario itself must not resolve the ambiguity by fiat: it should be underdetermined, given the facts of the story, which reading is correct.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. First, derive the backward induction outcome and state, node by node, what each player must believe about the others' rationality — and about the others' beliefs about rationality — for the reasoning to go through, being explicit about where second-order knowledge is needed. Second, pinpoint the exact node at which a deviation forces belief revision, and explain precisely why common knowledge of rationality cannot be consistently maintained there: whose rationality is impugned, and what consistent replacement beliefs the remaining players could adopt. Third, trace out how the two readings from constraint (ii) lead to different continuations, and connect this to the distinction between treating deviations as mere trembles versus treating them as informative about future play (forward induction reasoning). Fourth, identify the single structural feature of your game that is doing the philosophical work, and tell me what happens if it is removed — does the case collapse into a two-node interaction where the paradox no longer gets a grip? Finally, give the strongest response available to a defender of the textbook theorem — for example, one who restricts the common knowledge assumption to the beginning of play, or who reinterprets rationality as rationality only at nodes that are actually reached — and assess whether your scenario survives that defense.
