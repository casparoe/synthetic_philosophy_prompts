Imre Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations argues that informal mathematics grows by a Popperian logic of conjecture and refutation rather than by the accumulation of deductively secured truths. His own case study is the Euler polyhedron formula; I want you to take his machinery somewhere he never took it (he died in 1974): the history of the four-colour theorem. The salient facts, none of which you should need to look up:

- 1879: Alfred Kempe publishes a proof based on what came to be called Kempe chains; it is accepted by the mathematical community for about eleven years.
- 1890: Percy Heawood finds a flaw — a local configuration of regions that Kempe's chain-recolouring argument cannot handle — but salvages a five-colour theorem from the wreckage of the same argument.
- Early twentieth century: the salvage route matures into a general strategy: exhibit an unavoidable set of configurations, one of which must occur in any minimal counterexample, and show that every member of the set is reducible, i.e., cannot occur in a minimal counterexample.
- 1976: Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken announce a proof requiring roughly a thousand hours of computer time to check reducibility across an unavoidable set of nearly two thousand configurations; a public controversy follows over whether an unsurveyable, machine-checked argument is a proof at all.
- 1997: a streamlined proof (Robertson, Sanders, Seymour, Thomas) cuts the configuration set substantially but remains computer-dependent.
- 2005: Georges Gonthier produces a fully machine-formalized and mechanically verified version of the proof.

The analysis I want, in four parts:

1. The mapping. Apply Lakatos's categories — monster-barring, exception-barring, lemma incorporation, monster-adjustment, hidden lemmas, proof-generated concepts — to each stage, precisely. Is Heawood's intervention the exposure of a counterexample to a hidden lemma, and is the five-colour salvage lemma incorporation or exception-barring? Are "reducible configuration" and "unavoidable set" proof-generated concepts in Lakatos's sense, and does that strengthen or weaken the parallel with the polyhedron case?

2. The asymmetry. In Lakatos's own case study, the engine of progress is refutation of the conjecture: counterexamples to Euler's formula force the concept of polyhedron itself to evolve. Here the conjecture was never refuted — it was true all along — and every episode of refutation struck a proof, not the theorem. Does Lakatos's dialectic run at the level of proofs as comfortably as at the level of conjectures, or did he construct a theory of theorem-refutation and merely assume it transfers?

3. Where the machinery strains. The 1976 controversy did not pit a proof against a counterexample; it pitted communities against each other over the concept of proof. Can this be described as monster-adjustment or exception-barring applied to "proof" itself, and if so, who was barring which monster? More sharply: Lakatos's method presupposes proofs as surveyable thought experiments that mathematicians can deform, probe, and refute. What happens to the method when lemma-checking is delegated to a machine no human can survey?

4. The verdict on quasi-empiricism. Run the argument in both directions. Does the episode vindicate Lakatos — mathematics advancing through a fallible, revisable, partly empirical practice, with computation making the empirical character literal? Or does the 2005 formal verification show that the history was always, in principle, ordinary deductive mathematics with an unusually long proof, so that the quasi-empirical reading mistakes a sociological episode for an epistemological one? Give your judgment, with the strongest point on the losing side acknowledged.

Where Lakatos's framework is simply silent about some feature of the episode, say so rather than forcing an application.
