I have no academic training in philosophy, but I read a lot of popular books on political ideas, and I've been stuck on a passage from a recent one — a general history of democracy aimed at ordinary readers. The chapter on modern disillusionment with politics leans heavily on Simone Weil, and I'd like to know whether I can trust its account of her, because it struck me as both fascinating and somehow too neat.

Here is the relevant passage, as accurately as I can reproduce it from my notes:

"Simone Weil, the French philosopher writing during the Second World War, argued that political parties should be abolished outright. For her, parties are not imperfect instruments of democracy but its enemy from within. A party's true end is never the public good but its own growth, which is why every party carries totalitarianism in its nature: a multi-party system, on her account, is simply a dictatorship that has not yet finished consolidating itself. Because members must defend the party line whatever they privately think, parties train citizens to stop reasoning for themselves and to prefer victory over truth. Indeed, Weil held that political disagreement is largely manufactured by parties, and that ordinary citizens, left to themselves, would discover they agree on most questions once parties stopped dividing them. She therefore concluded that genuine democracy is impossible until parties are abolished and elections are replaced by direct citizen deliberation."

The book then uses this to argue that our current party-based systems are beyond repair and should be abandoned rather than reformed.

What makes me suspicious is that elsewhere I've picked up the impression that Weil was writing during the war in hopes of building a better France afterward — which doesn't sound like someone who thought democracy was doomed — and the claim that people would basically agree with each other if not for parties seems too convenient to be a serious philosopher's position. But I can't say where the summary goes wrong, if it does.

Could you go through it and tell me, with as much confidence as the record allows: which parts are accurate to Weil's actual view, which parts are distortions or overstatements, and what she in fact proposed instead of parties? I'm not looking for a survey of different scholarly readings with the verdict left to me — I'd like you to commit. If a point is genuinely contested among people who know her work, say so, but then still tell me which way you'd call it and why. And finally: once the summary is corrected, does the book's conclusion — that party politics cannot be reformed, only abandoned — follow from what Weil actually argued, or was the passage doing rhetorical work her view won't support?
