Okay so here's my situation. Next month I'm giving a paper at a workshop on rationality over time defending what I half-jokingly call the "no bad move" reading of Quinn's self-torturer. Quick recap in case it's fuzzy: the self-torturer has a device with a thousand-odd settings, each click up pays him a nice sum of money and raises his pain by an amount too small for him to notice, but click by click he walks himself up to a setting where he's in agony and would now pay more than he earned to be back at zero. My claim: there is no individual step at which he acts irrationally, full stop. Each click is worth it given what he can discriminate and prefer at that moment, and the real moral of the puzzle is that stepwise rationality can reliably march a perfectly rational agent into disaster. The fix, I argue, is not a better decision theory or some new diachronic rational requirement (no resolute-choice machinery, no special norm of plan-following) -- it's self-binding and institutions. Ulysses contracts, not axioms.

The audience: mostly formal decision theorists who cut their teeth on money-pump and Dutch book arguments for transitivity and think diachronic rationality is exactly where a view like mine goes to die; plus a contingent of sorites/vagueness people who suspect the whole puzzle is just the sorites in a trenchcoat and that once you sort out the semantics of "noticeably more painful" the problem evaporates; plus one behavioral economist who will want to know what any of this predicts about actual humans. Nobody there is sympathetic to me by default.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the six to eight objections I'll actually get from THIS crowd (not generic philosophy-of-rationality fare), ordered roughly by likelihood x damage. Phrase each one the way that faction would put it -- the decision theorists will talk pumps and separability, the vagueness people will talk tolerance principles and forced-march reasoning. For each: what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a note on where that reply is softest. Also tell me the single question I'm hoping nobody asks. I suspect it's something about whether my view makes the self-torturer exploitable by anyone who can offer him the dial, but correct me if there's a worse one lurking. Finally, one of the vagueness people will definately say that my "institutions not axioms" line just relocates the puzzle to the question of when it's rational to bind yourself, since from the inside, at setting 3, signing the contract looks like giving up free money. Give me something better than a shrug for that one.
