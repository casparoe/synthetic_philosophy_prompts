I teach a second-year metaphysics course at a small liberal arts college, and next month we're doing personal identity. Every year I lecture on Locke's memory criterion and then "present" Reid's brave officer objection, and every year the students nod along like it's a cute logic puzzle and then proceed to not take it seriously on the exam. The transitivity failure just doesn't land when it's a diagram on a slide -- boy, officer, general, arrow, arrow, no arrow, done. So I want to try making them walk into the contradiction themselves.

Design me a classroom activity for about 16 students, 75 minutes, that stages the brave officer problem as something they have to actually decide rather than observe. My half-baked idea: students get cast as people at different life-stages of the same "person" (or maybe as a panel that has to rule on identity claims across a long chain of stages, where each adjacent pair obviously shares memories/character but the endpoints share nothing) -- but I'll defer to you on the exact setup. What I care about is that at some point the room should find itself committed to "A is the same person as B" and "B is the same person as C" and yet unable to stomach "A is the same person as C," and they should feel genuinely stuck about which judgment to retract, not just dutifully write down "transitivity violated." Include the setup and materials, roles or rules, a rough timeline, and a mechanism that forces real commitments (some kind of verdict they have to hand in or bet on, not just open discussion, because open discussion lets them stay wishy-washy).

Also: a debrief plan that only afterwards connects what happened to Locke and Reid, and that pushes them past the obvious move of "just distinguish real memories from apparent ones" -- I'd like the debrief to surface at least one way of saving the memory criterion (maybe by weakening identity to something like overlapping chains of psychological connectedness) and one cost of each escape route. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., students gaming the setup, or treating the stages as obviously different people from the start so nothing feels paradoxical), and be honest about which feature of the actual philosophical problem the activity can't capture -- I suspect the fission-style worries and the question of whether identity is really what matters are things I'll have to supply by lecture, but tell me what you think.
