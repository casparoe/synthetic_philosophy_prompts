My philosophy club at school has been fighting all week about the torture vs dust specks dilemma — the one where you either have one person tortured horribly for 50 years straight with no breaks, or an absurd number of people (the version we read said 3^^^3, a number so big you couldnt write it out in your whole life) each get one dust speck in their eye, blink, and go on with their day. Most of us said the dust specks, obviously, because a speck is basically nothing. But our club president argued the dust specks are actually the worse option, since a tiny harm times a big enough number stops being tiny. It turned into a whole thing, and because I lost a related bet I now have to present a poem at Fridays meeting.

Write me a villanelle — 19 lines, the two repeating lines, think Do Not Go Gentle Into That Good Night shape — that argues the dust specks side is worse. Heres the catch though: I want the form itself to make the argument. One repeated line should carry the "come on, its just a speck" intuition, and the other should carry the multiplication answer, so that every time the refrains come back around the poem is literally doing the thing the specks-dont-count side denies — the same tiny thing landing again and again until it adds up to something you cant wave off. By the time the two lines meet at the end they should hit different than they did at the start, even though they havent changed. Keep the actual details of the case straight (one person, 50 years; the speck lasts a second and does no lasting damage), and no goofy rhymes like "specks" with "utility calculus" or whatever. I want our president nodding along and the other side feeling a little cornered by the last line.
