Is there a name for this thing I keep noticing in how countries remember their defeats? I'm not talking about the classic Lost Cause move — "we lost but the cause was noble" — that's well documented for the American South and I get how it works. What I keep seeing is subtler: the defeat gets retroactively redescribed as something close to a *choice*, or as the moment the nation actually became itself, and the commemorators don't even seem interested in litigating whether it was a military disaster.

Some cases. Masada gets retold less as "we were besieged and the Romans won" and more as "we chose death over submission," so the loss becomes the point. Dunkirk — objectively a catastrophe, an army barely escaping — becomes the moment Britain found out who it was, and somehow looms larger in the national story than any actual victory. Serbia commemorates Kosovo 1389 not despite losing to the Ottomans but *because* of it, as if the defeat consecrated the nation. Gallipoli does similar work for Australia: the founding bloodletting is a failed invasion of someone else's country, and nobody seems to think that's a problem for using it as the founding moment.

So the pattern, roughly: the defeat isn't defended as a secret win, and isn't mourned as a tragedy either. It's converted — into the founding act, the proof of national character. And part of the conversion usually involves making the defeat feel chosen or providential in hindsight, even when it obviously wasn't chosen at the time.

Does this specific conversion have a name, and is it distinguishable from (a) lost cause ideology, where the point is that the cause was just, (b) martyrdom narratives generally, and (c) victimhood nationalism? All three seem adjacent but none is exactly it — you can have the conversion without claiming the cause was right (Gallipoli, arguably), and you can have a lost cause without the defeat being treated as the nation's birthday. If there's no established term, how would you carve it? What are the boundary cases — does it count if the defeat genuinely was chosen ex ante versus redescribed as chosen afterwards? Does it still count if everyone involved knows it was a disaster? And is there any work on *why* defeats can do this founding work when victories apparently can't, which is honestly the part that interests me most?
