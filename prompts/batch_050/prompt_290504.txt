Take the thirty-year dispute between the heuristics-and-biases program and the fast-and-frugal-heuristics program as a case study in peer disagreement. The salient facts, so we are working from the same record: from the early 1970s, Kahneman and Tversky's research program argued, using tasks like the Linda conjunction problem and base-rate neglect studies, that human judgment systematically violates probability theory. Beginning in the 1990s, Gerd Gigerenzer and his group argued that these results were largely artifacts of unnatural task formats — that presenting the same problems in frequencies rather than single-event probabilities substantially reduces the errors, and that simple heuristics are often ecologically rational, outperforming more 'rational' procedures in real-world environments. Both camps were credentialed, respected, and working from largely the same published evidence. Neither camp's leader ever substantially conceded to the other. Late in his career, Kahneman ran an adversarial collaboration with Gary Klein on intuitive expertise and reached surprising agreement with him, though never with Gigerenzer. And over time, the broader field moved partway toward Gigerenzer's position: frequency formats and ecological rationality are now mainstream topics, and some of the original heuristics-and-biases findings have weakened under replication pressure.

Analyze this episode through the debate between conciliationism and steadfastness in the epistemology of disagreement. Concretely:

- By the lights of the equal weight view, what should Gigerenzer have done when he found himself, in the mid-1990s, holding a position rejected by the overwhelming majority of his peer field? Does the number of disagreeing peers matter on that view, and should it? Does conciliation toward a majority differ in kind from conciliation toward a single peer?
- Christensen's independence condition holds that your assessment of a peer's reliability must not rest on the very reasoning under dispute. Each camp, however, believed the other's experimental methods were systematically misleading. Is that a violation of the independence condition, a coherent application of it, or evidence that the condition cannot be satisfied in live scientific disputes?
- The Kahneman–Klein collaboration produced convergence only after joint work generated new first-order evidence. Does this vindicate steadfastness — the claim that neither side should move on higher-order evidence alone — or does it instead vindicate conciliationism done properly, where 'splitting the difference' is achieved by pooling evidence rather than by splitting credences? Say what conciliationism actually predicts should happen in an adversarial collaboration.
- The eventual partial vindication of Gigerenzer's position raises a retrospective problem: if conciliationism would have counseled him to concede in 1996, and conceding would have been an epistemic error, is that a counterexample to conciliationism, or does it merely show that conciliationist advice is fallible like any other advice? Consider whether the conciliationist can consistently say that his steadfastness was irrational but lucky, and whether 'rational but wrong' versus 'irrational but right' verdicts in historical cases carry any evidential weight for or against a normative theory.
- Finally, say what the episode shows about the framing of the philosophical debate itself. The standard model assumes two peers, shared evidence, and a single disputed proposition. Here the disagreement was between research programs that partly disagreed about what the evidence even was, and about which competencies count as expertise. Does that make the episode simply out of scope for the disagreement literature, or does it show that the literature's idealizations cause it to miss the cases that matter most?

Where a theory is silent or indeterminate about some step of the analysis, say so rather than forcing a verdict.
