I help teach an undergraduate ethics seminar, and a student recently pressed me on why it seems perfectly responsible to take a historian's word for what happened in 1914, but somehow deficient to take even a very wise friend's word for what one ought to do. I went home and tried to write down my own verdicts on cases, and they turned out to be messier than I'd assumed. I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them, and I'm happy to be told that some of my verdicts have to go. Here they are. First: my six-year-old asks why she mustn't pull the cat's tail, I tell her it hurts the cat and hurting creatures is wrong, and she accepts it entirely on my say-so; this seems not just fine but the only way moral learning can begin. Second: on a work trip abroad, a colleague who has lived in the country for years tells me it would be disrespectful to refuse the host's second pouring, and I take her word for it; also fine. Third: a friend who went vegan after researching industrial farming tells me eating industrially farmed meat is wrong, walks me through her reasons, and I stop buying it; this seems fine, maybe even admirable. Fourth: the same friend tells me that euthanasia is always wrong, I cannot see the force of her reasons but trust her character and vote accordingly; this strikes me as a failure, even if she happens to be right. Fifth: a man keeps a burdensome promise to his late mother solely because his rabbi told him that promises to the dead are binding, and he would otherwise have broken it; he does the right thing, I think, but something is missing in how he arrived at it. Sixth: a nurse follows her hospital's ethics committee when it rules that withdrawing treatment in a particular case is permissible, though she couldn't reconstruct their reasoning unaided; this seems fine, and ignoring them would arguably be negligent.

What bothers me is that my verdicts don't line up along any single obvious axis. Cases three and four both involve deferring to a trusted friend on a contested moral question, yet I judge them differently. Cases one and five both involve acting on another's authority without independent understanding, yet the child's deference seems healthy and the grieving son's seems hollow. Cases four and six both involve accepting a moral ruling one cannot reconstruct, yet the committee's authority seems to legitimate deference where a friend's does not. I've considered the hypothesis that the real variable is whether the testimony transmits reasons the recipient could in principle come to understand for herself — but then the child's case should trouble me more than it does. I've also wondered whether the distinction is between etiquette and empirical-adjacent matters, where testimony is plainly fine, and core moral questions, where it isn't — but the nurse's case and the vegan friend's case sit on opposite sides of that line in the wrong way.

So: is there a single principle of moral deference that captures all six verdicts, or close to all? State it precisely, clause by clause, and show me which case motivates each clause. If you think my tentative reason-transmission principle is the right seed, repair it; if you think it's hopeless, replace it. Then tell me two things: which of my six verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate and so must be revised or abandoned, and what your principle predicts for a case I haven't considered — a person in the near future with access to a machine intelligence that has been demonstrated, over millions of cases, to give more reliable moral verdicts than any human deliberator, who simply does whatever it recommends without engaging its reasoning. If wholesale deference to the oracle turns out fine on your principle, I want to know whether that's a mark for the principle or against it.
