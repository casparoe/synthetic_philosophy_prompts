I sit on the assessment panel for foster and adoptive applicants in my region, meaning I literally help decide who gets to parent. A few weeks ago, mid-argument with a friend over beers, it hit me how strange my job is: we grill prospective adopters for months, but anyone who has a kid biologically gets a total free pass. My friend brought up a proposal from political philosophy (I believe associated with Hugh LaFollette) that the state should license parenthood the way it licenses driving, since it's an activity that's risky, requires competence, and can seriously harm others. I pushed back with something I half-remembered from an ethics seminar, the idea that children have a right to an "open future" -- roughly, that parents may shape a kid's life but not close off the options the kid will need as an adult to choose their own path. The argument ended unresolved, but now I can't stop wondering whether my panel's actual checklist smuggles in answers to these questions without ever saying so.

So: write me a decision procedure our panel could actually run when assessing an applicant, built explicitly on those two ideas -- the licensing rationale (what minimal competencies and dispositions must be verified because the stakes are a child's safety) and the open-future constraint (what plans or commitments would be disqualifying because they'd foreclose the child's later options). I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it should punt to a human because the question is one of values rather than checkable facts. The tricky part, and the part I most care about, is drawing the line between illegitimate foreclosure (no schooling at all, refusing all medical care, raising the kid to believe leaving the community means damnation) and legitimate parental latitude (religious upbringing, unusual diets, countercultural lifestyles), since our current rubric is basically vibes on exactly this.

Then run the procedure on three applicants: a couple planning to homeschool with no outside curriculum and no vaccinations; a deeply religious couple who'll raise the child with strict rules but inside mainstream schooling and medicine; and a single applicant with well-managed chronic depression. Ideally one of these should produce an answer my panel would hate -- tell me which and why. Finally, be honest about the spillover: if the procedure's logic is consistent, does it commit us to licensing biological parents too, and if we flinch at that, what does the flinching show about the procedure?
