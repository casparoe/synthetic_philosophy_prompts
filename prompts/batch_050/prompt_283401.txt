Since I retired from the lab (forty years in protein crystallography, which prepared me for none of this) I've fallen in with a philosophy reading group at the library, and we just spent a month on Haidt's The Righteous Mind. We got into a fairly heated argument about whether any of this psychology tells you who's actually right about politics, and I realized my own views on it are a bit of a grab-bag. Before next week's meeting I want to write down where I've landed and have someone check whether the pieces even fit together, because I suspect I'm trying to have things both ways somewhere and can't see where.

Here's roughly my position: (1) Descriptively, moral foundations theory seems broadly right to me — liberals build morality mostly on care and fairness, maybe liberty, while conservatives spread their concern across care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity. (2) I don't think the extra conservative foundations are mere prejudice or disguised harm-avoidance; loyalty and sanctity strike me as genuinely moral concerns, whatever weight they deserve. (3) I'm unimpressed by evolutionary debunking arguments in ethics generally — where an intution came from tells you nothing about whether it's justified, that's just the genetic fallacy. (4) I also think the descriptive psychology is normatively neutral: the fact that one side reasons with five foundations and the other with two doesn't by itself tell us whose morality is better, in either direction. (5) But then on the famous dumbfounding cases — the adult siblings who sleep together once, in secret, with contraception, or the family that cooks and eats their dog after it's killed by a car — I'm fairly confident nothing wrong has happened, and the people who insist otherwise are just confabulating reasons on top of a disgust reaction. (6) And when I look at the sweep of history, moral progress seems to me to consist largely in the moral domain shrinking toward harm and fairness: blasphemy, dietary rules, sexual purity used to be moralized and mostly aren't anymore, and good riddance.

So: audit me. Which of these genuinely pull against each other and how hard, which pairs only look inconsistent, and for the real conflicts, what's the cheapest revision — what would I have to give up, and what would it cost the rest of the picture? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything as equally bad. And one more thing: separate what's genuinely settled here from what people who actually work on this stuff still fight about. I gather the measurement side of foundations theory and the standard reading of the dumbfounding experiments are both contested, so don't present some embattled position as the consensus just because it happens to be mine.
