I'm drafting a paper for a graduate seminar on early modern philosophy in which I want to defend Émilie du Châtelet's essay on happiness, the Discours sur le bonheur, not as a period piece but as a live position — call it du Châteletianism about the good life. When I sketched it for a friend, she said it was obviously incoherent but couldn't tell me where the contradiction was, which I took as a challenge. So here is the package I intend to defend, stated as my own commitments. I hold: (1) strong passions are necessary for happiness — a person without them is not serene but inert — so the practical task is to govern the passions, never to extinguish them; (2) because most of what matters lies outside our control, happiness should be anchored as far as possible in what depends on us alone, and study is the model passion precisely because it never abandons us, never tires of us, and remains available when every other source fails; (3) even so, love is among the greatest sources of happiness, and its dependence on another person is a risk worth accepting rather than a defect to be engineered away; (4) happiness requires shedding prejudices and comforting falsehoods, including religious superstitions, since you cannot build a durable happiness on beliefs you would reject if you looked at them squarely; (5) yet perfect lucidity is incompatible with happiness — a mind stripped of every illusion is left facing a sad reality — so we should knowingly protect certain harmless illusions about ourselves, our standing, and our prospects; and (6) durable self-contentment requires actually being virtuous rather than merely being thought virtuous, because no one can stay on good terms with herself while living off pleasures she privately disapproves of.

What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture on du Châtelet scholarship. Tell me which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, identify what exactly would have to give, what the least costly revision would be, and what that revision costs the overall position. For any pair that dissolves on inspection, explain briefly why the apparent conflict was an artifact. Then answer two closing questions. First, which single revision would clean up the most? Second, once the cheapest repairs are made, is the surviving package still recognizably du Châteletian, or has it quietly become a Stoic or Epicurean position wearing her vocabulary — and if the latter, does that matter for whether my paper's thesis survives?
