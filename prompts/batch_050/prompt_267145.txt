I TA a first-year moral and political philosophy class, discussion sections of about 24 students, 80 minutes each. For our unit on the limits of the criminal law I want an activity that gets them to feel a specific puzzle in their gut before they ever hear it stated: it can be perfectly legal to do A, perfectly legal to ask for B, and yet a serious crime to offer to refrain from A in exchange for B. In past years I've just presented this on slides and watched them nod along while clearly thinking the law is just obvious here, which means they've missed the entire problem. So: design me a game, staged over several rounds, where students individually vote on or enact the permissibility of each component and then watch themselves convict the combination. I'm imagining something like: fictional character cards with secrets, a student who controls whether a secret gets published in the class "newspaper," class votes on whether publishing is allowed (mean but legal), then votes on whether asking for money is allowed, then the round where the deal is offered and the votes flip, with the tally kept visible on the board the whole time so the flip is undeniable. Please give concrete rules, the actual materials I'd need, and a minute-by-minute timeline.

The part I care most about: build in escalation rounds that each break the students' first theory of where the wrongness lives. Specifically I want (1) a round where the secret's "victim" genuinely prefers to pay, so the deal leaves everyone better off and they have to explain why it's still banned, (2) a round where the secret-holder would have stayed silent anyway, so the threat is empty, (3) a round where the demand isn't money but some legal action, like dropping out of the student council race, and (4) a round structured like a lawyer's settlement letter -- pay up or we sue and it becomes public record -- which as far as I understand is basically legal, so they have to find the line between that and the criminal version themselves. I don't want any legal vocabulary on the table until the final fifteen minutes; no "extortion," no "coercion," nothing that lets them pattern-match to a category instead of thinking. If a student who's taken crim law blurts out the category early, tell me how to handle that without lying to the room.

Then give me the debrief: map specific moments of the game onto the structure of the puzzle -- the two-rights-make-a-crime shape, the mutual-benefit wrinkle, the question of whether the wrong is in the threat, the demand, or the joining of them -- and tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it (e.g. students insisting the answer is just "privacy," which collapses the moment publishing was voted legal; someone saying "it's coercive" and thinking that's an explanation rather than a restatement). Finally, be honest with me about which feature of the real phenomenon a classroom game with fictional secrets simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by just telling them.
