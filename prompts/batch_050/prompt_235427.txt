So at christmas dinner my brother-in-law (a cop, very "the law is the law") and I got into this huge argument about what makes something count as law in the first place, and I've been chewing on it for weeks now. I have a bunch of gut verdicts about imaginary and semi-real cases, and what I want is for someone to tell me what principle, if any, is generating them -- or to tell me honestly that two of my verdicts can't both stand. Here are the cases and my calls: (1) A dictator issues a short, crystal-clear criminal code. It's published everywhere, never changed, applied exactly as written to everyone, even his own ministers. It's also monstrous -- death penalty for petty theft. My verdict: this is somehow still law, in a thin sense, even if evil. Brother-in-law says no, law has to be just to be law. (2) A genuinely nice democracy whose constitution is all vague noble phrases, where judges basically decide each case on what seems fair, and outcomes are usually kind but totally unpredictable. Verdict: weirdly this feels *less* like law than the dictator's code. (3) The government passes a statute but only records it in some registry nobody can access, then prosecutes people for violating it. Verdict: that's not law at all, that's a trap with extra steps. (4) A retroactive law criminalizing something that was legal when the person did it. Verdict: no. (5) Two statutes that contradict, so whatever you do you've broken one. Verdict: no. (6) A tax code that changes every few weeks; every change is properly voted and published, but no accountant can keep up. Verdict: it fails as law despite all the procedure being perfect. (7) A forgotten 80-year-old ordinance still on the books, enforced only against people the mayor happens to dislike. Verdict: the rule exists on paper but this is not rule by law. (8) Laws that are fully public but written in legalese so dense that even lawyers disagree about what they require. Verdict: it IS law, but something has gone badly wrong, in the same neighborhood as case (3).

My tentative principle is something like: a rule is only really law if an ordinary person could find out what it demands of them and could actually comply with it before acting. That handles most of my cases, I think, but please stress-test it rather than just agreeing. The pair I'm most worried about is (3) and (8): for an actual citizen, an unpublished law and an incomprehensible published law are equally unknowable, yet I call one "not law at all" and the other "law with a defect" -- and I can't tell if that distinction is deep or just me being charitable to familiar dysfunction. Also (1) and (7): in both cases the text is fixed and clear, but I give opposite verdicts, apparently because of enforcement, so does my principle even cover that or do I need a second clause? Tell me what principle (or small set of clauses, each tied to the case that forces it) best captures these verdicts, which verdict you'd overturn if you had to to get something simpler, and what the resulting account predicts for cases I haven't listed -- like an algorithm that issues each citizen personalized rules based on their data and applies them perfectly, or a village with no written law at all but unwritten customs everyone actually knows and follows. I don't need citations or anything, just the principle worked through carefully.
