I lead a mixed undergraduate and master's seminar in philosophy of mind (14 to 18 students, one 80-minute meeting) and want a structured in-class activity built around the following question, which I will pose to them only in this homemade form: imagine a vast sealed vat of small frictionless marbles whose collisions are governed by simple mechanical rules, and suppose that certain recurring large-scale configurations of marbles behave in stable, predictable ways, so that an observer could describe the whole system either marble by marble or in terms of those stable configurations and their interactions. The question the seminar keeps circling is whether the large-scale configurations genuinely do any causal work -- whether it is ever right to say the pattern shoved the marbles around -- or whether all the pushing happens at the level of individual collisions, with the pattern-talk being at best a convenient summary and at worst a seductive illusion. I want an activity in which the students feel both horns of this in their bodies rather than hearing arguments about it: first the strong pull of the thought that only the substrate ever causes anything, and then the equally strong pull of the thought that prediction, explanation, and control live at the pattern level and cannot be recovered from below in practice.

Please design the activity as a simulation with assigned roles. Most students should physically enact the substrate, moving as tokens under simple local rules you specify (the rules must be learnable in under five minutes and must reliably generate at least two kinds of stable, nameable emergent structures when enacted by a dozen people in a room), while a small team of analysts observes and is scored on its ability to predict and influence where the system goes. Include a second phase in which the analysts are forbidden to refer to the emergent structures by name or to reason about them as units -- they may speak and plan only in terms of individual tokens -- so that the group experiences directly what is lost when the high-level description is banned. Also include a third phase or a rule variant that stages the downward-causation question itself: some rule of the simulation keyed to the presence of an emergent structure, so that it looks for all the world as though the pattern is doing something to the marbles, and the students must then argue about whether anything was added beyond a redescription of the collisions.

Give me the full setup: materials, room arrangement, the exact local rules, the scoring or payoff scheme for the analyst team (no real money), the timeline across the 80 minutes with phase transitions marked, and instructions for me as facilitator at each transition. Then give me the debrief plan: which moments in the activity map onto which claims (the causal completeness of the physical, the predictive indispensability of the high level, the distinction between a description's being useful and its referring to something causally efficacious), the questions I should put to the room, and the traps to avoid, such as letting the debrief settle the question by authority when the point is that the activity leaves it genuinely open. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- the local rules that students will misremember, the emergent structures that will fail to stabilize, the analysts who will quietly cheat by thinking in the forbidden vocabulary -- and how to handle each failure without restarting. Finally, be honest about which feature of the actual philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, that in the real case the high-level describers are themselves made of the substrate, which no classroom role-play can capture) and what I must supply by lecture or reading to cover that gap.
