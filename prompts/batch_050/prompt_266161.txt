Okay so I've been going around in circles on higher-order evidence for a while and I want to settle it by just listing my gut verdicts on cases and having someone tell me what principle I'm actually committed to, if any. Don't give me a menu of the standard positions (conciliationism, steadfast, whatever) and leave it there -- pick one principle, state it precisely, defend it, and tell me straight out which of my verdicts I'd have to give up. I'd rather be wrong and committed than vaguely right.

Cases:

1. I check a long arithmetic calculation three times, get the same answer, then a colleague who is just as careful as me and has no stake in it checks it and gets a different answer. I think I should drop to basically 50/50, even though my derivation still looks airtight to me on re-reading.

2. Same setup but instead of arithmetic it's whether rent control reduces housing supply, or whether compatibilism is true -- some politically/philosophically loaded thing where me and my epistemic peers are split down the middle. Here I do NOT think I should drop to 50/50, or anywhere near it. If I did that with every contested belief I'd believe almost nothing interesting.

3. I find out that my belief that my startup will succeed was largely caused by wishful thinking -- a therapist convinces me of this, convincingly. But my evidence (market research, early traction) is unchanged and still looks good. I feel like the causal discovery alone should lower my confidence, even though no new first-order evidence arrived. I know some people say the higher-order stuff only matters when it tells you something about the evidence itself, and by that standard this discovery is inert. My gut still says it matters.

4. I take a pill that I know makes people wildly overconfident in their own reasoning for six hours, and while on it I "prove" to my own satisfaction that I'm sober enough to drive. Obviously I should distrust the conclusion. But note: the reasoning feels identical in quality to the reasoning in case 1. So whatever principle handles 1 had better handle this the same way, or explain why not.

5. The akrasia one: I cannot get my mouth around sincerely saying "p, but I rationally shouldn't believe p." It strikes me as straight-up absurd, like asserting and taking it back in the same breath. But case 1 seems to be exactly that situation: my derivation still compels belief in the answer, and the higher-order evidence says my compelling-feeling belief is irrational. So either akrasia is sometimes rational (and my gut in this case is wrong) or something has to give in case 1. I lean toward biting the bullet on akrasia but I'm not thrilled about it.

So: is there a single principle that delivers 1-4, and what does it force me to say about 5? I suspect 2 is where any clean principle goes to die -- I clearly treat peer disagreement about math differently from peer disagreement about values/politics, and I can't tell if that's a principled distinction (something about the evidence being shared and checkable vs. not) or just motivated reasoning on my part. Tell me which. And if the principle you land on says I have to abandon one of my verdicts, say which one and don't hedge -- I want a verdict, not a survey. Also say what your principle predicts for one case I didn't list: I'm an investor who learns that half of my past successful stock picks were decided while I was sleep-deprived. Do I downgrade only those picks, or my current stock analysis too?
