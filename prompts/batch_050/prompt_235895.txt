I teach a second-year undergraduate course in philosophy of science, and I have just finished grading a batch of essays in which roughly two-thirds of my students treated a high heritability estimate as though it meant that a trait is "mostly genetic" in the individual, resistant to environmental intervention, and therefore a legitimate basis for explaining differences between groups. Clearly my lecturing has failed, so I want them to run into the truth themselves before I am allowed to say anything at the board.

Design a structured classroom activity for 24 students and a single 75-minute session, using only index cards, dice, and a whiteboard (no computers), that forces participants to discover the actual logic of heritability as a population-relative statistic about variance. Specifically, the activity must make them feel, in sequence, three distinct points. First, that heritability is a claim about the causes of differences within a particular population in a particular range of environments, not about the causes of any individual's trait. Second, that two groups can each exhibit very high within-group heritability for a trait while the difference between the group means is entirely environmental. Third, that a trait can be highly heritable and yet drastically modifiable by a cheap intervention, so that heritability tells you nothing about malleability.

The activity should work as follows in outline (you supply the details): students receive hidden "genotype" values and publicly assigned "environment" conditions, generate trait values by a simple rule combining the two, and are then asked to make explicit, recorded predictions -- before each new round changes the parameters -- about what the resulting group statistics will look like. The scoring or stakes should reward accurate predictions, so that students whose implicit theory of heritability is wrong lose points publicly and feel the failure rather than merely hearing it corrected. I want the second point above to emerge from an actual partitioning of the class into two "populations" whose data the students themselves compute and compare on the whiteboard, and the third from a rule change midway through that catches them off guard.

Please provide: the complete rules, including the exact rule for generating trait values and how to calibrate the numbers so the intended effects actually appear in a sample of 24; a minute-by-minute timeline; the prediction questions to pose at each stage, worded so that the common misconception yields a confidently wrong answer; a debrief script mapping each moment of the activity onto the formal notion of variance partitioning; and an honest account of two things -- what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run (and how to recover), and which feature of real heritability estimation the activity structurally cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply in lecture afterward.
