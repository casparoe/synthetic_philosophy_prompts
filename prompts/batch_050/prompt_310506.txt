A colleague closes out every argument about inner alignment with the evolution analogy: selection optimized us for inclusive fitness, we ended up caring about sugar, sex and status instead, so a model trained against a reward signal will end up pursuing proxies rather than the objective and there's basically no hope of getting its goals right through reward design. The punchline is always contraception and junk food. Audit it. Map the correspondences explicitly (what's supposed to play the role of the outer optimizer, the mesa-objective, the training distribution, the reward), then the disanalogies, sorted into ones that matter for the argument versus ones that don't. Two I keep hearing and cant adjudicate: first, evolution never got to write or even observe an explicit reward signal while we do, so maybe the analogy proves too much about any learned system; second, the human case looks more like capability generalization failing under distribution shift than like a mesa-optimizer running its own goal, since nobody's shown the brain holds one coherent objective. Does the no-hope-through-reward-design conclusion survive once the breaks are on the table, or does the analogy only support the weaker claim that alignment can fail on deployment shift? If some other comparison fits better (employees gaming KPIs, domesticated animals), say which and why. And be clear about what's basically settled -- that goal misgeneralization happens in trained agents -- versus what's still genuinely contested among people working on this; don't present the strong reading as consensus.
