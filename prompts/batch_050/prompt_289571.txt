Last weekend I listened in on a long argument between two friends of mine, Elena and Marcus, at a dinner party, and it ended unresolved and a bit heated. The trigger was small -- Marcus mentioned that he always feels a swell of pride hearing his country's anthem, and Elena said she found that feeling intellectually indefensible -- but it quickly escalated into a full dispute about whether patriotism is a virtue or a vice. I'd like you, acting as a careful moral philosopher, to adjudicate their exchange. Please think through the disagreement slowly, evaluate each move on its own merits rather than declaring a wholesale winner, and be willing to say that a given move is partly right. The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went roughly as follows.

(1) Elena opened by arguing that patriotism is pride and loyalty directed at an accident of birth: nobody chooses their country, so loving it specially is arbitrary partiality, and any morality worthy of the name should tell us to value people impartially regardless of which side of a border they were born on. Special concern for compatriots, she said, is just a bias we've dressed up in noble language. (2) Marcus replied that by that standard we'd have to condemn love for one's parents and friends too, since we don't choose our parents either, and partiality toward them is unchosen and arbitrary in exactly the same way. Special obligations arising from relationships we find ourselves in, he claimed, are among the most morally creditable things there are -- patriotism is gratitude and loyalty toward the community that raised and sustains you, and loving your own country doesn't require believing it superior to others, any more than loving your mother requires believing she's the best mother in the world. (3) Elena conceded the family analogy has some force but pressed two points. First, the analogy is imperfect: your parents are particular people you actually know and who actually did things for you, whereas a nation is an abstraction, and the "it raised you" story papers over the fact that the same polity has done terrible things to others. Second, even if patriotism were innocent in principle, it is hopelessly corruptible in practice: the disposition to prefer one's own is reliably captured by chauvinism and war fever, so a character trait that slides that easily into vice shouldn't be cultivated -- we don't praise tendencies that need constant policing to stay decent. (4) Marcus answered that the corruption argument proves too much: generosity slides into profligacy, courage into recklessness, and even impartial benevolence slides into cold neglect of everyone near you, yet we respond by educating the virtue, not abolishing it. He offered a criterion for the healthy version: genuine patriotism is compatible with, and even requires, criticizing one's country, because the patriot wants the country to deserve the love it gets. He added that Elena's impartial ideal would dissolve every particular attachment and leave a morality no human being could actually live. (5) Elena's final move was to turn his criterion against him: if loving your country well means wanting it to be just, then the real object of your loyalty is justice itself, and the country is only its temporary vehicle -- at which point the view is just cosmopolitanism wearing a flag pin, and the distinctively patriotic element has evaporated. If instead the attachment survives discovering that the country doesn't deserve it, then her corruption worry stands unanswered.

At that point dessert arrived and the conversation was deliberately steered elsewhere. Please adjudicate the dispute move by move: for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or partially right, with a brief justification, and note any moves where one party concedes more or less than they realize. Then give your overall verdict: who, if anyone, got the better of the argument and on which specific points; whether Elena's final move succeeds or equivocates; and what both parties are missing -- for instance, whether they are even arguing about the same object (pride, attachment, loyalty, and willingness to sacrifice are not one thing), and whether "virtue or vice" is the right frame at all.
