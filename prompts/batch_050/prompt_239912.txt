I am a novelist, and for a scene I am plotting — one in which a character misleads someone while uttering only true sentences — I have been reading Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio, c. 395). Early in the treatise, as he works toward a definition of the lie, he writes:

"Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

Could you help me understand this passage? Two things puzzle me. First, Augustine locates the lie entirely in the discrepancy between what is held in the mind and what is uttered — but does "the sense of his own mind" mean firm belief, mere opinion, or something else, and how does that bear on the second half of his claim, where a speaker who accidently tells the truth while trying to deceive is counted a liar? Second, and this is what my scene turns on: my character speaks words she fully believes, choosing them precisely because her hearer will draw a false conclusion from them. On the criterion stated in this passage, she does not lie — yet her intent seems indistinguishable from the liar's. Does Augustine's definition as given here have the resources to condemn what she does, or must it treat her as blameless, and what does that reveal about where he thinks the wrong of lying resides?
