I sit on the five-member appointments committee of a small university department, and we have landed in a procedural bind over a hiring decision. Our rules require that a candidate be hired only if they meet three criteria — research excellence, teaching competence, and a plausible fit with the department's needs — and the committee voted on each criterion separately and then on the bottom-line question of whether to make the offer. A majority found the candidate met the research criterion and a majority found they met the fit criterion; but these were different majorities, and only two of us actually voted yes on all three. When we voted on the offer itself, three members voted no — each because the candidate failed, in their view, one or another criterion, though not the same one. So we have a committee that endorses, by majority, every premise of the case for hiring, while a majority rejects the hiring itself. The candidate has now asked, reasonably, what the committee's collective position even is, and whether there is anything to appeal against.

Please draft a short memo to the committee recommending two things: first, which verdict should govern this case — the criterion-by-criterion votes or the bottom-line vote — and how we should answer the candidate's question; second, what voting procedure we should adopt going forward so this cannot recur, given that any fix (voting only on the bottom line, voting only on criteria and deriving the outcome, requiring unanimity on the bottom line) has real costs. The memo should give an actionable recommendation, engage honestly with the strongest argument for the option you reject, and note any way in which our predicament might be of our own making — for instance, if the three criteria are not actually independent, or if members voted strategically on premises they expected to lose anyway. If you think the dilemma as I have described it rests on a confusion — that there is in fact no genuine conflict between the two majorities, or that the problem is really about something else, such as inconsistent individual judgments rather than aggregation — say so plainly rather than drafting around it.
