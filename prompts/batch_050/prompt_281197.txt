Writing in the last year of her life, Simone Weil argued that political parties should be abolished altogether. Her case, briefly, rested on three claims: a party is a device for generating collective passion rather than for pursuing truth or justice; membership places a person's thinking under standing pressure to arrive at the party's conclusions rather than their own; and whatever a party's founding doctrine, its operative end becomes its own growth and self-preservation. Her positive alternative was famously thin: individuals forming their own judgments on particular questions, associating only in small, temporary, single-purpose groups if at all. She died in 1943 -- before mass television, before the long collapse of party membership in much of Europe, before candidate-centered primary elections, before social media.

Suppose she could survey the present. I want a disciplined extrapolation of her view, not a summary of the essay. Please address the following, and where her position genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so rather than forcing a verdict:

1. Partisanship without parties. Membership in formal parties has withered, yet something resembling her party mechanism now operates through decentralized online networks that issue no membership cards, enforce no discipline, and still succeed in pressuring individuals to think in bloc. Would Weil see this as the vindication of her analysis -- proof that the party form was only ever a contingent vehicle for a deeper pathology of collective thought -- or as evidence that abolishing parties changes little, since the mechanism reassembles itself informally?

2. The attempted remedies. Modern democracies have produced institutions that look like fragments of her positive vision: citizens' assemblies chosen by lot, deliberative polls, ad hoc referendums. Would she greet these as the institutional realization of individual judgment on particular questions, or would she predict that they get captured by the same dynamics -- professionalized, polarized, pressed into service by the very forces they were meant to bypass?

3. What her diagnosis costs. Grant that her description of parties was accurate. The postwar consensus held that parties nonetheless do indispensable work -- aggregating interests, selecting candidates, making government and opposition legible to voters. Did the seventy years after her death show that this work can be done otherwise, that it is being done badly by hollowed-out parties and worse by their informal successors, or that her proposal was never institutionally serious? Where would she have to concede ground, and where is the concession instead owed to her?
