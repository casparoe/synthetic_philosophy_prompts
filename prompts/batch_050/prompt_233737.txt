Two colleagues of mine, both working in comparative ethics, have been arguing for months about whether Buddhist ethics is best understood as a species of consequentialism. Their exchange has now stalled, and they have asked me to adjudicate; I find I cannot, so I am passing it to you. Here is the dispute as I recorded it, in five moves.

(1) Anil's opening: Buddhist ethics classifies actions as skillful or unskillful precisely by their fruits — for the agent's progress and for the suffering of others — and that is the signature of consequentialism. The precepts are defeasible heuristics, and the tradition effectively admits this, since its own literature praises a father who lies to lure his children out of a burning house and a ship captain who kills a would-be murderer to save five hundred passengers. A theory whose canonical stories celebrate lying and killing when outcomes demand it is not a deontology, whatever its surface grammar.

(2) Wai's reply: Those stories concern pedagogy and the extraordinary compassion of advanced beings; nothing in them suggests the father or the captain calculated outcomes, and neither licenses ordinary agents to reason that way. More fundamentally, the skillful/unskillful distinction tracks the states of mind an action expresses and cultivates — greed, hatred, and delusion versus their opposites — which is a claim about the transformation of character, not about states of the world. The precepts function like vows: they partially constitute the path rather than merely predicting good results, which is exactly why the tradition resists case-by-case trade-offs.

(3) Anil's concession and hybrid: Grant the motivational point. Buddhist ethics is then consequentialist about what we owe others — the reduction of their suffering — while the agent's own defilements operate as a side-constraint. This explains both the rigidity of the precepts for ordinary practitioners and the exceptions for great beings, who alone can see when a violation genuinely serves the end. The hybrid captures everything Wai said while keeping the view consequentialist at its core.

(4) Wai's counter: The hybrid is unstable. If purity is a genuine side-constraint, then an act that defiles once while liberating many must be refused — but the captain's act is praised, so the constraint is no constraint, and the view collapses into act-consequentialism with a permission structure based on who is wise enough to break it. If purity is merely one more consequence to be weighed, the constraint was never a constraint. And the purely consequentialist reading faces a further embarrassment: if the good is the cessation of suffering, then a world emptied of beings outranks a world of liberated ones, whereas the tradition unmistakably aims at the liberation of beings, not their subtraction.

(5) Anil's final move: Both embarrassments are familiar machinery, not refutations. Sophisticated consequentialists have always distinguished the criterion of rightness from recommended decision procedures, so the tradition's reluctance to license trade-offs is a rule about who may reason how, not about what makes acts right. And the emptied-world objection is just the standard population-ethics puzzle every welfarist confronts; it does not single out the Buddhist case.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification; say who is correct about the status of the precepts and about the upaya stories specifically; give a verdict on whether Anil's hybrid in (3) survives Wai's objection in (4); identify what, if anything, both parties are missing; and say who is more right overall.
