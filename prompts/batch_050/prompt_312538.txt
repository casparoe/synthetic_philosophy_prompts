A colleague of mine, who works in software engineering and reads philosophy of mind as a hobby, has constructed an argument that he claims establishes a startling result: strictly speaking, software bugs never cause servers to crash. He presents it as a scaled-up version of the causal exclusion argument, and I have to admit I cannot find the flaw, though I find the conclusion incredible. His argument:

(1) Causal closure: every physical event that has a cause at time t has a complete sufficient physical cause at t. A server crash, being a physical event, is fully accounted for by the transistor states, voltage levels, and physical processes in the hardware immediately prior.

(2) Exclusion: if an event has a complete sufficient cause C at t, then no further item D, distinct from C, is also a cause of that event at t -- unless the event is systematically overdetermined, in the way of a death caused by two bullets that each arrive and suffice independently.

(3) The crash is not like the two-bullet case. Hardware failures and software faults are not rival, independent executioners each waiting to do the job alone; they do not compete.

(4) The null pointer dereference is genuinely distinct from the transistor states: the very same pointer dereference can be realized by indefinitely many different hardware configurations, and the very same hardware configuration would realize a different software state given different memory contents, so neither is identical to the other.

(5) Therefore the null pointer dereference does not cause the crash. The transistor states do that, completely, on their own.

(6) Nothing in steps (1)-(5) turns on anything specific to software. Any higher-level property -- being a pain, being a hurricane, being a recession -- is distinct from its physical realizers in exactly the sense of (4), and its ostensible effects have complete physical causes by (1).

(7) Therefore no higher-level property ever causes anything. Pain does not cause wincing; the hurricane does not smash the window; only fundamental physics causes.

He happily accepts (7) across the board, but I want to resist it while keeping as much of his setup as possible. I am most reluctant to give up (1), since closure seems like the one claim here with genuine scientific credentials, and I would also prefer to keep (4), since the multiple-realizability point seems like straightforward common sense about the relation between levels.

Please tell me which step or premise I should reject. If the argument is valid, identify which premise is least costly to surrender and explain what one thereby commits to. If it contains a subtle flaw -- an equivocation, a shift in what "cause" or "distinct" or "sufficient" means between steps -- name the step precisely and explain why it looked acceptable. Either way, I would like the alternatives priced: how would an identity theorist, a defender of systematic overdetermination, an interventionist about causation, and someone who thinks causes must be proportional to their effects each diagnose the argument, and what does each diagnosis cost when applied to the software case, which is the one instance where my colleague's conclusion strikes me as simply false?
