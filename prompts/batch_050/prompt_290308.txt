The exam question was the following: "Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument is usually presented as targeting mental-to-physical causation. But Kim also presses a version of it against mental-to-mental causation: if my pain causes my feeling of anxiety, and the anxiety is realized in some physical state, the realizer of the anxiety already has a sufficient physical cause, and the pain looks crowded out. Explain how the exclusion argument generates this problem for mental-to-mental causation, why a non-reductive physicalist cannot simply reply that mental causes operate 'at their own level' alongside physical causes, and what options remain for preserving the causal efficacy of the mental (for example: reduction, epiphenomenalism, systematic overdetermination, denying causal closure, or denying the exclusion principle). Conclude by indicating which option you find most defensible and why."

Here is the student's answer:

"The exclusion argument for mental-to-mental causation runs as follows. Suppose my pain M causes my anxiety M*. By the causal closure of the physical, the physical realizer P* of my anxiety has a sufficient physical cause P in my brain. Now my pain M is itself realized in some physical state, and unless M is identical with that physical state, the pain appears to be an additional, distinct cause of P*. By Kim's exclusion principle — no event has more than one sufficient cause at a time unless it is genuinely overdetermined — either M's causal contribution is excluded, or the case is one of overdetermination. But systematic overdetermination of every mental effect by both a mental and a physical cause would be an incredible coincidence, so the non-reductive physicalist is pushed toward the conclusion that the pain did not really cause the anxiety; only the underlying physical states did the causal work.

The non-reductive physicalist cannot escape by saying mental causes operate at their own level, because the causal inheritance principle — originally formulated by Donald Davidson — holds that if a mental property is realized in a physical property on a given occasion, the causal powers of that mental property instance are a subset of the causal powers of its realizer. So the mental 'level' adds no causal work beyond what the physical level already does, and claiming a separate level of causation just relabels the problem rather than solving it.

The remaining options are these. First, reduction: identify the pain with its neural realizer, so there is only one cause after all. Kim himself ultimately endorses a non-reductive version of this strategy, retaining the autonomy of psychology while acknowledging the dependence of the mental on the physical. Second, epiphenomenalism: accept that mental properties are causally inert, which preserves non-reduction but at the cost of denying that my pain does anything. Third, overdetermination, which as noted is implausible when systematic. Fourth, one could deny causal closure; this is dialectically cheap, since closure is merely an empirical generalization of neuroscience that future brain science could overturn, and a philosopher is under no obligation to accept a scientific conjecture that might be falsified. Fifth, one could deny the exclusion principle, holding that a cause and its realizer are not in competition.

In my view, reduction is the most defensible response, since it respects closure and the exclusion principle while preserving the reality of mental causation, although it requires giving up the claim that mental properties are multiply realizable in a way that blocks identification with physical properties."
