I'm an epidemiologist by training, and the vocabulary I actually think in is the potential-outcomes framework plus Pearl's ladder of causation: associational claims of the form P(Y|X), interventional claims of the form P(Y|do(X)), rung-three counterfactuals like Y_x(u), directed acyclic graphs, confounding, back-door adjustment. This semester I'm sitting in on a philosophy of language seminar, and we keep circling the distinction between indicative and counterfactual (subjunctive) conditionals — the standard example pair contrasting "If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did" with "If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have," and the surrounding debate: the material-conditional analysis and its apparent failures, Grice's attempt to defend it pragmatically, Stalnaker's and Lewis's possible-worlds semantics, and Adams's thesis that the acceptability of an indicative conditional tracks the corresponding conditional probability. I have decent fluency with the formal machinery but keep getting lost in what the philosophers take themselves to be disagreeing about.

I suspect some of it maps cleanly — the Oswald pair looks to me like the difference between updating on evidence (learning that Oswald didn't do it, and conditionalizing) and intervening or counterfactually supposing (holding the causal structure fixed and setting Oswald's action otherwise) — that is, rung one versus rung three, seeing versus doing. If that mapping is right, say so precisely and work out what Stalnaker's selection semantics and Adams's thesis each become in causal-inference terms.
