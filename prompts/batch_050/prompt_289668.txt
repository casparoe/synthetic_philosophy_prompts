Write a short story about the difference between time averages and ensemble averages in risky decision-making.

The setup: a village where, every year, each household may stake its entire savings on a coin flip that pays out 1.5x on heads and 0.6x on tails. The game has a positive expected value across the whole village, and every year the village's total wealth grows -- driven by a few lucky households -- while the median household is slowly ruined, because each family's wealth compounds multiplicatively over time. Tell the story through two characters: a statistician who keeps pointing to the village-wide average, and a farmer who keeps pointing to what actually happens to individual families who play year after year. I work as a software engineer and first encountered this idea in a blog post about why "positive expected value" can still be a terrible bet when you have to survive the sequence of outcomes rather than average across parallel copies of yourself, so I'd like the story to make that distinction visceral rather than stated. No character should use terms like "ergodic" or "expected value"; the mathematics should live in what happens to people. Aim for 800-1200 words, and end in a way that leaves open whether the statistician's optimism or the farmer's caution is the right guide for a single lifetime.
