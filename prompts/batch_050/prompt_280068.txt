I teach philosophy at a mid-sized university and, for my sins, sit on the Faculty Appeals Panel (they wanted someone outside the dispute, so here I am). We have to rule on the Reyes case, and I want you to draft our decision memo.

The facts, roughly. Dana Reyes, a senior lecturer, had told colleagues for weeks that she'd consider quitting if the proposed course cuts went through. At the meeting where the cuts were approved, she stood up, said "Fine — then I resign, run the department without me," and walked out. Two attendees later said they took it as hyperbole; the chair took it literally and emailed the dean that night accepting the resignation. HR started the paperwork. At 7am the next morning Reyes emailed that she'd spoken in anger and was withdrawing it. To late, says the administration: a resignation is a resignation. Reyes appealed.

Complicating bit: the faculty handbook says resignations "shall be submitted in writing to the dean." Nobody knows whether that clause defines what a resignation IS (so an oral outburst isn't one) or merely regulates the paperwork (so hers was valid, if irregularly documented). Meanwhile the dean has already offered her line to someone else, and the administration warns that voiding this resignation means anyone can un-say a resignation the morning after.

Write me the memo. Bottom line first — uphold the resignation or void it — then the reasoning. I want real work done with the speech-act angle: was the oral utterance a failed performative (a misfire) or a defective-but-performed one (an abuse, roughly in Austin's sense), and does that distinction actually decide anything given the handbook clause? Does uptake matter, given half the room didn't take her seriously? Resignations are unilateral acts, unlike marriages or bets — so what was the chair's "acceptance" even doing, and can it fix a misfire? And do the prior weeks of talk about quitting matter — settled intention versus the act itself?

Give the administration's strongest case honestly before responding to it — and if you think they actually win, say so. The panel is genuinely split and I'd rather be persuaded than flattered.
