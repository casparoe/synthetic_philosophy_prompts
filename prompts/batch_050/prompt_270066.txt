I'm a novelist -- literary fiction, three books out, and I teach a couple of seminars a year on a creative writing MA, mostly point of view stuff. Last term a student of mine asked my something I still haven't shaken: how come in fiction we can just open a door into a character's consciousness -- interior monologue, free indirect style, an omniscient narrator who dips in and out of heads -- when in real life we're sealed inside our own skulls and stuck guessing about everyone else's? It sent me down a bit of a philosophy rabbit hole and I've landed on what gets called the problem of other minds, plus the standard fixes: the argument from analogy, inference to the best explanation, and the more behaviour-leaning and simulation-leaning stories about how we actually do it.

Here's what I want. Restate that whole debate -- the problem itself and the main responses -- in the vocabulary my students and I genuinely use: point of view, psychic distance, free indirect discourse, head-hopping, showing versus telling, reliable versus unreliable narrators, the contract with the reader. Be as faithful as you can, and don't quietly import craft assumptions and pass them off as translation. My suspicion, which you're welcome to demolish, is that a chunk of it won't translate at all, because fiction hands the reader the exact thing the philosophers say we never get -- direct access to another interior -- so the problem might dissolve on the page rather than get solved. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate have no counterpart in craft terms, and for each, your call on whether that's my vocabulary missing something real or the philosophers worrying at something less substantive than it looks.

Format-wise: exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion -- maybe the problem in my terms, the responses in my terms, then the residue and your verdict, but allocate as you see fit. And British English throughout, please; the American spellings grate on me more than they should.
