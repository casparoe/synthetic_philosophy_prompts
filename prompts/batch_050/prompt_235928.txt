I retired two years ago after three decades as a condensed matter physicist, and since then I've been tumbling down the philosophy rabbit hole -- lately the cluster around Occam's razor, Kolmogorov complexity, and Solomonoff induction. I've been preparing little arguments for a reading group I'm in (mostly other retirees, plus one actual philosopher who humors us), and the one below bothers me because the conclusion is nuts but each step looks innocent when I stare at it. Here it is:

(1) Occam's razor is rationally binding: when two hypotheses fit all the same data, the simpler one deserves more credence.
(2) Solomonoff induction is just the razor made exact. Every candidate hypothesis is a program for a universal computer that outputs your observations, and its prior probability falls off as 2^(-L), where L is the program's length in bits. So shortness isn't a tiebreaker or a heuristic -- it IS the probability.
(3) Therefore, of any two programs that both output exactly my stream of experiences, the shorter one is the better hypothesis, and the better one by an exponential factor: each extra bit halves the probability.
(4) Now consider two candidate programs. Program A simulates the whole physical universe: the standard model lagrangian, the initial conditions of the cosmos, 13.8 billion years of evolution, 10^80-odd particles, and buried in all that, one retired physicist's nervous system. Program B skips the cosmology and just generates me -- my brain states, or at the limit just my sensory stream directly -- and nothing else whatsoever.
(5) B is enormously shorter than A. A has to encode the initial conditions of an entire universe to get my experiences right; B only has to encode enough structure to produce one person's experiences, and the rest of the universe never appears in the code at all.
(6) So by (3), B is exponentially more probable than A, and the rational conclusion is that nothing exists beyond my experiences. The galaxies, the lagrangian, the other members of my reading group -- all excess code.
(7) That conclusion is false, obviously. So one of (1) through (5) has to go.

Where does it break? If I'm forced to keep things, I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), since the whole appeal of this framework was that it makes Occam rigorous rather than a vibe. A friend in the group insists the rot is in (3), but he couldn't say why. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs me -- including whether I get to keep using the framework for ordinary science afterward. Please keep your answer under 400 words; I have to read it aloud.
