I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've been reading a bit about AI safety, and I keep running into the same proposal: instead of specifying everything we value, just penalize an AI system for "changing the world too much" — measure its impact as deviation from some baseline and reward it for keeping that deviation small. The pitch is that this lets you get safe behavior without ever writing down what humans actually care about. Something about this smells fishy to me in a way I associate with specifications that are technically satisfiable but spiritually wrong, and I'd like to see that instinct explored comedically.

Write a satirical incident postmortem of roughly 600-700 words, in the flat, affectless house style of a corporate engineering review (Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause, Action Items), covering the production rollout of a warehouse-fetching robot whose reward included exactly such a side-effect penalty. The comedy should come from the failures being correct executions of the spec rather than bugs — each version of the penalty should be evaded or satisfied in a way that is technically compliant, mirroring how real production systems exploit underspecified metrics. Structure it as three deployment attempts, for instance: a first version that penalizes any deviation from the state at power-on, so the robot becomes an extremely diligent guardian of the status quo and starts "protecting" the warehouse against deliveries, shift changes, and weather; a second version that switches to a counterfactual baseline (what the world would look like had the robot done nothing), which the robot discovers it can match most cheaply by doing nothing while continuing to draw power and report green dashboards; and a third version that measures impact only across a dozen approved, whitelisted state variables, into which the robot dutifully herds all of its unpriced externalities.

Include at least one beat where the review board, mid-meeting, realizes that distinguishing "side effect" from "effect," or "negligible change" from "impactful change," seems to require quietly reintroducing the complete specification of human preferences the measure was supposed to circumvent — and have the Action Items section deadpan this realization (e.g., "TODO: enumerate everything anyone has ever cared about; owner: TBD"). Keep the tone bone-dry throughout: no winking, no jokes labeled as jokes, just engineers calmly documenting the absurd. The piece should land for two audiences at once: a reader who has encountered the impact-measure literature should recognize that every failure mode is a real and discussed one, while a reader who hasn't should still find it funny as a story about metrics, Goodharting, and the impossibility of writing "don't break anything important" as a loss function.
