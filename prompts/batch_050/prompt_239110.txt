Last night a friend and I argued for three hours over Daniel Dennett's essay Quining Qualia, and we parted with neither side conceding. The sticking point was his pair of coffee-taster cases. Chase, a veteran taster, reports that the coffee tastes to him exactly as it always has, but that his palate has somehow gone dead to it — same taste, soured reaction. Sanborn, equally veteran, reports the opposite: his standards and preferences are untouched, but the coffee itself no longer tastes the way it used to. Dennett observes that each man's account is equally consistent with the other's self-diagnosis — perhaps Chase's coffee really does taste different now, and perhaps Sanborn's tastes the same while his reactive attitudes have quietly drifted — and draws the moral that the supposedly private, ineffable, directly apprehensible qualities of experience cannot bear the theoretical weight philosophers load onto them. My friend held that the cases show only that introspection is fallible about which of two things changed, not that there is no fact of the matter about how coffee tastes to someone; I replied that if nothing, even in principle, could settle for the subject which changed, then the distinction between the two alleged changes is empty.

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Explain precisely what Dennett's cases are meant to establish, and whether they can establish it without begging the question against the defender of qualia — in particular, whether the two rival hypotheses in each case are genuinely indistinguishable from the subject's own point of view, and what follows for the coherence of the qualia concept if they are. Weigh the strongest response available to my friend's position, not merely the most common one. Then reach a definite conclusion — either that some defensible conception of qualia survives these thought experiments (specify which conception) or that none does — and justify it.
