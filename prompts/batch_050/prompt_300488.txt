I chair the grants committee of a regional arts foundation that funds narrative feature films. Before us is an application to fund a biographical drama about a real, deceased public figure — say, a mid-century labor organizer who has been dead for forty years and whose only surviving close relative is a grandchild. The screenplay is widely agreed to be excellent, and its overall portrayal is admiring. The difficulty is one invented scene: the subject, alone at night, burns a letter, and the film makes clear that the letter contained evidence she had promised to hand over. No such event is attested. The director's defense is that historians genuinely dispute whether the subject suppressed the evidence, that the record is silent in both directions, and that the scene simply dramatizes one live scholarly hypothesis rather than asserting a known falsehood. He adds two further points: the film also contains ordinary compressions and composite characters that no one objects to, so some fabrication is clearly licensed; and the scene is, in his view, the most truthful thing in the film, because it forces the audience to confront the question historians have politely buried. The grandchild has written to us in strong objection, arguing that audiences will leave the theater believing the scene happened, and that for most viewers this film will be the only account of her grandmother they ever encounter. Our funding guidelines require "fair and respectful treatment of real persons" and do not define the phrase. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee before we vote: recommend funding, declining, or funding with specified conditions; state what principle about dramatizing the dead the committee would be adopting under each option; and engage seriously with the strongest argument for the side you recommend against, including the director's claim that a scene which fills an evidential silence in one direction rather than the other is not the same kind of act as contradicting the record.
