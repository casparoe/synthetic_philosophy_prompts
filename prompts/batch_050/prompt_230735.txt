I serve on my university's Enrollment Policy Committee, a standing faculty-student body that advises the registrar on registration rules, and we have a live case I need to frame before our vote next month. The background: oversubscribed seminars here are allocated by lottery, and this semester a group of students has petitioned us to authorize a sanctioned "seat exchange" — an online marketplace, administered by the registrar, on which a student who wins a seat in an oversubscribed course could sell it to another enrolled student, with transfers processed officially and enrollment records updated accordingly. The trigger is that the spring section of a small seminar on the moral limits of markets drew roughly four hundred lottery entries for eighteen seats, and the petitioners — at least half-serious, but aware of the irony — argue that willingness to pay allocates scarce seats to the students who value them most, that both parties to a sale end up better off (unlike under the lottery, where losers get nothing), and that the university hardly has clean hands when it comes to rationing by price: tuition itself, tiered housing, and the point-bidding course-allocation systems used by some business schools are all precedents of one kind or another.

The committee is split three ways, which is part of what makes the memo hard to write. Our economists find the efficiency argument decisive and regard the unease on the rest of the committee as a puzzle to be explained rather than a reason. Several humanists and most of the student members find the proposal repugnant, but they articulate that repugnance in two distinct registers that pull toward different policies: some object that the exchange lets family wealth operate in a sphere where the university has deliberately excluded it (a lottery was chosen precisely so that a rich student and a poor student have the same shot at the seminar), while others object that attaching a price to a seat changes what enrolling in the course means — that a seminar about the limits of commodification, entered via purchase, becomes something its participants can no longer take seriously, and that this corrosive effect would spread to registration generally. A third faction raises a design worry orthogonal to both: once seats have resale value, students will enter lotteries for courses they have no intention of taking, purely to harvest and flip the winnings. A softening proposal on the table would permit a swap-only exchange — seat for seat, no money — which some committee members find perfectly acceptable and others find no better in principle, and I want the memo to force that disagreement into the open, since it seems to diagnose whether the real objection concerns inequality of resources or the introduction of pricing as such.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting, addressed to the full committee. It must arrive at a definite, actionable recommendation — authorize the exchange, reject it, or authorize it with specified conditions (a price cap, a prohibition on speculative entry, swap-only, or something else you design) — and it must make the committee's reasoning available for the record, not merely announce an outcome. In particular: state explicitly whether the recommendation rests on the inequality objection, the corruption objection, or the speculative-entry objection, and give the committee's answer to the strands it does not rest on; address the strongest version of the efficiency argument, including the claim that willingness to pay tracks how much students value the seat, and whether the budget-constraint reply to that claim is adequate; explain why the tuition and point-bidding precedents do or do not settle the matter; and take a position on the swap-only variant that is consistent with the memo's treatment of the cash version. Where the committee's own practices (priority registration for athletes and graduating seniors, say) sit in tension with the recommendation, flag the tension honestly rather than papering over it. Please write in American English.
