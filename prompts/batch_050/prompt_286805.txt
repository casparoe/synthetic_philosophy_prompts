So I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I keep ending up in arguments about this stuff with a friend who did a philosophy degree. The current fight: I'm plotting a thriller where the protagonist, a retired forensic accountant, is convinced the firm's CFO is cooking the books. She's right. But halfway through the book I want the reader to feel that her being right is worth less than it seems, because she's right for basically no reason -- a hunch stitched together from vibes and one misread spreadsheet. My friend said I can't do that, because true belief that turns out lucky is just as good, practically speaking, as knowledge: she acts on it, the CFO goes down, same outcome either way. I disagree, and I've ended up defending something close to the classic answer from the Meno: knowledge beats mere true belief because it's stable, tied down -- the way Socrates contrasts beliefs that run away like untethered statues with ones that are fastened by an account of the reasons why. My version is roughly: knowledge is true belief that would survive pressure (new evidence, interrogation, time), and that stability is what makes it more valuable, both practically and as an achievement.

What I want from you: what would count *against* that position? I know some of the obvious pressure points -- cases where a well-grounded, stable belief gets lucky anyway (the barn-facade style cases), the worry that stability is a matter of stubbornness rather than epistemic virtue, the line (I associate it with Kvanvig) that once you've got truth, anything extra you add is either redundant or valuable only insofar as it gets you truth, and the knowledge-first people who think the whole project of analyzing knowledge as beefed-up true belief is backwards. But I want the map of exposure done properly: which objections would a committed stability theorist actually concede as damaging, which would they wave away, and does my position predict anything its rivals don't -- like, is there a case where stability and some rival account (credit-worthiness, safety, whatever) come apart, and one of them clearly gives the wrong verdict? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.

Also, since I'm going to build a novel around whichever answer survives: be honest about whether the stability view has been patched so many times (stable *and* non-lucky *and* arrived at competently...) that it's no longer really one position, and whether that should bother me. And tell me what would change your own verdict -- which single argument or case would flip you to thinking my friend is right and lucky true belief is no worse than knowledge, or at least where your analysis is most likely to be going wrong.
