I teach a seminar on aggregation in normative ethics, and two of my strongest students have deadlocked all semester over whether very small harms, spread across very many people, can ever add up to something morally worse than a single severe harm. One defends straightforward aggregation: badness is additive, so a sufficiently vast number of momentary, barely noticed irritations can outweigh one person's year of agony. The other defends lexical priority: there is a threshold of severity such that no quantity of trivial harms, however large, is worse than one harm above the threshold; the trivial harms are not merely outnumbered, they are not even in the running. I have asked them to settle their dispute by producing a joint statement in the style of an adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to draft the document they should come back with, roughly 900 words.

The statement must do four things. First, set out the claims both sides can sign without reservation. These will be thin, and finding ones that are not vacuous is part of the work: I expect candidates such as the claims that aggregation is at least sometimes permissible within a single life, that real policy choices do trade small harms to many against severe harms to few, and that each side's intuitive verdicts about extreme cases sit in tension with at least one principle that side otherwise accepts — but the drafters should confirm, refine, or correct these. Second, state the surviving disagreements in wording each party would accept as a fair description of its own position, without either side's loaded vocabulary. Third, identify the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. My own suspicion is that the list includes whether badness is cardinally measurable and continuous across the trivial-to-severe range, whether an irritation so mild that it is never consciously registered is a harm at all, and whether the within-life case differs in principle from the across-persons case — but the statement should treat these as hypotheses, not as given. Fourth, for each crux, describe a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side must say about it, so that neither can retroactively reinterpret the result.

To keep the statement honest, require it to engage three specified cases. Case one: fix the trivial harm precisely — a faint half-second itch behind the eye, noticed in the moment and instantly forgotten, with no after-effects, no knock-on consequences, and no diminishment of anything the person values — so that neither side quietly substitutes something worse or something that is nothing at all. Case two: a public-health agency must choose between a program that spares one identified person ten years of severe chronic pain and a program that spares three hundred million strangers one unpleasant minute each, suffered once; the statement should record each camp's verdict and whether either camp is embarrassed by its own answer. Case three, which I devised to break the deadlock: one person will suffer that same trivial itch a billion separate times, spread across a long life, versus a different person suffering one week of severe pain. If the anti-aggregationist aggregates within the single life here, the statement must extract their explanation of why the number of persons changes the principle; if they refuse to aggregate even here, they must own that verdict explicitly rather than changing the subject.

Finally, the statement should quarantine any dispute that turns out to be verbal — about what words like harm, worse than, or matters mean — from the genuine disagreements, and it should close by having each side name what it would count as an argument or result against its own view; if either side cannot name anything, the statement should say so plainly and treat that fact as itself a finding.

Write the entire document as continuous prose, the way a signed statement would actually read: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and no citations or reading lists — the students should be reasoning from the arguments, not reporting the literature.
