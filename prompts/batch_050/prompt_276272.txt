You are a professional philosopher of mind with particular expertise in phenomenal consciousness and the knowledge argument. I am preparing for a doctoral qualifying exam, and I want to stress-test my intuitions by finding out what principle, if any, underlies my verdicts on a family of cases. Think carefully: before committing to an answer, sketch at least two candidate principles and run each against every case below; only then argue for the survivor or for a principled revision of my intuitions.

Here are the cases and my honest verdicts.

Case 1: Mary, the vision scientist, has lived her whole life in an environment devoid of color and knows every physical fact about color perception. Shown a ripe tomato for the first time, she learns something. My verdict: what she learns feels like a fact about the world, not merely a new skill.

Case 2: A neuroscientist knows every physical fact about the processes involved in pain but has never felt any. On first touching a hot stove, my verdict flips: she does not learn a new fact. Pain just is what those processes are like from the inside; she gains acquaintance with something she already knew everything about.

Case 3: A human expert knows every physical fact about bat echolocation. My verdict: she does not and cannot know what echolocation is like, but this reflects a limitation in her as a subject, not a gap in the physical facts.

Case 4: A chemist knows every physical fact about the taste of pineapple, then tastes it. My verdict: she gains abilities — to recognize, imagine, and remember the taste — and that seems to exhaust the sense in which she "learns" anything.

Case 5: A musicologist knows every physical fact about a symphony she has never heard, including every fact about the brain states she will be in while listening to it. My verdict: she can still be surprised on first hearing it, and her surprise seems factual in character, which sits oddly with Case 4, since by hypothesis she already possessed every physical fact about her own future states.

Case 6: Could Mary, still in her colorless environment, work out what red looks like by extrapolating from an intermediate shade, the way one might infer a shade lying between two shades one has seen? My verdict: she could not — but I cannot say whether this is principled or merely a contingent limit on human imagination.

What I want from you: a principle, or a small set of ranked candidates, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it; a verdict on the hard pairs (1 against 2, and 4 against 5) saying whether they can be reconciled or which verdict I must surrender; the principle's pronouncements on two cases I have not considered — a subject who knows all the physical facts about her own experience while she is having it, and a creature with a complete physical science but a wholly alien sensory repertoire; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate.

Finally, end by telling me what would change your mind: which of these case verdicts, reconsidered, or which argument not yet canvassed, would most threaten the principle you settle on, and where in your own reasoning you are least confident.
