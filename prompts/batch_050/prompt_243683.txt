So my friend and I just spent like two and a half hours on the phone having the same arguement we've had three times now, and I'm sick of it going in circles. It started because there's this poem we both love, and she said it's OBVIOUSLY about the poet's dead brother because the poet said exactly that in an interview, and I said the poet doesn't get to just declare that retroactively, the poem never mentions a brother once. Then it spiraled into the whole general question of whether the author's intention settles what a text means. We both kept using words like "intentionalist" at each other without agreeing what they cover, so I want to force the issue with something more rigorous.

Write me a decision tree that sorts a person into a named position on authorial intention and textual meaning. It's for a reading group of smart people who know zero philosophy, so every branch node has to be a concrete question about a case, not a jargon question -- nobody should need to know the position names to answer, and no node should be literally "do you think authorial intention matters?" Sort by what people believe, not by what label they like.

Specs:

- About 6 to 9 branch nodes, each phrased as a question about a specific scenario, with yes/no or three-way answers leading to the next node.
- The leaf positions have to include at least: strict actual intentionalism (whatever the author genuinely meant fixes the meaning, full stop); hypothetical/moderate intentionalism (meaning is the intention a reader would be best justified in attributing, given the text and public evidence); pure conventionalism (the words mean what public language conventions make them mean, intentions irrelevant); reader-response style views; and the view that the best interpretation is whichever makes the work most valuable while staying consistent with the text. Add any genuinely distinct position I've missed; note "unnamed" for any coherent leaf nobody defends.
- Three of the nodes have to be built around these cases (or close variants): (a) the author years later insists the work means something nobody can find in it; (b) the author privately intends something the words, as publicly used, just can't mean; (c) the authorship is unknown or disputed, so there's no one to consult.
- Before the tree itself, lay out the logical space: list the answer-combinations your questions generate, then place the positions in it. For any combination that's empty, one sentence on why -- is the view incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored?
- Flag at least one pair of leaves your tree can't cleanly separate, and say what extra question would split them (or admit no question can).
- At the very end, place me and my friend on the tree based on the positions I described above, and tell me which single node is the one we're actually disagreeing at -- I suspect we agree on more than the arguement made it seem.
