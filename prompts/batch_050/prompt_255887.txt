I have no formal training in philosophy, but a while ago I got stuck on a claim I came across in a popular book on knowledge: that if you know something, you thereby know that you know it. I gather philosophers call this the KK principle. It sounded obviously right to me at first -- how could you genuinely know something while being in the dark about whether you know it? -- but the more cases I thought up, the shakier it got. I would like to pressure-test my thinking, so please act as a professional epistemologist with particular expertise in knowledge-first epistemology and debates about the transparency of one's own mental states.

Here are the three objections I have come up with on my own. Please do not simply repeat any of them back to me.

(1) The nervous exam candidate. A student has studied thoroughly and reliably produces the correct answers, but she is convinced she is failing and takes herself to be guessing. It seems she knows the answers -- her responses are not lucky, they come from solid retention -- yet since she does not even believe that she knows, she cannot know that she knows. So knowing seems compatible with lacking second-order knowledge.

(2) The regress. If knowing implies knowing that you know, then presumably knowing that you know implies knowing that you know that you know, and so on without end. But a finite mind cannot actually sustain infinitely many ever-thinner layers of confidence and self-monitoring. Any point at which the chain breaks looks arbitrary, and if it breaks at level four or five, I do not see why it could not just as well break at level two.

(3) The gradual change. If some condition changes imperceptibly -- a light dimming very slowly, or my own state as I drift from fully awake toward sleep -- then at each moment I can only judge my situation with some margin for error. There will be points where I am still, say, clearly enough awake to know I am awake, but where I could easily have been wrong about whether my state has shifted, and that error-possibility seems to undermine my knowing that I know, even when the first-order knowledge is intact. Since every condition shades off at the edges somewhere, this seems to threaten the principle quite generally, not just in odd cases.

Now for my request. Think carefully, and before settling on an answer, brainstorm several candidate objections on scratch paper, so to speak. Then give me one more objection to the KK principle that is not on my list. It must be genuinely different in structure from mine: not a rewording, not a special case of one of them (in particular, not just another instance of a gradual change or another case of someone who fails to believe that they know), and not a mere combination of two of them. Explain the objection carefully, in terms a non-specialist can follow, and say what feature of knowledge it exploits that my three objections leave untouched.

Finally, be honest with me about whether your objection is actually good. If a defender of the KK principle has a natural reply, give the reply fairly and tell me which side you think comes out ahead, and why. I would rather hear that my principle survives my best efforts than collect a weak objection for the sake of having a fourth one.
