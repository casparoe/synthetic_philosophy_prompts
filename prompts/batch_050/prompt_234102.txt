I keep a birdwatching notebook, and for years I have recorded not just what I saw but how sure I was, because I once read, in a popular book on reasoning under uncertainty, that this is the rational thing to do: when you are not sure what you saw, you put your new confidence in the proposition at whatever level your glimpse warrants, and you scale your confidence in everything else up or down in proportion, keeping the relative standing of the possibilities within each branch intact. So a smudged shape in poor light that leaves me seventy per cent sure it was a waxwing becomes a seventy–thirty split, and my confidence that waxwings migrate through the estuary shifts accordingly. It is only this year, comparing notes with a friend who watches the same estuary, that I have noticed something odd about the rule. Over a foggy week I recorded three uncertain glimpses in a particular order — confident, doubtful, middling — whereas my friend, who was away, received my whole notebook at once and pooled the entries into a single summary before adjusting his views. We ended up with noticeably different final numbers, and when we re-ran my week with the same three glimpses in a different order, we got a third number. Nothing was ever learnt with certainty by anyone; nobody reused a glimpse or forgot one; the total information was identical. Only the sequence and the packaging differed.

The reason I am fairly sure this is not some mundane error is that the effect vanishes the moment the evidence is hard. If every observation in the notebook had been a definite sighting — yes, it was a waxwing, no ambiguity — then the order in which I processed them, and whether I processed them one at a time or in a lump, would make no difference to where I ended up; the final view is fixed by the total evidence alone. The path-dependence appears precisely, and only, when the inputs come in degrees. A colleague in medicine tells me the same thing happens with ambiguous test results: two inconclusive assays applied in succession leave the patient's record in a different state depending on which was read first, which alarms her, though her statistician friend shrugged and said the rule is the rule. I should be clear about what I am not describing. Nobody is counting the same evidence twice; this is not the psychological quirk where first impressions anchor you; and no one has forgotten anything, so it is not whatever the literature calls the problem of losing information over time. Each step, taken on its own, looks impeccably reasonable — that is what makes the accumulation strange.

Does this phenomenon have a name — the fact that, under graded or soft updating, the final state of belief retains a permanent fingerprint of the order and grouping in which the evidence arrived, whereas under all-or-nothing updating it does not? If there is an established term, I would like to know how the phenomenon is distinguished from adjacent ones, since I gather there is nearby work on sequences of uncertain observations that I have not been able to place. If there is no established term, please coin one, define it precisely, and mark its boundaries: does it afflict every rule for taking up uncertain evidence, or is it a defect of this particular proportional rule that a better rule would eliminate — and if the latter, what does the better rule do differently, keep a diary of the evidence itself, or something else? A bare label on its own will not satisfy me; I want to understand where the edges of the thing are.
