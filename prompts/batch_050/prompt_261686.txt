Bit of background: I'm in a small reading group that's been working through Dialectic of Enlightenment, and last month we hit the culture industry chapter. Half the group found it exhilarating, the other half found it unreadable, and somehow I got landed with explaining it at the next meeting. While prepping I found a paragraph on someone's critical theory blog that seemed like a handy crib, but before I repeat any of it I'd like to know how much of it is actually fair to Adorno and Horkheimer. Here's the paragraph:

'The culture industry, for Adorno and Horkheimer, is basically a system of social control that comes disguised as entertainment. Hollywood films, radio and hit songs are standardised products rolled off an assembly line, differing only in superficial details, and their real function is to keep the population docile: people come home exhausted from work, the industry feeds them amusement that reproduces the rhythms of work itself, and the cycle repeats. Crucially, audiences are duped by all this — they mistake the products for genuine pleasure and fail to notice the manipulation, which is what makes the system so effective. The two authors, German Jewish exiles who ended up in Los Angeles during the war, made little effort to hide their contempt for mass-produced trash, and the argument boils down to an elitist defence of the great European art tradition against vulgar American popular taste, which is why the chapter is so often dismissed as pure snobbery. And since it was modern media technology that made the culture industry possible in the first place, their conclusion is that cinema and radio are inherently manipulative — which, as the blogger points out, means Adorno would have hated streaming and social media even more than Hollywood, and that nothing produced for a mass commercial market can ever count as real art.'

Could you go through this as if you were a lecturer in continental philosophy auditing a student's summary — claim by claim — and tell me what Adorno and Horkheimer actually argue versus what the blogger has slightly or seriously twisted? Two things I'm especially unsure about: first, the snobbery charge, since the chapter definately does read as sneering in places, so I'm half inclined to side with the blogger there; and second, the claim that audiences simply don't notice the deception, because I have a vague memory of something in the chapter suggesting people see through it all and consume it anyway, which would make the blogger's version a significant flattening. Then, whatever you conclude about the summary's accuracy, tell me whether the blogger's closing inference — that for Adorno nothing made for a mass commercial market can be art, so he'd loathe Netflix even more than the studio system — actually follows from what survives your corrections, or whether it's a further stretch stacked on top of the earlier distortions. Think it through carefully rather than skimming; and please write your answer in British English.
