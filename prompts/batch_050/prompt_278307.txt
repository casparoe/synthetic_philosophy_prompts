I am preparing a seventy-five-minute graduate seminar for machine-learning researchers who generally assume that scientific progress consists in fitting better models to a stable dataset under a shared evaluation metric. Plan how I should teach Thomas Kuhn’s distinction between a paradigm shift and mere theory change, together with his narrower claim that successive paradigms can be partly incommensurable.

Do not give the lecture itself; design its explanatory structure. Identify what must be established first so that paradigm is not heard simply as a synonym for a theory, model, or conceptual framework. Specify the order in which to introduce normal science, anomaly, crisis, paradigm shift, and incommensurability, explaining which distinctions depend on which. Make clear that incommensurability concerns shifts in standards, problem selection, categories, and sometimes the meaning of shared terms—not the absurd claim that rival scientists cannot communicate or compare results at all.

Build the session around the following invented case. Two research groups receive identical telemetry from an autonomous ocean observatory. The first group, working within a stabilization paradigm, treats brief synchronized fluctuations as sensor artifacts and evaluates success by long-run calibration accuracy. The second, working within an ecological-event paradigm, treats the same fluctuations as evidence of rapidly propagating biological events and evaluates success by detecting their onset, shape, and spatial spread. Each group can reproduce the other’s calculations, yet they disagree over what constitutes an observation, which discrepancies count as anomalies, what a good explanation must predict, and whether the shared term event is being used in the same way. Explain why this case can carry the distinction better than a familiar historical example, which features it must preserve, and which features might misleadingly suggest that the dispute is merely about model accuracy.

Map the misconceptions I should expect, in roughly the order in which they are likely to arise: that a paradigm is just a large model; that normal science means intellectual conformity; that anomalies automatically refute paradigms; that a shift occurs when the evidence reaches a fixed threshold; that incommensurability means complete mutual unintelligibility; that paradigm choice is therefore irrational; and that a paradigm shift literally changes the physical world rather than reorganizing what scientists notice, describe, and treat as requiring explanation. For each misconception, indicate the point in the sequence where I should prevent or correct it and the particular feature of the ocean-observatory case I should use.

Include one interactive checkpoint in which the students must classify three further developments in the case: an improved artifact-detection algorithm within the stabilization paradigm, a revision of event taxonomy within the ecological paradigm, and a subsequent change in which phenomena count as successful predictions for one group. The checkpoint should reveal whether they can distinguish theoretical revision, methodological change, and genuine incommensurability without treating the categories as mutually exclusive. Conclude by specifying what I should deliberately leave out at this level—such as the details of Kuhn’s historical case studies and later refinements of his terminology—while still flagging their existence, and by proposing one final question that would show whether the class has understood why rational comparison can remain possible even when no neutral metric settles the dispute.
