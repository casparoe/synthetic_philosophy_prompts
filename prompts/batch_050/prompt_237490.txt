I'm in a medieval philosophy reading group (don't ask how this happened, it started as a book club) and last week we did Book 1 of Abelard's Ethics -- the one sometimes called "Know Yourself." At one point Abelard says, in the translation we're using and this is me paraphrasing from memory, that carrying out the deed adds nothing to the sin itself, that the whole of the guilt is in the consent, and he gives this example where two men carry out the exact same hanging, one out of an old grudge and one out of zeal for justice, and only the first one sins. I think he also says somewhere that the people who persecuted the martyrs did something terrible but didn't sin in the strict sense because they genuinely believed it pleased God, and that judges can rightly punish someone -- he mentions a mother who accidentally smothers her baby -- even where there's no sin, to scare other people into being careful.

Anyway, two people in the group, Theo and Marisol, got into a real argument applying this to a case Theo invented, and I stayed out of it because I genuinely can't tell who's right. The case: two coworkers, Aaron and Ben, each separately decide to sabotage a teammate's big presentation by deleting her files the night before. Aaron knows perfectly well it's wrong, feels gross about it, and does it anyway -- but the files get auto-restored from a backup and nothing happens. Ben has talked himself into sincerely believing it's basically fine, just office politics, everyone does it -- and his attempt works and tanks the presentation. (1) Theo said the Abelard passage settles that outcome luck is morally inert, so the two are equally guilty, since both consented, and it doesn't matter whose attempt worked. (2) Marisol said he's misreading the passage: Abelard's consent isn't just deciding to do something, it's consent to what you believe offends God, so on Abelard's actual view Ben didn't sin at all while Aaron did, and far from equalizing them the passage convicts the guy who harmed nobody and acquits the saboteur. (3) Theo said fine, then the view is refuted by the case, because Ben's belief was obviously self-serving rationalization and any theory that rewards talking yourself into a clear conscience is bankrupt. (4) Marisol said Abelard bites that exact bullet on purpose with the martyrs example, and that the passage is about sin before God, not about what anyone should do -- Ben still did what ought not be done and is fully punishable, like the mother in Abelard's own example -- so Theo's "refutation" just conflates sin with what a community should sanction. (5) Theo came back that if that's the defense, the famous passage does almost no work: "the deed adds nothing" shrinks to "the deed adds nothing to one very specific ledger," and the slogan that the sin is in the consent becomes close to useless as guidance for how to actually treat people. (6) Marisol said it's not useless at all, it relocates where the moral danger lives -- what you should interrogate in yourself is your consent, not your outcomes.
