I am a novelist in the early stages of a book set in a country I am inventing, and I have been trying to design its information regime from the ground up: who may know what about whom, and what the state owes its citizens in the way of concealment and exposure. In reading around for models, I have been struck by how radically real societies differ on this, and by how little of the difference seems to be argued for explicitly. In the United States, as I understand it, the default runs toward openness: arrest records, mugshots, court filings, voter registrations, and property ownership are largely public, and a person's bankruptcy or unprosecuted arrest may follow him for decades through private background-check companies, on the theory that transparency disciplines both state and citizen. Germany, again as I understand it, runs nearly the other way: rooted in a postwar constitutional jurisprudence of informational self-determination, it treats data about persons as presumptively theirs, obliges the press to anonymize offenders once their sentences are served, restricts the retention and resale of personal data, and has at times seen mass resistance to census-taking itself. China, by contrast, has built systems that not only expose but actively publicize: blacklists of judgment defaulters whose names, photographs, and partial ID numbers are displayed on billboards and at cinemas, on the apparent theory that shame is a legitimate instrument of administration.

Here is what I would like from you. Reconstruct what each of these three regimes presupposes about the citizen's relationship to the state: what each takes privacy to be (a form of property in information, a condition of dignity and self-authorship, a mere interest to be weighed against security and efficiency), what each takes the state's default epistemic position toward the citizen to be (entitled to inquire, forbidden to inquire, duty-bound to inquire), and what each implies about who must justify themselves when information changes hands. Then tell me which of these presupposition-sets is most internally coherent and which smuggles in a contradiction — I have a suspicion that the American model presupposes a libertarian account of information while relying on a collectivist account of security, and that the Chinese model presupposes a paternalist account of the state while denying that the state itself can ever be the sort of thing one hides from, but I may be wrong on both counts. Finally, and this is the novelist's question: if I want my invented country's regime to embody a coherent philosophy rather than an accidental patchwork, which commitments must I settle first, and what downstream rules would each settlement force?

Please answer in American English. I am not asking for a literature survey or a comparison of statutes; I want the implicit political philosophy of each practice laid bare, with clear markers of where a regime genuinely requires its presuppositions and where it is merely compatible with them, so that I can see the fork points in my own design.
