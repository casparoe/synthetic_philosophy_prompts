I run a small continuing-education workshop at a community arts center — about 14 adults per session, half working graphic designers and half curious amateurs, two hours on a Tuesday evening. Next month's session is built around a question I want them to feel in their hands before they ever hear it stated: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? A letter's identity seems to survive almost any mutilation of its parts — you can remove the crossbar, replace curves with angles, stretch it, hollow it out — so long as some elusive whole is preserved, and yet nobody in the room will be able to state a checklist of features that all and only 'a's share. Likewise, a single typeface can have a recognizable personality running through all 26 letters without any one stroke or serif appearing in every letter.

Design a staged activity that makes participants collide with this themselves. I'm imagining something like: a first round in which each participant draws an 'a' under a tight, arbitrary constraint (only straight segments, no more than five strokes, must fit in a square grid), so the room ends up with fourteen radically different but unambiguous 'a's; a second round involving classification of letterforms I've planted, some clearly 'a's, some clearly not, and some engineered to split the room; and a third round in which small groups invent a miniature alphabet of five or six letters in a coherent style of their own, then swap and must guess which new letter belongs to which invented alphabet. But you should feel free to restructure this entirely if a different sequence would make the phenomenon bite harder.

Give me concrete rules, constraints, and materials for each stage (nothing requiring computers or type-design software — index cards, markers, and grid paper at most), a minute-by-minute timeline, and instructions for how to construct the ambiguous planted forms so that disagreement actually happens rather than everyone politely agreeing. Then a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the conceptual lessons: why checklist definitions of letterhood fail, why resemblance to a prototype seems to do the work instead, and why the 'spirit' of an alphabet looks like a property of the relations among a letter's features rather than of the features themselves. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I suspect at least one group will produce an 'alphabet' whose letters are indistinguishable from each other, and I suspect the designers in the room will try to settle classification disputes by appeal to professional authority — and how to handle those failures without lecturing. Finally, be candid about which aspect of the phenomenon the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in discussion; my own worry is that drawing static letterforms can't capture the way letter-identity depends on the role a shape plays among its 25 siblings, but tell me whether you think that or something else is the real gap.
