The exam question was:

Epicurus argued that death is not bad for the person who dies. (a) Reconstruct his argument as carefully as you can, making its key premise about harm explicit. (b) Lucretius later offered an argument comparing the time before our birth with the time after our death. Explain it, and say whether it is a separate argument from (a) or the same one. (c) Deprivationists hold that death is bad for us because it deprives us of the good experiences our lives would otherwise have contained. Does deprivationism give an adequate answer to both Epicurus and Lucretius? Illustrate with the case of someone cryonically frozen for a century and then revived: on each view, is the freezing bad for them?

Rubric: 25 points. 8 for (a), 6 for (b), 8 for (c), 3 for the cryonics application.

Here's the student's answer:

(a) Epicurus's argument runs like this: anything that is bad for a person has to be something that affects them, which requires them to exist at the time. While we are alive, death has not arrived, so it isn't affecting us. Once death has arrived, we no longer exist, so there is no subject around for it to be bad for. There is therefore never a time at which death is bad for anyone. Being dead is like dreamless sleep: nothing to us.

(b) Lucretius's symmetry argument is essentially the same insight viewed from the other side. The stretch of nonexistence before you were born did you no harm, and you don't look back on it with dread. The stretch of nonexistence after your death is a mirror image of it, so the rational attitude toward it is whatever your attitude toward prenatal nonexistence already is: indifference. Since it's the same point run in the other direction, the two stand or fall together.

(c) The deprivationist replies that death is bad because it deprives you of the good experiences you would otherwise have had, and that you can be harmed without experiencing the harm — someone whose savings are stolen while they're in a coma is harmed even if they never find out. This works against the no-subject argument, because it drops the requirement that a harm be felt. But it cannot handle the symmetry argument: if death is bad because of the goods it takes from you, then the equally long nonexistence before your birth took just as much from you, so the deprivationist is committed to calling your late birth a tragedy too, which nobody believes.

On cryonics: for the Epicurean, a frozen person experiences nothing, so freezing is nothing to them while it lasts, though if revival works then the person exists again later, so it's unclear whether the argument even applies. For the deprivationist, freezing costs you the experiences of the frozen years, but if you're revived you still get your remaining years, just later — so freezing turns out not bad at all, merely a delay, which ends up as a point in the Epicurean's favor after all.
