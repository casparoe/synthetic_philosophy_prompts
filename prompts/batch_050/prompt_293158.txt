I am a novelist -- eight books published, the last three of them alternate history -- and I have agreed, somewhat rashly, to deliver the closing address at the annual banquet of an alternate-history fiction society. The audience is awkwardly split. One half will be working novelists, people whose advances depend on the premise that history turns on knife-edges: kill this archduke, lose this letter, and the twentieth century unspools differently. The other half will be academic historians, several of them openly skeptical of our genre, who tend to regard the great-man-on-a-knife-edge conceit as a professional embarrassment. The organizers have asked for fifteen minutes, in the after-dinner slot, on whatever I like. I want to use it to defend a thesis I have arrived at the hard way, because it is a thesis that partly repudiates my own most successful book.

Here is the position, in the terms I have come to use privately. I divide contingencies into the absorbent and the generative. An absorbent contingency is one the system swallows: remove it, and the broad pattern of events re-forms by another route within a generation, because the forces pressing toward that pattern had many channels available to them. A generative contingency is one whose removal genuinely forks the century -- the counterfactual world, followed honestly, diverges further with each decade rather than healing back toward ours. My claim is that almost all the contingencies our genre loves are absorbent, that genuine forks are far rarer and usually far less picturesque than novelists assume, and that my own best-known novel -- built on a single poisoned diplomat -- rested on an absorbent contingency I dressed up as generative. The divergence in that book should have closed like water behind a ship within thirty years, and I knew it by the final draft and kept quiet because the book was working. The speech is therefore partly a public recantation, and it has to carry that weight without turning into self-flagellation.

Please write the address. It should be built around the absorbent/generative distinction and make the distinction do real structural work -- shaping what I can concede to the historians, what I can still defend to the novelists, and how the recantation lands as growth rather than confession. It should develop one extended worked example that a mixed audience can follow without specialist knowledge (a widely familiar historical case or a lucid invented one), and it should end by reframing rather than renouncing the genre: my genuine belief is that counterfactual fiction's value was never the depiction of divergent worlds but its function as an instrument for detecting which contingencies were actually load-bearing, and I want the novelists to leave feeling their craft has been promoted, not indicted, and the historians to leave unable to dismiss it. Fifteen minutes spoken, warm but unsentimental, no slide-friendly bullet-point cadence, and nothing that requires the audience to trust me on dates they would want to check.
