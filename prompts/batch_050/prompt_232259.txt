I'm prepping for a reading group on the unity of the proposition and I put together an argument that I'm now worried proves way too much, because the conclusion is that nobody has ever said anything true or false, which... can't be right, since that conclusion would itself be neither. Act as a professional metaphysician / philosopher of language for this. Think carefully, and before you commit to an answer, brainstorm a couple of different candidate weak spots in the argument and then tell me which one you'd actually attack.

Here's the argument:

(1) Take the proposition that Socrates is wise. If it were nothing more than its constituents — Socrates, and wisdom — sitting next to each other, it would be a mere collection, like a list. But a list isn't true or false of anything; it just sits there. So a proposition can't be a mere collection of constituents.

(2) So a proposition must contain something extra that binds the constituents into a unity — a tie, or a relating relation, call it what you like. The difference between the proposition and the list has to be some further item the proposition has and the list lacks.

(3) This tie is either itself a constituent of the proposition, or it isn't.

(4) Suppose it's a constituent. Then the proposition is now the collection {Socrates, wisdom, the tie}, and by the reasoning in (1) this is again just a list unless something further ties the tie to Socrates and wisdom. But that further tie will also be a constituent, and so on without end. So on this horn, no finite proposition is ever unified.

(5) Suppose instead the tie is not a constituent. Then the proposition contains nothing beyond Socrates and wisdom that does the unifying — the tie does its work, so to speak, from outside the proposition. But then as far as the proposition itself is concerned, it's still just the list, and we're back to the problem in (1).

(6) Either way, no proposition is a genuine unity. So there are no propositions, only lists. And since sentences get their truth or falsity from propositions, nothing anyone says is ever true or false.

The conclusion is obviously unacceptable, so something has to give. A few things I want from your diagnosis:

- Name the exact step you'd reject and explain why it *seemed* fine — I want to understand what seductive picture of propositions or of "items in a collection" is doing the work in the background.
- Price the main escape routes honestly. E.g. the Fregean move (the tie isn't an ordinary constituent because predicates are somehow "unsaturated" and don't need to be glued to anything — but then what stops that from being a label for the problem rather than a solution?); the move that says the regress in (4) is real but benign (every level is entailed by the one below, none needs to be a constituent — but is that distinction stable?); and the Bradley-style move of denying that unity can be decomposed at all, which seems to save the phenomena by giving up on analysis entirely.
- If it matters, I really want to keep (1), since the difference between a proposition and a list seems to me the one non-negotiable starting point here. Tell me what I have to pay for keeping it.
