At a philosophy reading group, two members spent the entire session arguing about the Sleeping Beauty problem, and the rest of us couldn't settle it. Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday; a fair coin is flipped; she is awakened on Monday regardless, awakened again on Tuesday only if the coin landed tails, and her memory of any prior awakening is erased each time. On each awakening she is asked her credence that the coin landed heads. Maya defended the thirder answer, Daniel the halfer answer, and the exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Maya opened with a betting argument. Suppose Beauty is offered, at each awakening, an even-money bet on heads. If she always accepts, she wins once when the coin lands heads but loses twice when it lands tails, so she loses money in expectation. The only odds at which the offered bets are fair are 2:1 against heads, which corresponds to a credence of 1/3. So Beauty's credence on awakening should be 1/3.

(2) Daniel replied that the betting setup is rigged against heads in a way that has nothing to do with her evidence: Beauty is charged for a bet exactly twice as often in the tails world, so the expected loss is an artifact of the experimental design rather than a revelation of her credence. He said he is happy to grant that the rational *betting rate* is 2:1 while denying that this fixes her *credence*, since the two come apart whenever the number of decision points varies with the outcome. As for credence itself: Beauty knows with certainty on Sunday that she will be awakened, so awakening brings no new information, and there is nothing to conditionalize on. Her credence should stay at 1/2.

(3) Maya countered with the extreme version of the case: replace the single extra awakening with a million awakenings on tails. On Daniel's view, Beauty wakes up and, because she expected to wake up, must remain at 1/2 on heads even though her waking is a million times more common on tails. She said this is a reductio of the "no new information" principle: what Beauty learns on waking is not the tautology "I am awakened at some point" but the self-locating fact "this awakening is happening now," and that fact is twice (or a million times) as likely on tails.

(4) Daniel conceded that the extreme case is uncomfortable but said the discomfort is generated entirely by Maya's rule that one should distribute credence uniformly over all of one's possible awakenings, which he called an unargued principle of indifference over temporal locations rather than a theorem. He then put a variant back to her: suppose the coin is not flipped until Monday evening, after the first awakening. On Monday, everyone agrees Beauty's credence in a not-yet-flipped fair coin should be 1/2. But if Maya's thirder reasoning is correct, Beauty's credence must then drop to 1/3 between Monday and Tuesday even though nothing happens in the interim except sleep and erasure, which he described as a diachronic incoherence in the thirder position.

(5) Maya's last move was to say the dispute had been miscast from the start: "What should Beauty believe?" is ambiguous between "what credence should she assign to heads, given this awakening?" and "what fraction of her awakenings occur in heads-worlds?", and the halfer and thirder answers are simply correct answers to different questions. Daniel replied that this was a truce, not a solution, since there is still a single question the experimenter actually asks Beauty at each awakening, and she has to give one number.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and justify the rating. Then say who, if anyone, is more right overall, and identify anything both parties are missing.
