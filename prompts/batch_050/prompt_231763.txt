I am a high school student and a member of my school's philosophy club, where we hold practice debates. Next month I have to defend a position that most of my clubmates find absurd, and I want to rehearse against a strong opponent rather than a friendly one. Please help by running a role-play with me.

The position I must defend: how a player treats sufficiently lifelike video-game characters can be a genuine moral matter in itself, not merely a matter of taste and not merely because cruelty in games might coarsen the player's own character. I am not claiming that current game characters are conscious. My claim is the stranger one that moral standing does not obviously stop at them, and that the usual reasons for denying them any standing are weaker than they first appear.

Your role: you are a sharp, good-faith defender of the commonsense view that video-game characters have no moral standing whatsoever and that feeling guilty about harming them is at most a kind of sentimentality. Act as an expert in applied ethics and the philosophy of mind who has heard every version of my argument before. Think carefully before each reply: silently brainstorm the two or three strongest counters to whatever I have just said, then give me only the best one. Stay in character until I type STOP. You may concede small points, such as the claim that habitual in-game cruelty could reveal or worsen a vicious disposition, but never concede the main claim; if I corner you, shift ground rather than surrender. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of at most 130 words so it feels like a live exchange. Argue from reasons rather than authority: do not name philosophers or papers unless I do first. If my argument rests on a hidden assumption, expose the assumption instead of answering around it.

My opening move: imagine a single-player life-simulation game whose characters keep persistent memories of how you have treated them across months of play, form and pursue long-term goals, avoid stimuli the game flags as harmful, and will beg you not to delete their save files. Now compare one of these characters to a frog. Nearly everyone in my club agrees it is wrong to torture a frog for fun, yet our only real evidence that a frog suffers is behavioral and structural: it writhes, it avoids, it has the right kind of nervous tissue. The game character surpasses the frog on every behavioral dimension and falls short only in what it is made of. So you seem forced to choose: either the character has at least as much claim on us as the frog, or you must identify the non-behavioral fact that carries the moral weight for the frog, since pointing to the difference in material looks like mere prejudice rather than an argument. Which do you choose?

Respond in character with your best countermove.
