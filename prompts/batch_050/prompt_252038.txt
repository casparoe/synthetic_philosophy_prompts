I am a PhD student in the philosophy of science, currently serving as a teaching assistant on an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics module. The convenor has asked me to draft one essay question for the final examination, on dispositions, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Candidates will have 75 minutes.

The cohort has covered the simple conditional analysis, Martin's electro-fink, Lewis's revised conditional analysis, and the standard distinction between finks and masks, with Bird's antidote cases as the stock example of the latter. I do not want a question that can be answered by reciting any of this. I want one that tests whether students understand what the fink/mask distinction actually turns on, and what it costs to keep it. To that end, please build the question around the following three cases, which the paper will present to candidates as a short stem:

Case 1. A fragile vase is fitted with a device that monitors for incoming impacts. When a blow is detected, the device floods the vase's microstructure with a bonding agent, so that by the time the impact lands the vase is sturdy and survives intact.

Case 2. The same vase is fitted with a device that, on detecting an incoming blow, fires a sheath of shock-absorbing gel around it. The vase is as easily broken as ever, but no impact reaches it with enough force to break it.

Case 3. A vase is manufactured with a polymer coating that does two things at once: it penetrates a few microns into the ceramic and strengthens it, whilst its outer layer absorbs impact energy. The vase survives blows that would break an uncoated vase.

The question should ask candidates to classify the first two cases, and then to use Case 3 to interrogate the distinction itself: is the coated vase's fragility finked or masked, and does the answer depend on which object we take to bear the disposition — the vase, or the vase-plus-coating? It should then press the further issue of what the difficulty shows: whether finks and masks mark a genuine metaphysical difference or merely two ways of describing interference at different grains, and what this means for attempts to repair the conditional analysis — in particular, whether Lewis's requirement that the disposition persist through the stimulus copes with masks, and whether an 'ideal conditions' rider can avoid triviality.

Please supply:

1. The question text, worded exactly as it should appear on the paper, including the stem.

2. A model answer of roughly 600 words.

3. A marking scheme totalling 20 marks, built around five or six named moves a top answer must make, the marks each is worth, and for each a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker candidates get it wrong — for instance, treating Case 2 as a fink simply because the manifestation fails to occur, or assuming the conditional analysis faces only the fink objection.

4. A short statement of what separates a first-class answer (70+) from a good 2:1 — the specific moves the former makes that the latter typically omits.
