Someone in my phil probability reading group hit me with this argument a few weeks ago and it's been eating at me, because the conclusion is obviously nuts but I can't find the step that breaks. Background: the Principal Principle says that if you know the objective chance of A is x, and you don't have any funny "inadmissible" information (like a message from the future about how A turns out), then your credence in A should be x.

(1) The Principal Principle is true.
(2) Suppose I know a die is fair: the objective chance of six on each roll is 1/6 and the rolls are independent.
(3) Then by PP, my credence in six on the next roll should be 1/6, no matter what my other admissible evidence is.
(4) Past frequency data is admissible: since the rolls are independent, the last 10,000 rolls carry no information about roll 10,001 beyond what the known chance already gives me.
(5) So even if six has come up 40% of the time over those 10,000 rolls, as long as I still know the chance is 1/6, my credence in six on the next roll stays 1/6.
(6) For that frequency data to count as evidence against my belief that the chance is 1/6, there would have to be some route from frequencies to chance-conclusions that doesn't run through my credences about particular outcomes. But (3)-(5) just showed credences about outcomes won't budge while the chance-belief is in place, and my only grip on what chances are is via the credences they license -- that's the whole point of PP.
(7) So once held, a belief about objective chances is incorrigible: no amount of recalicitrant frequency data can rationally dislodge it.
(8) But every belief we have about chances comes from frequency data, or from theories that bottom out in it.
(9) So beliefs about chances are either incorrigible dogma or never get off the ground, and either way nobody ever actually knows any chances -- meaning PP is a principle with no applications.

The conclusion can't be right; scientists learn chances from data all the time, and I want to keep PP itself. I've gone back and forth on (4) and (6) without getting anywhere. Which step should I reject, why does it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the answer is that I have to give up something in (2)'s idea of what it is to "know" a chance, tell me what I lose -- I thought that was the easy case PP was built for.
