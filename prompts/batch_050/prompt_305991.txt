I am a novelist at work on a book set partly in eighteenth-century Königsberg and partly in the present day; one of my contemporary characters is a veterinarian who loses her license for a mercy-killing she refuses to apologize for, and the historical strand follows a student taking notes in Kant's ethics lectures. To get the moral texture of the book right, I have arranged a long recorded conversation with a Kant scholar — a real defender, not a museum guide — who argues that Kant's much-maligned account of our duties concerning animals has been caricatured and, properly read, is more subtle and more defensible than the standard animal-ethics literature allows. She tends to deflect criticism with the rejoinder that the critics are attacking the lecture notes rather than the position, and she is skilled at retreating to systematicity: every objection, in her hands, becomes a lesson in how Kant's ethics actually works.

Here is the passage around which I want the conversation to turn. In the ethics lectures, in the section on duties regarding animals, Kant tells the anecdote of Leibniz, who, after examining a worm under his microscope and admiring its structure, carefully set it back down on its leaf so that it would not come to harm — and Kant offers this as a model of how a human being should comport himself toward the merely living. This is in the same stretch of text where Kant insists that our duties toward animals are in truth only indirect duties toward humanity, that cruelty to animals is wrong because it hardens the heart against the suffering of human beings, and that even gratitude toward an old horse or dog matters as practice for gratitude among persons. I will read the anecdote aloud early in the interview.

Write me a sequence of roughly ten interview questions, in the order I should ask them, that does the following. Open with the Leibniz-and-the-worm anecdote itself: what work Kant thinks this story is doing, and why the exemplar of proper conduct toward animals is an act whose beneficiary is never wronged. Then press on the indirect-duty structure in ways that escalate: the objection that the view licenses anything if the agent's character could be shielded (the vivisector with an unusually robust heart, the cruelty committed in perfect privacy); the objection that the view gets the direction of moral explanation backwards — that we think cruelty to a dog reveals a bad character precisely because we already take the dog's suffering to matter; and the question of why, on her reconstruction, Kant bothers with analogies between animal and human feeling at all if the analogy does no moral work. Include at least one question on what the view implies for my veterinarian character, who acted against the law and would act again — ask the scholar to say, in Kantian terms, whether the character did something wrong and to whom the wrong was done. Anticipate, for each question, the deflection she is most likely to use (the lecture-notes move, the appeal to Kant's considered system, the claim that Kant is doing moral psychology rather than axiology), and give me the follow-up that forecloses it without my having to pretend to scholarship I don't have. End the sequence with a question about what Kant could not have known — what the last two centuries of knowledge about animal cognition does to the passage's quiet assumption that the worm matters only as an occasion for our virtue — and let it be a question she can answer only by saying where Kant would have had to change his mind.
