I am contributing a piece to a small anthology of satirical documents from imaginary polities, and my assigned topic is futarchy — the governance arrangement floated in the economics literature some twenty years ago, under which democratic publics vote only on what is to be maximised (a national welfare metric) while prediction markets decide which policies will in fact maximise it, summed up by its proponents in the slogan that citizens should vote values but bet beliefs. I would like my contribution to be the official welcome guide issued by the civil service of the Republic of Vesperia, an island state that in 2029 became the first country to adopt the system in full, addressed to newly naturalised citizens. Please write it at roughly 700–900 words, with section headings along the lines of 'Your One Remaining Election', 'How Markets Hire and Fire Ministers', 'Settling the Unchosen', and 'Frequently Asked Questions'.

The guide should be scrupulously sincere, written with the unbothered helpfulness of a council leaflet, and it should communicate, among other facts of Vesperian life: that the annual referendum on the welfare metric is the only vote citizens cast, policy itself being settled by markets; that a minister whose dismissal is priced as welfare-improving is deemed dismissed at the close of trading; that markets on policies which go unadopted are voided and all stakes refunded, so the counterfactual is never observed, with the final prices preserved for the curious in the National Museum of Unchosen Futures; that would-be market manipulators are warmly welcomed, since their losses subsidise the information of other traders, and may apply for a certificate of civic contribution; that civil servants, knowing implementation best, are the country's most consistently successful traders, the pension fund's outperformance being a point of institutional pride; that the markets' record of correct decisions is unbroken, since a policy is adopted only when markets already expect it to help; and that there exists a market on whether futarchy itself should be retained, the details of which I leave to you.

The humour must come from the system's actual logic exaggerated rather than invented. Without ever naming them as objections, the piece should land the standard worries: that the welfare metric will be gamed once it becomes a target (Goodhart's law); that prices measure correlation rather than the causal effect of a policy, since adoption is conditional on expected success; that only adopted policies are ever observed, so the markets can never be proved wrong; that influence over policy is proportional to capital rather than to citizenship; and that long-horizon policy markets are too thin and illiquid to bear the weight placed on them. The voice must stay deadpan throughout — no winking at the reader, no narratorial irony, nothing the guide's in-universe authors would not happily publish — yet a reader familiar with the original proposal should be able to tell exactly which objection each passage is quietly conceding. Do not name or caricature any real economist or public figure; if you need a founder, invent one. The target is the proposal and its confident rhetoric, not any person. Please write in British English.
