Here are my verdicts on when we literally *see* something by way of an image, and I want the principle behind them, if there is one.

1. An ordinary mirror: obviously I'm seeing myself. A funhouse mirror: still seeing, just distorted.
2. A live CCTV feed of the parking lot: yes, I'm seeing the lot.
3. A photograph of my great-grandmother taken in 1910: I'm seeing her. That's the whole point of treasuring the photo; it feels nothing like owning a drawing of her.
4. A super realistic oil portrait of her: no. I see the painting, and maybe I learn what she looked like, but I'm not seeing *her*.
5. That same 1910 photo after someone colorized and retouched it: still seeing her, even though some of what's now in the image is fake.
6. A camera whose sensor glitches and, by sheer coincidence, outputs a pixel-perfect image of the scene in front of it (the scene had zero influence on the image): that's not seeing, that's a cosmic fluke.
7. An MRI slice: the radiologist sees the tumor.
8. A sonar screen rendering a submarine from returning pings: I want to say yes, the operator sees the sub, at least its shape and position.
9. An AI-generated image of what my great-grandmother would have looked like at 90, built from her real photos: definately not seeing her.
10. A long-exposure shot that turns a waterfall into silky blur: still seeing the waterfall.
11. A seismograph trace of an earthquake: no. That's data *about* the quake, not a view of it.

So is there one principle here? 3 and 6 push me toward needing genuine causal/counterfactual dependence between scene and image. But 5 and 9 pull apart on human/machine meddling in a way I can't cleanly state, since both involve heavy intervention after the fact. And 7-8 vs 11 seem to demand something like preserved spatial structure, except sonar images are pretty constructed and 10 shows I'm fine with big deviations from naked-eye appearance. I gather philosophers call the strong version of this the transparency thesis, but don't just sort me into camps -- give me a principle, stated precisely enough that I can tell which clause is doing work in which case. If nothing saves all eleven verdicts, tell me which one you'd overturn and why its appeal is an illusion. And say what your principle predicts for cases I didn't list: a periscope, an ultrasound of a fetus ("we saw the baby today!"), a camera obscura, and a photocopy of a photocopy degraded almost to noise.
