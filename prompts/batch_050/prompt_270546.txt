A colleague of mine in the physics department has an argument about chaos and determinism that she thinks shows something deep, and I think proves too much — but I can't pin down where it slips. Here it is, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) A claim is scientifically meaningful only if some observation could, at least in principle, count for or against it. (2) The only observation that could count for or against determinism about a physical system is the success or failure of a prediction of that system's later evolution from its initial conditions. (3) In any system with sensitive dependence on initial conditions, states lying closer together than any finite measurement tolerance diverge exponentially, so beyond a finite prediction horizon the forecast is no better than chance — no matter how good the measurement is. (4) Every actual measurement has finite precision. (5) Therefore, for any system with sensitive dependence, long-run predictions always fail, and their failure proves nothing about determinism either, since the failure is fully explained by measurement error rather than by any indeterminism in the system. (6) Sensitive dependence is ubiquitous among nonlinear classical systems: gravitational three-body dynamics, fluid turbulence, the atmosphere. (7) So determinism is not a scientifically meaningful claim for an enormous class of systems that classical mechanics was supposed to describe.

Her conclusion already makes me suspicious, but what convinces me the argument goes wrong somewhere is that it seems to prove far too much. Run the same reasoning through again and it appears to show that no precise theory of these systems is scientifically meaningful at all — including the ensemble forecasts, statistical-mechanical predictions, and short-horizon trajectory models that we manifestly do test, refine, and confirm every day. If her premises really supported (7), they'd support that absurdity too. So something in the chain has to give.

Here's what I'd most like to keep: premise (4) seems untouchable, and I'm reluctant to abandon (1) entirely, since some connection between meaning and testability seems right even if crude verificationism is dead. I'm less attached to (2), (3), and (5) than she is, but I can't say exactly which one does the damage or why.
