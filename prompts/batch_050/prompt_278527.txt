I'm the senior planning engineer at a mid-sized coastal water utility (about 400k customers), and twice a year our capital projects committee has to decide which adaptation projects get funded for the next decade -- things like raising a pump station, relocating an intake, upsizing storm drains. Consultants keep handing us thick climate projection reports, and our current approach is basically "average the ensemble, add a safety factor, argue about it." I was complaining about this to a friend who works in catastrophe modeling and she said we don't actually have a climate problem, we have a decision problem: sometimes ensemble disagreement doesn't matter because the project is justified under every plausible future, and sometimes ensemble agreement is false comfort because all the models share the same structural blind spots.

What I want is a decision procedure my committee could actually run in a half-day session, per project, that starts from the decision rather than the projections: i.e., first figure out which climate variable the project's success actually hinges on and over what lead time, then ask what range of outcomes the project tolerates before it becomes a bad investment, and only then look at what the model ensembles and scenario spread say about whether we'll cross that threshold. It should have explicit steps for handling the cases that keep tripping us up: when to treat model disagreement as decision-relevant vs ignorable, when to distrust ensemble consensus (e.g., all models missing the same feedback), when a deep-uncertainty approach like stress-testing the project against storyline futures beats waiting for better predictions, and when the honest answer is "spend money on monitoring and design the project to be modified later" instead of building bigger now. Mark the points where the procedure hands off to a value judgment (like how much regret we're willing to tolerate, or how we weigh harms to current vs future ratepayers) rather than pretending those are technical questions.

Then run the procedure on two real-ish cases for us: (1) a $60M pump station raise justified mainly by tail-risk sea level scenarios where the models genuinely disagree, and (2) a cheap culvert upsizing that's robust under every scenario but that our board loves precisely because it feels "climate-proof" -- and be willing to tell me, if the procedure says so, that one of these is a bad use of money even though it'll feel politically awful to say. End with a short honest section on what this procedure can't do and where it'll misfire.
