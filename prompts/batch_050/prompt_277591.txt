I never went to university -- I trained as an electrician and have been working ever since -- but I read a fair amount and think about these things on my own time. My closest friend and I have an ongoing argument about what makes a working life go well or badly, and I have been tracking her actual verdicts, because I suspect she is following a rule she has never stated. Here are the verdicts I am confident about.

First: a distant relative of hers worked sixty- to eighty-hour weeks at a large corporation for twenty-five years, mostly out of fear of disappointing his family and company, and died of heart failure at forty-nine. She calls his life wasted. Second: a man we both knew slightly inherited a large sum at twenty-two and has spent the fifteen years since drifting -- no job, no projects, no real pursuits beyond passing the time comfortably. She calls his life wasted too. Third: a baker in our neighborhood works full days, earns modestly, loves her craft, and feeds people. She calls that a well-lived life. Fourth: a retired schoolteacher who fills her week with gardening and volunteer tutoring -- nobody pays her, nobody makes her do it -- is also, on her view, living well.

When I press her, she says things like: the baker "gives people something," the heir "never gave anything back," and the salaryman "gave everything to people who didn't even want it."

Now I want to know how she would judge three further cases, and I want your best prediction. Case X: a man works twenty-five hours a week at a job he chose freely and genuinely enjoys, but the work itself accomplishes nothing -- he produces reports that, as far as anyone can tell, no one ever reads, and he knows it. Case Y: a woman works seventy-hour weeks on a project she designed herself, believes deeply in, and that is in fact of real use to others; she shows no sign of exhaustion and would not want to work less. Case Z: a man with a small pension spends his days building extraordinarily intricate model ships in his basement, entirely alone; no one else will ever see them, he does not intend anyone to, and the hours are however many he pleases.

What I would like from you: first, state the rule my friend seems to be applying, stated precisely enough that it delivers verdicts; second, state at least two other rules that fit all four of her existing verdicts just as well but disagree with each other (or with your first rule) on some of the new cases; third, say what each candidate rule predicts for X, Y, and Z; fourth, give me your best guess at her verdicts, with your reasons, being honest if the track record genuinely underdetermines any of them; and fifth, describe one further case -- different from X, Y, and Z -- whose verdict would best discriminate between the rules you could not otherwise separate.

Please end your response with a final line of the form \answer{X: ..., Y: ..., Z: ...} giving your predicted verdicts in one or two words each (e.g., "well lived" or "wasted").
