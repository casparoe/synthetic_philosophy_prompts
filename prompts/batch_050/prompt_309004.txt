I am a Year 12 student working on an ethics extension task about voting, and I am trying to turn my judgements about particular cases into a defensible principle. My tentative principle is that one ought to vote when the possible consequences are serious enough, provided one has made a reasonable effort to become informed; where the result is effectively settled, expressive considerations may legitimately determine one’s vote. I suspect this is too simple.

Here are my verdicts:

1. In a close national election, Candidate A is competent but supports several policies I regard as unjust, while Candidate B has promised to weaken courts, restrict hostile journalists and make future elections less fair. I think an eligible voter has a strong duty to vote for A, even if doing so feels like endorsing A’s bad policies.

2. In a constituency where A is certain to win by tens of thousands of votes, I think it is entirely permissible to vote for a minor party whose programme better represents the voter’s views, simply to register support for that programme.

3. In an otherwise identical constituency where A and B are separated by only a few dozen votes, I think voting for the same minor party would be wrong. The voter need not positively approve of A; preventing B matters more.

4. A voter in the close national election knows nothing about the candidates beyond campaign slogans and chooses A by tossing a coin. Although A is objectively the much better candidate, I think the voter ought to have abstained and does not deserve moral credit for happening to choose correctly.

5. Another voter spends an hour reading the candidates’ published policies and several generally reliable news summaries, but because of an understandable gap in the reporting reaches the false conclusion that B is preferable. I think voting for B may still be permissible, despite the bad consequence the voter is trying to produce.

6. In a local referendum about whether to replace a small park with housing, an individual vote is extraordinarily unlikely to matter and the moral stakes are real but modest. If voting would require a parent to miss an important childcare obligation, I think there is no strict duty to vote, although voting might be admirable.

7. In the close national election from Case 1, the chance that one vote will change the outcome is also extraordinarily small. Nevertheless, if the same parent misses a less important social engagement in order to vote for A, I think they have done what they ought to do; merely saying “my vote almost certainly will not matter” would not excuse them.

8. In a country with compulsory voting where both viable candidates support a policy the voter considers profoundly unjust, I think casting a deliberately blank or spoiled ballot can be permissible. It communicates refusal rather than apathy, although it would not be permissible if voting for a viable but imperfect candidate were necessary to prevent something much worse.

9. If a voter knows that a candidate is slightly better overall but strongly prefers the worse candidate because of their accent, family background or social class, I think voting for the worse candidate is wrong even in a safe constituency. This seems different from expressively supporting a minor party whose policies one sincerely favours.

Is there a coherent principle, or a small family of principles, that captures most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely and identify which case motivates each qualification or clause. In particular, I am interested in how the principle should combine:

- the probability and seriousness of affecting an outcome;
- the distinction between endorsing a candidate and voting strategically for the lesser evil;
- the legitimacy of expressive voting;
- the voter’s epistemic responsibilities;
- the difference between a reasonable mistake and culpable ignorance;
- and ordinary costs or competing obligations.

Please also test my tentative principle against the cases and revise it if necessary. I would like to know whether I must abandon any of my verdicts, especially if Cases 2, 3 and 9 cannot all be accommodated without drawing an artificial distinction. Finally, please apply the resulting account to two cases I have not listed: an election using preferential voting, where a voter can rank a minor party first and the lesser evil second; and a workplace ballot in which abstaining visibly protects an unpopular colleague from being singled out. Please write your answer in British English.
