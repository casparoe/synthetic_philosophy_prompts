There's a chapter in this pop-science book on spacetime I picked up at an airport (trade book, not a textbook) that explains Einstein's hole argument like this:

"In 1913, while he was still groping toward general relativity, Einstein convinced himself via the hole argument that generally covariant field equations were impossible. Imagine a region of spacetime with no matter in it -- the 'hole.' Since coordinates are completely arbitrary in GR, you can describe the same hole using two different coordinate systems that agree everywhere outside it but differ inside, and the field equations have no way to tell you which description is the true one. The metric inside the hole is therefore undetermined, so general covariance has to go. Einstein duly abandoned it for about two years, and only came back when he realized that talk about individual spacetime points is physically meaningless -- only point-coincidences, like two worldlines crossing, are real. The moral for modern readers: GR shows that spacetime points don't exist, that reality is a web of pure relations, and that what you call space is, strictly speaking, an illusion."

Something smells off about the last part to me, but I can't put my finger on whether it's the last sentence or whether the rot starts earlier. A friend who did a phil of physics masters also mentioned that the argument was dug up and given a new lease on life by philosophers in the 1980s, and that the modern version isn't really about coordinates at all, which makes me even more suspicious of the chapter.

Can you audit it for me? Go through it claim by claim: what's historically accurate, what's a reasonable simplification for a general audience, and what's actually wrong or conflates two things that should be kept apart? In particular: is the arbitrariness of coordinates really what generates the problem, and does the 'only coincidences are real' move actually dissolve it or just relocate it? And then the thing I actually care about -- does the book's punchline (spacetime points don't exist, space is an illusion, relationalism wins) follow from anything that's true in the summary, or is it stapled on? If the modern philosophical version of the argument changes the verdict on any of this, flag that too.
