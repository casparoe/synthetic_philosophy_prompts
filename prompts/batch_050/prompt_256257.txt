I teach a second-year political philosophy module and every year the nationalism vs cosmopolitanism week goes the same way: half the class says "of course I owe my fellow citizens more than strangers abroad" and the other half says "that's just dressed-up favoritism," and then both sides talk past each other because nobody has separated the different reasons you might give for compatriot priority. So for next term I want to hand out a proper sorting instrument instead of letting them argue vibes. Please build me a decision tree (or a quiz with a scoring key, whichever you think works better -- but commit to one) that sorts students by their actual answers to concrete cases rather than by which label they find respectable. The cases should be things like: a limited disaster-relief budget and a choice between compatriots and much worse-off foreigners; whether it was wrong to feel more gutted by a bombing in your own country than a deadlier one far away; whether you may spend on your kid's tutoring while children elsewhere lack basics; whether the fact that you pay taxes into a shared scheme generates obligations that outsiders can't claim; and whether you'd accept a significantly worse economic deal for your country to benefit distant strangers.

The tree should be built from the logical space, not from a list of names: figure out which questions genuinely discriminate (whether special obligations are grounded in shared institutions and reciprocity vs. in relationships vs. in identity; whether they're reasons at all or just permissions; whether they concern distribution only or also rescue and partial concern; whether they can ever outweigh global duties or only operate within constraints cosmopolitanism sets), and only then locate actual positions as leaves. Leaves I definitely want included: strong compatriot priority of the "my country first, full stop" variety, liberal nationalism (Miller-ish), associative or institutional-duty views, the view that partiality is permissible but never obligatory, and hard impartialist cosmopolitanism where borders have zero moral weight. But if your best tree puts one of those somewhere awkward or splits it into two, do that and explain why -- I'd rather the tree be right than match my list.

Be decisive about the hard design choices rather than presenting alternatives for me to pick between: choose the order of the questions, and for every unoccupied combination of answers say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, picking one. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't separate and tell me honestly which question, if you could add one, would pry them apart. And end with a short committed verdict: which of the leaf positions the tree itself implicitly favors (trees are never neutral about what they ask first), because I want to be able to tell my students upfront whether the handout is rigged.
