I facilitate a monthly discussion group for criminal-justice professionals — prosecutors, defense attorneys, a sentencing judge, two probation officers — and our next session is on the dispute between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over whether anyone truly deserves to be punished. We have ninety minutes, there is no assigned reading, and while the participants have strong practical convictions about sentencing, none has philosophical training beyond a dimly remembered college course. Please design my session plan: the architecture of the explanation, not just a list of talking points.

Specifically, I need five things. First, the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why each depends on the last. My own sense is that the notion of basic desert — responsibility that justifies suffering simply because it is deserved, with no eye to future benefits — has to be built carefully before either position is presented, since both men reject it and their real disagreement is over what to construct in its place; tell me whether that sequencing instinct is right, and what has to be in place before the distinction between backward-looking and forward-looking justifications will make sense to this audience. Second, the single case the session should be built around and re-run under each view: specify what the case must contain (my instinct is an adult offender, sane, acting voluntarily by everyday standards, genuinely dangerous) and, just as important, what it must lack — mental illness, coercion, a sympathetic biography — so that neither side gets an easy exit and the disagreement is forced to show itself. Third, the misconceptions this particular audience will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off: that abandoning desert means offenders get away with everything, that consequentialist blame leaves the door open to punishing the innocent when useful, that the quarantine model is a euphemism for the same prisons with softer signage, that Dennett must secretly be a retributivist, and that if no one is ultimately responsible there is no point holding trials at all. Fourth, the check for understanding that would tell me the idea has actually landed — I want participants to be able to locate the disagreement precisely, rather than filing it as a generic free-will debate, and to state the strongest objection each philosopher presses against the other (the worry that blameless detention either under-deters or must be made unpleasant enough to become punishment again, on one side; the worry that forward-looking blame cannot in principle be confined to the guilty, on the other) in terms the opposing side would accept as fair. Fifth, what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level — the metaphysics of determinism, the manipulation argument, the empirical literature on deterrence — and how to say so without making the session feel evasive.
