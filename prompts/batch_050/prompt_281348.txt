I'm in a uni society that plays Nomic, the game Peter Suber invented where changing the rules is itself a move, and we've got a running argument that's now spilling out of game nights. One of our members is a second-year law student and she keeps saying the game is basically a constitution in minature — that Suber designed it as a model of how legal systems change themselves, so whatever goes wrong in the game must go wrong in real law too. Her conclusion: the game proves every legal system needs at least one genuinely unamendable rule somewhere, because otherwise you're stuck choosing between paradox and an infinite regress of higher and higher rules, and she reckons our society's little written constitution (yes, we run the society itself under one, lol) is defective since literally all of it can in principle be amended. To back this up she quoted this from Section 3 of Suber's The Paradox of Self-Amendment (1990): "(1) If the rules of change cannot authorize their own change, then they are either immutable, or they may only be changed by a second set of such rules, which in turn must either be immutable or mutable only by a third set, and so on to an arbitrary halt or an infinite regress. (2) But if the rules of change can permit and structure the process of their own change, then they must permit their own limitation and repeal." She reads this as forcing the immutable-rules option unless you're happy to live with contradiction.

I want to push back, but properly rather than by vibes. Can you audit the analogy she's leaning on — a legal system is a game of Nomic? Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the players, the initial ruleset, Suber's two-tier immutable/mutable split, the judge rule, a move, winning (does a constitution even have a win condition?). Then where the mapping breaks. I'm especially suspicious about the fact that in the game we all sat down and agreed to play by the initial rules — what, if anything, is the legal-world equivalent of that agreement, and does the answer quietly presuppose some authority sitting outside the rules of the system itself? Also courts and enforcement, and the fact that real legal change seems to happen through interpretation, custom and desuetude, not just explicit voted amendments like our moves. Then the actual verdict: which of these disanalogies matter for her specific conclusion and which are just noise? Does "every constitution needs an unamendable core" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the game — which keeps chugging along fine even when players amend the amendment rules — actually cut against her? If Nomic is a bad model of constitutional self-amendment, is there a better analogy I could bring to the next session? Please write your answer in British English.
