I'm in a reading group (more like a couple friends and a lot of coffee, honestly) working through Book I of Aristotle's Politics, and last week we got stuck on the chapters about household management, exchange, and money -- the part where Aristotle distinguishes a "natural" kind of acquisition, which is limited by what the household actually needs, from an unnatural kind aimed at unlimited accumulation, with retail trade and especially usury on the bad side. We were reading the old Jowett translation, and earlier in Book I there's this sentence we kept circling back to: "When several villages are united in a single complete community, large enough to be nearly or quite self-sufficing, the state comes into existence, originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence for the sake of a good life."

Anyway, two people in the group got into a genuine argument over whether that sentence saves or sinks Aristotle's whole take on money, and I stayed out of it, so I want a referee. It went roughly like this. (1) Maya said the sentence is the key to the whole doctrine: communities exist to satisfy needs but persist for the sake of living well, which means acquisition has a built-in limit set by something outside itself, and money-making pursued for its own sake has no natural stopping point and so counts as "unnatural" in a perfectly coherent sense. (2) Dev shot back that the sentence actually undermines Aristotle: it's the state, not the household, that's described as self-sufficing, and no real polis is self-sufficient without importing and exporting, so exchange beyond one's own needs isn't some parasitic excess -- it's precisely how a "complete community" achieves the self-sufficiency Aristotle praises, which makes his hostility to retail trade contradict his own setup. (3) Maya replied that Dev is confusing quantities with purposes: Aristotle's line isn't about how much stuff crosses borders, it's about the telos of the activity, and a merchant importing grain the city needs is serving use, whereas the person whose goal is accumulation as such has made an instrument into an end -- which is exactly the mistake the "for the sake of a good life" clause lets you diagnose. (4) Dev's final move was that this rescue makes the doctrine untestable and weirdly psychologized, since two merchants doing literally identical things would differ only in their inner aim, and worse, it backfires on usury: Aristotle calls lending at interest the most unnatural of all because money breeds money, but a loan that finances someone's fishing boat plainly serves need, so by Maya's own motive-test some usury would be natural and Aristotle is just begging the question with the word "unnatural."

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or right-with-qualifications, and justify briefly -- e.g., does "originating in the bare needs of life... continuing for the sake of a good life" actually supply the kind of limit Maya says it does, and is Dev right that the state's self-sufficiency requires the very commerce Aristotle dislikes? Then say who is more right overall, and whether there's something both of them are missing -- for instance, whether Aristotle's "unnatural" is doing descriptive or normative work here, or whether the real limit he's gesturing at is set by the good life as an objective standard rather than by anyone's motives. Don't just split the difference; I want an actual verdict.
