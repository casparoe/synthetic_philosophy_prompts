I’m teaching a 75-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate course on creativity, with 18 students. We’ve just discussed Hofstadter’s knob-twiddling model: roughly, the idea that creative thinking often works by varying parameters of an existing concept while leaving others fixed, rather than conjuring something from nothing. I don’t want a lecture or a generic brainstorming exercise. Design one specific classroom game around transforming the familiar campus coffee break. The students should have to produce variations themselves and only afterward realize that the interesting choices were not merely what to change, but which features had to remain recognizable for the result still to count as a variation on that theme.

I’d like the game to build in a distinction between surface knobs, such as location, drinks, timing, or participants, and deeper knobs, such as its social function, who is included, what makes it enjoyable, and what would make it cease to be a coffee break at all. Give some teams a hidden constraint that locks one deep knob in place, and use a scoring system with what I’m calling a recognition tax: teams gain points for surprising transformations but lose points if the other students cannot tell that their result descended from the original coffee break. Ideally, teams will discover that wild novelty is easy, while satisfying novelty requires understanding which features of the theme are doing the real work. Please specify the exact setup, materials, team roles, rules, timeline, scoring, and example transformation cards, including at least one card that looks superficially creative but should probably score badly.

For the debrief, map particular likely moments in the game onto Hofstadter’s picture: parameter spaces, slippage between concepts, constraints, and why creativity can look both mechanical and mysterious. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially students treating this as a comedy contest or arguing that any arbitrary object can count if described cleverly, and how to handle those problems without stopping the game. End with a frank note about what the activity cannot show—for instance, whether all creativity really is variation on prior themes, or whether the game merely makes that model feel plausible. Commit to one runnable design, including the knobs, win condition, and debrief questions you think work best, rather than giving me a menu of alternatives; I want definite choices and reasons, not a survey of possible activities.
