I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and last week at a department happy hour a friend from the physics department hit me with the old line that philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds. Everyone laughed, I smiled weakly, and I've been stewing about it ever since. I suspect the quip is doing more work than it can legitimately do, but I can't quite pin down where it goes wrong. Can you lay out the different precise theses it could be making? I'm thinking the readings might vary along a few axes: what 'useful' is supposed to mean (instrumentally useful for producing results? useful for being a good scientist? useful enough to justify funding?), which parts of philosophy of science it covers (conceptual foundations, general methodology, metaphysics of physics?), and what the ornithology comparison actually implies (birds get along fine without ornithology, but then again ornithology was never *for* the birds). For each reading, say whether it's plausible, whether there's any real evidence for it, and whether it would actually support the dismissive use my friend put it to -- that working scientists can safely ignore philosophers entirely. I'd rather have a menu with verdicts than one reading developed at length.
