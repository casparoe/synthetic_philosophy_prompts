In "The Fixation of Belief" (1877), Peirce writes of Kepler that "perhaps his greatest service to science was in impressing on men's minds that this was the thing to be done if they wished to improve astronomy; that they were not to content themselves with inquiring whether one system of epicycles was better than another but that they were to sit down to the figures and find out what the curve, in truth, was." I am a PhD student in philosophy of science writing a paper on Peirce's four methods of fixing belief, and I want to test the passage's implicit claim — that what distinguishes the method of science is not individual brilliance but a shift in what a community treats as the thing to be done — against a real episode: the discovery that peptic ulcers are caused by Helicobacter pylori. Take the standard facts as given: the orthodoxy through the 1970s held ulcers to be caused by stress and excess acid; Robin Warren reported spiral bacteria in gastric biopsies in the early 1980s; Barry Marshall cultured the organism, had a paper rejected, and famously drank a culture himself in 1984, developing gastritis; antibiotic eradication subsequently cured most ulcers, and Marshall and Warren received the 2005 Nobel Prize. Please analyze the episode through Peirce's scheme. Which of the four methods best describes the ulcer orthodoxy — was it the a priori method (the belief was agreeable to reason, since no bacterium was thought able to survive stomach acid), authority, or something the scheme does not capture? What, on Peirce's account, was Marshall's self-experiment: an application of the method of science, an attempt to induce the irritation of doubt in others, or a rhetorical act that Peirce's epistemology cannot classify? And finally, where does the mapping strain: Peirce's method of science is communal and self-correcting only in the long run, yet the episode turned on one anomalous observation and a single dramatic act, and the eventual correction arguably owed as much to clinical frustration with recurring ulcers and to commercial interests as to any shared methodological commitment. Does the case confirm Peirce's picture, expose a blind spot in it, or show that the Kepler passage misdescribes how methods actually change?
