I am preparing a postgraduate philosophy-of-language podcast and will be interviewing a linguist who defends a normality-based account of generics. Roughly, she holds that ‘ducks lay eggs’ does not mean that all ducks, most ducks, or even only female ducks lay eggs; it says, defeasibly and relative to a contextually supplied standard, that the normal or characteristic ducks in the relevant respect do so. I understand the attraction of this approach when there are obvious exceptions, but I am less clear about how it avoids defining ‘normal’ as whatever makes the generic come out true, or why prevalence sometimes seems irrelevant: ‘mosquitoes carry malaria’ may be acceptable although only a small minority do, whereas ‘books are paperbacks’ sounds false despite the prevalence of paperbacks. I also want to explore whether one account can cover biological classifications, artefacts, social roles and potentially harmful generics such as ‘boys don’t cry’.

Draft twelve interview questions that press the guest beyond a restatement of her view. Include at least one question each on exceptions, low-prevalence generics, the difference between statistical normality and characteristic functioning, circularity in specifying relevant normality, contextual shifts in standards, and the contrast between descriptive and normative generics. For every question, add a concise explanation of the theoretical pressure it applies, the reply the guest is most likely to give, and a follow-up question that challenges that reply without merely repeating the original objection. The questions should be suitable for an informed but non-specialist audience and should use examples sparingly rather than simply multiplying them. Please write the response in American English.
