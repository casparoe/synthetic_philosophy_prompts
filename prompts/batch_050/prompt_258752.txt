The proposition is this: I, the very person I actually am, could have come into existence from a different sperm-and-egg pair than the pair I in fact came from. Note that this is stronger than the claim that a person qualitatively identical to me could have been born to other parents; it concerns numerical identity across a change of gametic origins. My own credence is roughly 0.15; a colleague's is roughly 0.8, and I want to locate the disagreement.

Open your reply with your own number in a single sentence. Then decompose it by assigning a rough probability to each of the following sub-questions, with a line of justification for each, and state how they combine into your final figure.

First, the probability that origins are essential to the identity of a human person at all — that some form of Kripkean necessity of origins holds for persons, rather than only for artefacts or natural-kind specimens.

Second, conditional on some origin-dependence holding, the probability that the identity-fixing entity is the sperm-and-egg pair rather than the zygote or some later developmental stage. Bear in mind two complications: a zygote can undergo monozygotic twinning, which suggests that no single person can be strictly identical to the zygote it developed from; and the sperm and egg arguably cease to exist at fusion, which sits awkwardly with gamete essentialism. This sub-question matters because weaker candidates for the identity-fixing stage leave more room for one and the same person to have had different gametes.

Third, the probability that the question has a determinate answer at all. A counterpart-theoretic treatment in the style of Lewis would recast de re modal claims about persons as context-dependent similarity claims, so that the original proposition comes out true under some counterpart relations and false under others, rather than being simply true or false.
