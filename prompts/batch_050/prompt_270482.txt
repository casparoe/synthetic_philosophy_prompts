I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting a column on whether morality can demand anything of people it has no motivational grip on. For it, I need a thought experiment built to order. Construct a scenario in which Bernard Williams's internal reasons theory and an external reasons theory deliver opposite verdicts about one specific agent and one specific action: the internalist must conclude that the agent has no reason to do the thing, the externalist that they do. Constraints: the agent must be a psychologically ordinary, decent person -- no sociopaths, fanatics, or amoralists -- and the setting must be everyday (a workplace, a family, a neighborhood), sketched in one paragraph. The hard part is this: Williams allows that a reason can be reached from the agent's existing motivational set by sound deliberation, so engineer the case such that every plausible deliberative route from the agent's actual motivations to the action is blocked, without making the agent irrational, ignorant, or deluded -- the blockage should fall out of the structure of the situation, not out of a defect in the person. After the scenario, show the work: state the agent's motivational set, walk through the two or three most plausible deliberative routes and explain why each fails, give each theory's verdict and why, name the single feature of the case doing the work, and say how a defender of Williams would most plausibly attack the construction. Maximum 700 words.
