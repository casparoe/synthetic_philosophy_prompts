I'm drafting an essay for an undergraduate political theory journal and would like critical comments before I revise. The essay is on John Stuart Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861), specifically the final chapter, "Of the Government of Dependencies by a Free State," read against the earlier chapters on the criterion of good government.

The essay's notes, in outline:

1. In Chapter II Mill argues that the goodness of a government consists largely in what it does to the people governed — how far it cultivates their active, intellectual, and moral capacities — and in Chapter III he concludes that self-government is best precisely because participation is educative. Passive subjects, on this view, are stunted citizens-in-waiting.

2. In Chapter XVIII Mill confronts the case of dependencies like India. He argues, in paraphrase, that a free people is generally unfit to govern a subject people directly: the home electorate neither knows nor cares about the dependency's affairs, will indulge its prejudices at the dependency's expense, and cannot be held to account by those it rules. His preferred remedy is rule through an intermediary professional body — his model being the East India Company — a permanent corps of specialists who know the country, are checked by the metropolitan government but insulated from its public opinion.

3. My essay's thesis: this produces an unresolved tension. The criterion of Chapter II evaluates government by its developmental effect on the governed, yet Mill's institutional solution for dependencies prioritizes expert insulation over the governed's participation, and he never specifies what would count as the dependency graduating to the self-government his own criterion ranks highest. The "trusteeship" has a trustee but no stated trust conditions — no test by which India would be deemed ready.

4. I then argue the tension can be partly, but only partly, dissolved: Mill's developmental criterion is forward-looking in a way that lets him treat expert rule as instrumentally justified *if* it is organized to raise the dependency's capacities (education, native employment in administration, local institutions), so that participation is deferred rather than denied. But I concede Mill gives no deadline, no metric, and no transfer mechanism, which makes the deferral structurally indistinguishable from permanent rule — and I claim this is a defect in the theory, not merely in Mill's application of it.

Please comment on the following, and be blunt:

- Interpretive accuracy: is the tension I construct in section 3 one the text actually contains, or am I importing a modern demand (an explicit decolonization criterion) that Mill's framework would not recognize as a fair test? If I've mischaracterized either the Chapter II criterion or the Chapter XVIII proposal, say where.
- The resolution in section 4: is the "deferred participation" reading textually sustainable, or does it paper over the possibility that Mill simply ranked security and good administration above participation for peoples he deemed unready, full stop?
- Overreach check: flag any claim in the outline that goes beyond what Mill wrote, especially anything I attribute to the Chapter XVIII argument that is really my own reconstruction.
- What is the single strongest objection to the essay's thesis, and which section should I rewrite to meet it?
