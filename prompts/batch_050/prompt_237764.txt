My pragmatics professor has this running bit where she classifies whatever anyone says in class as either conversational implicature, conventional implicature, plain what's-said, or nothing of interest. Her calls so far:

- "Can you pass the salt?" at dinner -- implicates a request, not a question about ability. Conversational.
- "I'm almost out of gas" / "There's a station just around the corner" -- implicates that it's open and actually sells gas. Conversational, and only works in that context.
- A reference letter for a philosophy job that reads, in full, "Smith's handwriting is lovely and his attendance record is spotless" -- implicates Smith is a terrible philosopher. Conversational.
- "She kicked off her shoes and flopped onto the bed" -- implicates the shoes came off first. Conversational.
- "Some of the students passed," said by a teacher to the dean after she's finished grading every single exam -- implicates that not all passed. Conversational.
- "He's an Englishman, and therefore brave" -- the bit about bravery following from Englishness is implicated but conventional, glued to the word "therefore" and not cancelable.
- "Even Maria passed the exam" -- that Maria was one of the least likely to pass is implicated, conventional, glued to "even."
- "It's raining," said flatly when someone asks what the weather's like -- nothing beyond what's said.

Next week she's promised us a stumper, and she's already leaked it: a teacher who has only graded about half the exams, and who has told the dean exactly that. The dean asks how it's going, and the teacher says, "Some of the students passed." Question: does she implicate that not all of them passed?

Predict the professor's verdict. The trouble is I can't tell which test she's been implicitly running, because every verdict above is consistent with at least two: (a) the cancellability test -- if the inference can be explicitly cancelled without contradiction, it's a conversational implicature, otherwise not; and (b) a calculability test where the hearer has to be able to reason via the maxims, which in the "some" case means reasoning like "if she knew they'd all passed she'd have said so" -- and that reasoning seems to break down when the hearer knows the speaker can't yet know the stronger thing. The new case is exactly where (a) and (b) come apart. So: give me your best prediction, say which rule you think she's actually running and why (feel free to argue it's a third rule I've missed), and design the one further verdict of hers -- a new case plus her call on it -- that would settle which test it is for good.
