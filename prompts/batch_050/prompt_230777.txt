I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been puzzling over something that came up at work. Our team was debating whether to build a prototype of a new distributed locking scheme, and a colleague argued we didn't need one: he just walked through the protocol step by step on a whiteboard, imagined two clients racing under network delay, and showed the scheme would deadlock. No code was run, no measurements were taken, yet everyone in the room came away treating his imaginary scenario as a genuine discovery about the scheme, not merely an opinion. This reminded me of famous cases from physics I half-remember from popular science books: Galileo allegedly refuting the view that heavier bodies fall faster by imagining a heavy and a light body tied together, and Einstein imagining what he would see if he could chase a beam of light. In none of these cases did anyone build anything or measure anything, yet the conclusions seem to have advanced actual science.

I've since encountered two broad ways of explaining how this is possible, and I'd like you to compare them. The first, more deflationary view says a thought experiment is just an ordinary argument wearing a narrative costume: everything in the conclusion was already contained in the premises plus background knowledge, and the imaginary scenario is only a rhetorically effective way of making the contradiction or entailment vivid, much like my colleague could in principle have written out his deadlock proof as dry logical steps instead of telling a story about two clients. The second, more generous view says thought experiments do more than repackage what we already know: by forcing our concepts into extreme or novel configurations, they expose tensions and possibilities that pure deduction from stated premises would never have surfaced, somewhat the way that actually running a program on an unexpected input teaches you things you could not have gotten by staring at the source code, because execution does work that mere inspection does not.

Please compare these two views in depth. In particular: which of them better explains why the Galileo falling-bodies case felt like a refutation rather than a mere restatement, and can the deflationary view account for the fact that thought experiments sometimes persuade experts who were already fully aware of the relevant premises? Also, as a test case, tell me which view better fits my colleague's whiteboard deadlock argument, given that it changed real engineering decisions, and whether the analogy to running code versus reading code actually supports the generous view or accidentally collapses into the deflationary one. Finally, give me your verdict: which view comes closer to explaining how thought experiments in science can yield knowledge, and what is the strongest objection your preferred view has to survive?
