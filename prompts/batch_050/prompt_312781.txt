Consider a logic tutor who is trying to get a stubbornly literal-minded student to draw the conclusion *Q* from two accepted premises: *P*, and *if P then Q*. The tutor's predicament is essentially the one dramatized in Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise: whenever the tutor adds another premise — for instance, *if (P and (if P then Q)) then Q* — the student cheerfully accepts that premise too, yet still declines to draw the conclusion, on the grounds that nothing yet *forces* the move from the stock of accepted propositions to the conclusion itself. The regress that results is familiar. What I want explained is the deeper diagnosis of what goes wrong, and what the viable exits look like, structured around the following three purported solutions and the question of whether any of them succeeds:

1. **The axiomatic solution.** Treat modus ponens as an axiom schema rather than a rule: add every instance of *(P ∧ (P → Q)) → Q* to the premise set. Explain why this conflates two different roles that a logical principle can occupy — being a truth *within* the system versus being a permission to *move between* truths — and why the regress shows that no finite (or even infinite) stock of premises can substitute for at least one genuinely operative rule of inference.

2. **The semantic solution.** Justify modus ponens by a metalanguage argument: show, from the truth table or the model-theoretic definition of the conditional, that whenever *P → Q* and *P* are true, *Q* is true. Explain the sense in which this argument itself proceeds by modus ponens in the metalanguage, and use this to articulate the distinction between *premise-circularity* (where the conclusion appears among the premises) and *rule-circularity* (where the conclusion is reached using the very rule whose legitimacy is in question). Is rule-circularity always vicious? Argue for your verdict, addressing the suggestion that rule-circular justifications can still be epistemically informative for a subject who follows the rule unreflectively but has not yet endorsed it reflectively.

3. **The meaning-constitution solution.** Hold that accepting *if P then Q* while refusing to infer *Q* from *P* is not merely irrational but betrays a failure to understand the conditional at all: modus ponens is constitutive of the meaning of "if...then." Explain how this move attempts to convert the regress into a reductio — the student who never draws the inference was never really affirming a conditional — and then explain the main pressure against it: that it threatens to make all logical disagreement impossible (anyone who rejects a rule simply means something different by their connectives), and that it seems to license "tonk"-style connectives defined by rules that permit inferring anything from anything.

Your answer should be an explanation, not a survey: develop each position carefully, state precisely where each one stands in relation to the original regress, and conclude by saying what kind of thing you take the justification of deduction to be, given that it cannot take the form of an ordinary deduction. Aim for a treatment that someone who already knows the Carroll dialogue will still learn from.
