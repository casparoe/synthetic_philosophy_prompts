I am a novelist in the early stages of a book set in the American Deaf community in the 1990s, at the height of the controversy over pediatric cochlear implants. My central family — Deaf parents, a deaf child — must decide whether to implant, and I want the intellectual spine of their conflict to be the equality-of-what debate rather than a generic clash of values. So: take the cochlear implant controversy as a real episode and analyze it through the three great metrics of egalitarian justice — welfare, resources, and capability. For each metric, tell me what it says deafness is: on the welfarist reading, is the case for implantation as straightforward as the hearing majority assumed, and what happens to that reading if a flourishing Deaf life reports high welfare? On a resourcist reading, does the implant figure as a correction of brute bad luck or as the destruction of an endowment the community values? On the capability reading, whose list of central capabilities is doing the work, and does the capability to communicate pick out spoken language specifically or language as such — because the whole dispute seems to live in that gap. Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the metrics themselves? Does the Deaf community's insistence that deafness is a difference rather than a deficit reveal that all three metrics quietly presuppose a standard human form of life, and if so, which metric survives that revelation best? Be precise about where each theory is silent rather than forcing a verdict; I need my characters to be right about different things.
