So I've recently ended up on the board of my housing co-op and we've hit a fight that made me think about moral compromise seriously for the first time. Two members have genuinely different values -- one thinks keeping dues low for poorer members is a matter of justice, another thinks deferring the building's energy retrofit is a betrayal of future generations. Both are right on their own terms, and the only way out seems to be some kind of trade where each side gives the other something it morally cares about in exchange for getting something it cares about more. I've been reading around about whether these "gains from moral trade" are actually coherent or whether compromising on values is just selling out with extra steps.

Here are the standard worries about moral compromise I've collected so far, none of which I need repeated:

1. The integrity objection -- treating your values as tradable goods erodes your character; someone willing to bargain on justice stops being the kind of person who cares about justice.
2. The complicity objection -- in a trade you end up actively helping the other side do something you think is wrong, and that wrongness now sticks to your hands in a way it wouldn't if you'd just lost the vote.
3. The non-fungibility objection -- economic gains from trade work because both parties want the same kind of thing (welfare, roughly), but values aren't commensurable that way, so there's no real sense in which both sides "gain."
4. The slippery slope objection -- once you accept that your values are on the table, you get picked off piecemeal; every round of bargaining leaves you a bit further from what you actually believe, and there's no principled stopping point.
5. The power objection -- compromises get struck where bargaining power dictates, so "gains from trade" is usually just a polite description of the stronger side getting most of what it wanted.

Is there a sixth objection that's genuinely different from these five? Not a rewording, not a special case (please don't just hand me "complicity but for institutions" or "slippery slope but faster"), and not just the observation that some particular compromise was a bad deal. Ideally something that would still apply even if the trade were fair, one-off, and left everyone feeling like winners. And once you've given it to me, tell me honestly whether you think it actually holds up, because I have a board meeting thursday and I'd like to know if I'm about to do something defensible.
