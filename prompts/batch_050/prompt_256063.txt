Some time ago I stumbled on the centipede game while reading about game theory on my own, and it has bothered me ever since. Two players take turns deciding whether to stop a growing pot of money or pass it to the other; passing increases the total at stake but means the other player now gets the chance to stop it and take the larger share, and the game ends automatically after some fixed, commonly known number of rounds. The standard analysis seems to say that the player in the last round would stop, so the player before that should stop first, and so on, until the whole thing unravels and the very first player ends the game immediately — a conclusion that feels both logically forced and absurd, since both players walk away with crumbs when a feast was on the table. I have been arguing about this with a friend who insists the unraveling argument is simply correct and that anyone who passes is making a mistake, but I suspect something more subtle is going on.

Here is what I would like: construct a modified version of this game in which two perfectly rational, purely self-interested players — no altruism in the payoffs, no mistakes, no trembling, no infinite horizon, no repetition, and no external enforcement — pass the pot back and forth for most of the game as the rational thing to do. The catch is that the modification must be a single, small, and realistic change to what the players know or believe about the situation (for instance, some sliver of uncertainty of the sort that exists in every real human interaction), not a change to the payoff structure or to the players' preferences, and it must not amount to secretly making one player irrational. Keep the game finite and keep the stakes ordinary — money between two strangers, not spy thrillers.

After presenting your game, do the full bookkeeping. First, restate briefly why backward induction unravels the original game. Second, walk through your modified game step by step and show exactly at which node the unraveling breaks and why, tracing the reasoning carefully enough that I can check each link. Third, identify the single structural feature doing the work, and tell me what happens as that feature is shrunk toward zero: does cooperation collapse all at once at some threshold, or erode gradually, and which behavior should I expect if the game were actually played? Fourth, steelman my friend: how would someone who takes the original backward-induction argument to be unanswerable attack your construction — for example, by claiming that your modification just relocates the puzzle rather than solving it — and what is the best reply? Finally, say in plain terms what your case shows about where the original paradox actually comes from: is the problem with backward induction itself, or with the assumption that every detail of the situation is common knowledge?
