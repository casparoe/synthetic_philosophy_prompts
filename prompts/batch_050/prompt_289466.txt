I'm a PhD student in intellectual history, and I run a reading group on the Enlightenment's legacy that has, over two semesters, ground to a halt on what I initially assumed was the central question: was the Enlightenment, on balance, a source of human progress or of new and more insidious forms of domination? The strange thing is that the group has not stalled for lack of agreement about the historical record. Everyone concedes that the same century that produced the Encyclopédie and the early abolitionist movements also produced scientific racism, the height of the Atlantic slave trade, and colonial expansion frequently justified in the language of reason and civilization. Everyone concedes the statistical improvements in life expectancy, literacy, and material welfare that the defenders emphasize. We have read the Frankfurt School critique, in which enlightened reason allegedly turns back into mythology and becomes an instrument of control; we have read Foucault's ambivalent late assessment; we have read Habermas's rejoinder that the critique of reason remains indebted to reason's own norms; and we have read recent unabashed defenses that treat the whole critical tradition as ingratitude toward the only historical current that ever improved anyone's life. Nobody has changed their mind, and I notice we keep having the same evening over and over.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are not actually answering the same question: one camp is asking about the internal logic of a set of concepts — whether the Enlightenment's drive to render everything calculable and controllable contains domination in germ — while the other is asking a counterfactual-causal question about historical credit and blame — whether things would have gone better or worse, for slaves, colonized peoples, and ordinary workers, had that intellectual current not existed. I also suspect a quieter disagreement about what even counts as the Enlightenment: a canon of texts and arguments, or a set of institutions and practices, such that one side exhibits the philosophes' own writings about slavery while the other exhibits the abolitionist movements those writings helped inspire, and each treats its exhibit as the true measure of the thing. But I may be flattering everyone with too charitable a taxonomy.

What I would like from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, not a verdict on the Enlightenment itself. Is this a genuinely substantive dispute, a partly verbal one in which both sides defend different referents under one name, a hidden empirical disagreement about historical causation and counterfactuals that neither side can settle with the evidence available, or a collision of starting intuitions — say, about whether ideas are to be judged by their best self-understanding or by the uses made of them — that neither side can argue for without begging the question? Please engage with what the two traditions actually claim rather than with caricatures, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, a class of historical evidence that could genuinely bear on it, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question itself is ill-posed. If you think my two-questions hypothesis is wrong or incomplete, say so and explain why. One request about form: write your answer as continuous prose, as if in a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
