At a memorial gathering last month, several people said of the deceased that she lives on in all of us, and a friend of mine who had just read Douglas Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop insisted that this is not merely a comforting figure of speech but something close to literally true. His argument ran roughly as follows: a self, on Hofstadter's view, is a pattern or symbolic structure rather than a substance, and since that pattern is partly constituted by habits, dispositions, turns of phrase, and ways of seeing that get absorbed into the minds of everyone who knew the person well, copies of the self -- admittedly coarse, low-resolution copies -- persist distributed across many brains after the original brain stops. He leaned heavily on an analogy: the self is like software, and a person's friends and family are like machines that have each been running imperfect partial installations of it, so death is less like the destruction of the program and more like losing the fastest machine it ever ran on.

I want to know how far this software analogy can actually be pushed before it breaks. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly -- what in Hofstadter's picture plays the role of the program, the hardware, the installation process, the running process, and the original machine -- and then identify where the mapping fails or goes silent. In particular, I would like you to address the disanalogies that seem most threatening to me: software can be copied bit-for-bit while a self-pattern presumably cannot; a program running on two machines is numerically one piece of software in a way that a person instantiated in two brains may not be; the surviving brains are not getting new input from the deceased, so whatever persists is a static snapshot rather than an ongoing loop; and it is unclear whether a coarse copy in someone else's head has anything like a first-person perspective at all, which seems central to what a self is supposed to be. Some of these may matter a great deal for my friend's conclusion and some may be superficial -- I want you to sort them rather than just list them.

The question I ultimately care about is whether Hofstadter's consolation survives the audit: once the disanalogies are on the table, is there still a defensible sense in which the dead person genuinely persists in others, or does the analogy merely dress up the ordinary fact that people are influenced by those they love? If you think the software analogy was quietly carrying the conclusion, say so, and tell me whether some better analogy (a musical work surviving in musicians' interpretations, a language surviving in scattered speakers, or something else) does more honest work. Finally, please indicate what would change your verdict: which consideration, if it turned out differently than you assume, would flip your assessment, and where in your reasoning you feel least secure.
