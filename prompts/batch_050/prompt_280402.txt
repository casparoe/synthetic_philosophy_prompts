I spent about forty years running instruments in a geochemistry lab -- retired now, reading epistemology for fun, possibly a mistake. In the lab we had a strict rule: you never certify an instrument using only its own readings. A pH meter that agrees with itself ten thousand times has told you nothing about whether it's right. You calibrate against buffer solutions with known values, you intercompare meters, you send things off to a standards lab, and at the top of that chain, as I understand it, they eventually run out of other instruments to check against and have to do something else entirely.

Now I keep bumping into what philosophers call the bootstrapping problem, or "easy knowledge," and it looks to me like exactly my old calibration rule, except that half the theories under discussion seem to violate it cheerfully. So what I want is the spec sheet. If a theory of epistemic justification is going to be adequate for this area, what must it be able to do? Give me the full list of requirements: the cases it has to get right (the woman with the gas gauge who concludes the gauge works, the fellow who knows the table is red and concludes it isn't white under red lights, whatever the other standard cases are -- spell them out), the distinctions it isn't allowed to blur (I'm guessing: using a source vs. having prior reason to trust it? justifying a belief vs. justifying reliance on the instrument that produced it?), and the bits of actual scientific practice it may not declare irrational -- because if a theory tells me metrology labs have being doing it wrong for a century, I know which of the two I'm keeping.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, so I can tell a real constraint from one camp's hobby horse. And I have two candidate requirements of my own I want vetted: (1) the theory must explain why intercomparing several instruments is legitimate practice while self-checking a single one is not, even though both look like "track record" reasoning of a sort; (2) it must say something sensible about the bottom of the calibration chain, where there is nothing left to calibrate against. If either of these is just the prejudice of a man who spent too long around pH meters, tell me.

Two further questions. Is there some subset of these desiderata that no theory could jointly satisfy? If the wish list is internally inconsistent, that's a result, and I'd rather have it stated plainly than smoothed over. And which single requirement does the most damage to the main contenders (reliabilism, evidentialism, whatever the current menu is) -- a quick scoring against the list is welcome, but the list itself is the main deliverable.

Last thing, and I mean it: keep it clear throughout what is actually settled in this literature and what is still genuinely fought over, and don't present some school's position as the consensus when the neighbors disagree. I've caught philosophy writing doing that a few times already. A table for the spec is fine.
