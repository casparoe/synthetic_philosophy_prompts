You are a decision theorist who has spent a decade advising philanthropic funders on when rigorous evaluation is worth its price. I chair the programs committee of a grantmaking foundation (roughly $30M disbursed annually), and I need a decision memo the committee can vote on at next Thursday's meeting.

The case. A nonprofit we have funded twice before runs a teacher-coaching program in two provinces of a lower-middle-income country. Its own monitoring data look strong, but the program has never had a controlled evaluation. It now proposes expanding to eight more provinces at $3.8M over three years, and asks us to fund an independently run randomized evaluation alongside the expansion at $420k — money that, spent directly, would instead reach roughly eleven thousand additional children. Two committee members have staked out positions. One argues we should fund program delivery only: if we trust the monitoring data enough to expand at all, we should expand fully rather than divert funds to measuring what we already believe. The other invokes Good's theorem — the expected value of free information is never negative — and calls the evaluation obviously worth funding, on the grounds that no one ever lost money learning something. Two wrinkles complicate this. Asked privately whether a null result would halt the expansion, the implementing partner said the ministry of education is enthusiastic and they expect to scale regardless of what the study finds. And two other funders are watching our vote and have signaled they will follow our lead.

Some context on me: I have read rationalist blogs and decision-theory forums for fifteen years and know the standard value-of-information arguments. Do not re-derive expected utility for me. What I need is the theorem's premises audited against this case, not the jargon repeated.

The memo should do four things. First, state Good's theorem precisely enough to identify its premises: perfect Bayesian updating, a decision that is genuinely made after the information arrives, a decision-maker who chooses to maximize expected utility under the posterior, information that is free, and a single agent whose payoff does not depend on what others believe. Second, check each premise against the facts above — in particular the implementer's stated intention to scale regardless; the fact that our committee's own decision rule is ours to set, so the theorem may apply to our choice even if it cannot apply to theirs; the fact that this information is not free; and the theorem's silence about effects on third parties such as the other funders and the ministry, who are not ideal Bayesian agents and may misread a low-powered null. Third, give a clear recommendation: fund the evaluation, decline it, or fund it with stated conditions (specify the conditions — e.g., a pre-committed decision rule, a power analysis, continued funding tied to results). Fourth, present the strongest version of the losing side's argument and explain why it does not change your recommendation.

Before drafting, spend a short paragraph brainstorming: list the premises, the ways each could fail here, and the two or three considerations that should drive the recommendation. Then write the memo itself, addressed to the committee, in under 800 words, in a register appropriate to a formal funding decision.

End your response with a final line of exactly one of these forms: \answer{FUND}, \answer{FUND WITH CONDITIONS}, or \answer{DECLINE}.
