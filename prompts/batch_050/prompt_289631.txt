I am setting the agenda for a two-year, six-person policy research group examining whether new nuclear power should play a significant role in decarbonising European electricity systems. The group can undertake conceptual analysis and systems modelling, but not engineering development or new public-opinion fieldwork. Use Germany’s completed nuclear exit and France’s continued reliance on a large reactor fleet as contrasting test cases, not as simple success and failure stories.

To prevent different disputes from being conflated, provisionally distinguish three levels of analysis:

- the asset level: whether an existing reactor should be closed, life-extended, or replaced;
- the programme level: whether a state can repeatedly build reactors at predictable cost and schedule;
- the system level: what value nuclear generation has within a grid increasingly shaped by variable renewables, storage, interconnection, and demand flexibility.

Within that framework, rank the following open questions according to what the research group should prioritise:

A. How should the marginal value of firm low-carbon generation be estimated as renewable penetration rises, particularly during rare but correlated periods of low wind, low solar output, and high demand?

B. How should system costs be allocated when nuclear, wind, and solar each impose different requirements for balancing, transmission, redundancy, and curtailment, and when high renewable output reduces the utilisation of capital-intensive plants?

C. To what extent can repeated reactor construction generate learning effects, and under what institutional conditions does a fleet become a standardised programme rather than a series of one-off megaprojects?

D. How should low-probability, high-consequence risks, long-lived waste, and proliferation concerns be compared with the more diffuse and cumulative risks of climate change and fossil-fuel dependence without double-counting or making the comparison rhetorically predetermined?

E. Which institutional arrangements make consent, siting, compensation, and long-term waste governance politically durable, and does centralised nuclear power face fundamentally different legitimacy problems from distributed renewable generation?

F. How materially will hotter summers, cooling-water constraints, and other climate impacts affect reactor reliability and cost, and are these effects best treated as plant-level risks or as correlated system risks?

Please identify the four questions that most deserve the group’s time and defend the ordering using explicit criteria: decision relevance, tractability for conceptual and modelling work, likely neglectedness, and the degree to which other questions depend on its answer. Where one of the questions is commonly discussed but badly formulated, reformulate it rather than merely ranking it. Also identify one question outside your top four that appears important but is, on reflection, overrated or downstream of another issue.

Be explicit about uncertainty. State what evidence or argument would most plausibly change your top ranking, where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and whether the asset–programme–system distinction survives scrutiny. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or a general literature survey.

Conclude with a final line in exactly this form, substituting your chosen labels in order of priority:

\ranking{A > B > C > D}
