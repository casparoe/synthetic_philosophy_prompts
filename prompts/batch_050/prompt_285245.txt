So my mate and I got into it last night over Sartre — bad faith specifically — and we ended up properly stuck, so I want this settled by someone who isn't either of us. Her line is that bad faith is basicly diagnosable from the outside: watch how someone talks about their life and you can tell whether they're treating themselves as a thing with a fixed nature. My line is that's rubbish, and that on Sartre's actual view two people could live externally identical lives where one is in bad faith and the other isn't, because bad faith is about how you relate to your own facticity and transcendence, not about anything an observer could clock.

So: construct a thought experiment that settles it, one way or the other. Two characters whose observable lives are the same — same job, same routines, same things they'd say down the pub about why they do what they do — but where a careful application of Sartre's account gives different verdicts: one in bad faith, one not. Constraints:

- Realistic and contemporary. No amnesia plots, no estranged twins, nothing that reads like a parlour trick.
- Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph, then analyse.
- The difference between them can't just be that one secretly believes he's free and the other doesn't — that builds the verdict in by stipulation and my friend will (rightly) call it cheating. The difference has to fall out of how each one organises his relation to what he is — his past, his body, his role — versus what he's projecting himself towards. Ideally the reader genuinely can't tell which is which until you explain it.
- The character in bad faith shouldn't be a coward or a weasel. Sartre's waiter is perfectly likeable. Make mine decent, even admirable.

After the scenario: walk through the verdict for each character and why the framework delivers it, say which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if you strip that feature out. Then be honest about the other side — tell me where my friend's attack would bite hardest, i.e. where the "externally identical" claim is most likely to break down, and what argument or consideration would change your verdict on whether the case actually meets the spec. If it turns out the two can't be kept externally identical without smuggling the difference in, say so rather than papering over it.

Answer in British English, and don't bother with chapter-and-verse quotations from Being and Nothingness — argue from the structure of the view itself.
