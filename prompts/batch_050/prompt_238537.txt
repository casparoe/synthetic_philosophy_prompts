I'm designing the problem sets for a graduate seminar I'll be co-teaching on decision theory, and I keep running into a mismatch between my own gut reactions to choice problems and the tidy theoretical treatments in the readings. I'd like you to act as a decision theorist and help me figure out what principle, if any, is actually driving my verdicts. Please think carefully and brainstorm several candidate principles before settling on one -- I care more about the honest fit than about landing on a famous theory. Here are my verdicts, as raw as I can report them. (A) An urn has 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in a proportion nobody knows; one ball will be drawn. I'd rather bet $100 on red than on black, and I'd rather bet on black-or-yellow than on red-or-yellow. (B) Someone offers me a coin from the back of a drawer that might be biased in either direction, and I can bet $100 on whichever side I name, or I can bet $100 on heads with a coin I know to be fair. I am genuinely indifferent, and the possibility that the drawer coin is rigged against me doesn't move me once I've picked my side. (C) Choosing between two medical treatments: one with a well-studied success rate of about 70%, and a newer one whose true success rate experts put somewhere between 50% and 90% with very little data behind it. I strongly prefer the well-studied treatment, and I'd keep preferring it even if the new one's range narrowed to 55-85%. (D) Two charity raffles: one where exactly 10 of 1,000 tickets win, one where the organizers admit somewhere between 5 and 15 of 1,000 win but won't say how many. I mildly prefer the second raffle -- I realize this sits awkwardly with (A) and (C). (E) Two job offers at firms whose chances of major layoffs within five years are, respectively, well estimated at 20% and estimated at somewhere between 10% and 30%. I prefer the first firm, and noticeably more strongly than the midpoint arithmetic seems to warrant.

What I want from you: state precisely the principle (or at most two rival principles) that best captures this pattern -- whether it's something like maximizing the worst-case expected utility over a set of priors, a second-order probability with a non-neutral attitude toward the distribution of chances, an attitude that varies by the source of the ambiguity, or something else -- and identify which specific verdict motivates each clause of your principle. Cases (A) and (B) seem to me the hardest to reconcile, and (D) seems to embarrass any uniformly ambiguity-averse reading, so tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which one I should be most willing to give up. Lead with your bottom-line answer in the first sentence -- the principle you'd bet on, or the verdict that no single principle fits -- then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my cases. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which further case of mine, if I reported a different verdict on it, would most forcefully point to a rival principle, and where your reconstruction of me is most likely to go wrong.
