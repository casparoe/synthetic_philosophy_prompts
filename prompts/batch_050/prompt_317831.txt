I lead the oversight team at a mid-size AI research lab. Our setup is a recursive-decomposition pipeline for answering hard questions: when a task is too difficult for a single contractor to answer reliably, it gets split into sub-questions, those get answered (often by a fast model trained to imitate the full pipeline's past outputs, i.e. distilled from it), and a human at the top combines the sub-answers into a final answer. New subject-matter teams keep asking to onboard their task families onto this pipeline — roughly two or three proposals a month — and right now go/no-go decisions are made by whoever happens to be in the meeting, based on intuition.

I want you to turn the underlying theory of iterated amplification and distillation into a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run on each proposal, in under an hour, without needing to have read the original research agenda. Concretely:

1. Give the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests or questions. For each, state what counts as a passing answer, what evidence the proposing team must supply, and whether failure is a hard stop or a flag for human judgment. The procedure should make explicit use of the distinctions the theory says matter — for example: whether the task has the property that checking an answer is easier than producing one (and what happens to the pipeline when it doesn't); how errors compound as decomposition depth grows and where the bottleneck sits (the leaf answers, the sub-question decomposition step, or the human aggregation at the root); whether breaking the task apart changes what is being asked, so the pieces no longer sum to the original question; and what the distillation step can silently drop, since the small model imitates the pipeline's outputs but not its reasoning, and may fail on inputs unlike those in its training distribution.

2. Mark clearly where the procedure hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values or irreducible judgment, not something a checklist can settle.

3. Say what the procedure cannot do: which failure modes it will systematically miss (I am thinking here of cases where sub-answers are individually plausible but jointly misleading, or where the distilled model's confident errors are correlated rather than independent), and what monitoring we'd need outside the procedure itself.

4. Run the procedure on these three proposals as worked examples: (a) summarizing a scientific literature, where every factual claim in a sub-answer carries a citation a reviewer can spot-check; (b) verifying the correctness of a formal mathematical proof by decomposing it lemma by lemma; (c) producing balanced assessments of contested geopolitical questions, where there is no ground truth and the decomposition itself must choose what the relevant sub-questions are. I expect the procedure to reject at least one of these — likely (c), which is also the proposal our leadership most wants approved — and I want the rejection to be stated in terms of which specific test it fails, so I can defend the decision internally.

Assume the reviewer running this has technical competence but no background in the alignment literature, and that every decision needs to be sign-off-able by a non-specialist program director.
