Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Iris and Tomas, who both admire Bertrand Russell's Marriage and Morals (1929) but read it in sharply opposed ways. Iris takes the book's central lesson to be emancipatory: traditional sexual ethics, she argues, rested on superstition, fear, and the subordination of women, and once contraception and female independence have removed the old practical grounds for taboo, consensual sexual conduct between adults is nobody's moral business but their own. Tomas counters that Iris has read only half the book. He points to Russell's insistence that marriage and parenthood engage something deeper than passing pleasure, his claim that jealousy is a powerful and partly legitimate emotion rather than a mere hangover from property-thinking, and his view that a society of casual, childless liaisons would leave most people emotionally starved. On Tomas's reading, Russell is not a libertine but a reformer of marriage who thought sexual freedom worth having largely because it makes genuine, non-possessive lifelong love possible.

Their conversation should turn on a concrete disagreement: whether Russell's own framework can condemn a couple who, with full honesty on both sides, keep their marriage open and agree never to have children. Iris says Russell's principles leave no grounds for objection once deceit and harm are absent; Tomas argues that Russell's stress on children, jealousy, and the difference between profound and trivial attachments gives his position real critical bite even against fully consensual arrangements. Both speakers should be equally sharp and charitable. Have them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than restate prepared positions, let each concede at least one point, and end the dialogue at a moment of genuine, unresolved tension rather than agreement.
