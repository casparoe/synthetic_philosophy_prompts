Hospitals and courts draw a pair of distinctions that puzzle many observers. A physician may give a terminally ill patient escalating doses of morphine, intending to relieve pain, even when it is practically certain that the medication will hasten the patient's death; but the same physician may not give an injection intended to end the patient's life, even at the patient's explicit request and even where the dose would be smaller and the outcome identical. Likewise, a physician may withdraw a ventilator and let the patient die, but may not smother the patient to reach the same result on the same schedule. Professional codes and, in most jurisdictions, the law endorse both distinctions, often under the label of the doctrine of double effect.

What does this practice presuppose about the moral relevance of intention, and about the difference between acting and omitting? Reconstruct the implicit theory, then test it. First, does the pattern of permissions actually require an intent/foresight distinction, or could the same pattern be defended on other grounds — the institutional risks of licensing intentional killing, the counterfactual question of whether the physician would still act if death somehow did not occur, or a view about what an agent's plan treats as a means to its end? Second, is the presupposed view even coherent, given that agents can often redescribe their intentions so that one and the same upshot figures as a means under one description and a side effect under another? Third, say what would have to change in clinical practice and law if the implicit theory were replaced by (a) a view on which only outcomes and valid consent matter, or (b) a view on which intention matters but the counterfactual test just proposed is the wrong way to identify what an agent intends.
