I chair the academic integrity board at my university, and I need a written opinion I can circulate before we vote on a case that our current policy doesn't cleanly cover.

The facts: a master's student submitted a thesis containing five references that do not exist and two real papers cited for findings they do not contain. Every one of these errors traces to output from a large language model the student used while drafting. The student admits to using the model, admits to not verifying its outputs, and insists he never intended to deceive anyone. Nothing else in the thesis is fabricated; his data and his analysis check out.

Our policy defines academic dishonesty as the knowing misrepresentation of sources or results, and prescribes sanctions calibrated to intent: deliberate fabrication is the most serious tier, negligent failure to verify sources is a lesser tier, and honest error is no violation at all. The board is split three ways. One faction says that submitting invented references is fabrication full stop, since the text the student signed his name to contains falsehoods presented as scholarship. A second says there was no intent to deceive, so at worst this is negligence. A third faction, invoking Harry Frankfurt's distinction between lying and bullshit, argues that the case fits neither of our categories: the LLM's output is not a lie because nothing in it aims at representing truth or falsehood at all, and the student's offense is that he transmitted text with a comparable indifference to whether it was true — and that this indifference, in a piece of academic writing, is itself a distinct kind of misconduct our policy doesn't yet name.

You are a philosopher with long experience advising institutional bodies on cases where the conceptual categories do the real work. Think carefully, and begin by mapping the space of available classifications before committing to one. In particular, work through whether Frankfurt's distinction (lying as truth-sensitive deception, bullshit as indifference to truth, error as failed aim at truth) applies cleanly here, and to whom it applies: the model, the student, or the document. Consider whether indifference can be attributed to a person who sincerely claims he trusted the tool, whether trusting a system one knows to be unreliable is a form of indifference, and whether the student's signing and submitting the text changes the classification of what the model produced.

Then draft the opinion itself, no more than about 800 words, containing: (1) a clear recommendation on which tier of violation, if any, the student committed, with reasons; (2) an honest and specific treatment of the strongest argument for the most serious competing classification, and why it should nonetheless lose; (3) a recommended sanction consistent with your classification; and (4) a one-paragraph policy amendment the board could adopt so the next such case doesn't require this much philosophy. The opinion should be usable as written — a document the board could vote on, not an essay about the problem.
