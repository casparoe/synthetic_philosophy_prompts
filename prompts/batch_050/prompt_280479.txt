Three months ago I became a naturalized citizen, and at the ceremony I swore an oath to support and defend the constitution and laws of my new country. The oath moved me more than I expected, and it has left me with a question I cannot put down: what exactly did I thereby undertake, and what, if anything, could release me from it? That question has brought me back to Edward Snowden. Please analyze the 2013 disclosures through the lens of political obligation, in three stages.

First, run the case through the three standard grounds of political obligation: consent, fair play, and the natural duty of justice. Consent theory seems most directly implicated. Snowden swore to support and defend the Constitution and signed secrecy agreements as conditions of his employment and clearance. Does an oath to an institution bind when the oath-taker judges the institution to be violating the constitution the oath names? Is there a hierarchy among the things one has promised, or does consent theory simply generate a contradiction here? Fair play says that whoever accepts the benefits of a cooperative scheme must obey its rules: does that principle even grip a contractor, and does it survive the discovery that part of the scheme was concealed from most of its participants? And if obligation rests instead on a natural duty to support just institutions, does the whole case collapse into a judgment about whether the surveillance apparatus is a just institution — in which case the theory tells us almost nothing we did not already believe?

Second, test the case against the standard Rawlsian criteria for civil disobedience: the act must be public, nonviolent, and conscientious; performed within fidelity to law; addressed to the majority's sense of justice; and accompanied by willingness to accept the legal penalty. Snowden planned covertly, left the jurisdiction rather than face trial, and the disclosures unfolded over years. Say which criteria he plainly meets, which he plainly fails, and — more interestingly — which criteria force us to admit that the framework was built for a different kind of case: domestic protest against visible laws, not exposure of secret programs whose contested lawfulness was itself part of the secret.

Third, reverse the direction. What does the episode show about the theories? Does it reveal that consent theory has no resources for conflicts between a promise and the values the promise serves? Does Rawls's account presuppose a nearly just society with an open forum, so that whistleblowing about clandestine state activity falls outside its jurisdiction — and is that a principled silence or a damning gap? Finally, if the disclosed programs were themselves unlawful, does that change the analysis or merely its rhetoric?

I am less interested in a verdict on Snowden the man than in knowing which theory, if any, describes the obligation I took on this spring, and what it would take for that obligation to lapse.
