I help allocate grants for a small philanthropic foundation, and over the past year I've been trying to write down the distributive principles that actually drive our decisions, partly to check them against what I've been reading in distributive ethics and partly because our board keeps asking why we fund what we fund. Here is roughly where I've landed, and I'd like to know whether the position hangs together before I put it in a strategy document.

First, I'm a prioritarian: the moral importance of a benefit depends on the absolute well-being level of its recipient, counting for more the worse off that person is, with no reference to how anyone else happens to be doing. Second, I take the levelling-down objection to be decisive — not only against strict egalitarianism but against any alleged value that would be improved by making the better-off worse off whilst helping no one; as I see it, nothing can be even pro tanto good if achieving it is good for nobody. Third, I hold a person-affecting principle for choices amongst a fixed population: one outcome cannot be better than another unless it is better for someone. Fourth, I think complaints of comparative unfairness are genuine and not reducible to facts about absolute levels — when two equally deserving people end up unequally well off through sheer luck, something has gone wrong that isn't captured by how well off each of them is, and the complaint survives even in cases where nothing can be done about it. Fifth, I regard the priority weights as gradual rather than lexical: in principle, a sufficiently large benefit to someone well off can outweigh a smaller benefit to someone badly off. Sixth, in our actual grant-making I find myself unwilling to trade at almost any rate — I would rather secure a modest benefit for the very poorest than a benefit many times larger for the moderately well-off, and I cannot identify the ratio at which I'd switch.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture on distributive ethics. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each one say precisely what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. If you think one of the apparent tensions dissolves on inspection, show why rather than just asserting it. I'd also like to know which single revision would clean up the most of the set. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in each of your diagnoses, and single out the point in your analysis where you are least sure of yourself.
