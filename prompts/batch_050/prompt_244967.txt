I teach an undergraduate seminar on distributive justice (about 18 students, one 75-minute session), and I'd like you to design an in-class activity that makes participants feel the force of the utility monster objection to aggregative consequentialism — not just understand it on paper.

Here is the specific experience I want the activity to produce. Students should begin by adopting, as a group, a decision rule they mostly endorse: something like "allocate our resources so as to maximize the total good in the room." The rules of the activity should then make one participant's good systematically easier to produce than everyone else's — a conversion rate, an appetite, a capacity that grows or dwarfs the others' — so that following the group's own rule requires funneling more and more to that one participant while everyone else gets squeezed. I want the moment where the majority realizes they are being outvoted by their own principle, and where the "monster" realizes that the rule everyone agreed to has made them the villain. Ideally the squeeze should emerge from choices the students made themselves earlier in the activity, so that the objection feels like something they built rather than something I announced.

Please include:

1. Complete rules and materials, including a payoff or point scheme that requires no real money but gives students something they actually care about optimizing (small real stakes are fine — e.g., something that affects a trivial but real outcome like who chooses the seminar's break snack).
2. A timeline for the 75 minutes, including how to introduce the decision rule without naming the objection or telegraphing the trap.
3. The debrief: a mapping from specific moments in the activity to the structure of the objection (the asymmetry in capacity for good, the impartial aggregation rule, the intuition that the resulting distribution is unjust even though it maximizes the total), and then a guided tour of where each step can be resisted — e.g., denying interpersonal comparability, switching to average or prioritarian rules, building in constraints or rights — and what each resistance costs.
4. A candid note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g., students colluding to spite the monster, the monster refusing to play along, the point scheme feeling too artificial for anyone to feel the pull), and how to handle each failure mode.
5. An honest account of which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, that points in a game are not welfare, or that real monsters are alleged to exist only in thought experiments — and what I must supply in discussion to bridge the gap.

Finally, where your design or debrief touches on the philosophical literature (e.g., the origin of the example, which theories it is usually taken to threaten, whether it is generally regarded as a decisive objection or merely an intuition pump), please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested verdict as the consensus.
