I spent forty years as an entomologist and only got around to moral philosophy after retiring, so bear with me. I've been going by my gut on how much different creatures' suffering counts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, I'm actually operating on.

Here are my verdicts. An elephant in pain vs a mouse in what looks like the same pain: the elephant's seems to matter more. A parrot vs a cow: the parrot matters at least as much, even though a cow's brain dwarfs a parrot's — and I've read birds pack neurons in far denser than mammals anyway, so maybe sheer size was the wrong variable to begin with. An octopus, most of whose neurons are in its arms: its suffering seems to matter a lot. A honeybee — an old colleague showed me some learning experiments that genuinely rattled me — maybe a little. A person born with an unusually small brain: not one iota less than anyone else, full stop. And a hypothetical that keeps nagging me: an AI with more computational units than a human brain has neurons. I feel nothing for it, but I can't say why.

The hard pair, as I see it: elephant over mouse suggests brain scale matters; parrot equal to cow, and the small-brained person, suggest it doesn't — or only between some thresholds and not within them. The octopus muddies things further, since there's plenty of neural tissue but organized in a totally alien way.

So: what principle captures most of these? Which of my verdicts does a cleaner principle force me to drop? And what does it say about cases I haven't listed — a blue whale vs a human; a pig vs a human infant; a chicken given a dog-sized brain but otherwise unchanged; a giant tortoise that does everything, perhaps including suffering, very very slowly?

One more thing. If the answer ends up being richness of experience rather than any anatomical number, be honest about whether richness is even measurable in principle, or whether we've just kicked the bump under the rug. Four decades of fieldwork taught me to distrust quantities nobody can operationalize.
