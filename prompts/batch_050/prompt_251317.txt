I teach a two-hour evening philosophy class for adults with no formal training in the subject, and our next session is on the epistemology of testimony: when, and on what grounds, we are entitled to believe what other people tell us. I want to structure the session around the dispute between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimonial justification, anchored on the following passage from Section X, Part I of David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding:

"To apply these principles to a particular instance; we may observe, that there is no species of reasoning more common, more useful, and even necessary to human life, than that which is derived from the testimony of men, and the reports of eye-witnesses and spectators. This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word. It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses."

What I want is a teaching plan, not the lecture itself. Please give me the following, in this order:

First, the order of ideas and its rationale. Hume's passage compresses a chain of dependencies: the pervasiveness of testimony, the claim that our assurance rests on observed conformity between reports and facts, and the implied reduction of testimonial warrant to perception, memory, and induction. Tell me which of these must be felt by the students before the next can be stated, and why. In particular, advise me on where to place the circularity pressure point -- the question of how anyone could establish the "usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses" without relying on other people's testimony -- given that raising it too early makes reductionism look like a blunder and raising it too late makes it feel like a trick.

Second, the carrying example. Propose the single case the session should keep returning to, and justify its features: I suspect it should be unremarkable everyday testimony rather than courtroom evidence or miracle reports, and it should be a case where the reductionist's track record of "observed conformity" is at least arguably absent, so that the anti-reductionist alternative gets a fair hearing. Explain what the example must have and what it must lack.

Third, the misconception map. List the misunderstandings I should expect, in the order they will surface, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Anticipate at least these: that reductionism is the claim that testimony is generally unreliable; that anti-reductionism is the claim that one should simply take people at their word; that the dispute concerns how often people lie rather than what justifies belief; and a particular misreading of the passage, namely that Hume's "observation of the veracity of human testimony" means each hearer has personally compiled a track record for the speaker at hand.

Fourth, a short gloss I can give on the two load-bearing phrases -- "no other principle than our observation" and "usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses" -- that is faithful to Hume without importing the technical vocabulary, and a note on whether the passage commits Hume to checking testimony as a whole or only piecemeal.

Fifth, a check for understanding: something the students should be able to do by the end that would show the distinction has landed rather than the slogans been memorized -- ideally a task requiring them to state each position in terms a holder of the other would accept, and to say what sort of case would embarrass each.

Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to say openly that I am leaving out.

The plan itself should run to no more than 900 words, written in American English; the Hume passage does not count toward that limit.
