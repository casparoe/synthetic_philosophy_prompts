Consider three independence referendums from the last three decades, with the salient facts as follows. In Quebec in 1995, a referendum on sovereignty failed narrowly, roughly 50.6 percent voting no against 49.4 percent yes, in a process the Canadian federal government contested but did not physically prevent; afterward the Supreme Court of Canada held that Quebec had no unilateral right to secede under Canadian or international law, but that a clear majority on a clear question would create an obligation on the rest of Canada to negotiate. In Scotland in 2014, the UK government agreed in advance to the terms of a referendum, the no side won by about 55 to 45, and the result was accepted by all parties without incident. In Catalonia in 2017, the regional government held a referendum that Spain's constitutional court had declared illegal; turnout was roughly 43 percent amid a police crackdown, about 90 percent of votes cast were for independence, the regional parliament subsequently issued a declaration of independence, and Madrid responded by suspending regional autonomy and prosecuting or driving into exile the movement's leaders.

Analyze these three episodes through the lens of the two main families of normative theories of secession. The first family, remedial-right theories, holds that a group has a right to secede only as a remedy for serious injustice -- grave human-rights violations, broken agreements, or the unjust annexation of its territory -- and that absent such injustice, borders should remain as they are. The second family, primary-right or choice theories, holds that a territorially concentrated group whose members democratically express a settled wish to govern themselves has a right to secede without needing to prove any injustice at all, subject perhaps to procedural conditions.

Work the mapping in both directions, and be disciplined about it. First, run each episode through each theory: which, if any, of the three cases counts as permissible secession on each view, and what exactly does the work in the verdict -- the absence of grave injustice, the clarity of the majority, the prior consent of the host state, or the legality of the process? Second, take up two complications the standard statements of these theories tend to leave underdescribed. One is the recursive-minority problem: in 1995 the Cree of northern Quebec and much of anglophone Montreal made clear they did not wish to leave Canada, and any theory that grants a majority of Quebecers the right to take those communities out of Canada seems to license the same logic in reverse, yet a theory that licenses the recursion seems to make secession impossible in principle. Say what each theory should actually say about such sub-minorities, and whether the answer generalizes or is merely convenient. The other is the bootstrapping problem posed by Catalonia: on a remedial-right view, Catalan secessionists presumably lacked the right before October 2017, but if the crackdown, the suspension of autonomy, and the prosecutions constitute serious injustice, the right may have come into existence as a consequence of the state's response to the attempt. Does this show that remedial-right theories are unstable -- that they hand repressive states a dilemma between permitting lawful secession and manufacturing the grievance that justifies it -- or can the theory be formulated to forestall this result without collapsing into a choice theory?

Finally, reverse the direction of fit. The Scottish case went smoothly precisely because the host state consented, which neither theory treats as central: remedial theories focus on injustice, choice theories on the seceding group's will, and neither has much to say about why an Edinburgh-style negotiated process produced the most legitimate outcome of the three. Do these episodes, taken together, suggest that both families are missing a variable -- something about process, reciprocity, or the host state's standing -- and if so, sketch what a theory would look like that puts that variable at the center, and identify which of the three episodes poses the hardest test for your sketch.
