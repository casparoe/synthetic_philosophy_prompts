I've been chewing on Benacerraf's dilemma lately -- the problem of squaring a normal face-value semantics for math (numbers as objects, theorems as plain old truths) with any halfway naturalistic story about how we could know those truths, given that abstract objects don't cause anything. Instead of attacking the dilemma head-on, I want to figure out what I actually think mathematical knowledge is by going off my gut on cases, and then see what the gut verdicts add up to.

My verdicts:

1. I know that 7+5=12. Knowledge, obviously.
2. A kid who memorized the times tables but freezes on word problems still knows that 6x8=48. Thin knowledge, but knowledge.
3. A grad student who believes the four color theorem because she read that the proof checks out and she trusts the journals: knowledge, even though she's never read a line of the proof.
4. Same for me and Fermat's Last Theorem -- I believe it entirely on expert say-so and I'm pretty sure I know it.
5. Someone in 1850 who believes Goldbach's conjecture because it's held for every even number anyone bothered to check: not knowledge, even if it turns out to be true.
6. A set theorist who believes the continuum hypothesis is false because the alternative just feels more natural: not knowledge.
7. Ramanujan-type case: a guy believes some gnarly identity because it came to him in a dream, and other people later verify it. After the verification he knows it, but the night before he didn't.
8. A student proves a lemma from a false premise she sincerely believes, the lemma happens to be true anyway, and she correctly derives a real theorem from it: she doesn't know the theorem (or the lemma), even though everything she ends up asserting is true. Gettier cases exist in math, fight me.
9. A cryptographer verifies a zero-knowledge proof that some theorem holds, so she's certain to within astronomical probability but has never seen anything like a proof and couldn't produce one: I say she knows the theorem.
10. No single human has surveyed the entire classification of finite simple groups -- it's spread over hundreds of papers by dozens of authors. I think the community knows it, and each expert working in the area knows it, even though no skull contains the whole thing.

Here's what I want. First, is there one account of mathematical knowledge that delivers all ten verdicts, or at least nine? State the principle precisely and tell me which case motivates each clause of it. If you think it takes two competing principles, give me both and tell me which verdicts split them. Second, I suspect there's tension I haven't fully faced: 3 and 4 say testimony gives me knowledge of causally inert truths with zero causal contact on my end, 5 says overwhelming enumerative evidence doesn't, and 9 says near-certain probabilistic evidence without any proof does. If a principle saves all three of those, spell out exactly how it threads that needle, because it seems hard. Third, whichever principle wins, does Benacerraf's dilemma survive it? Like, if testimony and proof-assistants and zero-knowledge protocols can all transmit knowledge of abstracta without anyone causally interacting with them, maybe the causal constraint the dilemma leans on was already dead and the whole thing dissolves -- or maybe my verdicts just show I've quietly abandoned naturalism. Tell me which. Finally, what does the winning principle predict for two cases I haven't listed: an AI system generates a proof, no human checks it line by line, but a proof assistant verifies it -- do the developers know the theorem? And: a community adopts a new set-theoretic axiom purely because of it's fruitful consequences -- does anyone know the axiom is true?

Answer in American English please.
