So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, but my roommate did a philosophy minor and she's obsessed with G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903), specifically chapter VI where he argues the only things valuable in themselves are roughly friendship/love and the appreciation of beauty. She talks about it like it's a spec doc, and honestly it kind of is. One chunk she keeps quoting at me is this, from §117:

"I think that the additional presence of a belief in the reality of the object makes the total state much better, if the belief is true; and worse, if the belief is false. In short, where there is belief, in the sense in which we do believe in the existence of Nature and horses, and do not believe in the existence of an ideal landscape and unicorns, the truth of what is believed does make a great difference to the value of the organic whole."

I've been collecting her verdicts on cases, and they all seem consistent with Moore:

1. A stunningly beautiful planet nobody will ever see vs. an ugly rock nobody will ever see -- she says the difference in value is real but negligable, basically a rounding error.
2. Contemplating a real sunset vs. an equally beautiful painting of a sunset (you know it's a painting in the second case) -- the real one is better.
3. Deeply admiring a friend who turns out to be a total fraud (the warm feelings were genuine, the admirable qualities weren't there) -- she says that's unfortunate, worse than the identical feelings toward someone who deserved them.
4. Crying at a symphony while actually hearing none of the notes or how they relate, just raw emotion -- she says that's worth basically nothing.
5. Daydreaming an imaginary landscape you don't believe exists at all -- still genuinely valuable, she says.

Here's the case I want to spring on her: she's in what she firmly believes is a hyper-realistic VR garden demo. Gorgeous. She's admiring it with exactly the right emotion and full awareness of its beautiful qualities, but she's telling me "shame none of this is real." Plot twist: it's not VR. Through a mix-up the headset is transparent and she's looking at an actual garden. Her belief ("this isn't real") is false, and the object is real.

My question: what does she say, and more importantly what's the rule she's actually running? I can think of at least two rules that fit verdicts 1-5: one where value tracks the truth of her belief about the object (call it the belief-matching rule), and one where value tracks whether the object is genuinely real regardless of what she believes (the authenticity rule). Those come apart on my VR case, and I think they come apart on nothing else in her track record. Tell me which rule you think she's running, what it predicts, and -- if you think the record genuinely underdetermines it -- what additional case would settle it. Also, does the Moore passage above actually decide this, or is it ambiguous between the two rules in a way she probably hasn't noticed?

Please actually commit: give me your best guess at her verdict and her rule, with reasons. Don't just lay out both options and shrug -- I want a call, and then the strongest objection to your call, and why you think it survives anyway. A few paragraphs is fine.
