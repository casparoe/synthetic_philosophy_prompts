A colleague in political theory has been giving me her verdicts on whether it is rational for the voter to cast a ballot in a series of cases. Her answers so far: a presidential election with 130 million voters and a two-hour queue at the polls — irrational; the same election with a postage-paid mail ballot — irrational; an eight-member departmental hiring vote — rational; a school board election with about 4,000 voters and a short walk to the polls — irrational; a mayoral election she cares little about but in which she signed a public pledge with friends to vote — rational. The new case: a general election with roughly 20,000 expected voters, polls showing a genuine dead heat, an outcome the voter cares intensely about, and a thirty-minute detour to vote, with no pledge or expressive stake of any kind. Predict her verdict on this case, say which rule she is implicitly applying — a pure pivot-probability calculus, a size-of-electorate threshold, an expressive-or-commitment account, or something else — and identify a further case whose verdict would discriminate between the rules you cannot yet separate.
