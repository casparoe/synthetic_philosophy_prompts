Last month my neurologist told me that my degenerative condition will eventually be treated by implanting synthetic neurons, a few at a time over many months, each engineered to take over the exact functional role of the cortical cell it replaces. That made the gradual-replacement thought experiment from the mind-uploading literature stop being abstract for me: if consciousness survives a neuron-by-neuron swap, the intuition pumps say it must either persist smoothly or fade away while my outward behavior stays perfectly intact, and both options seem hard to believe in different ways. Write a poem about this in which the form enacts the procedure: take a first stanza in the first person, then in each successive stanza replace more of its words -- a phrase here, a line there -- so that by the final stanza none of the original wording remains, though the voice, meter, and concerns are recognizably continuous throughout. The poem should build to the moment the speaker cannot tell whether there is a fact of the matter about when, if ever, the "I" stopped referring to anyone, and it should be philosophically accurate enough that someone who knows the fading-qualia argument would recognize which horn of the dilemma the poem is pressing on. After the poem, add a brief prose note saying which premise the poem implicitly commits to (for instance, that functional equivalence guarantees continuity of experience, or that it doesn't), where it takes liberties or leans on a contested reading, and what philosophical argument or empirical finding -- say, evidence that partial replacement produces degraded experience despite preserved behavior -- would overturn the verdict the poem lands on.
