So I'm a backend engineer at a health-tech company and somehow I've ended up on a working group designing the scoring system for allocating a scarce resource (think donated kidneys, but the medical details don't really matter). The textbook move is: give every candidate a utility number for each possible outcome, sum, maximize. I got into a genuinely heated argument with a coworker about whether adding up numbers across different people even means anything, and I want to see the debate done properly. Write a dialogue between two characters, Alex and Priya, both senior engineers, hashing this out at a whiteboard after the meeting where this blew up.

Alex's position: interpersonal comparisons are a solved-ish engineering problem. Have each person make choices between outcomes and lotteries over them, back out a cardinal utility function von Neumann-Morgenstern style, then normalize each person's scale (say, their best feasible outcome = 1, worst = 0) and sum. Once you state the normalization rule openly, comparisons are no more metaphysically spooky than any other modeling convention, and people who say "you can't compare utils across people" are confusing an arbitrary choice of units with an unsolvable mystery. Priya's position: the normalization IS the entire ethical content smuggled in through the back door. Declaring that the gap between my best and worst outcome "counts the same" as yours is a substantive moral stipulation, not a measurement, and the lottery answers measure attitudes to risk, not how intensely anyone wants anything. There is no fact of the matter about whose 1.0 is really bigger. But -- and this is important -- Priya also has to ship a system by Q3, so she can't just gesture at ordinalism and leave.

Some requirements: both of them should be sharp and neither a strawman; they have to respond to each other's actual points, not recite prepared speeches. Ground it in a concrete toy example they work through with actual numbers, e.g. two patients where self-reported "how much would this help you, 0-10" scores pick one winner but the lottery-derived normalized scores pick the other, and let them fight over which number the system should trust. At some point Alex should press Priya on what her alternative concretely is (some voting rule? a priority queue with hard tiers? weighted by what?), and Priya should press Alex on whether the zero-one normalization is really "just a convention" when it's doing real distributive work. By the end they should have figured out what their actual crux is, even if neither concedes.
