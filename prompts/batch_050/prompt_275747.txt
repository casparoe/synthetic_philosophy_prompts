I got roped into grading my cousin’s practice exam after making the mistake of mentioning that I’d recently fallen down a Bertrand’s paradox rabbit hole while thinking about random cracks across the circular ceramic plates I make as a hobby. I need a second opinion before I assign the grade, since I’m not totally confident I’d catch every subtle issue.

The exam question was:

A circle has radius R. Consider the claim that if a chord is chosen at random, the probability that it is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle can be 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4, depending on the sampling procedure. Reconstruct those three answers. Does Bertrand’s paradox show that probability is subjective? Does the principle of indifference select one answer as the correct one? Explain what role symmetry and the sampling mechanism play.

Here is the student’s answer:

The side of an inscribed equilateral triangle is √3R.

First, fix one endpoint of the chord and choose the other endpoint uniformly around the circumference. If the angular separation is θ, the chord has length 2R sin(θ/2). It is longer than √3R exactly when θ lies between 2π/3 and 4π/3. That interval has length 2π/3 out of 2π, so the probability is 1/3. Strictly speaking, though, this method conditions on one endpoint rather than choosing the whole chord, so it is not quite a distribution over chords.

Second, fix a direction for the chord and let x be the signed distance of its midpoint from the center, measured along the perpendicular diameter. Choose x uniformly on [-R, R]. The chord is longer than √3R exactly when |x| < R/2. The favorable interval has length R, while the whole interval has length 2R, so the probability is 1/2.

Third, choose the midpoint uniformly over the area of the circle. The chord is long enough exactly when its midpoint lies inside the concentric circle of radius R/2. The probability is therefore the ratio of areas, π(R/2)² / πR² = 1/4.

There is no mathematical contradiction because the expression random chord does not specify a complete probability space. Each answer is correct for its own sampling mechanism. Bertrand’s paradox therefore does not show that probability is subjective; it shows that probabilities are underdetermined until a mechanism or measure has been supplied.

The unqualified principle of indifference is ambiguous because different parameterizations divide the geometry up differently. However, once indifference is applied to symmetry-equivalent transformations rather than to coordinate choices, the second answer is selected. Rotational symmetry lets us fix the chord’s direction, and uniformity in x treats all parallel chords equally. The area method does not treat nearby parallel chords equally, since equal intervals in x contain different amounts of area. So 1/2 is the canonical answer if the problem is understood as asking for the maximally symmetric random chord rather than for the result of a particular physical process.
