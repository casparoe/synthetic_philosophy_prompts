I volunteer a couple of mornings a week at a food bank, and over the past year I've become quietly obsessed with the logic of the system my visitors navigate: the sanctions for being seven minutes late to an appointment, the fraud rhetoric that treats every claimant as a suspect until proven otherwise, the placements where people stack shelves for their benefits rather than a wage, the way "genuinely seeking work" gets measured by activity logs rather than by anything to do with work. I'm not an academic — I just keep thinking that the system already sorts people into "deserving" and "undeserving," it merely refuses to say so out loud.

I'd like you to write a piece of satire that says it out loud. Specifically: a white paper from an invented think-tank (give it a plausible, faintly Orwellian name) proposing a single "Deservingness Score" for every benefit claimant — one number, updated monthly, that folds together fraud-risk flagging, sanction history, job-search activity, and hours of unpaid "community contribution," with score thresholds determining not only benefit levels but access to a reformed workfare ladder through which a claimant can earn their way back up. The proposal should be written entirely deadpan, in the smooth, reasonable house style of policy pamphlets, with section headings, an executive summary, and a worked example of an imaginary claimant whose month goes slightly wrong.

The satire has to work by exaggerating features that genuinely exist rather than inventing absurdities from scratch: every mechanism in the Score should be a slightly-honest version of something real (conditionality, sanctions, fraud investigation, workfare placements), so that someone who knows the welfare literature recognises each one, and so that the objections a critic would raise — punishing lateness as moral failure, treating poverty as a character defect, making the claimant work below the minimum wage for their own benefit — are felt by the reader without ever being stated. The authors of the white paper must never notice what they've done; no winking, no last-paragraph confession. About 800 words, please, and in British English throughout.
