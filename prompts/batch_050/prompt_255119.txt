My dissertation advisor died last month and I've been asked to speak at her memorial. Here's the complicating factor: she spent a good chunk of her career defending a modernized version of the old Epicurean argument about death -- roughly, that since you don't exist after death, there's no subject around for whom death is bad, so death itself can't be a misfortune to the person who dies (only to the people left behind, maybe). She published on this for years, taught it every fall, and apparently told her partner she wanted the memorial to "actually take the arguement seriously" rather than do the usual thing where everyone agrees it's consoling nonsense and moves on. So now I have to give a eulogy at a philosophy department memorial where a third of the room are philosophers who know her papers cold and will notice immediately if I strawman the view or cheat, and two-thirds are her partner, her nieces, neighbors, old college friends -- people who came to grieve, not to attend a seminar.

Write the speech for me, around 900 words, six or seven minutes spoken. The hard part I need you to actually solve: the Epicurean view, taken seriously, seems to forbid the central move of every eulogy -- saying that her death is a loss *for her*, that she was robbed of years, that it's tragic she won't see her students finish. If I just say those things anyway, I'm implicitly rejecting the position she built her career on, at her own memorial. If I lean all the way in and say death was nothing to her, I've written the coldest speech ever delivered. Find the honest path between those -- there is one, I think, having to do with what the argument does and doesn't say about the living, and about what was bad *before* her death rather than because of it. No afterlife talk, she was a committed atheist, and no jokes about the void, her partner asked.

Structure your response as: first the full speech text, then a section called "Where the argument did the work" (maybe 200 words, explaining which moments in the speech are shaped by the Epicurean constraint and how), then a section called "What I cut and why" (around 150 words, the tempting eulogy moves you left out because they'd betray either the philosophy or the audience).
