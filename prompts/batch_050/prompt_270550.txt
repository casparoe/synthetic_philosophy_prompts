I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long feature on how historians assign responsibility for the Cold War. I've provisionally adopted the post-revisionist consensus: that the opening of Soviet archives in the 1990s broadly vindicated the traditional view that Stalin's ideologically conditioned expansionism, rather than Western encirclement or a tragedy of mutual miscalculation, bears primary responsibility for the conflict's onset. What would count against that position? I want its exposure mapped, not just its critics listed: which specific archival findings — concerning Stalin's intentions in 1945–47, his responsiveness to Western restraint versus Western firmness, or the degree of his control over communist parties abroad — would actually dislodge it, and which would merely embarrass it; what the position predicts about counterfactuals (would a more conciliatory Washington have changed anything?) that its rivals don't; and whether it has absorbed so many qualifications — Stalin sought security rather than conquest, the conflict was overdetermined, ideology mattered only at the margins — that it no longer risks anything at all. A historian I interviewed added a deflationary thesis: that 'who started it' is a malformed question conflating three distinct ones — whose actions were causally necessary, who first escalated a tense peace into a structured rivalry, and whose intentions were aggressive — and that the orthodox–revisionist dispute persists mainly because each school is answering a different one of the three. Tell me what would count against that deflationary thesis too, including whether it merely relabels the disagreement rather than dissolving it. For both positions, distinguish what their defenders would themselves concede as damaging from what only opponents treat as damaging, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
