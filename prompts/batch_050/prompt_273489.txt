You are a philosopher of science with a reputation for scrupulous fair-mindedness, brought in to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues in a philosophy of biology department who have argued about function ascriptions for years. One defends an etiological (selected-effects) theory of biological function, on which a trait's function is the effect for which ancestral tokens of that trait were selected; the other defends a causal-role theory, on which a trait's function is whatever contribution it makes to some capacity of the containing system, with no reference to history. Your task is to draft the joint statement they could both sign at the end of a good-faith collaboration.

Before drafting, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: list every candidate point of agreement, every candidate disagreement, and every case that either side has wielded against the other, and only then decide what makes it into the final document.

The final document should have three parts:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive, not peace-treaty boilerplate. Candidates worth considering include: what both theories are trying to explain (the normativity of function ascriptions, the practice of functional explanation in biology); which crude accounts both reject (for instance, that functions are merely whatever effects a trait happens to have, or merely what its bearers intend); and which commitments both accept about the difference between a function and an accident. Include only what both sides would genuinely sign, and flag any candidate agreement that turned out, on inspection, to hide a disagreement.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one point of disagreement in neutral wording, then give each side's own position phrased so that side would accept the wording as fair. Among the rows you should seriously consider: whether a trait that malfunctions can still possess the function it is failing to perform; whether a beneficial structure appearing for the very first time in a lineage, with no selection history, already has a function; whether an exact molecular duplicate of an organism assembled by chance in a single moment, with no ancestors at all, would have organs with functions; and whether causal-role analysis is objectionably permissive about which system capacities it is relativized to.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to revise its view. For each crux, propose a test case both parties agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about the case in advance. Build at least two of your test cases from the following scenarios, which neither party has previously written about: a de novo gene arising in a laboratory-evolved bacterial population that drifts to fixation and happens to contribute to the population's stress tolerance without ever having been selected for; a fully synthetic minimal cell, engineered from scratch, whose components were designed to play roles but have no evolutionary history whatsoever; and a human organ that no longer performs the effect for which it was selected but now contributes detectably to a different capacity of the organism. For each scenario, state what the etiological theorist predicts about the correct function ascriptions, what the causal-role theorist predicts, and what result, if any, would count as a genuine embarrassment for either side rather than merely a counterintuitive bullet to bite.

Conclude with a short honest assessment: is any portion of this dispute verbal rather than substantive, and if so, which portion, and why? Note, in particular, whether the two theories might be answering different questions (one about why a trait is there, one about how a system works) and, if you think so, explain what residual disagreement would survive that diagnosis.

Aim for the tone and rigor of a published consensus statement: no caricature, no straw formulations, and no hedging designed to make both sides feel they won.
