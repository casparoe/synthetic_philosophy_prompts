Read Borges's Funes the Memorious through the philosophy of natural kinds. After his accident, Funes perceives and remembers every particular in exhaustive detail and loses the capacity to abstract: general categories strike him as empty fictions, and the private numbering system he devises, which assigns each number its own proper name instead of building it out of a recursive scheme, is a classification that groups nothing. The standard moral drawn from the story is that all categorization is a lossy, interest-relative convenience we impose on a seamless flux. Push on that reading. Does the story actually undermine natural kinds, or does it quietly presuppose real structure — after all, Funes's flawless perception is of genuine particulars, and his failure is one of systematization, not of accuracy? Then turn the lens around: does the trajectory of the sciences, from folk taxonomy to the periodic table, vindicate the thought that some carvings answer to nature and not just to us, or does Funes supply the conventionalist with a standing reply to any such argument? Indicate which scenes or details do the philosophical work, where the story resists being conscripted into either camp, and what, if anything, fiction can show here that the standard debates (promiscuous realism, the theory-dependence of kinds) cannot. I am writing on the story for a philosophy of science seminar and want a reading that is faithful in both directions.
