Consider an investor, Mrs Ashworth, who must decide how to allocate a windfall of £100,000 that she cannot afford to lose. She is choosing between two products. The first guarantees steady compounding at three per cent per year for twenty years. The second is a venture which each year either doubles the invested sum or reduces it to forty per cent of its value, with equal probability, the outcome each year being independent of previous years. The venture's expected one-year return is positive: on average, the invested sum grows by twenty per cent annually. A friend reasons that, over twenty years, the law of large numbers will smooth out the unlucky years, so the venture is plainly the wiser choice.

Ergodicity economics challenges this reasoning by distinguishing between the ensemble average (the mean outcome across many parallel copies of the investor at a single point in time) and the time average (the growth rate experienced by a single investor compounding sequentially over many years). Please describe the most important implications of this distinction for Mrs Ashworth's decision. In particular, address (i) how the distinction reframes the apparent attractiveness of the venture and whether the friend's appeal to the law of large numbers survives scrutiny, (ii) what it implies about the rationality of purchasing insurance or accepting unfavourable bets in order to avoid ruin, and (iii) what it suggests about the widespread practice of forecasting long-run wealth from average annual returns. Where the ergodicity framework is contested or where the ensemble perspective remains legitimate, please note that as well.
