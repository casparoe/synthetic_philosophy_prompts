In a philosophy of mind discussion group I belong to, an argument about whether current large language models genuinely understand anything has been running for months without either side conceding an inch. The debate is usually framed in terms Dennett popularized: Darwin's theory of natural selection was a strange inversion of reasoning because it explained how exquisite biological competence -- the apparent design of organisms -- could arise with no comprehension anywhere in the process; Turing's work was a parallel inversion, showing how competence at reasoning tasks could be produced by mechanisms that comprehend nothing. The question that divides us is where these systems fall. One side argues that the inversions establish a general point: fluent performance produced by a process with no grasp of meaning is competence masquerading as comprehension, and the models are simply the latest and most impressive instance. The other side replies that comprehension was never anything over and above a certain kind of functional organization -- the right dispositions to respond, infer, correct, and apply -- and that if a system displays enough of that organization, withholding the word understanding is just residual dualism about mechanism.

My own suspicion, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are applying different implicit tests without noticing: one asks what the system can do, the other asks how it does it and what its states are causally connected to, and each test is self-certifying because passing or failing it is built into what the test counts as evidence. If that is right, the disagreement is at bottom about which phenomena the concept of understanding is answerable to, and no new benchmark will touch it.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who is right. Is this dispute verbal -- two legitimate precisifications of understanding talking past each other -- or is there a substantive question underneath, such as a hidden empirical disagreement about what internal structure these systems actually have, or a disagreement about which cases should count as data for fixing the concept (ordinary speakers' judgments, developmental psychology, the functional role understanding plays in our explanations)? Or does the impasse mark bedrock: rival intuitions about whether meaning requires a particular kind of history or embodiment, of the sort neither side can argue the other into? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says and assumes, and then say what progress would look like given it -- a conceptual distinction that would dissolve the dispute, an empirical or formal result that would force one side to update, or a reframed question that both sides could recognize as the real one. If your diagnosis is that no such resolution is available, argue for that instead, and explain why the debate nonetheless keeps feeling to participants as though it could be settled.
