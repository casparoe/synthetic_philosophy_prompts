I run a small newsletter where I recommend novels, albums, and the occasional gallery show to a modest subscriber list. Two weeks ago, at a dinner I hosted, two friends of mine got into an argument that has made me genuinely uneasy about my own practice, and I'd like it settled properly. My friend Mara mentioned that she'd told her book club to skip a much-hyped novel because a critic whose taste she trusts called it emotionally hollow, and my friend Daniel pushed back. He said Mara has no business repeating that verdict as though she knew it: she hasn't read the book, and aesthetic judgments, unlike ordinary factual claims, can't simply be borrowed. You can repeat the words "the prose is restrained and devastating," but if you've never experienced the prose, the sentence in your mouth is gossip dressed up as knowledge. Testimony can tell you where the museum is; it can't make the painting beautiful for you.

Mara didn't back down. She replied that we accept testimony everywhere else without a shred of guilt -- she believes the bridge she drove over that morning is safe without having inspected it, and she believes Antarctica is cold without having been -- so exempting aesthetic claims looks like special pleading for a romantic ideal of the lone sensitive observer. Daniel countered that the disanalogy is in the reasons: when an engineer's testimony about the bridge is challenged, there are load calculations standing behind it that anyone could in principle check, whereas the reasons behind "emotionally hollow" -- the flattened dialogue, the manipulative final act -- only count as reasons for someone who has actually encountered them, so Mara can believe the critic but cannot be said to know the novel is hollow in the way testimony lets her know the bridge is safe. Mara's final move was to argue that even first-hand aesthetic judgment is built on secondhand scaffolding -- genre conventions learned from other readers, critics who taught her what to listen for in a record -- so Daniel's "pure first-hand experience" is a fiction, and practically speaking, her policy of trusting one well-calibrated critic has wasted far less of her time than Daniel's see-everything-yourself principle has wasted of his.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general verdict: for each of the four moves above -- Daniel's opening claim, Mara's bridge-and-Antarctica reply, Daniel's point about reasons not transferring, and Mara's scaffolding and track-record rejoinders -- say who is right about the specific point being made and where each overreaches. I'm especially interested in whether the disagreement is really about knowledge at all, or whether it conflates knowing that a work is good with the different business of appreciating it, and whether a position exists that both sides are missing. Finally, I'd like you to be candid about the epistemic status of your answer: state how confident you are in your overall adjudication, and single out the single move in the exchange where you are least sure of your ruling, saying what consideration would most likely change your mind on it.
