I've been thinking about the Pasadena game again, having first come to it years ago through the Pascal's mugging literature, and I want to pressure-test my understanding of the solution space. In case the details matter: a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads; if this happens on flip n, the payoff is (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n/n dollars, so odd rounds pay you and even rounds charge you. The probability-weighted payoffs form the alternating harmonic series, which converges conditionally to ln 2, and by the Riemann rearrangement theorem the same terms can be re-summed to literally any real number, which is the precise sense in which the game's expected value is undefined rather than infinite.

Here are the responses I have already collected and don't need repeated. (1) The feasibility response: no actual casino has unbounded funds, so cap the payoffs, the series then converges absolutely, and the paradox evaporates. (2) The privileged-order response: compute the expectation in the natural order in which the rounds occur, get ln 2, and play whenever the entrance fee is below that. (3) The permissivist response: where the expectation is undefined, expected utility theory is simply silent, and playing and declining are both rationally permissible. (4) The risk-aversion response: declining marginal utility of wealth tames the enormous late-round payoffs. I already know (4) is blocked by re-denominating the game directly in utilities so that every possible outcome is strictly positive, so don't offer it. (5) The scope restriction response: undefined expectations merely show that such idealized games fall outside decision theory's jurisdiction, which I include for completeness though it strikes me as (1) in different clothes.

What I want is a sixth response with a genuinely different structure. It should not be another way of fixing a summation order, not another appeal to physical or institutional feasibility, not another retreat into permissiveness or silence, and not a mere conjunction or special case of the listed items. Explain how it actually handles the Pasadena game as specified, and then whether it survives the utility-denominated variant that kills (4). After that, give me your honest verdict on whether the response succeeds, and if it fails, identify the exact step where it breaks. Finally, situate it relative to the St. Petersburg paradox and the two-envelope problem, which I take to be cousins of the Pasadena game rather than twins, and say whether your proposed response generalizes to them or reveals why they need separate treatment.
