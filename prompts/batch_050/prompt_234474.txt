A friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether love is answerable to reasons, and instead of reaching a resolution we produced a pile of cases on which my verdicts feel secure individually but jointly, I suspect, inconsistent. I know the standard menu of positions — the appraisal picture, associated with Velleman, on which love is a response to the beloved's valuable qualities; the bestowal picture, associated with Frankfurt, on which love confers value rather than detecting it; and Kolodny's suggestion that the reasons for love flow from the relationship and its history rather than from the person considered in isolation. What I want to know is which coherent principle, if any, my actual judgments commit me to. The cases and my verdicts are these.

First, the twin: I love my partner, and suppose I meet her identical twin, raised apart, who shares her temperament, humor, and values down to the last detail. My verdict is that I have no reason whatsoever to redirect my affections, and that someone who felt genuinely indifferent between the two would display a defect in love rather than a refinement of rationality. Second, trading up: I meet someone who possesses all of my partner's good qualities in greater degree — funnier, kinder, more perceptive about me. My verdict is that staying is not irrational; there is no reason here that I fail to heed. Third, dementia: were my partner's personality to dissolve until few of the qualities I fell in love with remained, I think love not only may but ordinarily should persist; leaving because she is no longer witty would be a failure of love, not an achievement of reason. Fourth, unrequited love: a person can love someone admirable from a distance, with no relationship and no prospect of one, and I want to say this love can be perfectly fitting — sad, perhaps, but not a mistake. Fifth, the abusive partner: someone who goes on loving a person who cruelly mistreats them is not merely unlucky; I think they have reason to stop loving, and a friend who helps them fall out of love has done them a real service. Sixth, the question "why do you love me?": both stock answers strike me as defective — listing her qualities invites the reply that someone else has them more richly, while saying there is no reason makes the love arbitrary in a way that misdescribes it.

My friend claims the first three verdicts amount to the simple observation that love tracks the particular person rather than a bundle of qualities, but I cannot see how that squares with the fourth case, where there is no shared history to do any tracking, or with the fifth, where the particular person seems to be exactly what one should stop tracking; and the third and fifth together seem to demand that love be simultaneously insensitive and sensitive to the beloved's qualities. Is there a single principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures all six verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to the next case that arises, with an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate? If something has to give, tell me which verdict is the most expendable and why, and say what the resulting account predicts for two cases I have not listed: loving someone partly because of how they love you, and the love of a newborn child, who has qualities but, so to speak, no excellences yet.
