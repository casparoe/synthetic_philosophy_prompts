I have spent the past few months with Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind and have come to accept, at least provisionally, its central negative thesis: that what he calls the official doctrine — mind as a privately accessible theatre of inner episodes, the ghost in the machine whose hidden operations cause the body's public behavior — is not a false empirical hypothesis but a category mistake, arising from the assumption that mental-conduct words pick out processes running parallel to bodily ones. With it I accept the positive corollary that words like intelligent, knowing, and voluntary signify multi-track dispositions and capacities rather than occurrences in a special realm.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want more than the standard complaint that Ryle was a behaviorist; he denied it, and I find the denial more interesting than the label. Specifically, I would like you to work through the following.

First, the regress argument. Ryle's argument that construing intelligent performance as the doing of an act plus an inner operation of theorizing about it generates an infinite regress seems to me the load-bearing piece. What assumptions does it need — for instance, that the inner operation must be of the same logical type as the outer performance — and under what reading of the doctrine does it fail? Could a Cartesian grant that intelligent action requires no prior episode of theorizing and keep the rest of the ghost?

Second, the category apparatus itself. Ryle never states an independent criterion for when two expressions belong to different categories, so the diagnosis seems to presuppose a theory of categories he does not supply. Does this make the category-mistake verdict unverifiable, or does the argument survive on a thinner, purely logical notion of absurdity? Sketch what a workable criterion would look like, and whether Ryle's own positive account would pass it.

Third, a test case I have been turning over. Suppose neuroscience, some centuries from now, establishes that deliberate actions are reliably preceded by discrete, content-bearing brain events that play out, step by step, the course of the action before it occurs — something remarkably like the doctrine's inner rehearsals — and that agents, suitably calibrated, know these events' contents in advance. Which components of the official doctrine would this vindicate, and which would Ryle still call a category mistake? If you answer that nothing is vindicated, explain why the doctrine's causal picture, private episodes causing public behavior, receives no partial support. If you answer that something is vindicated, say how much of Ryle's position that costs him.

Fourth, the positive theory's exposure. Which phenomena press hardest on the dispositional account: occurrences that present themselves as episodes (pangs, flashes of insight, silent counting), first-person authority, the phenomenology of effort? For each, say whether a committed Rylean should concede it as a genuine counterexample or absorb it, and what the absorbing move costs.

Please order the potential defeaters you raise from those that would sink the position to those that are merely awkward. Then, as an epistemic check, identify the single point in your own assessment where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and state what argument or evidence bearing on that point would lead you to a different verdict — including the possibility that your ranking of the defeaters is inverted.
