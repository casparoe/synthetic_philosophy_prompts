I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one side effect is that social choice theory has become my default vocabulary for any claim that several reasonable desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied: Arrow's theorem, Condorcet cycles, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship, single-peaked preferences, mechanism design. Recently I read about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, and rather than another summary, I want the material translated into the vocabulary I already think in.

To fix the shared content so we're working from the same definitions: consider a risk score for a binary outcome, say loan default or recidivism. The score is calibrated if, among everyone assigned score x, a fraction x actually default. It satisfies equalized odds if false-positive and false-negative rates are equal across demographic groups, and predictive parity if the fraction of people flagged high-risk who actually default is the same across groups. Results from work in the mid-2010s, usually credited to Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan and separately to Chouldechova, show that when base rates differ between groups and prediction is imperfect, no score can satisfy all of these properties at once, except in degenerate cases. Please restate this entire situation in the vocabulary of social choice theory, as faithfully as you can manage: what plays the role of the voters, the alternatives, the preference profile, and the aggregation rule; whether calibration, equalized odds, and predictive parity correspond to axioms like unanimity, non-dictatorship, and independence of irrelevant alternatives; and whether the impossibility theorem is structurally the same kind of object as Arrow's — a clash of independently attractive axioms — or something different wearing similar clothes.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the fairness debate translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in social choice vocabulary at all? Some candidates I suspect won't survive: that the "alternatives" here are individual people whose lives are affected by the score, that base rates are empirical facts rather than preferences, and that the three criteria are statistical properties of a predictor rather than normative constraints on an aggregation procedure. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it points to something social choice theory is missing as a lens for this problem, or whether it shows that the fairness debate is less like Arrow than its popular presentation suggests. And flag the places where the translation tempts you to fudge — I'd rather see the seams than a smooth retelling.
