I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, a retired professor of moral philosophy, has defended throughout his career a strict Kantian line on animals: because animals are not self-conscious rational agents, we owe them nothing directly; cruelty to them is wrong only insofar as it coarsens the dispositions we need for our dealings with persons, and kindness to them is commendable only as practice for kindness to persons. Late in the novel he is challenged at his own birthday dinner by a former student of his, now a veterinarian, and I want her objection to be philosophically serious rather than the familiar fare. To that end, here are the objections to his view that I already have on file and do not need repeated:

1. The isolated-act counterexample: the wrongness of cruelty does not seem to depend on any spillover, so the view cannot condemn the man who tortures a dog on his deathbed, or the last human alive who shoots a faithful horse before the end, since no future treatment of persons remains to be corrupted.
2. The contingency objection: the view makes the moral standing of cruelty hang on an empirical link between cruelty to animals and cruelty to humans, and that link is contestable; a psychology in which the two never connect would be licensed to do anything.
3. The argument from marginal cases: if direct moral consideration requires rational self-consciousness, then infants and the severely cognitively impaired fall outside it too, which no one will accept.
4. The internal objection that Kant's own examples betray him: his talk of gratitude toward the old dog that has served long and faithfully does not cash out as indirect duty, since gratitude is owed to a benefactor, not rehearsed toward a stand-in.
5. The simulacrum reductio: if cruelty is wrong because it rehearses and habituates cruelty toward persons, then tormenting a sufficiently lifelike doll or simulation should be wrong for exactly the same reason, which suggests the principle proves too much.

What I need from you is one further objection, not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or conjunction of anything on it. For each of the five, state in a sentence why yours is genuinely distinct from it. Because this will become dialogue, the objection must be stateable in two or three sentences of plain, forceful speech by an intelligent non-philosopher — no technical apparatus, nothing she could not say across a dinner table. Then, honestly: assess whether the objection is any good. Tell me how the old professor would most plausibly answer it in his own Kantian terms, whether that answer succeeds, and whether I would be cheating my readers if I let her objection stand as the stronger one.
