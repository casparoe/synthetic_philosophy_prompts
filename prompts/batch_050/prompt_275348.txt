I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and there is a pattern I keep noticing in arguments -- both online and in meatspace meetings -- for which I have no name. I would like to know whether the literature has a concept for it, so I will describe it as concretely as I can, the way it actually shows up.

First instance. A nurse on a ward tells the resident that a patient's rash looks to her like the early presentation of a serious condition. She says it tentatively, in plain language, without the technical vocabulary. It is noted and set aside. Two days later an attending physician says essentially the same thing, wrapped in the right terminology, and it is immediately credited and acted on. The nurse turned out to have been right.

Second instance. On a forum I read, someone posts a genuinely good argument written plainly, with a couple of typos and no signposting of familiarity with the canon. It sinks with no engagement. A week later a higher-status poster makes the same argument with the right formatting, the right hedges, and the right names dropped, and it is widely praised. Nobody involved, as far as I can tell, was thinking about the first poster's identity at all.

Third instance. A colleague of mine with a strong regional accent gets talked over in meetings; his suggestions are then adopted, sometimes within the same meeting, when restated by someone else in the standard corporate register.

The pattern I see: credibility is being assigned or withheld on the basis of the *packaging* of testimony rather than its content, and -- this is the part I want to stress -- independently of *who* the speaker is. I am aware, in a vague way, that there is a philosophical literature about people being systematically disbelieved because of the group they belong to. But that does not seem to be quite this. In my cases the same person, with the same identity, can be believed or disbelieved depending on how they happen to phrase things that day; my colleague's idea is rejected in his mouth and accepted in someone else's. The discount seems to attach to the message's clothing, not to the messenger. And it has a curious self-sealing property: the people doing the discounting are often making a defensible local judgment, since facility with the register genuinely correlates with competence, so each individual case looks like a reasonable inference even though the aggregate effect is that the nurse is ignored and wrong ideas from fluent people sail through.

So my questions. Does this specific phenomenon -- register- or packaging-contingent credibility, as opposed to identity-contingent credibility -- have an established name, or a literature? If the nearest concepts are the identity-based ones, I want to understand precisely how the thing I am describing differs from each of them and which fits best, rather than being handed a label and sent away. If nothing established quite fits, say so and coin something, with a definition and some boundary cases. In particular I would like the edges drawn: when is attending to register a legitimate use of evidence about competence, and when does it become the thing I am complaining about? A bare name will not satisfy me; the carving is the point.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- either the name of the established concept, or that there is none and what you propose instead -- then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
