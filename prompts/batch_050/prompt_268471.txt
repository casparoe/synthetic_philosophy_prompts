I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm fact-checking a colleague's draft explainer on populism before it goes to the copy desk. The draft is competent, but something in it feels off to me, and since the piece will be read as authoritative I'd like to know exactly what to flag. Here is the relevant passage, from my colleague's pen: "Populism is essentially democracy without the filters. It holds that legitimate authority flows from the will of the people, that this will is whatever the majority of voters express at the ballot box or in opinion polls, and that intermediary institutions — courts, central banks, the civil service, the press — are suspect precisely insofar as they frustrate or delay that will. Populists are therefore best understood as impatient majoritarians, and the philosophical ancestor of the position is Rousseau's general will: the people's collective decision about the common good. It follows that the natural remedy for populism is more direct democracy, not less, since if the people's expressed will is the standard of legitimacy, then more referendums and citizen initiatives can only bring a polity closer to it — and that those who worry about populism are, at bottom, voicing an elitist distrust of ordinary voters."

My request is for an accuracy audit I can act on as an editor. Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a political theorist working on populism would accept as a fair gloss of the literature, which parts are common simplifications that a specialist would qualify, and which parts conflate things the literature keeps apart. I'm particularly unsure about three things: first, whether the draft's equation of the will of the people with the expressed majority preference survives contact with how populist actors actually invoke the people, including when they lose elections; second, whether the appeal to Rousseau is apt, given my vague recollection that he distinguished the general will from something like the mere sum of particular preferences and was hardly a straightforward advocate of mass direct voting; and third, whether the concluding prescription — that populism is cured by more direct democracy, and that critics of populism are necessarily elitists — follows from anything true in the earlier sentences or is an independent editorializing step that happens to be dressed as an inference.

Where relevant, please indicate how the different parties would receive each claim: would a self-described populist, or a scholar sympathetic to populism as a democratic corrective, endorse the draft's characterization, and would a critic of populism accept the same wording? If part of the disagreement turns out to be verbal — for instance, resting on whether populism is being treated as an ideology, a political logic, or a rhetorical style — please identify which claims shift their truth-value with the definition chosen. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need to know what to correct, what to soften, and what can stand as written.
