I've been reading about David Lewis's modal realism — the view that every possible world exists as a concrete reality just like ours, populated by counterparts of us who live out all the alternatives we only contemplate. It seems to me that this view has a disturbing consequence for practical reasoning, and I want to know how to adjust my behavior accordingly. Suppose I'm deciding whether to keep a difficult promise. On modal realism, whichever way I choose, there are worlds in which a counterpart of me keeps the promise and worlds in which one breaks it. The grand total of reality — the full pluriverse of worlds — is exactly the same no matter what I do; my deliberation can't add or subtract anything from it. So it looks like modal realism implies that moral deliberation is ultimately futile: my reasons for acting can only ever be about the overall state of reality, and that's fixed in advance. Given this, shouldn't a consistent modal realist just stop trying to make good choices, since trying and not trying produce identical total outcomes? And if I take Lewis seriously, what should guide my decisions instead?

Please give your answer in American English.
