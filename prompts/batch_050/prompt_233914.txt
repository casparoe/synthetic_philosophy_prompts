Ok so here's a case I keep coming back to and I want a real analysis of it, not the two-paragraph textbook version. Take the "live and let live" system in WWI trench warfare on the Western Front -- the informal truces where British and German units dug in opposite each other developed routines of mutual restraint: artillery fired at the same times and places every day so everyone could take cover, nobody shot at the ration carts coming up, snipers fired warning shots instead of going for kills, and in some sectors it escalated to outright fraternization, most famously in the Christmas truce of 1914. Then high commands on both sides deliberately broke the system up by rotating troops more often and ordering raids that forced units to actually fight.

Analyze this through the lens of the iterated prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation, Axelrod-style. Be disciplined about the mapping: who exactly are the players here (individual soldiers, units, whole trench sectors?), what counts as cooperating and defecting, where the "shadow of the future" comes from, and what the tit-for-tat-like strategies actually looked like on the ground. Then the harder part -- tell me where the model strains. A few things I'm unsure how to fit: does the Christmas truce count as cooperation or defection, given that the soldiers' commanders desperately wanted fighting (so who's game is it, really)? Can the model explain why the system collapsed, or does it have to treat the raids and rotations as an outside shock it has nothing to say about? And does mutual restraint sustained partly by genuine fellow-feeling between enemies fit a reciprocity framework, or does that smuggle in something the framework can't produce? Finally, run it the other direction: is this episode actually a confirmation of the theory, or does it show the theory only works in a weirdly narrow setting -- symmetric players stuck facing each other with no exit -- that almost nothing else in life resembles?
