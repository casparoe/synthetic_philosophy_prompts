Since retiring from experimental physics, I have been reading Nietzsche's On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life, and one claim in particular caught my attention. In the third section (in the standard division of the essay), Nietzsche argues roughly as follows: because we are the products of earlier generations, we are also the products of their errors, passions, and even crimes; we cannot sever ourselves from that chain, but we can and must, from time to time, sit in judgment on our inheritance and condemn parts of it -- even though condemning one's own origins is always a dangerous, potentially self-lacerating business. Please do not treat this paraphrase as settled; part of the task is to interpret it.

With that as background, please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Devise a realistic scenario involving a single scientific research institute (no fictional physics, no omniscient observers, nothing a competent administrator could not actually attempt) whose members relate to the institute's own history in all three of Nietzsche's modes -- monumental, antiquarian, and critical -- and engineer the scenario so that the verdict of Nietzsche's analysis falls out of the institutional details rather than being stipulated: each mode must visibly serve the institute's intellectual vitality in one respect and damage it in another, and the critical mode in particular must emerge as both necessary and genuinely perilous in the way the passage above suggests. Then (a) state what Nietzsche's framework predicts for the institute and why, (b) identify the single structural feature of the case that does most of the work, and say what happens to the verdict if it is removed, and (c) sketch how a defender of pure antiquarian piety toward the institute's past would attack the construction. Please write your answer in American English.
