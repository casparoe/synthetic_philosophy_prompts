The instructor has agreed to let me try marking one exam script before she does, and I want a reliable second opinion on my judgment. Here is the exam question and the student's full answer. Exam question: Is conceivability a reliable guide to possibility? In your answer, explain the challenge posed by a posteriori necessities, and assess whether the distinction between prima facie and ideal conceivability rescues the link between conceivability and possibility.

Student's answer: Philosophers since Hume have treated conceivability as evidence of possibility: if we can coherently conceive a state of affairs, that state of affairs is possible. This principle faces an apparent problem from Kripke's a posteriori necessities. Water is necessarily H2O, yet we can seemingly conceive of water turning out not to be H2O; Hesperus is necessarily Phosphorus, yet we can seemingly conceive of their being distinct. If conceivability implies possibility, these identities would be contingent, so either the identities are contingent or the conceivability principle fails.

Kripke's own conclusion is that conceivability is simply not a guide to possibility. The appearances of contingency, he argues, are illusions generated by confusing the scenario we actually conceive — a substance that looks and behaves like water but has a different microstructure — with water itself lacking that structure. The qualitative evidence that fixed our reference could have been produced by a different substance, and that is all we ever conceive.

Chalmers responds by distinguishing prima facie conceivability (what seems conceivable on first reflection) from ideal conceivability (what survives ideal rational reflection), and negative conceivability (we cannot rule the hypothesis out a priori) from positive conceivability (we can construct a coherent positive conception of it). The Kripkean cases are negatively conceivable but not positively conceivable, so they leave untouched the thesis that ideal positive conceivability entails possibility. Zombies, by contrast, are ideally positively conceivable, and therefore possible.

Conceivability is thus a reliable guide to possibility provided we restrict the thesis to ideal conceivability; prima facie conceivability counts as defeasible evidence only.
