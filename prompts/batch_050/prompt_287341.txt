ok so i fell down a rabbit hole about this old fight between W.K. Clifford and William James over whether its ever ok to believe something without enough evidence. Clifford has this story about a shipowner who talks himself into trusting his old creaky ship instead of paying for an inspection, the ship goes down, everyone drowns, and Clifford says the guy is guilty even if he genuinely, sincerely believed it was seaworthy -- he had no right to that belief on the evidence he actually had. People online bring up the ship constantly, like believing on shaky evidence is basically launching an unsafe vessel, end of discussion.

But James pushes back that some beliefs -- religious, moral, even trusting your friends -- are nothing like that: nobody drowns if you're wrong, and for some of them you literally cannot gather more evidence before deciding, life makes you pick a side.

So how far does the ship analogy actually hold? Walk me through it: whats the ship in an ordinary belief, whos the passengers, whats the inspection, whats the sinking. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, especially for beliefs that only affect the believer, and for cases where refusing to decide is itself a decision with its own risks (i think thats james' point about forced choices?). Does the ship picture quietly smuggle in the assumption that every unjustified belief has victims? And once all the disanalogies are on the table, does Clifford's big sweeping conclusion -- nobody, anywhere, should ever believe anything on insufficient evidence -- still survive, or does it really only cover ship-type cases where other people get hurt?
