I teach a college seminar on aesthetics and next week we're doing Tolstoy's What is Art? -- the cranky late essay where he trashes most of the Western canon, Shakespeare included, and more or less repudiates his own great novels as bad art by his own lights. So here's the paper topic I want to assign. Read Anna Karenina against the theory in What is Art?: does the novel pass Tolstoy's own test? Take his criteria seriously -- art as the infection of one person's feeling by anothers, plus his conditions of individuality, clarity, and above all sincerity, and his demand that real art unite people in a shared religious-moral perception rather than amuse an idle upper class. Where would Tolstoy say the novel obviously fails (he'd say almost everywhere, I assume), and are there scenes -- I'm thinking of Levin mowing with the peasants, the death of Nikolai Levin, maybe the ending -- that actually meet the infection standard better than the old man would have admitted? And then the harder question the paper has to land on: if one of the greatest novels ever written fails its author's own theory of art, does that sink the theory, or should we take seriously the possiblity that Tolstoy was right and our reverence for Anna Karenina is just class taste dressed up? I want a verdict at the end, not a both-sides shrug.
