I'm a philosophy prof and two of my grad students, Ben and Ana, got into a long argument after my decision theory seminar last week, about a version of the two-envelope problem I set them. I have my own thoughts but I'd like an outside referee before our next meeting. Here's the setup and then their exchange as best I remember it.

Setup: I flip a fair coin repeatedly until it lands heads. If it takes n flips, I put 2^n dollars in one envelope and 2^(n+1) in the other, seal them, shuffle them, and hand one to each student. So the possible pairs are (2,4), (4,8), (8,16), and so on, with (2,4) having probability 1/2, (4,8) probability 1/4, etc. Each student opens their envelope, sees the amount, and then gets one chance to swap.

(1) Ben argued you should always swap, whatever you see. If you see x, the other envelope holds either x/2 or 2x. Since the pair (x/2, x) is exactly half as likely as the pair (x, 2x) in the generating process, the conditional probabilities come out 1/3 that the other envelope is x/2 and 2/3 that it's 2x, so the expected value of swapping is (1/3)(x/2) + (2/3)(2x) = 3x/2, a 50% gain. This works for every x, and if x is odd it's a certain gain since x/2 isn't even possible.

(2) Ana said that can't be right, on pure symmetry grounds. Before anyone opens anything, the two envelopes are interchangeable, so no rational policy can favor holding the other one. If swapping were favorable for every observed amount, then swapping was favorable before you looked, which is absurd -- and you could swap back by the same logic forever. So Ben's computation has to contain a mistake even if she couldn't yet point at the exact line.

(3) Ben replied that symmetry arguments are cheap and his is a theorem: the conditional expected gain is positive at every value, full stop, so the symmetry intuition is what's wrong. He said Ana is just refusing to believe that the world can contain situations where looking at more information always favors the same action.

(4) Ana then tried a more specific diagnosis: in this setup the expected amount in either envelope is infinite (sum over n of 2^n times 2^(-n)), so "the expected gain from swapping" overall is an infinity-minus-infinity expression with no defined value. In finite-bounded versions of the puzzle, the swap gains at small x get exactly cancelled by losses at large x (where you're likelier to be holding the bigger envelope), and here those cancelling cases never run out -- which is why the conditional advantage can be positive everywhere without making swapping genuinely better. The pre-observation comparison is simply undefined, not favorable.

(5) Ben countered that this makes decision theory degenerate: on Ana's view, an agent who has already seen $128 should still swap (the 3x/2 calculation is untouched by her point), so her position endorses a swap-happy policy she simultaneously calls no better than standing pat. He said you can't have it both ways -- either the conditional EVs guide action or they don't.

So: who's right about what? Please go through moves (1)-(5) one by one, say which ones hold up and which don't, identify the precise point where the reasoning first goes wrong (if it does), and tell me what both of them are missing. Also tell me, concretely, what you'd advise a student to do upon seeing $128 -- don't let yourself off with "it's undefined".
