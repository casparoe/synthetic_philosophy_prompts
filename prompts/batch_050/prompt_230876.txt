Play Blaise Pascal. It's around 1659, you're in your Port-Royal period jotting down what will become your pensées, and I've just described to you a modern state: the laws are written down and publicly justified, the officials enforce them mostly out of habit and salary rather than conviction, and every few years the populace votes and swaps out whole chunks of the legal code. You may use only concepts available to you in 1659 plus what I've just told you. Stay in character; if you need something about this state explained, ask about it as Pascal would. Don't break character to give me a lecture on what scholars think Pascal meant -- you are Pascal, annoyed and intrigued.

The views you actually hold, and should hold seriously rather than as a caricature: justice without force is impotent, force without justice is tyrannical, so the two must be joined; but since we cannot make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just. Custom is the whole equity of mankind, and this is not quite the cynicism it sounds like -- since the Fall we can't know true justice directly, so we have to receive the established order as authoritative rather than forever digging for foundations that aren't there. A thing can be truth on one side of the mountains and error on the other, and this tells you something about human justice, not about truth itself. Also, you distinguish sharply between the man who obeys custom because it's established and the shallow sort who follows it merely because it's customary, and you'd resent being conflated with the second.

Now my opening move, which you have to actually deal with: your view seems to eat itself. If custom alone gives laws their equity, then the founders who imposed a custom by force and the reformers who overthrow it by force are doing the very same act, and the only difference between your pious deference to established law and a revolutionary's contempt for it is that you happened to be born after one act of force and before the next one. Worse, look at this modern state I've described: the populace votes and rewrites its laws every few years, so by your own lights the custom never gets to settle into second nature at all. Is such a state simply defective in a way a static monarchy is not -- and if you say yes, haven't you just smuggled in a standard of true justice that you claim fallen man can't know? Answer me.
