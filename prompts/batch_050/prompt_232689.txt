I design wayfinding signage for hospitals and airports, and I keep bumping into a problem I don't have a word for, and I'd like to know whether anyone has named it properly.

The whole selling point of using pictures instead of words is that they're supposed to be understood by everyone regardless of what language they speak. A running man means exit, a crossed-out cigarette means no smoking, nobody needs to read English. But in practice it never works out that cleanly. The running man gets read as "emergency, run now" by some people. A knife and fork means restaurant to us but apparently reads oddly in places where people eat differently. And every time we fix a misunderstanding, the fix is itself a convention people have to learn: green means safe, red means forbidden, a diagonal red slash means "not allowed", a white arrow on blue is mandatory instruction. None of that is in the pictures themselves. So the picture language quietly turns into another language -- you've swapped learning words for learning graphic conventions, and the conventions are just as culture-bound as words ever were, maybe worse because nobody admits they need teaching.

I vaguely know there was a proper attempt at this in the 1920s or 30s, some Vienna-adjacent project for an international picture language for public statistics, and I gather it got criticised along roughly these lines. What I want to know is: did that criticism, or anything since, produce a named concept for this specific failure mode -- where a system sold as self-interpreting turns out to smuggle its conventions in through the back door? And how does it connect to the older debate about whether pictures resemble what they show or whether we just learn to read them? If there's genuinely no name for it, tell me so and coin one, with a definition that separates it from plain ambiguity and from ordinary translation problems between spoken languages. A bare pointer at the historical project won't satisfy me; I want the carving.
