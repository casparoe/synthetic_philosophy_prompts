I have been keeping a private ledger of moral emotions — recording, for a series of cases, whether I judge the emotion felt to be fitting and morally useful, or fitting and harmful, or simply unfitting. I would like to know what principle, if any, lies behind my verdicts, and whether the principle supports the common claim that guilt is the better moral emotion and shame the worse one. Here are the cases.

1. An accountant embezzles from her employer over several years. Conscience-stricken, she confesses, repays the money with interest, and accepts the legal consequences. Her guilt seems to me entirely fitting and to have done exactly the work a moral emotion should do.

2. A boy from a poor family is mocked at school for his worn clothes and comes to feel a deep shame about his family's poverty. This shame seems to me wholly unfitting: he has done nothing wrong, and the emotion damages him without correcting anything.

3. A logistics executive signs off on a disposal practice she knows pollutes a river. For years she feels no guilt at all — she has elaborate reasons ready — but when a local campaign photographs the damage and her name circulates publicly, she feels intense shame, resigns, and spends her retirement funding cleanup. Here, awkwardly, the shame seems to me both fitting and useful, even though it is the very same reputational, self-directed emotion that harmed the boy in case 2.

4. A soldier who survived an ambush that killed his friends feels crushing guilt, despite having done nothing that a court or his comrades would fault. This guilt seems to me unfitting in its object yet somehow understandable, even admirable, as testimony to how seriously he takes his bonds to others.

5. A man who cheats on his partner feels no guilt but is terrified of being found out; when he is found out, what he feels is humiliation at being seen as the kind of man who cheats. The shame moves him to end the affair but not, I think, to become honest.

6. Two societies face the same problem of drink-fueled violence. One addresses it chiefly through guilt — preaching, confession, inner conviction of sin. The other addresses it chiefly through shame — public marking of offenders, loss of face before kin. Both succeed roughly equally in reducing the violence. I find myself unable to say that one society has chosen better, yet I also cannot shake the sense that the first society's members are inwardly freer.

Now: the standard picture I keep encountering is that guilt concerns what one did and shame concerns what one is, and that this is why guilt is the healthier, more reform-oriented emotion. That picture cleanly handles cases 1, 2, and 5, but it seems to get case 3 wrong — the executive's shame was useful precisely because it struck at who she was — and it says nothing obvious about case 4, where guilt itself attaches to no wrongful act, or case 6, where the comparison is between whole cultures rather than individuals.

Is there a single principle — or a small set of principles, each stated precisely with the case that motivates it — that delivers all six of my verdicts, or most of them? If some verdict must be surrendered, tell me which and why. I would also like to know what the resulting account predicts for two cases I have not listed: a person who feels shame at belonging to a nation that committed historical atrocities she played no part in, and a culture that deliberately set out, over a generation, to shift its dominant moral emotion from shame to guilt — whether such a project is even coherent, and what the account says it would cost.
