I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about the way AI researchers talk about their own systems. In papers, system cards, and safety documents, the same labs routinely say that a model "believes" a false claim, "knows" an answer it failed to give, "wants" to please the user, or "refused" a request — while in other contexts insisting that such talk is mere shorthand, since the system is just predicting tokens. Daniel Dennett's intentional stance seems to license at least some of this: on his view, attributing beliefs and desires to a system is justified when treating it as a rational agent is the best predictive strategy available, and the question of what it "really" believes may have no deeper answer than that. But it's not clear the lab practice is actually Dennettian. Reconstruct what the practice, as I've described it, presupposes: does it assume that belief-talk is merely a predictive device (in which case a model can "believe" things even with no inner states at all), or does it quietly assume something stronger — that the model's internal representations must play some causal role for the attribution to be true rather than just convenient? Is the pattern of attributing beliefs when discussing deception and withholding them when dismissing model testimony consistent under any single theory of belief attribution? And what would have to change in how AI systems are described, evaluated, and regulated if we took one of these positions seriously and dropped the other?
