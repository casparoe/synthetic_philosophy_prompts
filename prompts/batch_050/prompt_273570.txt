I'm trying to work out where I actually draw the line between legitimate persuasion and manipulation, and I'd like help extracting the principle, if there is one, from my case-by-case judgments.

Here are my verdicts. A recruiter who tells a candidate the truth -- that the team is collegial, the hours are long, the equity is unlikely to be worth much -- because she believes an accurately informed candidate who accepts anyway will stay longer: fine. A recruiter who says the same true things but has scripted the conversation so the candidate first hears the collegiality while tired and hungry at the end of a long interview day, and the equity caveat in a rapid monotone as the candidate is being walked out: not fine, even though nothing false was said. A doctor who frames a procedure as having a 90% survival rate rather than a 10% mortality rate, because she genuinely judges the survival framing more decision-relevant for this anxious patient: I say fine, though I feel uneasy about it. A politician who frames a tax as a relief package rather than a levy: not fine, even though the underlying policy is defensible and nothing asserted is false. A friend who wants me to come to a party and, knowing I'd refuse if told my ex will be there, simply doesn't mention it: not fine. The same friend omitting to mention that parking will be difficult, for the same reason: fine. A teacher who withholds from a struggling student that most students find the unit easy, to protect her motivation: fine, I think. An advertisement that pairs a car with images of attractive people and open roads, asserting nothing: I say fine, or at least not manipulation in the culpable sense, but I notice I can't explain why this differs in kind from the recruiter's scripted conversation, which is just as non-propositional in its mechanism. A charity that uses a vivid, identifiable victim rather than statistics, knowing the identifiable victim effect will dominate the donor's reasoning: fine, because the cause is good -- but I realize this justification would license almost anything.

My tentative principle is that persuasion is legitimate when it engages the target's capacity to recognize and weigh reasons, and manipulative when it bypasses or subverts that capacity -- but the charity case suggests I'm smuggling in the goodness of the end, and the advertisement case suggests my bypass criterion is too permissive about non-rational influence.

What I'd like: first, the most economical principle (or two candidate principles) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Second, an explicit statement of which of my verdicts each candidate principle cannot accommodate -- I expect at least one of the framing cases and one of the omission cases to be in tension. Third, what each principle says about three cases I haven't listed: a negotiator who deliberately negotiates in the morning when counterparties are known to concede more; a parent who gets a child to eat vegetables by renaming them after a cartoon character; and a browser that defaults users into organ donor status. Fourth, whether my tentative principle survives repair or should be abandoned.
