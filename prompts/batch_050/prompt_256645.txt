I'm preparing a one-page statement of my views for an epistemology reading group on the dogmatism paradox — the puzzle about whether knowing something licenses you to dismiss, in advance, any future evidence against it. Before I circulate the statement, I want a consistency audit. Here is what I hold:

(a) Fallibilism. One can know that p even though one's evidence does not entail p and it remains epistemically possible, for one, that not-p. Knowledge does not require certainty.

(b) Single-premise closure. If I know p, and I competently deduce q from p while knowing that p entails q, then I thereby know q, or am at least in a position to know it.

(c) Known-misleading evidence may be set aside. If I know that some body of evidence is misleading — that it points away from the truth — then I am rationally permitted to disregard it rather than adjust my confidence in light of it. Knowledge screens it off.

(d) A case verdict. A detective who knows the butler did it, and who receives an unopened lab report that might bear on the question, acts irrationally if she burns it unread on the grounds that whatever it says against the butler's guilt must be misleading. Knowing p does not license the pre-emptive dismissal of incoming evidence about p.

(e) Inquiry optimism. Looking further into what one already knows is always at least rationally permissible, and competent inquiry never by itself costs one knowledge; it is not possible to lose knowledge simply by responsibly gathering more evidence.

(f) Confidence and planning. Fallibilism is compatible with being highly confident of what one knows and with treating known propositions as a settled basis for practical reasoning — booking travel on what I know, not on what might turn out to be true instead.

Please tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real tension is. Where a cluster of commitments jointly generates the trouble, say so explicitly rather than forcing the analysis into pairs. For each genuine tension, state the cheapest available revision — which commitment to drop or weaken, and what that costs the overall view — and then tell me which single change would restore the most consistency for the least philosophical damage. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally problematic. Finally, indicate how confident you are in each tension you diagnose, and single out the one step in your analysis — or the one commitment of mine you feel most tempted to misread — that you are least sure about.
