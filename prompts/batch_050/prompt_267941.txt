I've been chewing on an idea I picked up from an old reading group on decision theory, and I've talked myself into a position I want stress-tested. The position, roughly: while you are genuinely deliberating about what to do, you cannot coherently assign probabilities to which option you'll end up taking. The thought is that deliberation treats your choice as open and up to you in a way that prediction does not -- the moment you start forecasting your own choice ("I'm 80% likely to cave and order the chips"), you've stopped deciding and started spectating, and the deliberation either collapses or the probability was idle decoration. I associate this with Levi and Spohn, though I'm not fussed about who exactly said what. I also think the position survives the obvious "but I can predict my future behaviour all the time" objection, because predicting what I'll do next Tuesday when I'm not currently deciding is a different matter from predicting the very choice I'm in the middle of making.

So: what would count against this view? Don't just give me the generic self-reference worries. I want the strongest candidate defeaters, each spelled out concretely -- specific cases where self-prediction during deliberation seems not just possible but useful (I'm thinking of things like: a dieter who correctly predicts they'll fail and uses that prediction to change the plan; an addict's recognition of their own patterns; someone who notices mid-deliberation that they're obviously going to pick the prestigious option and decides to interrogate that; the tickle-style cases from the evidential/causal decision theory literature). For each one, say whether it actually threatens the view or whether a careful defender can absorb it by distinguishing deliberation from mere musing, or token prediction from type prediction, or whatever. Also tell me whether the view risks being insulated into unfalsifiability -- is there any possible case its defender would have to concede as a genuine counterexample, or can they always redescribe the situation so the "deliberation" wasn't real deliberation? And a separate question I'm unsure about: does the view stand or fall with causal decision theory, or could an evidentialist hold it too?

Please be committed rather than even-handed: tell me which defeaters you think actually land and whether I should abandon or merely qualify the position, ranked from most to least damaging. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- no preamble, no restating the question back to me. And write in British English please.
