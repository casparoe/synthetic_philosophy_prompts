I'm running a session for an interdisciplinary cog-sci reading group (half philosophers, half ML people) and I want to hand out a one-page decision tree that sorts people by their actual take on the frame problem. The reason I need it is that half the room thinks the frame problem was solved by AI researchers in the 80s and the other half thinks it's a deep unsolved problem about how minds work at all, and they just talk past each other, so the tree's first job is to split apart the different problems that all get called by that one name before sorting views within any of them.

Specs for the tree:

- Start node should be something everyone in the room would accept, like: a system has to act without checking everything, so it can't explicitly verify every consequence and non-consequence of each of its actions before moving.
- The early branches should seperate at least three things that get called the frame problem: (a) the representational problem of formally saying what doesn't change when something happens, (b) the computational problem of figuring out what's relevant fast without considering everything, and (c) the philosophical problem of how relevance-detection is possible at all, i.e. whether any rule-governed machinery could ever just "see" what matters.
- Then keep branching to identifiable positions, which must include: nonmonotonic-logic/circumscription approaches, successor-state-axiom style solutions inside classical logic, heuristic or resource-rational approaches, Dreyfus-style "this is why good old fashioned AI was doomed" holism, Fodor-style "this is just the isotropy of central cognition showing up in a robot suit," and embodied/enactive "the problem dissolves once you stop modeling cognition as inference over a world representation."
- Every node has to be a question answerable by reacting to a concrete case, not by knowing labels. Build at least two nodes around these: the robot that reasons very carefully about whether it's safe to pull its wagon while the bomb is sitting on the wagon; a Yale-shooting-style glitch where a perfectly reasonable formalism predicts the wrong thing persists; the banal case where you open the fridge and the kitchen walls obviously haven't changed color.
- Where two leaves give identical verdicts on every case in the tree (I suspect circumscription vs successor state axioms are like this, and maybe Dreyfus vs Fodor), flag them as not actually separable and say what extra question would split them.
- For any combination of answers that's coherent but that nobody prominent seems to actually hold (like "the representational problem is fully solved AND there's no remaining philosophical problem" — or wait, is that just the default ML view?), include the leaf anyway marked [unoccupied] and say in one sentence whether the combo is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Keep it to about a page, no jargon at the nodes (jargon only at the leaves), and write the node questions so my ML friends can't pattern-match their answers off the vocabulary alone.
