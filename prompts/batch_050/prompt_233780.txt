Please explain something I keep getting stuck on about generics — sentences like "ducks lay eggs". I get the basic textbook point that generics tolerate exceptions in a way that overtly quantified claims don't: "ducks lay eggs" is fine even though drakes, ducklings, and non-breeding females don't lay eggs, while "all ducks lay eggs" is just false. What I don't get is *which* exceptions are tolerated and why. Three cases that seem to pull in different directions:

1. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" sounds true even though only a small minority of mosquitoes (females of certain Anopheles species) actually transmit it.
2. "Books are paperbacks" sounds false even though plausibly a majority of books in circulation are paperbacks.
3. "Sea turtles return to the beach where they hatched" — that's something the nesting females do; the males basically never come ashore again, so it's false of half the species, and yet it sounds perfectly fine.

So neither raw proportion nor "how many individuals" seems to decide it. The explanation I usually see is that the property has to be "characteristic" of the kind, or connected to the kind's nature in the right way — but honestly that feels like it just relables the intuition rather than explaining it. Can you tell me what actual work "characteristic" is doing here? I.e., what would have to be true of the world (not of our words) for "mosquitoes carry malaria" to be true while "books are paperbacks" is false?

Then stress-test whatever account you land on with this made-up case: imagine a frog species where one subspecies, about 5% of the population, secretes a toxin lethal to humans, and the rest are harmless and look identical. Is "frogs of this kind are poisonous" true? Does your answer change if (a) the toxic subspecies is on the verge of extinction, or (b) a single mutation next year would make every frog of the species toxic? Don't give me a survey of positions — pick the account you think actually works and defend it against the hard cases.
