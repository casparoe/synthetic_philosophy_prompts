A colleague and I have been arguing about the centipede game for weeks, and I want a referee. The setup: two players alternate moves; at each turn a player may take, ending the game and keeping the larger share of the accumulated pot, or pass, which grows the pot and hands the move to the other player. The pot grows for a fixed, commonly known number of rounds, and if the final player passes, the game ends with the larger share going to the opponent. Here is the argument my colleague says settles everything.

(1) At the final node, the rational move is to take: taking yields strictly more than passing, whatever has happened before.
(2) A rational player who is certain the opponent will take at the next node takes at the current one, since passing then strictly lowers their payoff.
(3) Each player is rational, each knows the other is rational, each knows that the other knows this, and so on without limit — rationality is common knowledge between them.
(4) From (1), whoever moves last takes; from (2) and (3), the other player foresees this and takes at the second-to-last node; iterating the reasoning back through every node, the first player takes at the opening move.
(5) So rational play ends the game immediately, and both players walk away with the smallest payoffs on the board.

I cannot accept (5), because the argument seems to prove too much. (6) The premises invoke nothing beyond rationality, its common knowledge, and the game's finite structure, so parallel reasoning condemns cooperation in any finite interaction with a known endpoint — no matter how long the horizon or how small the temptation to defect. (7) Yet pairs who pass through the early nodes both end up far richer than anyone who takes at the start, and a standard of rationality that reliably costs both parties nearly the whole pot looks less like a theorem than a defect in the standard. (8) So something in (1)–(4) must give.

The trouble is that each premise looks unimpeachable to me in isolation, and the iteration in (4) looks like ordinary induction. If I have to give something up, I would rather qualify (3) than touch (1), but I want to know what that actually costs and whether it even blocks the conclusion. So: is the argument valid, forcing a choice among premises — and if so, which premise is least expensive to abandon? Or does it contain an illicit move, and if so, exactly which step fails and why did it look sound? If the argument trades on an equivocation or a confusion rather than presenting a clean choice, say that plainly instead of forcing a verdict onto it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the step you reject, stated in one line — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the setup.
