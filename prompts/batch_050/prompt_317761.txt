I'm writing a paper for my moral philosophy seminar on Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" and my friend Jonah (who has read way too much of the free will literature for his own good) hit me with this argument over coffee and I've been stuck on it for a week. Here's how it goes. (1) On Strawson's picture, moral responsibility just is a matter of being the fitting target of the reactive attitudes -- resentment, gratitude, indignation -- and there's no stance-independent fact of the matter "behind" the practice that could justify or condemn it from outside; the participant stance is simply part of human life as we live it. (2) Whether we take the participant stance or the objective stance toward someone is not something we discover but something we settle into, the way we might decide to treat a troubled friend as a patient for a while. (3) People genuinely do take the participant stance toward young children and psychopaths sometimes -- parents really do resent their three-year-old's deliberate defiance, victims really do hate psychopaths who hurt them -- and this resentment is every bit as natural and unchosen as any other reactive attitude. (4) Since the practice as a whole needs no external justification, no token of resentment within it can be ruled out as a mistake either; calling it "inappropriate" would smuggle in exactly the outside standpoint Strawson says we don't have. (5) So nobody is exempt from responsibility, not toddlers, not the severely mentally ill; exemptions are just stances we happen to prefer, not facts about anyone. The conclusion is clearly nuts but every premise feels like something Strawson would nod along to, which is exactly what worries me about the paper I'm writing, since I was planning to use Strawson to defend the exemptions.

Which step do I give up? I care most about keeping (1), since it's the backbone of my whole essay, so if the exit costs me (1) I'd like to know that plainly rather than be gently talked out of it. I'd also like to know why the bad step looked so Strawsonian, because I suspect my friend built it to be maximally annoying. Format it like this, in this order: "The exit" (name the step and say why it fails, roughly 150 words), "Why it looked fine" (why each premise has a reading that makes it tempting, ~120 words), "The cost of the alternatives" (what I'd pay if I rejected a different premise instead, ~150 words), and "Salvage" (whether Strawson's account of excuses and exemptions survives without the step I gave up, ~100 words).
