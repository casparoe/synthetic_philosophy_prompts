I'm in a small reading group that's been slogging through the equipment-analysis chapters of Being and Time — the hammer, readiness-to-hand versus presence-at-hand, the whole web of in-order-tos and towards-whiches, and especially the three ways stuff announces itself when it fails: when it breaks, when it's missing, when it's in the way. For the last session before the holidays everyone has to bring something funny that still does the philosophy justice, and I've decided mine will be an agony-aunt column. Problem is I'm not that funny.

Write the column for me, around 500-600 words, in the deadpan voice of a seasoned workshop advice columnist — invent a masthead — answering letters from equipment. At least three letters. The humor has to come from the actual analysis, not from goofy German words. Suggested letter-writers: a hammer whose head flew off mid-stroke and is distraught because its owner now just stands there staring at it, muttering about its weight and properties, when for twenty years it was simply absorbed in the nailing; a doorknob heartbroken that nobody in the house has ever once noticed it (the columnist should gently explain that this is the highest praise a piece of equipment can recieve, since being used well means being invisible); and a third of your choosing — maybe a smartphone that has never once managed to withdraw into use because it keeps pinging, or a bus that failed to arrive, or a toolbox left exactly where everyone trips over it. The columnist's advice should stay internally consistent with the view: breakdown as what lights up the referential totality of the workshop, failure as the moment the "world" shows itself, absorbed coping as the default that theory interrupts. Someone who's read these chapters should laugh because it's accurate — and ideally think the satire is fair — not because it's a cartoon. Don't have the columnist name-drop Heidegger or turn the jargon into punchlines; let the concepts carry the jokes, minimal winking, straight face throughout. Give it a signature sign-off.

After the column, step out of character for a short note (5-8 sentences) on where you took liberties: which jokes lean on a contested reading — say, how central breakdown really is to revealing worldhood versus just being one case among several, or whether a smartphone even fits the equipment analysis at all — and where you'd revise if a sharp Heidegger scholar pushed back. Basically, tell me which reading you're betting on and what counterargument would make you fold.
