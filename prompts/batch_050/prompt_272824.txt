In a fortnight I am presenting a paper to a formal epistemology workshop defending a thesis about Jeffrey conditionalization. My claim goes beyond the textbook defence in two respects. First, I argue that Jeffrey conditionalization is not merely a permissible response to uncertain evidence but rationally required whenever an experience redistributes an agent's credences across a partition while leaving her conditional credences on its cells fixed; any other response, I claim, either ignores part of what the experience teaches or imports information the experience did not contain. Second, I argue that the rule's notorious non-commutativity — the fact that successive Jeffrey updates on E1-then-E2 and E2-then-E1 can diverge — is a virtue rather than a defect, because it faithfully models the path-dependence of evidential impact. My position is softest on how an agent is supposed to identify the right partition, since experiences do not arrive pre-labelled.

The audience will be almost entirely Bayesian formal epistemologists: two senior members defend strict conditionalization and think uncertain evidence should be remodelled as certainty about one's own evidential states; two others work on diachronic Dutch book arguments; one works in accuracy-first epistemology; and one philosopher of perception doubts that genuinely uncertain evidence exists at all.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually face from this audience, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased as these people would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. State what I should concede up front and what I must hold, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Please write in British English.
