Here's something thats been bugging me. When people certify a hardware random number generator -- say for a state lottery, a casino, or cryptographic key generation -- they typically run its output through batteries of statistical tests (the NIST suite, Diehard, that kind of thing), and if the bitstream passes, the device gets the stamp of approval. But a test can only ever examine a finite string of bits, and there's a well-known tension lurking here: the digits of pi, or Champernowne's number (0.123456789101112...), will sail through plenty of these tests despite being produced by a short deterministic recipe, while a genuinely random source will occasionally fail some test purely by bad luck. So what conception of randomness does this whole certification practice actually presuppose? Is it treating randomness as a property of the sequence that comes out (product randomness) or of the mechanism that produced it (process randomness)?
