Write a short story about an attempt to implement something like coherent extrapolated volition — the idea, familiar to readers of LessWrong, that a sufficiently powerful AI should aim to do what humanity would want if we were smarter, better informed, more the people we wished we were, and had had more time to think and grow together.

The story should not be a generic "alignment goes wrong" parable or a debate scene with dialogue tags. Instead, it should dramatize a specific structural worry that I rarely see explored in fiction: that the extrapolated volition of a population may be path-dependent. Suppose the extrapolation procedure works person by person, resolving each individual's contradictions before aggregating, and suppose the aggregation step encounters Condorcet-style cycles — humanity's extrapolated self prefers outcome A to B, B to C, and C to A, depending on the order in which pairwise conflicts are settled. The final "coherent" volition is then partly an artifact of arbitrary sequencing decisions made by the engineers (or the AI) early in the process. In other words: the coherence was manufactured, not discovered, and yet everyone downstream experiences the outcome as the fulfillment of their deepest desires — because their desires have, by then, been shaped by the very extrapolation that was supposed to merely read them off.

Some requirements:

- Ground the abstract problem in concrete characters and sensory detail. For instance: an engineer on the original team who notices, years later, small ways in which the world's settled preferences bear the fingerprint of a scheduling choice she made in a hurry; or two versions of the same person extrapolated under different orderings who would have wanted incompatible things.
- Take seriously the unsettling possibility that this might not be a flaw. If there was never a pre-existing coherent volition to find, perhaps manufacturing one is the best anyone could have done — and perhaps human values were always partly self-authored in this way, even before any AI got involved. The story should let this thought breathe rather than dismissing it.
- No character should deliver a tidy resolution or a moral. End on a note that leaves the reader genuinely unsure whether what happened was a catastrophe, a success, or something for which those categories don't apply.
- Aim for roughly 1,500–2,500 words. American English, polished prose, no headers or bullet points — just the story.
