Is there a name for this? I keep noticing that we accept certain bare, unquantified statements about a kind of thing even when we know perfectly well that most members of the kind don't fit. "Ticks carry Lyme disease" is something I'd say to a child heading into the woods, even though only a small fraction of ticks are infected; "mosquitoes transmit malaria" sounds fine even though most mosquito species don't transmit it and most individuals of the right species aren't infected. Yet "books are paperbacks" is plainly false even though most books sold are paperbacks, and "ducks are female" sounds absurd even though about half of all ducks are female -- while the very same people who balk at that one happily say "ducks lay eggs," which holds of roughly the same half. So the acceptance threshold clearly isn't a statistic: sometimes a sliver of cases suffices, sometimes a solid majority isn't enough, and sometimes the statement seems to claim something about what's "characteristic" of the kind rather than about how common anything is. Is there an established concept for this class of statements, and an account of what makes the true ones true? And if my examples actually split into two or three different phenomena wearing the same grammatical costume, tell me which goes with which, rather than giving me one label for the whole lot. Please answer in no more than 300 words.
