Next month we're doing a session on classical Chinese thought and I want to do something on wu-wei, specifically the paradox of trying not to try -- the thing where the Daoist texts praise effortless, spontaneous action, but if you decide "ok, I will now act effortlessly," the deciding is itself a form of effort, so you've already failed. The Zhuangzi skill stories (the cook who carves the ox, the swimmer who just goes with the water) make it sound wonderful but nobody in my group believes you could get there by instruction, and honestly neither do I.

Design me an activity for the session that makes them actually feel this paradox rather than just nod at it. I want something with two phases ideally: first, a task where deliberate effort visibly degrades natural performance (so they feel that trying can hurt), and then a second phase where I instruct them to stop trying and they discover they can't do that on command -- the "be spontaneous NOW" problem. The paradox should hit them in the room before anyone names it. Give me concrete rules and materials (nothing that needs buying beyond pen and paper level stuff), a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, and then a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the philosophical problem -- including the follow-up question of whether wu-wei is even a coherent goal if every path to it runs through effort. Also tell me what's likely to go wrong the first time I run it (I suspect someone will just refuse to participate or treat the "stop trying" instruction as a joke), and be honest about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce -- my guess is anything involving skill built over years, since we have 75 minutes and cook Ding had nineteen years, but maybe you see a way around that.
