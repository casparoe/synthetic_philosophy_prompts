I teach a second-year undergraduate module in philosophy of computing, with about 18 students and a single 90-minute session to spend on P versus NP. They already know the definitions; what they don't yet feel is the asymmetry between solving and checking, and why its collapse would matter beyond computer science. Design a structured in-class game built around puzzles that are hard to solve but trivial to verify (word searches, subset-sum-style card problems, logic grid puzzles, or similar), in which one phase pits solvers against checkers under a scoring scheme that makes the effort gap palpable, and a second phase hands everyone a cheap "oracle" -- some mechanism by which solutions arrive almost for free -- so that the group can feel what evaporates when finding becomes as easy as checking: the status of the insight, the point of the search, the special credit owed to whoever solved it first. The debrief should then push on the further claim I want them to take seriously for the essay that follows: that if P = NP, much of what we call mathematical creativity or scientific genius reduces to mere verification, and that our practices of praising discovery tacitly assume the gap is real. The rules and scoring should carry this themselves, with the philosophical vocabulary introduced only afterwards.
