In Chapter III of An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894), C. Lloyd Morgan formulates the methodological principle now known as Morgan's canon, and then spends the surrounding paragraphs explaining how it is and is not to be used. I do not have the book at hand, so here is a close paraphrase of that passage rather than the exact wording:

We must never explain an animal's action as the result of a higher mental faculty if it can be adequately explained as the result of a faculty that stands lower on the psychological scale. This is a rule of interpretation, not a claim about what animals actually experience. The scale of faculties itself -- which capacities count as higher and which as lower -- is not to be assumed at the outset but established through careful observation of what animals of different kinds can be brought to do. Where the evidence genuinely supports the higher interpretation, the rule does not forbid it; it only forbids reaching for the higher interpretation when the lower one is sufficient.

I have three questions about this passage, and I'd like you to treat the paraphrase as the text under discussion.

First, what exactly does the rule prohibit? There seem to be at least two readings. On one, it is a special case of general parsimony: prefer the simpler hypothesis, where "higher faculty" just means "hypothesis that posits more psychological machinery." On the other, it does something Occam's razor does not: it presupposes a fixed ranking of mental faculties and directs us downward along that ranking, which could in principle conflict with parsimony whenever one higher faculty would explain a whole range of behaviors more economically than several distinct lower mechanisms. Which reading fits Morgan's text better, and does the tension matter in practice?

Second, Morgan insists that the scale of faculties must be established by observation rather than assumed. But his own famous application of the canon -- his analysis of his fox terrier Tony, who learned to open a garden gate by lifting the latch, and whom Morgan interpreted as acting by gradual trial-and-error learning rather than by insight -- seems to presuppose that we already know trial-and-error learning is "lower" than insight. Is this circular, or is there an independent way to fix the ordering of faculties that does not smuggle in the very assumptions the canon is meant to discipline?

Third, test the canon against a harder case than Tony's: a scrub-jay that re-hides its cached food when it notices another bird watching, but only if it has itself previously stolen food from other birds' caches. Researchers have taken this as evidence that the bird models what the observer knows. Apply Morgan's rule as you understand it from the passage. Then tell me honestly whether strict application of the canon has a systematic blind spot: does the rule, by always directing us downward, build in an asymmetry that guarantees we will underestimate animal minds whenever the evidence is genuinely ambiguous -- and if so, is that a defensible methodological conservatism or a prejudice dressed up as rigor?
