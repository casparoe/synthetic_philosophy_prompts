I spent forty years in evolutionary biology (population genetics mostly, but I've done my share of game-theoretic modeling), and since retiring I've been slowly working my way through the history of political philosophy. Right now I'm on Hobbes. I've read Leviathan twice and some secondary stuff, and here's my problem: when I read the state-of-nature chapters, my brain automatically translates everything into the vocabulary I actually think in, and I want to know whether that translation is faithful or whether I'm quietly mangling him.

So, concretely: please restate Hobbes's argument for why life outside the commonwealth is a war of every man against every man in the vocabulary of evolutionary game theory, as precisely as you can. Assume I'm fluent in payoff matrices, replicator dynamics, invasion analysis, evolutionarily stable strategies, iterated games, and the whole Axelrod-style literature on cooperation. Don't dumb it down.

The bits I especially want translated, one by one:

1. The rough equality of ability argument (the weakest can kill the strongest, by stealth or coalition).
2. The three causes of quarrel -- competition, diffidence, and glory. In particular diffidence, the business about anticipating and striking first for safety. That smells to me like preemption changing the payoff structure, but I want it done carefully, not hand-waved.
3. His claim that the war is a condition or a tract of time, a known disposition to fight, not actual fighting. I have no idea what the game-theoretic analogue of a *disposition* is and I suspect there isn't a clean one.
4. The covenant-and-sword move: why promises between individuals in the state of nature are void, and what work the sovereign is doing. Is the sovereign a correlated equilibrium device, a commitment technology, a third-party punisher, or none of the above?

Then the part I actually care about most: the residue report. Which parts of Hobbes translate cleanly, which parts only translate with distortion, and which parts have no expression in game-theoretic vocabulary at all? Some specific worries to address: my gut says the whole thing collapses into "one-shot interaction, defection dominates, therefore central punisher," and I strongly suspect that gut is wrong, because Hobbes seems to be doing something subtler than a prisoner's dilemma story -- e.g. the preemption point might make it more like a coordination problem with a bad equilibrium than a PD, and I'm not sure the folk-theorem apparatus he *should* have known about (cooperation sustainable in repeated play) is even available in his setup, or whether he implicitly rules out the repetition that makes it work. Also things like the sovereign being an *artificial person* made by authorization -- is that even the kind of thing a payoff matrix can represent?

Where there's residue, tell me which side it indicts: is the missing piece a limitation of game theory's vocabulary, or is it Hobbes running together things that look like one claim but arent?
