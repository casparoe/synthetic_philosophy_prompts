So there's this argument that recurs endlessly in my corner of the internet (SSC/LessWrong diaspora plus various splinter feeds and group chats) and I have literally never seen anyone change there mind about it. The fact pattern everyone agrees on: countries that rank highest on gender-equality indices — Norway, Finland, the Netherlands — have some of the most gender-segregated career choices, like relatively fewer women going into STEM than in places like Algeria or India that score terribly on those same indices. The so-called gender-equality paradox. Side one reads it as: when you remove economic pressure and social constraint, you get people's actual preferences, and those turn out to be gendered, so the remaining gap is mostly nature and justice is basically done once the constraints are gone. Side two reads it as: rich egalitarian countries are still saturated with gendered socialization, the indices measure laws and pay gaps rather than expectations, and someone choosing "freely" after a lifetime of marketing, teachers, and role models isn't the control group side one thinks they are. Both sides cite the same scatterplot. Nothing ever moves.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than adopted: the debate is stalled because both camps have quietly made themselves unfalsifiable. Side one can attribute any persisting gap to revealed preference; side two can attribute any pattern at all to residual socialization; and I've never heard either say what distribution of career choices would count against them. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A hidden empirical question (about what equality indices really capture, or what economic development does to expressed preferences), a conceptual one about what "free choice" has to mean for the inference to work, or two groups answering different questions — one about etiology, one about what justice requires — under a single heading? Diagnose it using what each side actually argues, and then tell me what progress would look like given your diagnosis: a specific study design, a within-country-over-time test, a distinction that dissolves it, whatever. Or, if you think the honest answer is that the question as posed is ill-posed, make that case. What I'm not looking for is a verdict on nature vs. nurture itself — I want the structure of why this thing never resolves.
