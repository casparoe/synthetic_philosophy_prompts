And here is the answer I have to mark:

"For Tolstoy, art is basically a means of communication. One person uses external signs to hand on to others a feeling they have genuinely lived through, so that the audience catches the feeling and experiences it themselves. That is why he rejects any definition of art in terms of beauty or pleasure: plenty of beautiful things transmit no feeling at all, and plenty of ugly things transmit real ones. Whether something is art is a matter of whether this infection actually happens, and the strength of the infection depends on three things: the individuality of the feeling, the clarity of its expression, and above all the sincerity of the artist, since an audience can tell when a feeling is faked.

So in the end sincerity is Tolstoy's real test of whether art is good. The more sincerely the artist feels what they transmit, the better the work, because a wholly sincere feeling cannot fail to be a worthy one.

Tolstoy also thinks good art unites people. Art that transmits feelings drawing people together, like ordinary human fellowship, is good; art that transmits feelings dividing them, like national pride or loyalty to one class or sect, is bad. This is why, despite the sneering of the critics of his day, he held up Shakespeare's King Lear as a case of art whose sincere craft lets it transmit feelings everyone can share.

Finally, he is careful to separate counterfeit art from bad art. Counterfeit art isn't art at all: it is produced by borrowing other people's feelings, by imitating, by striking effects, and by being merely interesting, and since no genuine lived feeling is there to transmit, nothing is actually communicated. Bad art, by contrast, really is art, it just transmits bad feelings. As for the dazzling painting only critics appreciate, Tolstoy would call it counterfeit rather than bad: the dazzle is exactly the sort of striking, interesting trick aimed at a refined class, so it is not even worth judging as art."
