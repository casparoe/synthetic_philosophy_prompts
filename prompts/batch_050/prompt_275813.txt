My uncle died last month and I've been asked to give one of the eulogies at the funeral. He was a retired philosophy prof who spent his whole career on Zhuangzi, and maybe a third of the guests will be his old colleagues and grad students who know the text cold. The rest are family, neighbors, his cycling club. I need a eulogy of about four minutes, spoken length.

The thing that makes this hard: the standard funeral moves -- he's in a better place, he lives on in our hearts, death is a cruel thief -- are all basically ruled out by the book he loved. Zhuangzi, when his own wife died, was found sitting on the ground banging on a pot and singing, and when his friend scolded him for it, he said he had wept like anyone at first, but then came to see her death as one more transformation, like the turning of the seasons. And on his own deathbed he told his students to skip the fancy burial -- above ground the birds get him, below ground the worms do, so why play favorites. My uncle brought these stories up constantly, including at dinner, which was annoying at the time.

If I give a normal eulogy, the philosophers in the room will know I've ignored everything he actually believed. If I just lecture on Zhuangzi, I'll seem to be telling his grieving kids that their grief is a philosophical error.

So write me a eulogy that takes a definite stand on what Zhuangzi's view of death actually amounts to -- pick one reading and commit, don't hedge between options -- and builds the whole speech around it. In particular I want it to pick a lane on whether Zhuangzi thinks grief itself is a mistake to be overcome, or whether grief is fine and natural and it's only the *fear of death* that's confused, because alot hinges on that. Then, after the speech, add a short note flagging anywhere you've leaned on a contested reading of the text, so I know which bits his colleagues are likely to give me grief about afterward.
