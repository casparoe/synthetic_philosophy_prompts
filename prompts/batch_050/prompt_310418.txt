I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I read around on consciousness and personal identity, and a reading group I'm in has asked me to write down my positions so the others can push back on them. Before I submit, I'd like a consistency audit. Here's where I've landed, roughly:

1. Consciousness is a matter of what a system does, not what it's made of: if the functional organization is right, the substrate (neurons, silicon, whatever) doesn't matter.
2. A single stream of consciousness must have a single subject. The unity of consciousness isn't just a contingent fact about how brains happen to be wired; it's part of what makes something one mind rather than a crowd.
3. The split-brain cases (patients whose corpus callosum has been severed, where each hemisphere can apparently perceive, reason, and act independently of the other) are best described as involving two centers of consciousness housed in one skull, at least during the experimental conditions where the dissociation shows up.
4. If I were to undergo a corpus callosotomy myself, I would survive the operation and continue as exactly one of the two resulting centers of consciousness, though I can't in advance know which.
5. Facts about who survives as whom can't be vague or indeterminate: either a future subject is me or it isn't, and there's no fact of the matter "in between."
6. What matters in survival is psychological continuity — connected memories, intentions, character, projects — rather than some further fact of numerical identity. If the psychology carries over, nothing important is lost.

What I'd like you to do:

- Go through these commitments and identify which pairs (or larger subsets) are in genuine tension, which merely look like they are but dissolve on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally.
- For each genuine tension, say what would have to give on each side — i.e., state the cheapest revision of my list that resolves it and what that revision costs me in plausibility.
- If there is one single revision that would clean up the most problems at once, name it explicitly.
- Please state how confident you are in the overall diagnosis, and single out the one point in your analysis you are least sure of, with a sentence on why.

I haven't signaled which commitment I'd be most willing to drop, so please don't assume one is sacred — but do tell me which revisions are cheap and which are expensive, given how the views support each other.
