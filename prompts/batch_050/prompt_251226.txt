I'm a TA on a first-year introduction to aesthetics, and in two weeks I get one fifty-minute section on the problem of aesthetic testimony. The students arrive straight from a short epistemology unit in which testimony was treated as a perfectly ordinary way of coming to know things: most of what they believe about history, geography, and science is second-hand, and the only live question was when a source is trustworthy. So they are primed to think that if a reliable critic tells you a novel is a masterpiece, then you know it's a masterpiece, and that's the end of it. The course lecturer has asked me to spend the hour making the acquaintance intuition — the thought that you cannot genuinely judge a work's aesthetic merit until you've experienced it for yourself — feel both natural and philosophically puzzling at once, and she warned me that last year's section collapsed within ten minutes into a debate about whether critics are actually any good at their jobs, which is exactly the wrong issue.

Don't teach me the material; plan the section. What has to be in place conceptually before the puzzle is even visible to them, and in what order do the pieces have to go, with the dependencies made explicit? I'd like one example to carry the whole hour, and I want you to say what that example must have and must lack. I'm torn between building it on a friend's enthusiastic verdict about a film and building it on a restaurant recommendation, and I suspect the food case lets the students shrug everything off as subjective taste; if you agree, explain why, and if you don't, tell me what the food case buys me that the film case doesn't. I'm also unsure whether to reach for the parallel with moral testimony — it's structurally neat, but I worry it drags moral realism into a room where it doesn't belong, so advise me on that. The hour should climax in some version of the question of why even perfectly reliable testimony seems insufficient for aesthetic judgment, since that is what rules out the easy diagnosis.

Tell me which misconceptions the students will form and at exactly which point in the sequence each will surface. I'm expecting at least three: that pessimism about aesthetic testimony is just the empirical claim that testimony happens to be unreliable in this domain; that it is snobbery dressed up as epistemology; and that the pessimist must be denying you learn anything whatsoever from a review. For each one, say how the order of the section should head it off in advance rather than leaving me to swat it down ad hoc. Finally, give me the single question I can ask in the last five minutes that will show whether they've grasped the difference between knowing a verdict and being entitled to it — or whatever distinction you think the section should actually end on — and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, saying so out loud to the class.
