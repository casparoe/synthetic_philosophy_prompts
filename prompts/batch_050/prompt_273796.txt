Here's one that came up in my grad seminar on formal epistemology a few weeks back and it's been driving me up the wall ever since. A student (clever, annoying, you know the type) wheeled out the following argument. The conclusion seems to me obviously crazy, but every time I think I've pinned down where it breaks, it wriggles out. Maybe you can do better.

The setup. There's a machine that will toss a genuinely indeterministic fair coin at noon. The objective chance of heads is 1/2, I know that, and I know everything relevant about the mechanism. There's also an oracle on the scene, stipulatively infallible, and here's the wrinkle: the oracle has been wired to the machine so that if the coin lands heads, it announces "heads" at 12:01, and if the coin lands tails, the oracle is instantly vaporized before it can make a sound. The vaporizer is independent of the flip, so the chance of heads is still 1/2. I know this whole arrangement.

Now the argument:

(1) The Principal Principle: if I know the objective chance of p is x, and the rest of my evidence is admissible, then my credence in p should be x.
(2) At 12:01 I hear the oracle announce "heads."
(3) This announcement is admissible evidence. Testimony from a known-infallible source is the gold standard of evidence; it would be intolerably convenient for PP if we got to declare it "inadmissible" just to save the prinicple.
(4) So by PP, my credence in heads, given my total evidence (which includes ch(heads) = 1/2), should be 1/2.
(5) But by ordinary conditionalization: P(heads | announcement) = P(heads & announcement) / P(announcement). The oracle announces if and only if heads, so P(announcement) = 1/2 and P(heads & announcement) = 1/2, giving P(heads | announcement) = 1.
(6) So my rational credence is both 1/2 and 1. Contradiction.
(7) Conditionalization is non-negotiable, so (1) goes: rational agents should not simply defer to known objective chances.
(8) And nothing in the argument really depends on the oracle being exotic -- any sufficiently good evidence about the outcome (a Laplacean demon who did the physics, a perfect crystal ball) generates the same clash. So deference to objective chance is irrational quite generally.

Now, (8) clearly proves too much. If it were right, I couldn't see what would justify anyone in deferring to chance at all -- not to quantum mechanics' statistical predictions, not to a climate model's output, not to the actuary's tables. That's the whole point of objective chance, that it's what credence should track. So something has gone wrong. But what?

Tell me which step you'd reject, and be honest about what rejecting it costs. A few constraints from my end: I'd very much like to keep conditionalization, and I'd also like to keep the verdict that the oracle settles it -- when an infallible source says heads, credence 1 seems like the one thing I'm sure of here. If your answer is that the announcement counts as "inadmissible" in Lewis's sense, then I want a characterization of admissibility that isn't just "evidence whose use wouldn't break the Principal Principle," because that makes the principle trivially unfalsifiable and hence empty. I'm aware Lewis himself was bothered by cases in this neighborhood -- undermining futures, the thing he gloomily called the big bad bug -- and that the standard repairs on offer (his admissibility restriction, the New Principle that has you defer to chances conditional on the total theory of chance, the idea that chance only guides the otherwise-uninformed) each pay a different price. Which price is the one worth paying, and why?
