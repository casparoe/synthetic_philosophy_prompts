I'm giving a toast at the wedding of my two closest friends next month, and I'd like you to write it. She is a concert violinist, he a pianist, and both have spent their careers wrestling with the same cruel asymmetry: the harder they reach for a great performance, the more it recedes, while their finest playing arrives when they stop commanding it. By their own account, their relationship began the same way — it only took hold once each stopped trying to manage it. I want the toast to draw on the Daoist idea of wu-wei, effortless action, but the assignment has a paradox built into it: advising newlyweds to try not to try is precisely the sort of instruction that makes trying worse, and a toast is itself a performance I could strangle by overengineering it. Write a toast of roughly four spoken minutes, for a mixed audience of conservatory colleagues who will appreciate the music details and non-musician family who won't. It should honor the couple's history of striving and letting go without turning that history into a formula, convey something like wisdom without issuing any advice that would defeat itself if followed, and say something honest about how two people committed to effortlessness might handle the stretches of a marriage when effort is unavoidable and letting go looks dangerously like giving up.
