So I'm a novelist (literary stuff mostly, a couple of midlist books, one adapted for radio which was weird) and I keep circling an idea for a story that I can't get off the ground myself, so I want you to write it for me, or at least give me something I can steal from. Call it 1500-2500 words.

The story should be built around what philosophers call the reference class problem -- the thing where any single case belongs to loads of different statistical classes at once, and the "probability" of the case comes out different depending on which class you sort it into, and there doesn't seem to be a principled way to say which class is the right one. I don't want an essay about this, I want it as the engine of the plot. Here's the setup I keep coming back to, feel free to adjust: in some town (time and place a bit indeterminate, not sci-fi, more like an off-kilter version of the recent past), every consequential decision gets made by an office that consults actuarial tables. Marriage licenses, building permits, who's allowed to keep bees, whatever. A man petitions the clerk who runs the office for something that matters enormously to him -- your choice what, but make it personal and specific -- and the answer flips depending on which table she pulls him under. As one kind of person he's a terrible risk, as another he's a fine one.

A few things I really want:

- At least three different tables/classes that are each genuinely defensible, not strawmen, and that give different verdicts on him. The drama has to be that both of them can argue for their preferred class in good faith.
- Somewhere in the story, someone pushes the logic toward the narrowest possible class -- the most specific description of this particular man -- and it should become clear that taken far enough that move makes the whole office useless, because a class of one doesn't give you a probability at all. But don't have anyone state this outright as a lesson.
- Nobody in the story says the phrase "reference class problem" or anything like it. No philosophizing dialogue. It has to work as fiction -- arguments over tea, forms being stamped, that sort of thing.
- The ending shouldn't resolve which class was right. Ideally the final decision turns out to rest on something that isn't in any table, and the reader should feel a bit sick about that.

Tone-wise: quiet, a little bureaucratic menace, no villains, definately no speechifying. If you can make me forget it's about a philosophy problem until I think about it afterwards, you've done it right.
