Last weekend I watched my roommate Maya and her boyfriend Dan nearly ruin a board-game night over a puzzle from this old cognitive science book she has, the kind where a tiny letter string changes and you have to say how a second string should change "in the same way." The first puzzle: abc changes to abd, so what does iijjkk change to? Maya said iijjll, obviously, since you take the rightmost letter and bump it one forward in the alphabet. Dan said iijjkl is at least as good: in abc there are no visible groups so you can't tell, but in iijjkk the natural unit is the letter-group, so what the example really shows is the rightmost *thing* advancing, and the kk group as a whole should advance to ll... except since a group advancing means its members advance, you get iijjll anyway — no wait, his actual answer was iijjkl, on the grounds that the rule is "advance the rightmost element" and the rightmost element of iijjkk, once you see the groups, is the group kk, and advancing a group one step in its own sequence (kk comes after jj) gives kl. Maya said this was cheating: once you're allowed to redescribe what counts as "the same thing" after seeing the target, any answer can be justified post hoc, and a puzzle where anything goes has no content. Dan said nothing goes -- obviously "iijjkk stays the same" or "aabbcc" are terrible answers -- but the puzzle never promised a deductively forced answer, it tests whether you feel the pressures in the string, and slippage between descriptions (letter vs. group) is exactly what real analogies do all the time. Maya said graded answers are fine but he still hasn't said what makes one better besides fidelity to the simplest rule, so "feel the pressures" is just her criterion wearing a hat.

Then Dan pulled out the book's nastier follow-up: abc goes to abd, so what does xyz go to? There's no letter after z, so Maya's strict rule either crashes or forces her to say the puzzle is broken, but everyone (he claimed) feels some pull toward wyz or xyd, which proves slippage is sometimes mandatory -- and once it's mandatory anywhere, it's legitimate everywhere, including his iijjkl. Maya fired back that the z case shows the opposite: slippage is a fallback you reach for when the literal rule breaks, like downshifting when a gear fails, not a license to downshift on a clear road; the xyz case is interesting precisely because it's the exception. Dan said she was just stipulating a hierarchy (literal rule first, slippage only under duress) that the puzzle itself never states, and that her "simplest rule" is only simplest relative to one way of parsing the strings, which is the very thing in dispute. At that point they both looked at me and I refused to get involved, so I'm bringing it here.

Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move -- not just who wins overall, but who's right about each specific claim? In particular: Is Dan's iijjkl answer defensible or does Maya's "anything goes" objection land? Is Dan right that the xyz case proves slippage is legitimate in general, or is Maya right that it only shows slippage is a fallback? Is Maya's "simplest rule" standard actually neutral, or is Dan right that it smuggles in a parsing of the strings? And finally, is there something both of them are missing -- e.g., a confusion between "the correct answer" as what the rule logically entails versus what a mind under competing pressures would most naturally produce, or something about what makes some redescriptions cheap and others expensive in a microdomain this constrained? I'd rather you take sides where sides can be taken than split the difference everywhere.
