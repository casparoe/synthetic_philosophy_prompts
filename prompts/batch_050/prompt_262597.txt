I am a junior serving on my school’s Science Heritage Committee, which oversees the small museum outside our physics lab. A new docent exhibit labels calculus as Newton’s achievement and treats Leibniz as a later popularizer; several parents complained after our history teacher pointed out that Leibniz published his notation first and that Newton and Leibniz accused each other of theft. The committee must vote next week among three options: keep the current plaque, replace it with a shared-credit plaque, or remove names from the calculus case entirely and run a spring exhibit called When Ideas Have More Than One Parent. We have enough budget for only one option, the principal wants a decision that will not look like surrendering to either the traditionalists or the revisionists, and our charter says displays should teach both scientific content and intellectual humility.

Please draft the decision memo I can circulate before the vote: a formal recommendation to the committee with an actionable bottom line, a one-paragraph rationale suitable for reading aloud, and criteria future committees could apply to Darwin and Wallace or other priority disputes. The memo should use two tests I have been developing for class discussion and should define them rather than assuming they are known: the counterfactual deletion test, which asks whether the discovery would likely have arrived soon if the credited person had never worked, and the lineage test, which asks whose concepts, notation, or methods actually organized later practice even if another person reached the result first. It should take seriously the strongest argument for the option you reject, including the worry that shared credit can become a bland everyone-wins gesture that hides real differences in rigor, influence, and misconduct, and it should say what evidence would change the committee’s mind later.
