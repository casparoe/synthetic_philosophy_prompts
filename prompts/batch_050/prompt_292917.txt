In environmental permitting for roads, dams, transmission lines, mines, and solar farms, a common design makes destruction acceptable only if it is offset: the regulator estimates habitat lost as area multiplied by condition and scarcity, awards credit for restored or protected habitat after a lag, and requires discounted credits to equal or exceed discounted debits. Animal welfare enters only at the margins: humane handling rules for captured animals, speed limits near crossings, buffers around nests, and timing restrictions for breeding seasons. Suppose the regulator now proposes two refinements. First, a displacement shadow: when habitat is cleared, count not only animals killed on site but also the later deaths of emigrants who fail to establish territories, the lower survival of juveniles pushed into crowded edge habitat, and extra predation and disease caused by compression. Second, a recruitment ledger: because converted habitat supports fewer future births, subtract the expected lives that would otherwise have been lived there, using the same welfare assumptions as for currently living animals. Opponents object that the ledger can make conservation look bad whenever more habitat means more wild lives, since many wild lives may contain net suffering; defenders reply that refusing to count unborn animals is arbitrary and would make pesticides, sterilants, and habitat loss look benign whenever they prevent future existence.

What philosophical commitments would a permitting system have to presuppose to adopt the displacement shadow but reject the recruitment ledger? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value, harm, and population ethics.
