"My position is that individuals can be morally responsible for collective harms even when their own contribution makes no difference. Consider sweatshop clothing. If I buy a t-shirt made in a sweatshop, the factory owner will not hire one extra worker or cut anyone's hours because of my single purchase. My purchase is neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition of the workers being exploited, so on a simple counterfactual theory of causation I caused nothing. Some people conclude from this that the purchase is morally neutral. But this commits a fallacy: it assumes that moral responsibility always requires causation. This assumption is clearly false, since we blame people all the time without showing they caused harm -- for example we blame a bystander who does nothing to help, even though the bystander caused nothing either. The better way to understand my purchase is as an act of complicity. Complicity is a legal concept from criminal law meaning you aided or abetted a crime, and the same standard applies in ethics: to be complicit you must actually intend for the wrongdoing to succeed, the way a getaway driver intends the robbery. A consumer who just wants a cheap shirt has no such intention, so strictly speaking complicity is rare and most consumers are off the hook on that charge. However, we can still fault consumers for free riding on the suffering of others, since they accept the low prices the system produces. Also, notice that if every consumer reasons 'my purchase makes no difference, so it is fine,' then the outcome is that the sweatshop system is nobody's fault, which shows the whole way of thinking must be mistaken somewhere. In conclusion, responsibility for collective harms does not depend on making a difference."
