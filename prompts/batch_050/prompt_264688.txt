I run a reading group that's been working through some conservative political thought -- Burke, and the whole Chesterton's fence idea (don't tear down a fence until you understand why someone put it up). About 10 of us, adults, sharp but not academics, one 2-hour evening session left. A few members are instinctive reformers and reckon the fence principle is just status-quo bias in folksy clothing, and the discussion keeps going in circles because everyone can simply assert their intuition about any real-world example.

Design a game we can actually play that evening that makes the fence principle land (or fail to land) in experience rather than assertion. Here's the hook I'd love, if you can make it work: the group inherits some rule-bound institution -- a trading game, a mock village council, whatever you think runs best -- containing several rules that look arbitrary or absurd, and they get a strictly limited budget of reforms they can spend abolishing or rewriting rules. Some of the weird rules should turn out to be load-bearing, ideally in a way that only shows up under conditions that rarely occur, so a rule can look pointless for many rounds before the one round where its absence wrecks everything. But don't rig it: at least one or two rules should genuinely be dead weight, so the reformers sometimes win and the takeaway isn't just "never change anything". I want them arguing about which rules are fences and which are junk, and feeling how hard that is to tell from inside.

Also cover: what happens if the group figures the trick out early, e.g. someone suspects every rule has a hidden purpose and refuses to reform anything at all -- how do you stop cautious passivity from being the dominant strategy? How do you make the hidden functions feel fair in retrospect rather than like gotchas? And I want the debrief to map specific moments of the evening onto the actual philosophical claims (the tacit-knowledge point, the problem that a rule's function may only appear under stress, the question of who bears the cost of finding out), and to be honest about the biggest disanalogy: in the game there is a designer who knows why every rule exists, whereas Burke's whole claim is that for real institutions maybe nobody does.

Format the answer with these sections in this order: "The game" (the basic pitch, materials, setup, ~200 words), "Rules of play" (the full runnable ruleset, ~350), "The hidden functions" (list of rules, which are load-bearing, which are junk, and how the rare-event mechanic works, ~250), "Running it" (timeline for the 2 hours, likely failure modes and fixes, ~200), "Debrief" (mapping the evening onto the philosophy, ~200), "The disanalogy" (what the game structurally can't reproduce and what I have to supply myself in discussion, ~100). If a real game with a rarely occuring rare-event mechanic can't be made to work in 2 hours, say so in "The game" and give me the closest thing that does work, rather than a broken version of my idea.
