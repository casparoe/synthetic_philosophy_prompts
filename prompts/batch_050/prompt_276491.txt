My dissertation advisor retires this spring after thirty-four years in the department, and I've been asked to give the main toast at her retirement dinner. I'd like you to write it. Roughly five minutes, so somewhere around 700 spoken words.

Some background you'll need. She's a philosopher of cognitive science whose career has an unusual shape: one early, much-cited paper on habitual patterns of explanation, and then decades of deliberately quieter, less fashionable work. Her private name for what she was avoiding was sphexishness -- the term philosophers borrowed from the digger wasp, which paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the entrance of its burrow, goes inside to check that all is in order, and only then pulls the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside, the wasp hauls it back to the threshold and runs the whole inspection routine again, and again, indefinitely, never registering that it's trapped in a loop. The standard worry this raises, and the one my advisor has put to every cohort of graduate students she's taught, is how any of us can be sure we're not doing the same thing with our research programs -- defending the same thesis, giving the same talk, mistaking the loop for a life.

The audience will be genuinely mixed. About half the room will be philosophers and cognitive scientists who know the wasp story and will catch every allusion; the other half will be her wife, her sisters, her neighbors, and the department's administrative staff, for whom all of this is new. The toast has to tell the wasp story well enough to land for the newcomers without patronizing the experts, and it has to land as affection rather than as a lecture about her own views.

Here is the structural work I want the sphex idea to do. The toast should raise, in good humor, the uncomfortable thought that her career might itself be exhibit A -- thirty-four years in one department, one famous paper she never stopped being asked about -- and then answer it. The answer should not simply be that she changed topics a lot, because I've come to think there's a symmetrical failure mode nobody has a good name for: the scholar who abandons every position the moment it becomes comfortable, chasing novelty the way the sphex wasp chases routine, never staying anywhere long enough to be wrong in an interesting way. Call the two failure modes rigidity and flightiness. The toast's real claim should be that her career threaded between them, and it should earn that claim with two or three concrete moments (I've listed them below) where she visibly broke a loop or visibly refused to break one.

The moments you may use: she once retracted, in print, the central example of her own famous paper after an undergraduate's objection she couldn't answer; she spent seven years on a book almost nobody read because, she told me, the question wouldn't leave her alone; and when the field's fashion swung sharply toward computational methods she neither followed nor denounced it but quietly learned enough to tell which of her own commitments it actually threatened.

Please close the toast on the free-will-adjacent question the wasp story always leaves open: what distinguishes a principled life from a rigid one, given that from the inside they can feel identical. Don't resolve it so neatly that the philosophers in the room groan; don't leave it so open that the family feels the floor drop out.

After the toast itself, add a brief note to me (not for delivery) flagging where the speech takes liberties: the classic wasp experiments have been questioned by later field researchers, and extending sphexishness from insect behavior to a scholar's character is a contested move. Identify the one line or beat in the toast where an expert in the audience is most likely to object that you've leaned too hard on a contested reading of the science or the philosophy, and say what specific consideration -- an objection raised at the dinner, a study I might look up -- would lead you to cut or soften that beat.
