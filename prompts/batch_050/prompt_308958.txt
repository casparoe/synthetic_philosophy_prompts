So I'm giving a 25 minute talk at a data visualization conference next month, and I need a prep sheet for the Q&A because I suspect it's going to be rough.

My talk is a pitch for what I call neo-Isotype. Basically I want to resurrect Otto Neurath's picture language project (the Isotype system he ran out of his Vienna museum in the 20s and 30s, with Gerd Arntz designing the pictograms and Marie Neurath doing the "transformation" work of turning data into pictures) and argue that modern dataviz has drifted away from the principles that made it good. My thesis in a nutshell: charts aimed at the general public should (1) show quantities by repeating identical symbols rather than by stretching one symbol bigger or by abstract encodings like bar length, (2) be built from a small standardized, publicly maintained symbol vocabulary, the way traffic signs are, so that reading charts is a skill you learn once and reuse everywhere, and (3) be designed for one-shot didactic communication to non-experts -- a chart that needs a tooltip or a paragraph of caption has, on Neurath's view, already failed. I argue this is more relevant now than in 1930 because the audience is everyone with a phone and the stakes (public health, elections, climate) are higher than what Neurath was dealing with.

The audience: mostly working visualization designers and a decent number of visualization researchers. These are people raised on Tufte (data-ink ratio, chartjunk) and on the grammar-of-graphics / Wilkinson lineage, plus the empirical graphical perception literature (Cleveland and McGill ranking position and length above area and volume, that whole tradition). A lot of them build interactive dashboards for a living. They're smart, opinionated, and many of them will have heard of Isotype mainly as charming vintage design.

Tell me: what objections are they actually going to raise, phrased the way they'd phrase them at the mic? Rank them by how likely x how damaging. For each, give me a short reply I can deliver in under 30 seconds and flag where the reply is weakest. What should I concede up front so I don't sound like a crackpot pushing repeating-little-man charts against the entire perceptual literature? What's the one reply that has to land for the talk to survive? And what's the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I have my guess about what it is (something about the fact that Neurath's whole system was built for top-down socialist planning education and maybe that political DNA doesn't survive transplantation into commercial dashboards and newsroom graphics), but tell me if you think it's actually something else. Also feel free to tell me if premise (3) is the real weak link and I should quietly drop it.
