Last week a friend and I got into an argument about human extinction that ran for most of an evening and ended exactly where it began. I want a diagnosis of what was actually going on, not a verdict on who was right.

The shared ground: we both agree that extinction would be bad in part because of the deaths it involves, and we both found compelling the two scenarios associated with Samuel Scheffler's work on the afterlife — one in which humanity has become universally infertile so that everyone now living dies naturally but no new people arrive, and one in which you learn that an asteroid will destroy humanity shortly after your own natural death. The point of these cases is that they involve no premature deaths, yet seem catastrophic anyway. We both felt the pull of that.

The disagreement was about what explains the catastrophe. My friend's position: every bit of the apparent badness in these scenarios reduces to harms to actual, existing people — the despair and pointlessness the infertile generation would feel, the collapse of meaning in our own projects upon learning about the asteroid. Strip out the psychology of the people affected and nothing bad remains; there is no subject left for anything to be bad for. My position: this explanation presupposes what it claims to explain. The despair is intelligible only because something worth despairing over has occurred; if nothing were lost beyond the deaths, the despair would be a confusion, and it does not seem like one. I also pressed that my friend's view makes the badness of extinction hostage to contingent facts about how the last humans happen to feel, which seems backwards.

Where we stalled: I accused my friend of smuggling the value of humanity's continuation into the word "despair"; my friend accused me of treating a strong emotion as evidence for an impersonal value that no one can say anything further about. My working hypothesis is that this is really a disagreement about whether badness requires a subject — that my friend is a person-affecting theorist in denial and I am smuggling in an impersonal axiology I cannot defend. But I am not confident of this. It might instead be a factual disagreement about the explanation of our own reactions, or two different questions under one heading — why extinction matters to those living through it versus whether it is bad simpliciter.

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement using the moves each of us actually made, assess my hypothesis against the alternatives, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction both sides could accept, a piece of psychology that would bear on it, a different way of carving the question — or an argument that this kind of disagreement has no resolution and why.
