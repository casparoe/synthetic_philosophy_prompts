In a reading group on free will, someone ended a discussion of Frankfurt-style cases by declaring that a person is morally responsible for what they did only if they could have done otherwise — as if that slogan by itself settled whether Frankfurt's counterexamples succeed. I suspect the slogan is doing more work than its surface suggests, since "could have done otherwise" can be cashed out in several quite different ways. Reconstruct the distinct precise theses the principle of alternative possibilities might be expressing: at minimum, consider what the alternatives are supposed to be alternatives *to* (the action, the decision, the outcome), when the agent must have had access to them, how robust or morally significant they need to be, and whether the principle governs responsibility generally or only blame. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or likely false, and — most importantly — which readings the Frankfurt-style counterexample actually targets and which it leaves untouched, so I can tell whether my colleague's invocation of the slogan was to the point or merely a reflex.
