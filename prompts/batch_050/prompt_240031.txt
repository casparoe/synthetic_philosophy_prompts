I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been thinking about Peter Singer’s drowning-child analogy and recently heard two friends disagree about how far it extends. I would like you to adjudicate their disagreement.

Assume, purely for the sake of argument, that there is a highly reliable charity through which a $200 donation will save one child who would otherwise die, and that the donor will not be needed to make another rescue immediately afterward.

Their exchange ran as follows:

1. Elena said that anyone who would ruin a $200 pair of shoes to save a drowning child must also be willing to donate $200 when doing so would reliably save a child abroad. Geographic distance, physical visibility, and the fact that the child is a stranger cannot by themselves explain why one rescue is obligatory and the other optional.

2. Marcus replied that the analogy overlooks three important differences. In the pond case, there is one identifiable rescuer, the rescue is certain to succeed, and the emergency appears to be a one-off event. Charitable giving usually involves many potential rescuers, imperfect information, and needs that will recur indefinitely. Singer’s example therefore establishes a duty to rescue, but not necessarily an unlimited duty to donate.

3. Elena accepted that many people could donate, but denied that this weakens anyone’s responsibility. If one hundred people are standing around a pond and only one person needs to jump in, each person still has a reason to ensure that the child is saved. Nobody could excuse themselves merely by saying that somebody else was equally capable of acting.

4. Marcus agreed that multiple potential rescuers do not eliminate responsibility, but said they change its content. If one hundred people can rescue one child at a total cost of $200, it does not follow that each person owes $200. Each might instead owe a fair share of the cost, plus cooperation in deciding who will act. Elena’s interpretation would generate one hundred separate $200 duties where only $200 is needed.

5. Elena answered that fair-share reasoning is inadequate when nobody has assurance that the others will contribute. If each person gives only $2 and the fund remains $198 short, the child dies. Since people cannot make their conduct depend entirely on others doing the right thing, each person retains a backup duty to provide more when the collective effort fails.

6. Marcus accepted the possibility of a backup duty but argued that it must have some limit. If every failed collective effort makes each bystander fully responsible, and if new emergencies continually arise, then every financially secure person could be required to surrender nearly all discretionary resources. That would leave people unable to maintain stable commitments to their families, communities, health, or long-term projects. He concluded that the main duty may be to help establish a reliable rescue institution, together with a temporary duty to fill gaps at significant but not personally ruinous cost.

7. Elena objected that ruinous is too convenient a stopping point. Marcus had not provided a principled boundary between a serious sacrifice and an excessive one, so his proposed limit could simply reflect whatever level of comfort the potential rescuer is reluctant to give up. The fact that there may be future drowning children does not seem to justify refusing to rescue the child presently in front of us.

8. Marcus replied that the limit need not be based on comfort. A one-time loss of $200 might be trivial within a person’s life as a whole, while indefinitely giving up every resource beyond bare subsistence would fundamentally alter that life. Repeated small sacrifices must be assessed cumulatively, not as though each were an isolated muddy-shoes incident. He said one can therefore accept an extremely demanding duty of rescue without accepting that morally decent people must erase all personal priorities.

Please evaluate each numbered move as clearly correct, partly correct, or mistaken, explaining your judgment in ordinary language rather than by appealing to philosophical authorities. In particular, address:

- whether distance, visibility, and identifiability are morally relevant differences, or merely psychologically salient ones;
- whether the presence of many possible rescuers changes the strength of each person’s duty, the content of that duty, or both;
- whether a fair-share view can answer Elena’s assurance problem without collapsing into her backup-duty view;
- whether Marcus can defend a sacrifice limit without making the limit arbitrary;
- and whether the analogy supports direct individual donations, the creation of institutions for collective rescue, or some combination of the two.

End with a point-by-point verdict on who has the better position overall, while identifying any important issue that both speakers have overlooked.
