I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I'm putting together the take-home final. The course spent several weeks on epiphenomenalism, and I've found that students can all recite the standard worry about it — that if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then our beliefs and utterances about our own experiences cannot be caused by those experiences, so it's mysterious how we could ever know about or speak truly of them — but very few can say precisely why this is a problem for the epiphenomenalist specifically rather than for everyone, or what moves are actually available in reply. I want one essay question that forces them past the recitation.

To that end, I'd like the question to be built around a thought experiment I'm calling the Bypass: imagine a creature physically and functionally identical to an ordinary human, except that its verbal reports and beliefs about its own experiences are produced by a dedicated "phenomenal report module" whose outputs are hardwired — when the module is in state R, the creature says and believes that it is vividly experiencing red, regardless of what, if anything, is going on at the phenomenal level. Now consider two versions of the creature: one in which the module's outputs happen always to match the creature's epiphenomenal experiences, and one in which the module has been rewired so that its reports systematically mismatch them. The question should ask students to use this pair of cases to articulate exactly what the epiphenomenalist's problem about phenomenal judgment is: whether the trouble is best framed as a failure of knowledge, a failure of reliability, a failure of reference, or a failure of the experiences to explain anything about our judgments; whether an epiphenomenalist can insist that phenomenal beliefs are partly constituted by, rather than caused by, the experiences they concern, and whether that constitutive maneuver saves self-knowledge or merely relocates the mystery; and whether the materialist or interactionist dualist is really better off, given that they face their own questions about how experience and judgment are supposed to be linked.

Please write the exam question itself (phrased as it would appear on the paper, answerable in roughly two hours), then a model answer of about 600–700 words written at the level of a strong undergraduate, and finally a marking scheme out of 30 marks. The marking scheme should identify the specific moves that distinguish an A answer from a B answer — for example, I want it to distinguish students who merely state that causally inert qualia can't cause judgments from students who can explain why the Bypass shows that even perfect correlation between experience and report isn't enough, and who can assess the constitutive reply rather than just name it. For each marking criterion, include a one-sentence note on the way students typically get that point wrong.
