I TA for an undergraduate course in democratic theory, and after last week's section on the boundary problem — the question of who gets to belong to the people in whose name decisions are made — one of my strongest students stayed behind and walked me through the following argument. I promised her a written response before Thursday's section, and I am stuck. Here it is, as I've reconstructed it. (1) A political decision is legitimate for a person only if that person had a fair opportunity to influence it; being significantly affected by a decision while having no say in it is exactly the situation democratic institutions are supposed to rule out. (2) The climate, trade, monetary, and immigration policies of large wealthy states significantly affect the interests of billions of people who are not citizens of those states — often more significantly, she notes, than those policies affect many citizens, since a tariff can destroy a foreign livelihood while merely annoying a domestic consumer. (3) So those non-citizens are entitled to a say in those policies. (4) An entitlement that cannot be exercised is not an entitlement but a condolence; honoring (3) therefore requires institutionalizing it — some form of transnational franchise, weighted external vote, or veto for affected outsiders. (5) But no bounded polity could function under (4): every decision draws a different circle of the affected, so the demos would have to be reconstituted vote by vote, and wherever the circle is large, outsiders would systematically outnumber insiders on the insiders' own domestic questions, which collapses self-government rather than extending it. (6) So (1) is false: being significantly affected by a decision does not, by itself, entitle anyone to a say in making it. (7) But if affectedness confers no entitlement, then the only remaining grounds for drawing the boundary are birth and present residence — accidents of geography and records of prior power — and a demos constituted by sheer accident cannot claim democratic legitimacy for the coercive decisions it then imposes on the outsiders it excluded.

She then added what she called the finishing move: any attempt to settle the boundary by democratic means already presupposes a demos entitled to do the settling, so the boundary can never itself be legitimated, and every democracy accordingly rests on a founding exclusion it cannot justify. Her conclusion is not that democracy is difficult to justify but that democratic legitimacy is an incoherent ideal — a concept that cannot be instantiated, only approximated by fraud.

I would like to reject this, obviously, but I want to reject it honestly. My own sense is that something went wrong somewhere between (3) and (6), yet every time I try to name the slip I catch myself denying things I actually believe — that it really is objectionable for people to be ruled by decisions they had no chance to influence, or that the cross-border effects in (2) are real, large, and morally non-negotiable. I would strongly prefer to keep (1) in some recognizable form, and I will not accept the incoherence conclusion, since it would make a sham of the rest of the syllabus I am supposed to be teaching. Which step do I reject? Explain why that step looked so compelling in the first place — I want to be able to show my student that the argument was worth making, not just where it fails — and then price the rejection honestly: what do I give up by rejecting it, what can I still say about cases like long-term resident non-citizens who are governed in every aspect of their lives without a vote, and does whatever survives my diagnosis give me any principled way to draw the boundary, or only a less embarrassing way to shrug at it?
