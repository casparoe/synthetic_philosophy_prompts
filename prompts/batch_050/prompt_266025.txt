I keep noticing a pattern in how people judge risky activities, and I would like to know whether it has an established name. In my town everyone drives, so each of us imposes a small risk of death on everyone else; nobody feels wronged by this, and a proposal to eliminate the risk by banning cars would strike us as absurd. Now suppose a firm wants to route trucks carrying mildly hazardous material through the same town, adding to each resident a risk smaller than the one each of us already imposes on others by driving. Nearly everyone finds this objectionable without the residents' permission or some payment — even though each resident's total risk barely rises. So the moral status of exposing others to risk seems to depend not on the size of the exposure but on whether it runs in both directions: apparently I may endanger you if you endanger me in the same way, or perhaps if I at least share the risk I impose. Is there an established concept for this two-way condition and its justifying force? It does not seem to be consent, since nobody agreed to the driving arrangement, nor compensation, since nothing is paid. If the concept exists, I would also like its edges: must the same two individuals endanger each other, or does it suffice that everyone in a community endangers someone; and does it matter that I cannot realistically opt out of imposing my share? Please indicate how confident you are in your identification, and single out the point you are least sure of.
