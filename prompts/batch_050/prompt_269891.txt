For my high school philosophy club's unit on ancient Chinese thought, I am designing a "which reading of the white horse dialogue do you actually accept?" decision tree, and I would like you to build it for me. The dialogue — Gongsun Long's White Horse Discourse (the Baima lun), associated with the School of Names — defends the thesis that a white horse is not a horse. It does so, roughly, by pointing out that a request for a horse is satisfied by a horse of any color while a request for a white horse is not, and by insisting that "white" picks out a color while "horse" picks out a shape, so the combined expression cannot simply amount to "horse." Interpreters offer many competing readings of what is going on, and my club members keep adopting labels without being able to say what those labels commit them to. The tree's job is to sort us by our actual views about language, negation, and kinds — not by which interpretation sounds most sophisticated.

Please follow these constraints:

1. The tree should begin from the one observation nearly everyone grants — the asymmetry about requests above — and branch through eight to ten questions before reaching a leaf. Each branching node must be a question a classmate could answer without having heard of Gongsun Long, and it should be anchored in a fresh test case rather than in the dialogue itself. Some cases I want used: whether "a toy gun is not a gun" is simply true; whether "a former senator is not a senator" and "a stone lion is not a lion" pattern with "a white horse is not a horse" or against it; whether someone who steals your white horse has thereby stolen a horse, and what that entailment does to the thesis; and whether the "is not" in the thesis should be heard as denying identity, denying kind-membership, denying coextensiveness of two classes, or something else entirely.

2. The leaves (six to eight of them) must include at least the following readings: (a) the equivocation verdict — the argument trades on sliding between two senses of "is not" and simply fails; (b) the strict identity or class-inclusion reading — the thesis says only that white horses are not identical to, or coextensive with, horses, which is true but trivial and not really paradoxical; (c) the intensional reading — the concept white horse differs in meaning from the concept horse even though every white horse falls under horse; (d) the mereological or compound reading — a white horse is a compound of whiteness and horseness, and a compound of two things is identical to neither; (e) the pragmatic or contextual reading — the sentence comes out true in some conversational settings and false in others, and the dialogue establishes only the first; (f) the rectification-of-names reading — the point is about disciplining names so they track realities for the sake of social and political order, not about logic or metaphysics at all.

3. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first lay out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the actual readings in that space. Mark at least two unoccupied combinations and diagnose each briefly — is nobody there because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored?

4. One node must separate readings that treat the dialogue as concerned with how names latch onto things — the School of Names' broader preoccupation with the fit between names and actualities — from readings that treat it as a free-standing piece of logic with no social dimension whatsoever.

5. At every leaf, state in one or two sentences what that reading is forced to say about the plain everyday truth that a white horse is a horse — that is, what it costs to hold the reading.

6. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and for each such pair specify what further question would be needed to pull them apart.

Present the tree in an indented or numbered format so I can print it as a flowchart handout, and keep the whole instrument to one page.
