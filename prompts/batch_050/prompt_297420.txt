Here are two decision-theoretic puzzles, stated precisely so you need no outside knowledge of them. In the counterfactual mugging, a reliable predictor flips a fair coin; if it lands heads, the predictor approaches you and demands $100, explaining that had it landed tails, the predictor would have given you $1,000 if and only if it predicted you would pay in the heads branch. In XOR blackmail, a blackmailer has sworn to send you a letter demanding $100 under threat of releasing your secrets exactly when a certain costly event (say, a $1,000 loss you cannot otherwise detect in time) has not occurred, so that receiving the letter is evidence the loss did not happen, and paying buys the blackmailer's silence about information that is actually worthless to you.

Construct a single unified thought experiment in which one agent faces both a counterfactual-mugging-style demand and an XOR-blackmail-style demand, engineered so that an agent who chooses policies rather than individual acts — one who asks which standing policy, had it been known in advance, would have produced the best expected outcome — pays in one case and refuses in the other. Constraints: (a) keep the story as ordinary as the puzzles permit — the predictor's reliability about the agent's publicly known policy may be stipulated, but there is no mind-reading beyond that policy, no exotic physics, and all payoffs are sums of money a normal person could gain or lose; (b) the two demands must be as structurally similar as possible — same stakes, same kind of counterparty, same informational packaging — so that exactly one feature of the setup differs between them; (c) the divergent verdicts must be derived from the agent's policy-selection reasoning, not stipulated. After presenting the scenario, (1) walk through the reasoning that yields payment in one case and refusal in the other, (2) identify the single structural feature that does the work and explain what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed, and (3) give the strongest objection available to someone who holds that the two puzzles are the same problem in different dress and that any policy-based agent must treat them alike. The purpose of the exercise is to test whether the distinction between these puzzles is principled or cosmetic, so a scenario that smuggles in the desired verdicts through payoff asymmetries or ad hoc stipulation counts as failure. Conclude by stating whether your construction shows the puzzles differ in kind or only in degree, and what it would take to change your mind.
