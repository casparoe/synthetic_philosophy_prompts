Two colleagues of mine sit with me on the allocation committee of a small grant-making foundation, and they keep having the same argument about our prioritization method. We score candidate cause areas on three factors — importance (how much good would be done if the problem were fully solved), tractability (the share of the problem that additional resources would solve), and neglectedness (the resources already directed at the problem by others) — and combine them multiplicatively into a priority score. Their argument runs as follows:

(1) Marta holds that the score is the decision rule: compute importance × tractability × neglectedness for each cause and direct the next tranche of funding to the highest scorer. Any departure from that ranking, she says, is just intuition smuggled in under the guise of rigor.

(2) Daniel replies that the score is at best a proxy for the only quantity that actually matters — expected good per marginal dollar — and that it is a biased proxy, because neglectedness is double-counted: the reason a crowded cause offers poor marginal opportunities is that the best interventions have already been funded, and that fact is already reflected in the cause's tractability.

(3) Marta answers that tractability is almost never estimated at the margin in practice; it is estimated as a judgment about the problem as a whole — how solvable it is in general. Neglectedness is exactly the correction that converts that aggregate judgment into a marginal one, so including it is not double-counting but repairing an estimate that would otherwise be systematically optimistic about crowded causes.

(4) Daniel responds that this repair works only if returns diminish in a particular way — roughly, if the fraction of the problem solved per dollar shrinks in proportion to the resources already spent. Where a problem has thresholds or increasing returns — a research program that yields nothing until a critical mass of funding is reached, say — the multiplicative correction points the wrong way, treating an underfunded cause as unpromising precisely when its marginal dollar is most valuable. He concludes that the three factors should serve as a checklist for what to think about, while rankings, where feasible, should be built directly from marginal cost-effectiveness estimates.

(5) Marta's final move is that those estimates are themselves constructed from the same three judgments, only less transparently; making the multiplication explicit forces comparable units and exposes the assumptions, whereas his preferred method re-derives the same arithmetic informally, with more room for motivated reasoning. The threshold cases he invokes, she adds, are rare enough that the multiplicative score remains the right default.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; say who is more right on the specific charge of double-counting; and identify anything important that both parties are missing.
