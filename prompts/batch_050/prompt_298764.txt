I'm writing a piece on the quiet return of the draft in northern Europe -- Sweden mothballed conscription in 2010 and then brought it back, Finland never let it go, and Swiss voters keep rejecting attempts to scrap their militia system. Here's the angle I can't crack: these are some of the most liberal, individualist countries on earth, yet they claim something no tax authority would dare claim -- a chunk of your actual life and body, not just your income. And almost nobody in the debate frames it the way we frame taxation, even though it's a much deeper taking. So what does conscription as actually practiced there presuppose? That citizenship is a debt you owe, that national defense is categorically different from other public goods, that a war is only legitimate if everyone's kid might have to fight it? And which of those does the practice strictly need, versus just fit nicely with? One more wrinkle: Sweden and Finland both let you do some form of civilian service instead, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a humane add-on or whether it smuggles in a completly different theory of what the state is owed. If these systems were rebuilt on a purely voluntarist, contractarian view of citizenship, what would concretely have to change?
