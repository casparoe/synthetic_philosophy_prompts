I drafted the following argument for a workshop on normative ethics, and the conclusion is one I refuse to accept, so something has to give. The setup is the standard hard case for moral constraints: five innocent people will be murdered by others unless you yourself murder one innocent person; there is no third option, no way to intervene without the killing, and no special relationship between you and anyone involved. Here is the argument:

(1) A murder is a grave moral evil, and, other things equal, five murders constitute a graver evil than one.

(2) If one available outcome contains a graver evil than another available outcome, and you can determine which obtains, then, other things equal, you ought to bring about the lesser evil.

(3) If you murder the one, the outcome contains exactly one murder; if you refrain, the outcome contains exactly five. So by (1) and (2), you ought to murder the one.

(4) But there is a moral constraint against murdering an innocent person, which is precisely what the case is designed to test: the bystander who becomes a killer to save the five acts wrongly, even so.

(5) (1)–(3) are each independently plausible and jointly entail the negation of (4). Therefore either the constraint is irrational, or at least one of (1)–(3) must be rejected.

My first thought was to reject (1): to say that the evil of a murder is not the kind of thing that aggregates, so that five murders are not a "graver evil" than one in the relevant sense. But then I noticed that this seems to prove too much in the other direction. If the badness of murders does not aggregate, it is hard to see why we should prefer a world with fewer murders to a world with more when the choice is not ours — why, for instance, we should hope the five are spared rather than being indifferent — and it seems to make nonsense of ordinary trade-offs among deaths that everyone accepts, such as diverting harm from five to one in pure rescue cases where no one is killed by anyone. So I would rather keep (1) and the constraint in (4) than reject either, which leaves (2) and (3) under pressure, and I suspect the trouble is somewhere in the move from the comparative badness of outcomes in (1) to what I, in particular, ought to do in (2)–(3). But I cannot make the suspicion precise, and when I have floated it, the reply I get is that from the standpoint of the six potential victims a murder is a murder regardless of whose hands commit it, and that refusing to minimize murders out of concern for my own moral record looks like self-indulgence rather than principle.

Diagnose the argument. Identify the exact premise or inferential step that should be rejected, explain why it looked innocent, and state what rejecting it commits me to — including what I owe the victims'-standpoint objection once the diagnosis is in place, and whether accepting the diagnosis in the murder case forces me to say anything uncomfortable about pure rescue cases where no agent-relative wrong is at issue. I am not asking for a menu of the standard positions with the choice left to me: I want a verdict. Tell me which step goes, defend that choice over the alternatives, and be explicit about the cost of paying it.
