I'm in a historiography seminar and we just spent two weeks on the Fischer controversy and the old fight over the war guilt clause (Article 231, the one in Versailles that saddled Germany with responsibility for WWI). Half the class keeps saying things like "Germany was responsible for the war" as if it's a perfectly normal claim, and the other half insists it's a category error -- nations don't have minds, don't form intentions, so they literally cannot be responsible the way people can, and the whole clause was just victors' politics dressed up as moral judgment. I realized I don't actually know what I'd want from a theory that settles this, so before I read more, write me the desiderata: what would a theory of national responsibility for a war have to get right to count as adequate?

Give me the requirements list with the case or consideration motivating each one. Some things I suspect belong on it, but push back or add: it has to handle the counterfactual dimension (if the war would have happened anyway had some leader died in 1913, does that change anything?); it has to respect the distinction between causing a war and being blameworthy for it, and between a government, a nation, and the individuals in it (conscripts who voted for nobody seem different from the general staff); it needs to say something about whether responsibility decays over time -- can present-day Germans still bear it, and if not, when exactly did it lapse and why then; it has to be consistent with whatever we'd say about easier cases like WWII, where basically everyone agrees on responsibility, and ideally also with harder ones like wars nobody wanted but stumbled into. Also: does the theory have to justify the practical consequences people hang on responsibility (reparations, apologies, guilt clauses) or can it treat those as a separate question? Flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied -- I have a hunch "nations are nothing over and above individuals" and "responsibility can outlive every individual involved" might collide, but I'm not sure.

If it helps, briefly check the main contenders against the list -- a straightforward collectivist view (nations are agents, full stop), an individualist reduction (all responsibility-talk about nations is shorthand for claims about leaders and citizens), and a pragmatist/forward-looking view (responsibility attributions are justified by what they do, e.g. reparations and reconciliation, not by metaphysics). But keep the weight on the criteria themselves, since that's where I actually want the thinking -- and tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidates.
