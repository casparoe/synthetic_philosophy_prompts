A few of us have been arguing about "cancel culture" in the pub for weeks and we keep going in circles, so I want to try a different tack. Rather than debate the definition, I'm going to list my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and ask whether there's actually a coherent principle underneath them, because I suspect there might not be. Here goes. (1) A famous comedian makes a cruel joke about a marginalised group, audiences complain, and his streaming special gets dropped — fine by me, that's just people choosing what to watch and a company responding. (2) A 28-year-old researcher gets disinvited from a conference panel because of tweets from when she was fifteen, which she has long since deleted and apologised for — this strikes me as plainly wrong, almost regardless of what the tweets said. (3) Thousands of strangers email a small bakery's suppliers demanding they cut ties because the owner donated to a legal but controversial political campaign — this feels wrong to me too, though I struggle to say why it differs from (1). (4) A warehouse worker is filmed outside of work saying something offensive, the video goes viral, and his employer sacks him even though his job involves no contact with the public — feels like a massive overreaction, but I'd be comfortable with the same employer sacking a schoolteacher for the same video, which is maybe inconsistent of me. (5) A professor publishes an unpopular paper and three hundred colleagues sign an open letter calling it shoddy and harmful, but she keeps her job and her platform — this feels entirely legitimate, just criticism, even though I imagine it feels horrendous to be on the recieving end of it and the sheer pile-on is part of what critics of "cancellation" object to. (6) A university quietly removes an author's books from a first-year reading list after similar controversy — this bothers me more than (1) even though no one is stopping anyone reading the books, I think because an institution is deciding on students' behalf rather than audiences deciding for themselves.

My tentative principle was that social sanction is legitimate when it's "market-like" — individuals withdrawing their attention, money, or custom — and illegitimate when it's "punitive", meaning coordinated pressure aimed at getting someone fired, deplatformed, or excluded rather than simply not engaging with them. But my own verdicts break this: (3) is coordinated economic withdrawal and I'd still call it wrong, while in (4) I want the schoolteacher sacked, which is punishment pure and simple. So maybe the real work is being done by something else — proportionality, whether the sanction fits the actual job someone does, whether there's any path to redemption, whether an intermediary is making choices for third parties? If there's a principled account that captures most of these verdicts, tell me what it is, state it precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases (what does it say about, e.g., a boycott aimed not at the offender but at an award giving them a lifetime honour, or a whole country being sanctioned for its government's actions?), and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. If no single principle survives, tell me which two of my verdicts are genuinely irreconcilable and which one you'd drop.
