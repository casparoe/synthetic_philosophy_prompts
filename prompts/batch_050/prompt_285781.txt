Construct a thought experiment in which two Nyāya pramāṇas deliver contradictory verdicts about one and the same proposition, and in which the conflict cannot be waved away as carelessness on either side. The specifications: an agent arrives at the belief that p by inference (anumāna) grounded in a genuine perception, where the inference instantiates a formally valid five-membered syllogism whose pervasion (vyāpti) the agent has properly established; and the same agent receives testimony (śabda) asserting not-p from a speaker who satisfies the classical conditions for being an āpta — direct acquaintance with the matter, competence to convey it, and no intention to deceive. Constraints: the scenario must be drawn from ordinary life (a farmhouse, a courtroom, a market), must fit in one paragraph, and must involve no appeal to scripture or to any subject matter knowable only through testimony; the proposition at issue must be one that is in principle perceptible, so that both pramāṇas are genuinely operative within the same domain rather than talking past each other.

After the scenario, do the following. (1) Lay out the inference explicitly: the pakṣa, sādhya, and hetu; the vyāpti; and the perceptual observation on which the reasoning rests, showing that none of the five hetvābhāsas is present. (2) Show why the utterance qualifies as śabda-pramāṇa rather than mere rumor or hearsay. (3) Adjudicate as a Naiyāyika would: given the standard doctrine that the pramāṇas do not conflict — each being an instrument of true cognition within its proper domain — determine whether the case as constructed forces a revision of that doctrine, or whether one of the two cognitions must, on inspection, turn out not to have issued from a properly functioning pramāṇa after all; identify which one fails, and what defect (doṣa) or extrinsic defeater exposes the failure. (4) Isolate the single feature of the scenario doing the philosophical work, and show what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into a banal report of human error, or into the familiar observation that inference can outrun its evidence? (5) Close with the strongest objection a defender of the non-conflict thesis could press against the case, and assess whether that objection dissolves the puzzle or merely re-describes it.
