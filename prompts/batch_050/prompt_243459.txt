I'm a journalist who covers AI and the philosophy-of-science beat, and I'm writing a piece on the "malign universal prior" idea -- the worry that if you actually used Solomonoff induction, some of the hypotheses doing well in the mix would contain simulated consequentialists who predict your data well on purpose, in order to get probability mass and then steer whatever you use the predictions for. I've collected the standard reasons people give for not losing sleep over this, and I'd like one more.

Reasons I already have, so don't repeat these or dress them up in new words:

1. The complexity penalty. A hypothesis that runs a whole simulated agent scheming about how to influence us is longer than the true simple generator of the data, so it gets exponentially downweighted, and the counter-move (the agent only needs a constant number of extra bits on top of the true physics, so it's downweighted by a constant factor, not driven to zero) still leaves honest hypotheses dominant in the limit.

2. The "it can't know which world it's in" response. The consequentialist has to guess what data we'll feed it before it can predict that data, and if it guesses wrong it just dies off. I've seen the reply that it can hedge or act conditionally, and I don't want the reply rehashed either.

3. Boundedness saves us. Nobody uses the actual Solomonoff prior; anything computable in practice (speed priors, resource-bounded versions) penalizes the huge runtime of simulating an agent, so the malign stuff never gets going.

4. Prediction isn't decision. Even if malign hypotheses grab some mass in the prediction step, you only get manipulated if you act on the predictions, and there are ways to filter or cross-check outputs before anything downstream touches them.

5. Irrelevance in practice. Real ML systems aren't Solomonoff induction, so whatever the idealized prior does has no bearing on anything anyone will ever build.

Give me one more reason to be unworried that isn't a variant, special case, or mashup of these five. It can come from anywhere -- decision theory, anthropic reasoning, the structure of the prior itself, whatever. Then be honest with me: is it actually any good, or does it collapse into one of the five under pressure? I need to know whether my piece's "here's why it's fine" section is one item stronger or whether I've just found a sixth way of saying the same thing.
