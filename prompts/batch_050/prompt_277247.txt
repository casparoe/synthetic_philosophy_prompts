I'm preparing a handout for an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of logic, and I want to give students a decision tree that classifies the main responses to the regress argument in Lewis Carroll's Achilles-and-the-Tortoise dialogue. In the dialogue, the Tortoise grants Achilles the premises of a Euclidean inference but refuses to draw the conclusion unless each successive conditional (if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true; and so on) is written down as an additional premise — generating an infinite regress, since each new premise still requires a fresh step to be used. The standard moral is that an inference rule like modus ponens cannot be treated as just one more premise, but philosophers disagree sharply about what follows for the justification of deduction itself, and my students reliably conflate positions that should be kept apart. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: start at the Tortoise's challenge and let each branch be labeled by a question someone could answer without knowing in advance which position it lands them in.

The distinctions I care most about, and which should therefore show up as branch questions, include: whether the regress shows merely that rules are not premises (a point about the form of inference, leaving justification untouched) or something deeper about justification as such; whether deduction stands in need of justification at all, or only of an explanation of why we are entitled to rely on it; whether, if a justification is attempted, rule-circularity — using modus ponens within the argument that modus ponens is valid — is acceptable, in contrast to premise-circularity, which plainly is not; whether logical truths can instead be grounded in conventions or our meaning-conferring practices, and whether such accounts fall to a regress of their own; and whether a skeptic who simply refuses to reason deductively can be answered at all. I would like roughly six to eight leaves. Among the occupied leaves I expect to find at least: the deflationary view that the regress has been dissolved once the premise/rule confusion is cleared up; a rule-circular validation on behalf of the already-reasoning subject; a conventionalist or meaning-based grounding; the view associated with Gilbert Harman that logic is not a theory of reasoning and so the demand for justification is misdirected; and a pessimistic leaf holding that no non-circular justification is possible and that deduction simply cannot be defended to a skeptic. Locate actual named proponents at the occupied leaves (names only — no paper titles or citations needed). If a coherent combination of answers is unoccupied, include it as a leaf anyway and diagnose briefly whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or neglect. Where two leaves end up closer than their labels suggest, flag the pair explicitly instead of papering over it.

Present the tree first as indented text, with each internal node phrased as a question and each edge labeled by the answer. Then, after the tree, give a JSON array with one object per leaf, using exactly these fields: "position" (a short name), "occupied" (true or false), "path" (an object mapping each branch question, stated briefly, to the answer taken), "proponents" (a list of names, empty if unoccupied), "core_commitment" (one sentence), "most_awkward_consequence" (one sentence on the hardest thing a defender must swallow, or for an unoccupied leaf, why the seat is empty), and "separation_notes" (a string naming any other leaf this one is hard to distinguish from, or null). Keep the questions concrete enough that a student who has read the dialogue but nothing else could answer them.
