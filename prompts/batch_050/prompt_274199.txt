I've been circling utilitarianism for years and what finally pushed me over the edge wasn't the usual intuitive pumps about trolley cases or whatever -- it was Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. My current position, roughly: if each person's good is representable by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function, and social welfare has to satisfy the same rationality axioms, and social welfare respects unanimous individual rankings, then the math forces social welfare to be a weighted sum of individual utilities, and once you add impartiality you get equal weights and you're a utilitarian whether you like it or not. I like that this isn't a vibe, it's a theorem. When people object to utilitarianism I keep catching myself saying: ok, but which premise exactly are you rejecting?

Since this result is now load-bearing for a bunch of my other views (population ethics, discounting, how I think about cost-benefit analysis at my day job), I want a serious stress-test before I build more on top of it. What would count against the claim that Harsanyi gives utilitarianism something close to a proof? I know some standard pressure points -- Diamond's fairness example where a coin flip over who gets the good looks ex ante better even though it's ex post equivalent to just handing it over, the complaint that social preferences don't have to be expected-utility-rational in the way individual ones arguably do, and the line I associate with Broome and Sen that the theorem only pins down a representation and never tells you the weights are welfare weights rather than anything else. But I want the full map: which objections, if they work, kill the argument outright; which only force a retreat to something weaker (ex ante prioritarianism? a fixed-population average view? something that keeps separability but drops impartiality?); and which are merely awkward. I'm especially unsure what to say about the separability of the social ordering across people and states of the world -- people keep telling me that's where the real action is and I honestly can't tell if that's right or just something people say.

Two more asks. First, distinguish between objections the theorem's defenders would themselves concede as damaging versus ones they'd dismiss as changing the subject, since I want to know what's internal pressure and what's external. Second, tell me where your own reasoning here is most likely to be going wrong: if you end up concluding that the theorem delivers less than I think, name the consideration that would flip you to my side (say, if you think I'm overstating what impartiality buys you, say what would convince you it buys utilitarianism outright rather than just a utilitarian-flavored functional form). No need for citations or a reading list, I can track down sources myself -- I want the arguments and a sense of how exposed my position actually is.
