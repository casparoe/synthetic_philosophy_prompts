My mate's economics course notes summarise Marx's theory of exploitation like this: 'Marx's argument that capitalism is exploitative rests on the labour theory of value. Labour is the source of all value, so a worker who produces £100 worth of value in a day but is paid £60 is simply being underpaid. Exploitation is therefore a kind of cheating in the wage bargain: the capitalist pays less for the worker's labour than that labour is worth, much as a shopkeeper might short-change a customer. Raise wages to fair levels and the exploitation disappears. This is also what Marx means by alienation — the worker is "alienated" from the portion of value he created that the capitalist pockets.'

My mate reckons this shows Marx's whole theory stands or falls with the labour theory of value, and since nobody buys that any more, exploitation-talk is dead. I'm doubtful — I have a vague memory that Marx thought exploitation happens even when workers are paid the full value of what they sell, which doesn't fit the short-changing story at all. Could you put on your hat as a careful Marx scholar and audit this summary? Go through it piece by piece and seperate what's accurate, what's a defensible simplification, and what Marx would flatly reject. Then tell me whether my mate's conclusion survives the corrections. Think carefully before answering, especially about where exactly the surplus is supposed to come from on Marx's own account.
