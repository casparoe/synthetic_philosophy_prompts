I'm helping run a reading group on the metaphysics of fiction, and at our last meeting we got nowhere on the question of when a sentence containing a name like Sherlock Holmes is true. To push the discussion forward, I wrote down my own verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like you to find the principle -- if there is one -- that systematizes them. Here they are. First, an ordinary claim like "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street," said by someone talking about the stories, strikes me as plainly true, while "Sherlock Holmes was married" is plainly false. Second, "Sherlock Holmes had an elder brother in the government service" seems true even to someone who has only seen a film adaptation and never read a word of Conan Doyle. Third, "Sherlock Holmes was born on January 6, 1854" feels to me like it has no determinate truth value at all, since the stories never settle it. Fourth, "Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any detective who ever actually existed" strikes me as true, and not merely true-relative-to-the-fiction -- it seems to say something about our world. Fifth, "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" is true. Sixth, "Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character created by Arthur Conan Doyle" is also true, and I realize this sits uneasily with the fifth verdict, since it's hard to see how something can bear the property of being a fictional character if there is nothing there to bear it. Seventh, if an adult who mistakenly believes Holmes was a real historical figure says "Holmes was the finest detective of the Victorian era," I think that person has said something false, even though the same words from a knowledgeable speaker talking about the stories would be fine. Eighth, since one story puts Watson's war wound in his shoulder and another puts it in his leg, I think neither "Watson's wound was in his shoulder" nor its negation is simply true of the Holmes canon as a whole.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of principles, delivers these verdicts -- or as many of them as can coherently be saved -- stating it precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases on my own. I'm especially interested in how your principle handles the comparison in my fourth case, which seems to resist any account that just prefixes everything with "in the fiction," and in what you do with the tension between my fifth and sixth verdicts, since I suspect one of them has to be reinterpreted rather than taken at face value. Also tell me what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed, such as "Conan Doyle tried to kill Holmes off but his readers wouldn't let him," and "Holmes would have found modern forensic science contemptible." Finally, I want to know where your answer is weakest: name the single consideration, case, or argument that would most plausibly force you to revise the principle you offer, and say which of my verdicts you'd sacrifice first if pushed.

Structure your response as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the principle itself, stated precisely; (2) how it handles the comparative and existence cases that a simple truth-in-fiction operator cannot; (3) the verdict or verdicts it cannot accommodate and why; (4) its verdicts on my two unlisted cases; and (5) the consideration that would most plausibly change your mind, and the verdict you would abandon first.
