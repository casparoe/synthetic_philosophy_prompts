I chair the terminology subcommitee of an international professional body for behavioral practitioners — our members are choice architects, basically: people who design government forms, app defaults, pension enrollment flows, ad campaigns. We're rewriting our code of practice and we've been stuck for three meetings on one word: manipulation. The code needs a definition with teeth, because anything classified as a "manipulative technique" has to go through an extra review process, so whatever we write determines what our members can just do versus what they have to justify.

Half the committee wants a purpose-built definition, roughly: manipulation is any influence that works by bypassing or subverting the person's capacity to reflectively endorse what they're doing. Clean, principled, tracks what actually seems wrong about manipulation. The other half points out that by that standard a teacher's infectious enthusiasm, mood lighting in a restaurant, and about half of all advertising count as manipulation, and a term that covers that much ground stops meaning anything — plus it drains the word of its sting right when you need it for the genuinely nasty cases. They want us to stay close to how the word is actually used, fuzzy edges and all.

Two wrinkles that make this less academic than it sounds. First, at least two national regulators have signaled they'll incorporate our code by reference, which means our definition could end up argued over in courtrooms by judges who will read it as an ordinary English word no matter what we say we meant. Second, the code gets translated into a dozen languages, and the nearest equivalent of "manipulation" carries pretty different baggage in several of them.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the next meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — adopt the functional definition, adopt the usage-tracking one, or a third option like coining a fresh technical term for the review-triggering stuff and leaving "manipulation" out of the code entirely — and commit to it, not just lay out the options. It needs to give the losing side's strongest point a fair shake, since I'll be sending this to both factions. And one more thing: please be upfront about which parts of your recommendation rest on positions that are genuinely contested among people who think about whether concepts should be repaired to do a job versus analysed as we find them, and which parts are more like common ground. Don't dress up one side of that debate as the settled view, because at least one committee member will know better and call me on it.
