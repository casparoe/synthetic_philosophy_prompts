I write a column on puzzles and paradoxes for a general-interest science magazine, and I am preparing a piece on the two-envelope paradox. For readers who need it recalled: you are shown two envelopes, one containing twice as much money as the other. You take one, and before opening it you are offered the chance to switch. The familiar argument says you should switch: if your envelope holds x, the other holds either x/2 or 2x with equal probability, so its expected value is 1.25x, which exceeds x. But the argument seems to prove too much, since it applies no matter which envelope you are holding and would equally justify switching back after the swap.

In my draft I run through the resolutions I take to be standard in the literature:

1. The inconsistent-variable diagnosis: the expected-value calculation misuses the symbol x. Conditional on the other envelope's being the richer one, x names the smaller sum; conditional on its being the poorer one, x names the larger. The two occurrences are not the same quantity, so the calculation that yields 1.25x conflates two different conditional expectations and never validly compares the envelopes.

2. The no-admissible-prior diagnosis: there is no proper probability distribution over the possible pairs of amounts for which switching is favorable conditional on every amount you might find. The paradoxical reasoning covertly assumes a uniform prior over an unbounded range, which no genuine distribution realizes; once a proper prior is specified, switching is unfavorable for some observations, and where the prior makes expectations infinite, the comparison the argument relies on is simply undefined.

3. The bounded-purse diagnosis: any actual offer comes from finite funds. If there is an upper bound on the total sum, then for sufficiently large observed amounts the other envelope must be the poorer one, and the apparent unconditional gain from switching averages out to zero across the possible observations.

4. The symmetry rebuttal: since nothing distinguishes the two envelopes before opening, any consideration that favors switching would, after the switch, favor switching back. A sound decision rule cannot recommend an endless oscillation, so something in the switching argument must already be wrong before any calculation is done.

As far as I can tell, this list covers the standard moves. I would like to end the column with a resolution my readers will not have encountered. Give me one further diagnosis or resolution of the paradox that is not on the list, where genuinely new means not a rewording, special case, or combination of the four above. Explain how your proposal relates to the listed ones — whether it subsumes any of them, presupposes one, or conflicts with one — and assess candidly whether it holds up under scrutiny, since I will be printing it under my own name.
