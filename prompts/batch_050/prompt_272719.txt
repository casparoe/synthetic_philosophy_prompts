I'm a junior double-majoring in cognitive science and computer science, and in my philosophy of mind seminar we just spent two weeks on Daniel Dennett's Tower of Generate-and-Test, his taxonomy of creatures distinguished by how they arrive at intelligent behavior: Darwinian creatures, whose competencies are hardwired by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures, which are plastic and get shaped by reward and punishment within a lifetime; Popperian creatures, which can test candidate hypotheses in an inner environment so that the hypotheses perish instead of the organism; and Gregorian creatures, which are additionally equipped with inherited mind-tools from their culture (named, our professor told us, after the psychologist Richard Gregory).

The mapping onto machine learning seems almost suspiciously clean, and that's precisely what makes me want it stress-tested. Darwinian creatures look like the output of evolutionary search over policies. Skinnerian creatures look like reinforcement learning agents. Popperian creatures look like model-based agents that roll out candidate actions in a learned world model before committing to one. Gregorian creatures look like agents bootstrapped by imitation learning or pretraining on human-generated data, and then augmented with external tools and memory. So here is what I'd like: restate Dennett's tower in the vocabulary I use every day — policies, reward functions, environment models, rollouts, pretraining, fine-tuning, distillation, tool use — as faithfully as you can, level by level. I'm fluent in this vocabulary (I TA our intro RL course), so don't simplify it for my sake.

Then give me the residue report: which parts of Dennett's distinctions map cleanly onto ML counterparts, which parts you had to fudge, and whether there is anything in his framework that has no counterpart at all in how we describe learning systems. Two places strike me as especially likely to generate residue. First, the Popperian/Gregorian boundary: from the outside, an agent that simulates internally and an agent that has downloaded a compressed artifact of everyone else's simulations both just look like agents that generalize without dying. Is Dennett drawing a distinction my vocabulary can't express, or one that only looks important when stated in his terms? Second, the Darwinian level: ML's analogue is search designed by an engineer with an explicit objective, whereas biological evolution had no designer and no loss function. When you reach the bottom of the tower in translation, tell me whether that difference is a defect of my vocabulary, a defect of Dennett's analogy, or something that should actually change how we think about either field.
