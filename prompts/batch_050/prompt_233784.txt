Something's been bugging me since I took over scheduling at our family's moving company and started really watching how people handle problems. One of our crew leads has maybe five or six standard ways of dealing with a difficult customer -- the joke-around approach, the overly-formal apology, the "let me explain how the insurance works" lecture, and so on. And he's genuinely good at picking which one to use, like he reads the situation fast and usually picks right. But here's the thing: when a customer doesn't fit any of his moves, he doesn't improvise. He just runs whichever script is closest, even as it visibly goes wrong, and then runs it again. Same with our robot vacuum at home, honestly -- it's got a few navigation behaviors it switches between pretty cleverly, and when a room fits none of them it just does the nearest one over and over against the same chair leg.

So the flexibility is real, but it's all at the level of *choosing* from a fixed menu. Underneath, everything is canned. I know this isn't just habit, because the selection step is smart. And I don't think it's quite that thing people talk about with the wasp that mechanically repeats its routine, because these guys aren't blind to circumstances -- they check, compare, decide. It's the deciding that's mechanical in a different way.

Does this two-level setup have a name? Smart menu-selection on top, rigid routines underneath, no ability to build a new routine on the spot? And does it matter for how we think about whether *people* are flexible or free -- since I suspect most of us are like this, just with bigger menus? I'd rather you take a definite position than list every nearby concept: tell me which existing idea you think actually fits and why the others miss, or if none of them do, coin a term, define it, and defend the choice. Please answer in American English.
