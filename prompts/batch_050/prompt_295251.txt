I retired three years ago after four decades as an experimental physicist, and I have been using my new-found leisure to read philosophy in a fairly unsystematic way. A former colleague put me on to Lewis Carroll's short dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (published in Mind in 1895), in which the Tortoise demands to be forced, by logic alone, to accept the conclusion of a Euclidean inference. Most of what I have come across about the piece dwells on its famous ending, but the exchange that actually stops me is this one, from the middle:

"You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."

"And why must I?"

"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

I should like help with three things, all turning on this passage rather than on the dialogue's more celebrated moments.

First, interpretation. The gloss I keep encountering is that the Tortoise confuses a rule of inference with a further premise: modus ponens is something one reasons with, not something one reasons from, so writing it into the notebook as another hypothetical was never going to help. But the Tortoise's actual words suggest something subtler. He does not deny the conditional; he says he might fail to see its truth, and on that basis decline to accept Z. Is the regress really generated by treating a rule as a premise, or rather by treating acceptance of a conditional as a state that compels nothing by itself? And if Achilles had refused to write down C and D at all, what ought he to have said instead — that is, what is it to accept a rule of inference, if not to assent to a proposition?

Second, a test of Achilles's concession. He grants that a reader who accepts every premise and every hypothetical yet never accepts Z is possible, if phenomenally obtuse. As an empirical matter, I doubt I could ever identify such a creature: anyone whose verbal and practical behaviour reliably conformed to the inference would, by the standards of attribution I used throughout my laboratory career, simply count as accepting the rule, whatever they professed. Does that show the Tortoise's position is not genuinely possible — that accepting premises is partly constituted by a disposition to infer from them — or only that the truly obtuse reasoner would be undetectable in practice? Which reading does the passage itself support?

Third, an application closer to home. Late in my career we came to rely on proof-checking and verification software, which draws conclusions without, I assume, accepting anything. If the regress shows that no stock of true propositions can compel an inference, does it follow that the correctness of an inference can never consist in further stored facts that a machine might contain? And was our habit of treating the software's output as one more premise in a published paper quietly resting on a rule that no one, anywhere in the chain, had written down?

I would rather have the reasoning worked through carefully than a survey of the literature, so please keep the answer self-contained.
