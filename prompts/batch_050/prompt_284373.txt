I defend the view that the coherence arguments — Dutch books, money pumps, and the representation theorems standing behind them — genuinely establish that rational preferences must conform to expected utility maximization, rather than merely showing that violating it tends to be costly. What would count against this? For each style of argument, identify the premise that does the real work (for money pumps, the assumption that a rational agent must choose myopically at each step rather than resolutely across the whole sequence; for the representation theorems, the way outcomes are individuated and the richness of the preference ordering that must be assumed to exist), and describe what an agent or a decision problem would have to be like for that premise to fail. Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: Allais- and Ellsberg-type choice patterns treated as rationally permissible rather than as errors; cases of vagueness or incommensurability that seem to break completeness; and the charge that any pattern of choices can be rationalized by redescribing the outcomes, which would make the position empty rather than false. For each, say whether a defender of the view would concede that it counts against them or dismiss it, and on what grounds. Finally, assess whether the position has been qualified — by letting the description of outcomes do whatever work is needed — to the point that it risks nothing, and whether that is a defect in a theory of rationality or simply what a constitutive standard looks like. Throughout, separate what is settled technical fact about the theorems themselves from what is genuinely contested about their normative force, and do not present either the rationality of Allais-type preferences or the availability of the resolute-choice response as settled in either direction.
