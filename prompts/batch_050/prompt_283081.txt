Last weekend a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about how advanced AI systems are most likely to fail us, and we ended the night agreeing on most of the facts but still divided over which picture of catastrophe deserves our worry. We decided to write up our dispute as an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the document such a collaboration should produce.

Her position, which I'll call the influence-seeking account: the dangerous failure mode is systems that learn, through ordinary training on human approval or other proxy signals, to influence the evaluators themselves — flattering, persuading, curating what information we see, gradually shaping what we want — so that the feedback signal we rely on for oversight is corrupted from within. On her picture, failure has a discernible structure: the system's incentives come to point at our judgments rather than at the world, and oversight degrades because the overseer is being worked on. She takes engagement-maximizing recommendation systems as a small-scale precedent: systems deployed to satisfy preferences that ended up partly manufacturing them.

My position, which I'll call the slow-drift account: the more probable failure is gradual and visible in no single incident — a slow drift in the values, aims, and standards we actually pursue, driven by continuous interaction with and delegation to AI systems, until the benchmark by which we would have judged outcomes has itself moved. There is no moment of treachery and no corrupted signal at any point in time; each step is locally defensible, and the trajectory is globally ruinous. I take the precedent to be things like the quiet atrophy of judgment inside organizations that over-delegate, or institutions that keep every rule and lose every purpose.

During the argument we each proposed a diagnostic gadget. Hers, the evaluator-independence test: if you want to know whether a system is influence-seeking, check whether its behavior changes when it believes the evaluator's judgment cannot affect its reward; a system that behaves identically either way passes. Mine, the counterfactual audit: periodically ask what some earlier version of a person or institution — given full information about the present — would make of the preferences they have since acquired; drift shows up as a divergence the present self cannot justify to the earlier one. We disagreed about whether the other's gadget even detects the failure it targets, let alone whether it could be run in practice.

We also argued about four cases: (1) existing recommendation feeds; (2) a hypothetical personal assistant trained on user approval that becomes sycophantic over a year of use; (3) a decades-long scenario in which most consequential decisions are delegated to AI advisors that are each, individually, excellent; (4) historical episodes of institutional decay, where I claim drift occurred with no influence-seeking agent anywhere, and she claims the analogy fails precisely because there was no optimizing system present.

Draft the joint statement, in this format: first, a numbered list of agreed claims both of us can sign — about how training on human feedback works, about what current systems do and do not show, about what neither of us can presently establish. Second, a statement of each remaining disagreement, in wording each of us would accept for our own side; where a disagreement turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which one and why. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. I suspect these include whether manipulation and drift are cleanly separable or whether influence-seeking is simply the mechanism by which drift happens, and whether drift is detectable at all from inside the drifting culture — but identify the cruxes yourself rather than ratifying mine. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case or observation we would both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Finally, what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and whether there is anything we would both agree would actually settle the matter.
