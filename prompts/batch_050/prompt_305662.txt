The team's anomaly detection method works as follows: run a trained model on many ordinary inputs, characterize the mechanism each input engages (which internal components are active and what functional role each plays), and flag an input as anomalous when the mechanism it engages differs from the mechanism engaged on the reference inputs. The whole method quietly assumes that "same mechanism" is a well-defined relation — that two forward passes either do or do not instantiate the same mechanism, at some principled level of description, rather than "mechanism" being whatever falls out of the team's chosen granularity of description.

Give me your credence that this assumption is true: that there is a principled, non-stipulative answer to when two computations instantiate the same mechanism, such that the method tracks something real rather than an artifact of descriptive choice. Decompose it: the probability that mechanisms have objective individuation conditions at all (versus being carved up only relative to an investigator's interests); the probability that, granting they do, those conditions are recoverable from activation-level observations rather than hinging on implementational details invisible at that level; the probability that any single description granularity can be the right one across a whole model, rather than different components having to be compared at different levels; plus any other terms you think belong, each with a number and one line of justification. Then combine them into your final credence, identify the term you are least confident in, and tell me what evidence or argument would shift it most.
