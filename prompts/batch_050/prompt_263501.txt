For the past few years I have run an informal philosophy-and-mathematics circle for adults, none of us with any academic training, and each winter I set an essay question which we then mark against a commissioned model answer and a marking scheme. This year I want the topic to be computer-assisted proof, and I would like you to supply all three items: the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and the scheme.

Let me explain where my own thinking has got to, because I want the question to force our members to engage with it rather than to write a general essay on whether computers can do mathematics.

The four-colour theorem was established in 1976 by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken, whose argument reduced the problem to a large collection of configurations and then had a computer check each one. No human being could read the entire verification, let alone re-perform it. Thomas Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture ran into a related difficulty at a greater scale: a panel of referees reportedly gave up on certifying the computational parts, and a fully formal, machine-checked version of the proof was completed only many years later. The standard worry people voice about such proofs is that they are "unsurveyable" — that a proof no one can take in as a whole cannot play the role proofs are supposed to play.

I have been turning over a comparison that I have not come across in anything I have read, and which I think cuts the issue more finely. Imagine that, instead of a computer, the checking had been carried out by an enormous team of clerks working for decades, each performing elementary hand calculations according to fixed instructions, with cross-checking procedures to catch and correct slips. The resulting record would be every bit as unsurveyable as the computer's output — no lifetime would suffice to read it — yet every step in it would be an ordinary piece of arithmetic that any of us could verify, and nothing would rest on trusting the behaviour of a machine. This suggests to me that there are really two distinct objections being run together in discussions of computer proof: the objection from unsurveyability, and the objection that deferring to a machine's say-so is epistemically different from following an argument. My clerk case shares the first feature with the real proofs but not the second, so it should force anyone with a view on the matter to say which objection they actually believe in and whether it still bites.

The essay question should therefore require candidates to take a position on whether unsurveyability gives us reason to deny that the four-colour theorem has genuinely been proved, and to use the clerk case (or some variant of their own devising) to show what their position implies about whether the real problem is the length of the checking or its having been done by a machine. Candidates must also say what they think proofs are for, since the whole dispute seems to turn on that. The question should be answerable in ninety minutes by a bright non-specialist: our members cannot be assumed to know formal logic, so the question and model answer should be stated without technical machinery.

For the marking scheme, please identify five must-hit points, assign marks to each totalling twenty, and add a one-line note for each on the way a weak answer typically fumbles it — for example, I suspect many candidates will simply assert that the clerk case is "no different" from the computer case without explaining which feature does the work, and I want the scheme to catch that. Finally, please indicate what separates a first-rate answer from a competent one.
