In a reading group on formal ethics last week, we spent a session on Pascal's mugging -- the mugger who promises astronomically large payoffs in exchange for your wallet, with the probability of his delivering scaled so that the expected value favors paying up. I raised the standard worry that expected value maximization seems to counsel handing over the money in a case where that is transparently absurd, and a friend from the rationality community waved the whole thing off with "shut up and multiply": the mathematics of expected value is not negotiable, small probabilities times large payoffs are still payoffs, and the mugging is only a puzzle if you let your intuitions bully your arithmetic. Later in the evening he deployed the same one-liner against a variant I had cooked up, in which the mugger approaches a million people, promises each a huge reward conditional on a one-in-a-million event, and points out that in expectation exactly one of the promises will be kept -- a setup I had designed precisely because it seems to give the large payoff a kind of aggregate, population-level warrant that the single-victim mugging lacks. He insisted the slogan dissolves both cases equally, which made me suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs at once and that he had not distinguished them.

Act as a decision theorist with philosophical training, and treat this as a serious exercise in precisification rather than a defense of either side. Before writing your main answer, spend some time brainstorming the different precise claims the slogan could be expressing -- from near-tautologies about how multiplication works, through substantive normative theses about rational choice under risk, through empirical or epistemological claims about when purported large payoffs are real, to methodological advice about when to defer to a formalism over an intuition. Then present your final answer as a menu of readings, ordered roughly from most anodyne to most radical, and for each reading state clearly: what exactly it claims; whether it is a platitude, a live and defensible position, or something actually false; and whether it is strong enough to support the use my friend made of it -- namely, dismissing the original mugging and my million-victim variant in one breath. Where a reading would dissolve the classic mugging but leave my variant standing (or vice versa), say so explicitly, since that asymmetry is what I most want to understand.

Conclude by identifying which reading you take people who actually use the slogan to be relying on, given that they typically reach for it exactly when an expected value calculation yields a repugnant conclusion; assess whether that reading is one the slogan's user could state in full without embarrassment; and flag any readings under which "shut up and multiply" is not an answer to the mugging at all but merely a refusal to engage with it. Think carefully, and do not pretend the menu has fewer items than it does just to give a tidy verdict.
