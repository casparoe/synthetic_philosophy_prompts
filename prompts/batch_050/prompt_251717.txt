I've spent years reading the rationalist blogs and forums, where the self is routinely described as a user illusion: a model the brain constructs and then mistakes for the thing doing the constructing. I've recently been reading Hume's Treatise of Human Nature alongside this material, and I was struck by how close the Appendix to that work (published 1740, a year after the first two books) comes to diagnosing a problem that, as far as I can tell, the modern illusionists never fully resolve.

Some background, in case it matters: in the section on personal identity, Hume argues that introspection never reveals a self -- only a stream of particular perceptions in perpetual flux -- and concludes that the mind is nothing over and above the bundle of those perceptions, with the felt unity supplied by resemblance and causation rather than by any underlying subject. Then, in the Appendix, he famously backtracks: he confesses that his account of personal identity has landed him in a labyrinth he cannot escape, and identifies two principles he can neither reconcile nor give up -- roughly, that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connection among distinct existences. His diagnosis is that the bundle account would face no difficulty if perceptions inhered in something simple, or if we perceived some genuine tie binding them; since we get neither, the theory cannot explain what makes the bundle one mind.

Here is what I'd like explained, at a level beyond the textbook summary:

First, what exactly is the contradiction or gap Hume has found? Many presentations treat his Appendix worry as vague anxiety, but it seems more specific than that. Is the problem that distinctness of perceptions makes their co-membership in a single mind inexplicable? That the principles of association he offers (resemblance, causation) are relations *among* perceptions and so presuppose, rather than explain, the perspective from which they are surveyed? Or something else? I'd like the strongest precise reconstruction of the bind, not just a gesture at it.

Second: the modern user-illusion or self-model view looks, structurally, like a doubling down on Hume's original position rather than a fix for his Appendix problem -- it says the unified subject is a representation the system generates, full stop. Does that dissolve Hume's two-principle bind, or merely relocate it? Specifically: if the sense of unity is a constructed appearance, Hume's question seems to reappear as *constructed for what?* -- since an appearance, unlike a lump of matter, arguably needs to be an appearance *to* something. Does the illusionist have a principled answer here (e.g., that the appearance is to the system itself, or to further subpersonal processes, with no additional subject required), and if so, why didn't that answer occur to or satisfy Hume? Or is the right reading that Hume's bind depends on an empiricist constraint -- that all content must be traced to impressions -- which the naturalized illusionist simply drops?

Third: what is the actual difference between *explaining* the appearance of a self and *explaining it away*? It seems to me the user-illusion framing trades on an ambiguity between these, and that Hume's Appendix is an early record of someone noticing the seam. If you think the distinction collapses under scrutiny, explain why; if it holds, state what a theory of consciousness would have to deliver to count as doing the former rather than the latter.

Please keep the explanation self-contained -- I'm not asking for a reading list, just the clearest rigorous treatment you can give.
