I am designing a graduate seminar unit on negative and minimalist axiologies — views on which what ultimately matters about a life is not the goods it contains but the evils it avoids or the tensions it dissolves. Write a decision tree that sorts positions about well-being and value, under the following specifications.

The tree's starting commitment, which every leaf must share, is experientialism: the value of a life depends solely on the character of the subject's conscious experience, not on desire-satisfaction in the world, objective achievements, or virtue. All sorting within the tree happens downstream of that commitment.

The following five positions must appear as named leaves. I define them only by slogan here; part of your task is to locate them in the logical space of the tree, so do not let the slogans do the sorting for you.

- Tranquilism: experiences are valuable only insofar as they are free of craving; pleasure matters chiefly as the relief of a disturbed state, never as an addition to an undisturbed one.
- Classical negative utilitarianism: only suffering has disvalue; reducing it is the sole imperative, and no amount of pleasure can offset it.
- Antifrustrationism: what is bad is frustrated preference; satisfied preferences add nothing beyond the absence of frustration.
- Symmetric hedonic total utilitarianism: pleasure and pain count on a single scale and aggregate across lives without remainder.
- Critical-range utilitarianism: lives contribute positively to the total only once their experiential quality clears a nontrivial threshold; below it they contribute negatively even if they contain more pleasure than pain.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names. Each internal node should be a single diagnostic question phrased as a concrete case, answerable by someone who has never heard the position names — for instance, cases along the lines of: a life of vivid pleasures, each preceded by an intense craving that is then fully satisfied; an experience machine set to deliver undisturbed, contentment-level calm; the addition of a new person whose life will be entirely free of craving and suffering; a stretch of suffering that is fully "paid for" by later joy. Design your own cases where you can improve on these, but the four contrasts they probe must each be probed somewhere. Aim for eight to eleven nodes total, questions included.

After the tree, do three things. First, give the full set of answer combinations the tree permits, and for every combination on which no named position sits, diagnose briefly whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or unexplored territory. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what further question would be needed to split them — pay particular attention to whether tranquilism and antifrustrationism, as slogans, can be distinguished by cases about experience alone. Third, note whether your tree sorts views about value only, or also views about what one ought to do, and identify any leaf whose label is standardly used for a practical ideal (a way of life) rather than an axiology, so that the tree does not silently conflate the two.
