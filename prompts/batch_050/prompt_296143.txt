The exam question was:

"In the twentieth century, Ramsey and de Finetti argued that degrees of belief violating the probability axioms expose an agent to a Dutch book — a collection of bets, each individually acceptable to the agent, that together guarantee a net loss. A family of later results in the same spirit (money-pump arguments, representation theorems) is often taken to show that rationality requires maximizing expected utility. Explain how at least two such coherence arguments work. Then evaluate: do they establish that rational agents must maximize expected utility? Your evaluation should discuss at least one limitation or objection."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

---

The best-known coherence argument is the Dutch book argument, due to Ramsey and de Finetti. Suppose an agent's degree of belief in a proposition p is 0.7 while her degree of belief in not-p is 0.5. Because these credences sum to more than 1, a bookie can sell her a bet that pays $1 if p is true for $0.70, and a second bet that pays $1 if not-p is true for $0.50. She pays $1.20 in total, but exactly one of p and not-p will occur, so she receives exactly $1.00 no matter what — a guaranteed loss of $0.20. The reasoning generalizes: any set of credences violating the probability axioms licenses a package of bets, each of which the agent regards as fair or favorable, that collectively guarantee a loss. The conclusion is that rational degrees of belief must satisfy the probability axioms.

A second coherence argument is the money-pump argument, which targets preferences rather than credences. Suppose an agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. If she currently holds C, she will pay a small premium to trade C for B, since she prefers B; she will then pay another premium to trade B for A; and then another to trade A for C, returning to her starting point minus the premiums. The cycle can be repeated indefinitely, draining her wallet. The conclusion drawn is that rational preferences must be transitive.

Results of this kind are often taken to vindicate expected utility theory. The von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem shows that any agent whose credences are probabilistic and whose preferences over gambles satisfy their axioms — completeness, transitivity, independence, and continuity — can be represented as maximizing the expected value of a utility function. And since violating these axioms leaves an agent open to Dutch books or money pumps, the axioms are requirements of rationality, and hence rational agents maximize expected utility.

However, the Allais paradox shows that this conclusion is too strong. Most people prefer a guaranteed $1 million over a gamble offering an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing; yet most of the same people prefer a 10% chance of $5 million over an 11% chance of $1 million. This pair of preferences is provably incompatible with the independence axiom. Because these preferences are so widespread, expected utility theory is refuted as a theory of rationality.
