I produce a philosophy-of-logic podcast, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a restrictionist reply to the Church–Fitch knowability paradox. For listeners: the paradox shows that the seemingly harmless principle that every truth is knowable entails, via a short derivation, that every truth is actually known. Our guest's position is that the knowability principle should be restricted to what she calls Cartesian propositions -- those whose being known is logically consistent -- so that the troublesome conjunct "p and it is not known that p" is ruled out as a substitution instance and the derivation never gets going. Draft eight to ten interview questions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The questions should press where the debate actually presses rather than invite her to restate the view. Specifically, I want: one question that makes her identify exactly which premise or inference of the standard derivation her restriction is meant to neutralize, and why that move does not instead simply deny that knowledge distributes over conjunction; one on the charge that the restriction is ad hoc -- a patch with no motivation except blocking the paradox -- with a follow-up for when she claims the Cartesian condition falls out of an independently plausible picture of what it would be for a truth to be known; one on the objection that the restriction is too weak, since Cartesian propositions can have non-Cartesian consequences and, given that knowledge is closed under known entailment, knowing them remains impossible; one on the objection that it is too strong, consigning to unknowability truths we seem able to grasp indirectly, for instance by knowing of some particular truth that it will never be known; and one on whether her reply rescues or instead abandons the semantic anti-realism that made the knowability principle attractive in the first place -- with a follow-up on how she weighs her restriction against rival responses such as revising the logic used in the derivation or relativizing knowability to situations or times. End the set with a question about what result in this literature would convince her to give the view up. Begin your response with the first numbered question itself, with no preface, overview of the paradox, or commentary about the interview.
