I've recently started working on a swarm robotics project, and a colleague's casual claim that our drone swarm "knows where the survivors are even though no single drone does" sent me back to Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary thought experiment, and now I'd like my own position audited before I embarrass myself in a team meeting. Here is roughly where I've landed:

1. Functionalism is true: what makes something a believer is the causal and informational organization of the system, not the material it is made of.
2. A sufficiently integrated ant colony literally has colony-level beliefs -- for instance, the colony knows where the food is even though no individual ant does.
3. No individual ant understands anything; whatever cognition exists resides entirely at the colony level.
4. Corporations and nations do not literally have beliefs: talk of what a company "wants" or a country "knows," over and above what its members want and know, is useful shorthand and nothing more.
5. A belief ascription to a system is justified only when it does predictive and explanatory work that cannot be done more cheaply at the level of the system's parts, and I take it this condition is met for the ant colony.
6. There is nothing it is like to be an ant colony -- it has no phenomenal consciousness -- but that is no obstacle to its having beliefs.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, ranking the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, identify exactly which commitments are doing the damage and what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would restore consistency at the lowest overall price. End your response with a final line of the form \answer{N}, where N is the number of the commitment I should revise.
