I’m a junior taking a Philosophy of Computing seminar, and this week we’re talking about the Church–Turing thesis and what kind of claim it even is. For a class assignment, write a deadpan parody of a formal scientific lab report, about 700–800 words, by a fictional undergraduate research group claiming that they have experimentally proven the Church–Turing thesis.

Give it a title, abstract, methods, results, limitations, and conclusion. In the experiment, the students gather a huge spreadsheet of procedures they consider obviously effective, implement some in Python, encode others as Turing machines, lambda calculus terms, recursive functions, and register-machine programs, then announce that the intertranslations are statistically significant at p < .05. Their conclusion should be that only one more semester of testing is needed to check every possible algorithm.

The humor should come from the real epistemological weirdness, not just from making the students sound dumb. Bring in the difference between proving equivalences among already-formalized models and proving that those models capture our pre-theoretical idea of an effective procedure. Show why the convergence of different models provides serious evidence without turning the thesis into an ordinary theorem. Also have the students mishandle alleged counterexamples involving oracle machines, idealized hypercomputers, unbounded memory, infinite precision, and physically impossible processes, without the report explicitly correcting them. Ideally, they should confuse the original Church–Turing thesis with the stronger physical version and treat finite hardware as both the proof and the main obstacle.

The tone should stay completely serious, like a parody in a fake academic journal. Include one limitations section where the authors almost realize that their procedure may be circular, then confidently miss the point. Please use American English and make it funny for someone who knows the basics but still understandable to classmates who definately don’t.
