I've been reading about the boundary problem in democratic theory -- the question of who gets a say in a decision, which seems to need answering before any democratic procedure can run, so no democratic procedure can settle it. The main answers on offer are roughly: everyone whose interests are affected should get a say, everyone subject to coercion should get a say, or the demos is whoever the existing political community takes to be a member. As far as I can tell the debate between these has been going in circles for decades: the all-affected people get accused of absurdity (do I get a vote on your zoning laws because the housing prices affect me?), the coercion people get accused of arbitrariness, and everyone dumps on the "existing polity decides" view as circular.

My hypothesis, which I'm not attached to, is that the debate has stalled because the sides are answering different questions under one name: the all-affected principle is really about when a decision is legitimate, while the membership view is really about who owes whom justification, and the coercion view is somewhere inbetween. So it's not that one principle is better, it's that "the boundary problem" is two or three problems wearing one hat.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement here -- about what democracy is for, or about some hidden empirical question (like how interests actually cluster) that would settle it if we knew it? And given your diagnosis: what, if anything, would count as progress? A sharper distinction between kinds of affectedness, a decision-procedure argument, or should we expect this to stay stuck, and why?
