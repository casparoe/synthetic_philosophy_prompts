Glaucon's challenge in Book II of Plato's Republic is usually taken to rest on two devices: first, the stipulation that we compare a perfectly unjust man who enjoys a reputation for justice with a perfectly just man who suffers a reputation for injustice; second, the ring of Gyges, whose power of invisibility is meant to show that a just person and an unjust person, each granted impunity, would converge on the same conduct — from which Glaucon concludes that nobody values justice for its own sake, only for its rewards.

Please argue that the second device, the ring, fails to establish this conclusion, even granting Glaucon as much of the surrounding setup as the argument needs. In doing so, address the following:

- Locate the precise inferential step you are rejecting. The ring story offers no observation; it offers a prediction about how the just man would behave, and then reads his motives off the predicted behavior. Is the flaw that the prediction simply presupposes what was supposed to be proved — that no motive other than fear of consequences restrains anyone? Or is it something subtler: that removing visibility removes not only external sanctions but also conditions under which the very traits being tested (shame, self-conception, one's standing in one's own eyes as a being among others) are constituted, so that the counterfactual describes a creature no longer relevantly like us?
- Engage the strongest defense of the ring, namely that it is merely a heuristic for stripping reputational incentives out of the motivational picture, and that asking what someone would do if unseen is exactly how we ordinarily test whether a motive is intrinsic. Explain why this defense does not rescue the inference, or concede the part of it you cannot defeat.
- Take a definite position on the challenge as a whole: once the ring is set aside, does Glaucon's demand — show that justice is choiceworthy in itself, by considering the just man under total reputational inversion — still have force, and if so, does it succeed on its own terms or fail for independent reasons? Do not present a menu of interpretive options; give a verdict on whether Socrates is being asked to do something coherent, something impossible, or something incoherent, and defend that verdict.

Where the argument depends on contested points (for instance, what exactly the ring is supposed to control for), state the reading you are relying on and why it is the most charitable one, rather than hedging across readings.
