I teach a philosophy of biology course for undergrads -- about 18 students, one 75-minute session, and I want to spend it on what makes something one organism rather than several organisms cooperating, or several things fused into one, or just a pile of parts. Design an in-class activity where they figure out how hard this question is by actually having to answer it, not by hearing a lecture.

Here's the rough shape I have in mind, but improve on it: give small groups a stack of case cards with short plain-language descriptions of things like a honeybee hive, a lichen, a Portuguese man o' war, a huge clonal aspen grove connected underground, a slime mold where thousands of single cells merge into a crawling slug, a human with their gut bacteria, a virus, a coral, maybe a tumor. First they just sort the cards into "one organism" vs "more than one / not an organism" by gut feeling. Then force them to write down a single criterion they'd stand behind and re-sort the cards using only that rule. Then hand out a second batch of cards designed to break whatever criteria groups will predictably have written (reproduction-based, genetic-identity-based, can't-survive-alone-based, etc).

What I need from you: the full card list with the actual text to print on each card, chosen so that the obvious criteria each fail on at least two cards (and tell me which criterion each card is there to break). The rules and timeline for each round. A debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the actual philosophical positions about biological individuality, without me having to say words like holobiont or superorganism until the very end. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (e.g. groups that refuse to write one criterion, or a case everyone sorts the same way that kills the tension). And be honest about which part of the real debate the activity can't reproduce -- I suspect the evolutionary-levels-of-selection stuff won't fit in the card game, but tell me if I'm wrong.
