I'm in a reading group on Kant's ethics, and after last week's session two members, Leo and Dana, got into an argument that spilled over into our group chat and never got resolved. The background: Leo's roommate had been dodging a former business partner who'd been harassing him and had shown up at their building twice. When the man knocked and asked Leo point-blank whether the roommate was home, Leo answered, "He told me he was spending the evening at the library." This was literally true as a report -- the roommate had in fact said those words to Leo that afternoon, as a suggestion for what Leo should say -- but Leo intended the man to walk away believing the roommate was out, which is exactly what happened. Dana's position (move 1): Leo lied, and the case doesn't even raise the hard Kantian question, since Kant notoriously holds that you may not lie even to the murderer at the door, so a fortiori you may not deceive a mere nuisance at the door. Leo's reply (move 2): he never asserted anything false; every sentence he uttered was true, and on Kant's own strict conception a lie is an intentionally untruthful declaration, so whatever else his statement was, it wasn't a lie, and the rigorist prohibition simply doesn't apply to misleading truths. Dana's comeback (move 3): this is a loophole that guts the theory, because Kant's actual test is universalizability, and a maxim of "mislead people with technically true statements whenever telling the plain truth is inconvenient" fails it just as badly as a maxim of lying does -- so Kant's principles condemn the evasion even if his definition of "lie" doesn't cover it. Leo's final move (4): Kant distinguishes the duty of truthfulness from any duty of candor; Leo owed this man nothing, the man had no right to the information, and staying silent or refusing to answer would itself have tipped the man off that the roommate was hiding -- so given that some response was forced, a true statement was the least-wrong option available, and Kantian duties don't demand more than that.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each of the four moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-contestable, and justify your rating in terms of what Kant's framework actually commits one to -- the formula of universal law, the duty of truthfulness as a duty to oneself and to others, and the question of who, if anyone, is owed the truth. Then, beyond scoring the moves: what is the single most important distinction that both Leo and Dana are glossing over, and does Kant's notorious position on the murderer at the door actually bear on Leo's case in the way Dana assumed at the outset, or did the dispute get misframed from the first move?
