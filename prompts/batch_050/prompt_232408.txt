I'm an SRE (site reliability engineer), about ten years in, so my whole mental model of institutional failure is postmortems, incident response, noisy alerting, cascading failures, that kind of thing. I was reading a popular history of classical Athens on the train and it struck me that the famous screwups of Athenian democracy read weirdly like ops disasters: the Mytilene debate, where the assembly votes to kill all the men of a rebellious ally city, then reverses itself the next day and has to race a second trireme to catch the first ship carrying the execution order; the Sicilian expedition, where the demos greenlights a catastrophically underspecified invasion and then can't bring itself to abort as it sinks; and the trial of Socrates, where the system executes one of its own sharpest critics on fuzzy charges. I've seen people online describe these as "democracy's bugs" but that's vague.

Here's what I'd actually like: restate each of these three failures in the vocabulary of incident response and distributed systems, as precisely as you can. E.g. is the Mytilene reversal an idempotent-command problem, or a race condition between two writes, or something like rolling back a migration after it's already partially applied? Is Sicily a deploy with no rollback plan and sunk-cost fallacy in the on-call channel? Is the Socrates trial a false positive from a badly tuned alerting rule, and if so what was the threshold? But the part I care about most is the residue report: tell me which parts of each episode map cleanly onto ops concepts, which parts you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart in SRE vocabulary at all. My suspicion is that things like the rhetoric in the assembly, or the fact that the "system" is made of people who can feel shame and change their minds, don't survive translation -- but I want you to check that suspicion rather than confirm it, and tell me whether the residue is a limitation of my framework or a sign that historians' talk of "democratic failure" is less precise than it sounds.
