In discussions of AI oversight at my company, and in the alignment-adjacent reading I do on the side, I keep running into the slogan "it's easier to verify an argument than to construct one." Most recently it was doing real work: someone used it to defend the proposal to oversee advanced AI systems by having two of them debate in front of a weaker human or machine judge, on the grounds that the honest debater holds a structural advantage -- any flaw in the dishonest debater's case is cheap to expose, while hiding that flaw is expensive.

Coming from software, my first association is the P vs NP intuition: checking a proof certificate is cheap, finding one is hard. But the more I sit with it, the less sure I am that this analogy is doing anything, because in the debate setting nobody hands the judge a complete certificate. The judge sees a natural-language exchange and can realistically inspect only a small, adversarially influenced sample of a very large tree of claims and sub-claims. So the slogan seems to slide between several different claims depending on how you cash out "easier" and "verify."

Here is what I would like. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing -- varying at least: what kind of cost "easier" refers to (asymptotic complexity, runtime for bounded agents, cognitive effort for a human auditor), what object is being verified (a complete formal proof, a sampled subset of a large argument tree, a live natural-language exchange), who does the verifying, and what the baseline construction task is (building a sound argument from scratch vs. building a merely persuasive one). For each thesis, give a verdict: platitude, substantive and plausible, or simply false -- with a sentence or two of justification, drawing on analogies from computing where they genuinely hold. Then tell me which of these theses the debate-as-oversight proposal actually needs to be true, and assess whether that thesis survives the following failure mode I care about: an argument that is locally sound everywhere the judge happens to look, but globally broken, where the dishonest debater's entire skill consists in steering which parts get inspected. Does that failure mode refute the needed thesis, merely weaken it, or reveal that the needed thesis was never the one people had in mind when repeating the slogan?
