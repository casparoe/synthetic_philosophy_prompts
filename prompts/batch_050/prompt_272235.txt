Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two contemporary defenders of classical kalām positions on the ground of moral truths: a faithful Mu'tazilite and a faithful Ash'arite, each committed to their school's historical doctrine on whether good and evil are established by reason or by revelation alone. Both accept the shared background of the tradition: God's existence and unity demonstrable by reason, the Qur'an as authoritative revelation, and the finality of prophecy. Their collaboration concerns the Islamic Euthyphro: whether acts are good or evil intrinsically, such that unaided reason can know them as such, or whether good and evil are constituted by divine command and prohibition.

The statement should contain four sections, in this order.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims, each one a sentence both parties could sign. Candidates to consider, keeping those that survive scrutiny and adding others: that God never lies and never in fact punishes the innocent; that this is a dispute within Islam, not over anyone's standing as a Muslim; that the dispute concerns the ground of moral predicates rather than God's actual conduct; that revelation settles many matters reason cannot reach, such as the details of ritual obligation; that neither side takes divine justice to be a standard imposed on God from outside Him. Where the two parties sign a claim for different reasons, append one sentence noting the divergence in grounds.

Second, a disagreements table with one row per residual disagreement and three columns: the issue in neutral wording, the Mu'tazilite position as a Mu'tazilite would state it, and the Ash'arite position as an Ash'arite would state it. Rows should include at least: whether reason can know the goodness of gratitude to a benefactor and the evil of injustice before revelation arrives; whether anything can be obligatory for God, as the Mu'tazilite doctrines of the optimum and of divine grace require; whether God's acts proceed from purposes He could in principle state, or whether ascribing purposes to Him is itself a confusion; whether God's refraining from punishing the innocent is necessary given what justice is, or contingent on His having promised as much; and what each side makes of the counterfactual question whether God could command injustice, including which side considers the question badly formed and why.

Third, a cruxes section. For each of three to five cruxes, give the concrete case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, and record each side's verdict on that case in advance, along with what each side would have to concede if the case were decided against them. Cases you might use, or replace with better ones: a morally mature person who dies before revelation reaches her — does she owe God gratitude, and is she blameworthy for withholding it; the fate of children who die young and of animals that suffer, where the two schools' verdicts about compensation diverge for instructive reasons; whether God could impose suffering on a creature with no compensating benefit to that creature; and the case of lying, on which both agree God does not lie — make each side state exactly what, on their view, secures that conclusion.

Fourth, a verdict on which disagreements are verbal. In particular, assess whether the dispute over obligation for God survives once both sides state what they mean by obligation, and whether the two operative definitions of justice — roughly, refraining from evil, versus not transgressing what one has no right over — render the headline dispute about God's justice partly terminological. State plainly which rows of the table you conclude are genuine disagreements and which are about words, with reasons.

Constraints: phrase every position so that its own side would accept the wording as fair; where you are unsure the classical sources support a formulation, flag the uncertainty rather than resolving it by fiat; and do not adjudicate the overall debate — the deliverable is a text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. Write in American English, in a formal register.
