Our philosophy of mind reading group has spent the past month on whether qualia, as traditionally conceived, are a coherent posit, and we closed with Dennett's essay Quining Qualia — in particular his case of the two coffee-tasters, Chase and Sanborn. Chase insists the coffee tastes exactly as it always has and that it is his appreciation of that very taste which has faded; Sanborn insists his standards and preferences are unchanged and that it is the taste itself which has drifted. Each member of the group has been asked to circulate a short statement of where they stand for the others to attack. Here is mine, and I would like an audit of it before I send it around.

I hold the following. (1) Dennett's treatment of the case succeeds: there is no first-personal test by which Chase or Sanborn could settle whether the quale changed while his reactions held fixed or the reactions changed while the quale held fixed, and a distinction that no possible evidence, even evidence available only from the inside, could draw is not a distinction at all — so the classical notion of qualia as intrinsic, ineffable, and immediately apprehensible properties of experience is incoherent and should be quined. (2) All the same, there is obviously something it is like for me to taste coffee right now, and my present experience has a phenomenal character that I can attend to, compare, and report. (3) While I concede, in light of the Chase–Sanborn case, that my beliefs about my past experiences are reconstructive and may be false, my judgments about my current experience are authoritative: I might misdescribe how things seem to me at this instant, but I cannot be mistaken about how they seem. (4) Consciousness is physically realized, and its scientific explanation will be exhausted by an account of the relevant functional and representational organization together with a sober third-personal treatment of what subjects say about their experience; nothing further is left over that calls out for explanation. (5) It is nevertheless possible for a perfect functional isomorph of me — same discriminations, same judgments, same reactive dispositions throughout — to differ from me in phenomenal character, as in the traditional inverted spectrum, and the same holds across time within a single person: if Sanborn's taste really did drift while every disposition and memory of his was held fixed, there would be a real fact of the matter about that drift, whether or not anyone, Sanborn included, could ever detect it.

What I want is an audit of this package, not a survey of the literature. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each? Which pairs only look incompatible and in fact sit together comfortably once the key terms are clarified? For each real tension, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and state what it costs. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally urgent, and end by telling me which single change would do the most to make the overall position coherent — without presuming in advance which of the five theses I would be willing to surrender.
