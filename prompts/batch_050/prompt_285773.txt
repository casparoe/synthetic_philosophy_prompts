Okay so background: I'm a PhD student working on philosophy of mind, and last week at our department reading group (we were doing Meditations I and II) two people got into it about the cogito and never resolved it, and it kept bugging me on the way home because I genuinely flip-flopped on who was winning. I'm going to try to reconstruct the exchange as fairly as I can.

Ana started by saying the cogito is overrated as an argument because it doesn't actually prove that *she* exists -- the most honest formulation would be something like "thinking is happening" or "there's a doubt," and the "I" is just grammar doing work it hasn't earned. She said Lichtenberg made this point centuries ago and nobody has really answered it. Marcus pushed back that this misses what's special about the cogito: it's not a syllogism where you infer from "thinking" to "a thinker," it's a performance. The very act of doubting presents a subject to itself, so the "I" isn't smuggled in -- it's given in the doing. He said that's why you can't doubt the cogito without instantiating it, whereas you can doubt "walking is happening, therefore a walker exists" no problem.

Ana said fine, maybe a performance shows *a* thought occurring *now*, but it still doesn't show there's a bearer of the thought rather than just the thought, and it definitely doesn't show the same bearer persists between thoughts -- the "I" who doubts at t1 and the "I" who concludes at t2 might be a bundle with no thread, as Hume would say. Marcus answered that Descartes never claims more than that "I exist" is true *while* it's being thought, so persistence is a red herring, and anyway the subject isn't some extra entity hiding behind the thought -- it's the thought's first-personal character, which the performance makes undeniable.

Then Ana made what she clearly thought was her killer move: she said that if that's all the cogito gets you -- a momentary, perspectival feature of a thought, true only while entertained -- then it can't do the job Descartes actually needs it for, which is establishing a *res cogitans*, a thinking substance, as the foundation everything else gets built on. So either the cogito is modest and useless for the Meditations' project, or it's ambitious and begs the question. Marcus said that's a false dilemma because the modesty is the point: you don't need the substance conclusion to come *out of* the cogito, the cogito just needs to be an unshakeable truth, and the substance stuff gets argued later.

They both looked at me and I mumbled something noncommittal like a coward. So: can you go through this properly? At each step, who's right about the specific point on the table?
