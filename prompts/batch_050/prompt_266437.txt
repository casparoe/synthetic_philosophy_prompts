I am a novelist planning a historical novel around the 1919 eclipse expeditions, when British astronomers photographed stars near the Sun from Sobral and Príncipe and used their apparent displacement to test Einstein’s general theory of relativity. I am not asking for scenes or plot ideas. I want a conceptual analysis of the episode through Peircean pragmatism: the pragmatic maxim, the fixation of belief, and fallibilist inquiry.

Treat the expedition as an episode in which a scientific community moved from doubt toward a provisional belief. First, specify exactly what was in doubt, and for whom: the validity of Newtonian expectations, the empirical meaning of Einstein’s theory, the reliability of the instruments and photographs, or the competence and impartiality of the observers. Distinguish the scientists’ doubt from the public’s later uncertainty and from the dramatic doubt a novelist might be tempted to impose on the characters.

Then apply the pragmatic maxim carefully. What different habits of expectation or courses of experience followed from accepting the Newtonian prediction, a no-deflection result, or Einstein’s predicted displacement? Did the expedition clarify the meaning of the rival theories, merely supply evidence for one of them, or both? Explain the difference in this case rather than treating meaning and evidence as interchangeable.

Next, use Peirce’s four methods of fixing belief—tenacity, authority, a priori reasoning, and scientific inquiry—as an analytical taxonomy. Where, if anywhere, did each operate among the astronomers, scientific institutions, journalists, and broader public? Do not assign one method to each group mechanically; show how they could coexist within the same person or institution. Also explain what it would mean for belief to be fixed at the level of a community rather than in an individual mind.

Finally, run the analysis in the opposite direction. What does the episode confirm about Peirce’s account of inquiry as public, self-correcting, and indefinitely fallible? Where does the theory seem too idealized, particularly given national prestige, wartime politics, institutional authority, and the theatrical announcement of a scientific revolution? Conclude by identifying what the episode would license a novelist to portray as genuine epistemic conflict, and what would instead be a melodramatic distortion of inquiry.
