I am a high school senior and I run my school's philosophy club. Last month we worked through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. One member objected that the argument proves too much, because a structurally identical argument would show that no belief is ever rationally held. Here is her reconstruction of the two arguments side by side.

The moral argument. (1) When you act, you act as you do because of the way you are: your character, values, and motives. (2) To be truly responsible for acting as you do, you must be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the respects that explain the action. (3) To be truly responsible for being the way you are, you must at some earlier time have knowingly and intentionally brought it about that you are that way. (4) But any such act of self-formation would itself be done because of the way you then were, so you would already have had to be responsible for that earlier way of being. (5) This forces either an infinite regress of prior self-formations or a first act of self-creation from nothing, which you could not be responsible for. (6) So no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything.

The epistemic parallel. (1*) When you form a belief, you form it because of the way you are: your reasoning habits, your standards of evidence, your background assumptions. (2*) For your belief to be rationally held, you must be responsible for the cognitive dispositions that produced it. (3*) To be responsible for those dispositions, you must at some earlier time have rationally chosen them, on the basis of beliefs you already rationally held. (4*) But those prior beliefs were themselves formed because of the way you then were, forcing the same regress. (5*) So no belief is ever rationally held; every belief traces back to unchosen cognitive equipment.

She insists (5*) is absurd, so one of the arguments contains a mistake. Here is what I want from you. First, identify the premise of the epistemic argument that is the best candidate for rejection, explain why it initially looked plausible, and state what rejecting it costs. Second, tell me whether your diagnosis of the epistemic argument carries over to the moral one: if the fix works there too, does Strawson's argument collapse, or is there a genuine asymmetry between being justified in believing and being responsible for acting that blocks the transfer? Third, the club member suspects the phrase "because of the way you are" is doing different work in (1) and (1*); assess whether that suspicion is right and whether it matters. Finally, I would most like to keep the claims that people can be blameworthy and that beliefs can be rational, so if your diagnosis forces me to give one of those up, tell me which and why.
