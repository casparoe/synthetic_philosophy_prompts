I was recently promoted to lead a team of six junior analysts at a research consultancy, and I need to settle our evaluation policy before the next review cycle. Our work consists of drafting analytical memos for clients, and every analyst now leans heavily on AI drafting tools. The volume of output has grown past the point where I can follow, let alone verify, the reasoning behind every claim, and much of the subject matter exceeds my own expertise.

Two policies are on the table. Under the first, I would grade deliverables purely by their results: spot-check a sample of each memo's conclusions, and reward the analysts whose conclusions hold up. Under the second, I would require each analyst to submit an annotated trail alongside the memo — which sources they consulted, which steps they reasoned through themselves, which they delegated to the tools, and why — and grade the quality of that trail as much as the final product.

What makes this hard, rather than a simple matter of taste, are four complications. First, the same tools that write the memos can fabricate a plausible-looking reasoning trail, so requiring documentation does not guarantee I am seeing the actual process. Second, I have caught cases where a memo's conclusion was correct but reached through a broken chain of reasoning; it passed every outcome check while the analyst's judgment quietly failed to develop. Third, the failures that matter most are the rare ones: an error that survives my spot-checks and reaches a client. Fourth, the analysts' long-term value to the firm depends on their becoming people who can reason without the tools, and I suspect outcome-only incentives push them toward total delegation.

Which policy should I adopt? More than the verdict, I want the reasoning: when is supervising the path worth more than supervising the destination, how should the answer shift as the tools get better at making destinations look good, and is there a principled way to allocate my limited checking effort between the two?
