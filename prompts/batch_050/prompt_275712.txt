An illusionist about phenomenal consciousness and a phenomenal realist have spent a semester in an adversarial collaboration and have agreed to write up the result as a joint statement both will sign. The realist holds that perceptual experiences possess phenomenal properties — intrinsic, qualitative features there is something it is like to instantiate — and that introspection, whatever its fallibility in detail, is not globally mistaken about the existence of such properties. The illusionist holds that introspection systematically misrepresents our experiences as having phenomenal properties, that no such properties exist, and that the proper research program is to explain why we are subject to this misrepresentation (including why it generates persistent intuitions of ineffability, intrinsicness, and epistemic immediacy) in entirely physical, functional terms.

Draft their joint statement. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both parties accept — for instance, that introspective judgments about experience are the phenomenon to be accounted for, that those judgments present experiences as having properties that resist functional characterization, and that a successful theory must explain both first-order phenomenology and our judgments about it — taking care that no agreed claim is so watered down that it smuggles in one side's conclusion; (2) a statement of each residual disagreement, phrased so that the side whose view it describes would endorse the wording, including at least the following: whether the sense that experiences have ineffable qualitative character is a datum that fixes the explanandum or a datum to be explained away; whether the illusionist's position is coherent in the face of the objection that the seeming of phenomenality is itself a form of phenomenality, and whether that objection equivocates between a phenomenal and a merely representational reading of 'seeming'; and whether an adequate physical-functional story about why we judge our experiences to be phenomenal would explain consciousness or merely change the subject; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that resolving them would rationally compel at least one side to revise its view; and (4) for each crux, the case, argument, or finding both parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance (for example, what each predicts a complete functional account of introspective report would or would not leave unexplained, or what each predicts about which thought experiments retain their force under careful stipulation). Where a disagreement that occupied them turns out on inspection to be terminological — as they may find with uses of 'consciousness' and 'experience' — say so explicitly and restate what, if anything, remains in dispute once the words are disambiguated.
