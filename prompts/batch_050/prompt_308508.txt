I am a novelist working on a book about a defeated political movement, and I have become preoccupied with a debate I only half understand: whether history is written by the victors, and more generally how power shapes which version of the past survives. I would like you to act as a scholar equally at home in the philosophy of history and in narrative theory, and to translate that debate into the vocabulary of my own craft, which I know well: focalization, the distinction between narrator and implied author, reliable and unreliable narration, free indirect discourse, and the way a reader's sympathies are managed by whoever controls point of view.

Take the main positions in the debate one by one -- the cynical version (the winners simply dictate the record), the subtler version (the winners determine which archives exist, which questions get asked, and which perspectives count as mainstream rather than eccentric), and the opposing view (that evidence and professional method constrain what any victor can get away with, so the past pushes back). Restate each as precisely as you can in narratological terms. Then give me what I will call the residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly into talk of narration and focalization, which parts you had to distort to make them fit, and which parts have no expression at all in the vocabulary of fiction. Finally, tell me whether the residue points to a defect in my framework -- something narrative theory cannot say -- or to something the historians' debate says less clearly than it thinks.

Think carefully before you answer, and if it helps, sketch candidate mappings briefly before committing to them. Be honest in the residue report rather than forcing correspondences; the failures are what interest me most. Keep the whole response under 650 words.
