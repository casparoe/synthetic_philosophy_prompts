A colleague and I disagree about the justification of moral trade: arrangements in which two agents with conflicting moral views each do what the other's view values more, so that the outcome is better by the lights of both views — for instance, each directing a donation to a cause the other favors, or each moderating a position the other finds objectionable, in exchange for the same. The standard defense runs through moral uncertainty: an agent who is uncertain between moral theories can treat trade as a way to do well by every theory she takes seriously. My credence that this defense succeeds — that moral uncertainty genuinely makes such trades rational and permissible for the agents involved, and not merely strategically useful — is about 0.3; hers is about 0.8.

Give me your own credence, but more importantly decompose the question. I would expect terms at least for: the probability that moral uncertainty is credence-like in the way the argument needs (graded, action-guiding, not reducible to ordinary empirical uncertainty); the probability that choice-worthiness is comparable across moral theories with different units of value, including theories with lexical priorities that seem to resist any splitting of the difference; the probability that an outcome "better by the lights of each theory taken seriously" can justify an action that each of those theories individually condemns; and whatever further terms you think the argument depends on. Assign a rough probability to each with one line of justification, state how you are combining them, and give the bottom-line number. Then tell me which term your estimate is most sensitive to, what argument or evidence would move it most, and which of these sub-questions someone at 0.3 and someone at 0.8 most likely disagree about.
