Here's what I want you to argue: that the reward signal in a reinforcement learning agent cannot count as a pleasure/pain-like valenced state, even in principle, because reward does a fundamentally different kind of computational work than affect does in animals.

Let me sketch the angle I'd like you to develop, since I think the usual debate talks past itself. In animals, affective states seem to do at least two jobs at once: they evaluate outcomes (this was good, this was bad) and they steer ongoing behavior online (pain makes you withdraw *now*, it grabs attention, changes arousal, biases planning). Call these the evaluative role and the steering role. The standard pro-valence argument for RL goes: reward is the scalar the agent is "for", so it's functionally analogous to pleasure. But in standard RL training, the reward signal never does the steering role at all. It gets consumed by the learning algorithm to nudge weights; the policy network doesn't "encounter" the reward the way I encounter pain, the TD error just adjusts parameters. Whatever does the online steering in a trained agent — the policy, the value estimates — was *shaped by* reward but doesn't contain it. So reward looks less like pleasure and more like a fitness function in evolution: a selection pressure on a lineage of policies, not a state of any system. And if the pro-valence side retreats to "fine, maybe the value function or the avoidance behavior is where the valence lives", then the reward signal has dropped out of the story entirely and the claim has deflated into "any goal-directed system has valence".

Please develop this into a real argument: state the premises explicitly and pin down which one does the heavy lifting. Then steelman the two objections I worry most about: (1) online/continual-learning agents where reward arrives during an ongoing episode and genuinely does modulate behavior in something like real time, so the shaping/steering line gets blurry; (2) the awkward point that animal pleasure might *also* just be a learning signal — if dopamine really does implement something like TD error, then maybe the disanalogy cuts against rich valence in *us* rather than against valence in RL agents. Say where the argument survives these objections and where it has to give ground, and finish by identifying the weakest premise and what would have to turn out to be true (conceptually or empirically) for the argument to fail.
