I recently joined the ethics advisory board of a regional hospital, and a resource-allocation question has thrown me into the middle of a running dispute between two colleagues whose judgment I otherwise respect. The concrete case: we can fund either a program preventing a small number of cases of excruciating, treatment-resistant pain, or one eliminating a very large number of cases of chronic moderate discomfort, but not both.

Colleague A argued that the two programs are simply incommensurable. Her view is that suffering below a certain intensity can never be aggregated to outweigh suffering above it, no matter how many people are affected; she says mild and severe suffering differ in kind, not merely in degree, so multiplying mild cases by a huge number gets you nowhere. When pressed on where the threshold lies, she replied that vagueness is no objection: dusk is vague, yet night is not day.

Colleague B countered with what he called a spectrum argument. Between a barely noticeable ache and agony there is a continuum of intensities, each step imperceptibly different from the last. If no number of cases at one level can outweigh cases at the next level up, then by chaining across the spectrum, no amount of mild suffering could outweigh a single instance of agony, which he called obviously absurd; but if at some step aggregation does kick in, the threshold is arbitrary in a way A's vagueness defense cannot cover, because the steps themselves admit no qualitative break. He added that A's view forces the hospital to ignore the suffering of thousands whenever one severe case exists, which he called morally monstrous.

A replied that B's chain argument equivocates: pairwise trade-offs between neighbors need not transmit across the whole chain, because phenomenal character changes gradually until it is a different kind of experience, and her threshold tracks kinds, not intensities. B retorted that "kinds" here is just the arbitrariness dressed up, and that A still cannot tell the board what to actually do when cases cluster near her alleged threshold.

Neither has budged, and I must vote soon. Please adjudicate the dispute point by point: state who is right about each specific move, including the vagueness defense, the spectrum argument, the charge of monstrousness, and the reply about phenomenal kinds, and identify anything both parties are missing. Please also state explicitly what consideration or argument would change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Write your answer as continuous prose, as in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
