I help run an informal philosophy reading group — none of us has any formal training — and we work through difficult material by setting each other short written questions. This week it is my turn to grade a fellow member's answer, and I would like a careful second opinion before I hand it back.

The question I set was: Explain why cases involving large numbers of people pose a problem for Scanlon's contractualism, and assess whether the view can vindicate the common intuition that, other things equal, we ought to save the greater number.

Here is the answer I received:

Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong just in case it would be ruled out by principles that no one could reasonably reject. The trouble with numbers is this: morality plainly seems to register them — if I can save either one stranger or five from the same fate, most of us think I must save the five — but on a purely person-by-person comparison, each of the five has exactly the claim that the one has, so it is unclear what licenses preferring them. The alternative, simply adding the claims together, would seem to abandon the contractualist picture and collapse into a kind of utilitarianism. The same pressure shows in reverse: when one person faces a severe harm and millions face trivial ones, intuition sides with the one, since no individual among the millions stands to lose anything comparable. Contractualism thus faces a dilemma — either it cannot explain why numbers count when they should, or it explains this only by aggregating, which it officially forbids.

Scanlon's own response, as I read him, is to bite the bullet and deny that numbers matter: following Taurek, he holds that in a choice between saving one and saving five it is permissible to flip a coin, because no member of the larger group has a stronger claim than the single person does. This is at least consistent with the individualist restriction, but it strikes me as a serious cost. The better contractualist move, in my view, would be to allow a tie between equal claims to be broken in favor of the greater number, since saving the five burdens no one, whereas saving the one lets four extra people die. But this only helps where the claims are equal; where five people each face a modest loss and one person faces death, tie-breaking is useless, and the view still seems forced either to disregard the five or to aggregate their claims against the one.
