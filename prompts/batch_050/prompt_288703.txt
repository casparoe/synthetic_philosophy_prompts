In a graduate seminar I help run on philosophy of law, we recently spent two weeks on the Hart–Devlin debate, and a student proposed that the whole dispute is best understood not through Wolfenden-era Britain but through France's face-covering controversy. I want to test that proposal properly. The salient facts, as I understand them: in 2010 France enacted a law prohibiting the concealment of one's face in public spaces, a measure aimed in practice at the niqab, carrying a small fine and, in some cases, a mandatory citizenship course. On the government's own figures, the number of women affected was tiny — a small fraction of French Muslim women. Polls at the time suggested broad majority support for the ban. When the law was challenged before the European Court of Human Rights in the S.A.S. v. France case, decided in 2014, the Court upheld it: it was unpersuaded by the public-safety and gender-equality justifications, but accepted France's argument that the ban protected the minimum requirements of life in society — the idea, roughly, that reciprocal visibility of the face is a condition of people living together. And there is a coda my student found telling: during the COVID-19 pandemic, the same French state that fined women for covering their faces in public required everyone to cover their faces in public.

Here is what I'd like from you. First, do the mapping carefully in the theory-to-case direction. On Devlin's side: he argued that a society is held together by a shared morality and may legally suppress conduct that arouses the genuine intolerance, indignation, and disgust of the ordinary reasonable person, even absent harm — and that failing to do so risks the loosening of social bonds. Does the French episode fit his picture, or does it misfit it in instructive ways? Consider: was the public's support the organic sentiment of Devlin's ordinary person, or something shaped by political campaigns? Is "living together" the social-fabric thesis wearing secular clothes, or is it a different idea entirely? On Hart's side: he rejected both the disintegration thesis and the equation of popular disgust with justification, insisting on evidence that enforcement protects something other than feelings — though he was more permissive than his reputation suggests about public offense. Does Hart's framework condemn the ban straightforwardly, or does his treatment of offense leave more room for it than a quick reading of the debate would suggest? Be precise about where each theorist's framework speaks to a feature of the case and where it is genuinely silent rather than forcing it to pronounce.

Second, run the analysis in the reverse direction: what does the episode show about the debate? I can see three candidate verdicts — that it vindicates Devlin, since even a self-consciously liberal republic reaches for morals-enforcement when its self-image feels threatened; that it vindicates Hart, since the ban bought no measurable cohesion, arguably aggravated the tensions it claimed to address, and had to be laundered into rights-language before a court would accept it, which suggests the harm principle exerts a gravitational pull even on its opponents; or that it reveals the debate's parochialism, since both men imagined a relatively homogeneous society deciding whether to enforce its morality, whereas the French case is about a majority defining the terms of citizenship for a small minority — a problem of whose morality gets to count as the society's, which neither theorist squarely addresses. Adjudicate between these, and commit: tell me which verdict you find strongest and how confident you are, distinguishing what the evidence of the episode supports from what is speculation. Finally, if you think the whole framing is confused — for instance, if you think the ban is better understood as republican ideology or symbolic politics rather than legal moralism at all, so that treating it as a Hart–Devlin case begs the question — say so plainly rather than politely complying with a misposed task.
