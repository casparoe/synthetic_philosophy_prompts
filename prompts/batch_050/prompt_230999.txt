I'm a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on causation, and this week we're doing causation by omission. Our professor has a running exercise where she gives us her verdicts on cases and we have to reverse-engineer the principle she's using. I'm stuck, so I'd like your help reconstructing her rule and predicting her next verdict.

Here are the verdicts she's given so far, as best I recorded them:

1. A lifeguard on shift naps on the stand while a child drowns in plain view. She says the lifeguard's omission is a cause of the death.
2. A tourist walking along the same beach notices the child struggling, could raise the alarm at no risk to himself, and walks on. She says his omission is not a cause of the death (whatever else is wrong with it morally).
3. A neighbor who explicitly agreed to water someone's plants while they were away forgets, and the plants die. She says the omission is a cause of the wilting.
4. A wealthy person who decides not to make a donation that, given the charity's track record, would very likely have saved a life. She says the omission is not a cause of any death.
5. Two guards on duty at a dam both fail to open the spillway during a storm; either one alone could have prevented the downstream flood. She says each guard's omission is a cause of the flood, not just the pair's omissions jointly.
6. The only pharmacist in a remote town refuses, on personal grounds, to fill a valid prescription she could easily fill, and the patient's condition worsens. She says the refusal is a cause of the harm.

The new case: at a crowded tram stop, a man falls onto the tracks with a tram approaching. There's a well-known and long-standing local custom — everyone in the city knows it — that whoever is standing nearest the emergency brake pulls it. One bystander, closest to the brake, sees the man fall, understands the custom, and deliberately doesn't pull it, while the people behind him hang back because they assume he will. The man is killed. The bystander had no assigned role, made no promise, and broke no law; the only thing that singled him out was the custom and everyone else's reliance on it.

What I'd like from you:

First, set out the candidate rules that fit all six earlier verdicts — I count at least three plausible ones (something duty-based, something based on deviation from what normally happens, something based on reasonable reliance or expectation), but tell me if I'm missing one or double-counting. For each rule, say what it predicts about the tram case and why. Then give your best guess as to which rule she's actually running, and therefore what her verdict on the tram case will be, with your reasons. Note that rule 5 (the two guards) seems designed to knock out a simple but-for counterfactual reading, so factor that in. Finally, describe one further case — ideally a realistic one, not a science-fiction variant — where the remaining candidate rules give different verdicts, so I can put it to her and discriminate between them.

One request about calibration: please keep separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on causation (for instance, that omissions can be causes at all, rather than the question being nonsense) from what is still contested (for instance, which principle picks out the cause-making omissions from the infinitely many non-causes). Where my professor's verdict pattern presupposes a contested stance, say so plainly rather than presenting it as the received view. I'd rather reason from the cases than from a bibliography, so please don't rest the analysis on named theories or citations — just the cases, the rules, and where the real disagreement lies.
