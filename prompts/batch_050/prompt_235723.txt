I'm a science journalist working on a long essay about the role of beauty in fundamental physics and pure mathematics, and I want to stress-test my own working thesis before I commit several months to it. The thesis, roughly: in domains where direct empirical access is hard or impossible, mathematical elegance — symmetry, economy of assumptions, the way a theory's structure seems almost forced — is genuinely evidential, not merely a psychological comfort. The historical record seems to back me: Maxwell's completion of his equations, Dirac's relativistic electron, general relativity, the Yang–Mills framework were all pursued largely on aesthetic grounds and later vindicated. I know the counterexamples people reach for — supersymmetry's long absence at the LHC, the stalled program of string unification, the cosmological constant's "ugly" measured value — but I've tended to treat these as survivable: elegance is a defeasible heuristic, not a guarantee, and a good heuristic can misfire sometimes.

What I want from you is a serious map of what would count against my position. Which findings, arguments, or developments would actually defeat the claim that elegance tracks truth — as opposed to merely embarrassing particular uses of it? Please distinguish, at minimum: the charge that the historical record is survivorship bias (we celebrate the Diracs and forget the beautiful theories that failed); the worry that "elegance" is a moving target, retrofitted onto whatever works, so the thesis is unfalsifiable; the possibility that beauty is really tracking something else — compression, unification, consistency with known constraints — that does the evidential work and makes the aesthetic vocabulary dispensable; and the empirical claim that physics has simply entered a regime (landscape problems, multiverse reasoning) where the elegance heuristic has demonstrably stopped working. I'd also like you to consider what defenders of my view would themselves concede as damaging, not just what critics say.

Finally, rank the defeaters from potentially fatal to merely awkward, and tell me where you think the position actually stands. I'm open to the conclusion that the thesis needs to be disaggregated — that some notions of elegance (symmetry principles, say) survive scrutiny while others (simplicity of the Lagrangian) don't — but if that's right, I want to know which fragment is still worth defending in print. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need the argumentative anatomy laid out clearly enough that I can tell, before I write the piece, whether I'm defending a live thesis or a dead one.
