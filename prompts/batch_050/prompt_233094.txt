Okay so I just got out of a three hour argument with a friend about what makes pain bad. Her view is that the badness is all in the minding of it -- the aversiveness, the "get this to stop" part -- and she kept bringing up people with pain asymbolia, who say they still feel pain, can locate it, describe it as burning or stabbing, but insist it doesn't bother them at all. My instinct is that the raw sensation can be bad even when someone claims not to care, but I couldn't really defend that well.

Here's why I actually care: I help write care policy at a small residential facility, and a lot of our residents can't reliably report pain (advanced dementia, some nonverbal). Staff are making these calls several times a day with maybe five minutes to decide, and none of them have any philosophy background. I want to turn whatever the right account of pain's badness is into a procedure staff can actually run: which observations (wincing, guarding, restlessness, smiling through it, flinching then seeming fine) count as evidence of suffering vs. mere sensation, in what order to check things, when the procedure says treat regardless of what the resident says or does, and where it has to hand off to the nurse's judgment because the question isn't one a checklist can settle. Be honest about what the procedure will get wrong and which cases it definately can't resolve. Then run it on two residents: one who reacts strongly to painful stimuli but cheerfully says it doesn't hurt (the asymbolia-like case), and a stoic who under-reports everything but has stopped eating. If your procedure gives an answer I won't like on one of these, say so rather than smoothing it over.
