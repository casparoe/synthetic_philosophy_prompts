I am a novelist working on a book about a real historical figure — a minor 19th-century naturalist, dead for over a century, with one living great-grandchild who has written to me asking that I treat her ancestor kindly. The documentary record is thin, and I have invented a subplot in which he plagiarized a colleague's discovery. Nothing in the record contradicts this, but nothing supports it either; I invented it because it makes the book better, and because the real colleague he overshadowed deserves, in some sense I can't quite articulate, to be avenged. My editor says the dead have no interests and can't be wronged, so only the great-grandchild's feelings matter. I'm not so sure — but I also notice that my scruples seem to apply only to inventing his vice, not to the sympathetic inner life I've given him, which is equally invented. Am I being inconsistent, or is there a principled line between kinds of fabrication here? I'd like advice on how to think about what I owe him, his colleague, and the great-grandchild — and I'd like you to state how confident you are in the view you give, and to single out the one point in it you are least sure of.
