I'm TAing an undergrad philosophy of mind course, and for our week on collective minds (built around Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary — the sentient ant colony in Gödel, Escher, Bach, who can hold a conversation even though no individual ant understands English) I want to make a decision-tree handout. The tree should walk a student from a shared starting commitment to a named position on the question: could the colony itself have a mind, or any experience at all?

Some specs:

- Start the tree at the commitment that the colony genuinely does process information at a level no single ant grasps — i.e. the student already accepts that colony-level patterns are real and do causal work. The tree should only sort out what follows about mentality, not relitigate whether emergence is real.
- Roughly 6-8 branch nodes, each phrased as a yes/no question a student could answer without knowing any of the labels. Use concrete cases at the nodes where possible, and I specifically want these two to show up somewhere: (1) replacing the ants with people carrying radios, where each person follows one ant's simple rules — does the resulting "nation brain" think?; (2) gradually upgrading the ants' signals from crude pheromone blobs to rich messages — does the colony wake up at some threshold, and if so, where?
- Leaves must include at least: Hofstadter-style symbolic-level functionalism (the colony thinks, at its own level), Searle-ish biological naturalism (only brains, wrong substrate), integrated information theory (the colony's integration is too low relative to the individual ants', so no), global workspace theory, List & Pettit style group agency (the colony can litterally have beliefs and intentions, but that settles nothing about experience), and panpsychism (yes, but trivially, since everything has some). If you think illusionism belongs as a leaf — nobody has phenomenal consciousness in the disputed sense, so the question dissolves — add it and say where it branches off.
- Each leaf gets: the position name, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment when applied to the ant case specifically (not its problems in general).
- Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: figure out which questions the positions genuinely disagree on, then note any combination of answers that nobody occupies and diagnose why (incoherent? unattractive? merely unexplored?).
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really separate — I suspect global workspace and plain functionalism end up in the same spot, so if they do, say what further question would split them.

Keep it to about a page, aimed at smart undergrads who've read the Ant Fugue dialogue but nothing else on the topic.
