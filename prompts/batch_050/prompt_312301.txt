I write a magazine column that debunks fringe history and archaeology claims, and my editor wants us to be more consistant about which ones we bother going after. Our track record so far: we went hard at a hit ancient-astronauts TV series (massive audience, and the whole premise basically says non-European peoples couldn't have built their own monuments); we went after a bestselling book about a lost ice-age civilisation that supposedly taught the Egyptians everything (again huge sales, again quietly takes the credit off the actual builders); we passed on an obscure forum theory that half of medieval maps are forgeries (tiny readership, hurts nobody); and we passed on a local radio regular who reckons Hadrian's Wall is Victorian (no audience, no stakes). New case on the desk: a podcast climbing the charts claims a famous chalk hill figure is thousands of years older than archaeologists date it. As far as I can tell it's just wrong rather than harmful -- nobody's heritage is being denied, no land claims, no funding diverted, the host seems sincere -- but it's audience is already bigger than either of the cases we debunked. What should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Give me the candidate rules that fit all four past calls, what each says about the podcast, which past case is the closest analog, and which further hypothetical case would tell the surviving rules apart. Format: a table, one row per candidate rule, columns = (1) the rule as you'd state it, (2) how it explains the four past calls, (3) its verdict on the podcast (debunk or pass), (4) what principle we'd effectively be adopting if we followed it -- plus a final row with your bottom-line recommendation and the tie-breaking reason.
