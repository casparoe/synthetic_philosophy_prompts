I'm in a philosophy seminar on formal epistemology and we just spent three weeks on accuracy-first epistemology / epistemic utility theory, and I think I've talked myself into something that can't be right, but I also can't see where. As I understand the Joyce/Pettigrew style arguments: you define a measure of how inaccurate a set of credences is (we did the Brier score in class), and then you show that any set of credences violating the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated -- there's some probabilistic set of credences that's guaranteed to be closer to the truth no matter how the world turns out. So the rationality of probabilism gets derived from nothing but the premise that accuracy is what epistemically matters. Fine so far.

But here's my confusion. If the accuracy arguments establish that rationality requires precise, probabilistic credences, then how can anyone in this literature still take imprecise credences (interval-valued ones, sets of probability functions) seriously as legitimate epistemic states? Wouldn't an imprecise credal state just be accuracy-dominated too, since for any way the world could be, a sharp credence at the right spot would beat it? It seems like accuracy-first epistemology, if it works at all, should settle the question against imprecise credences outright, yet people like Joyce himself (I think?) and others keep treating them as a live option. Are they just being inconsistent with their own framework, or is there a step in the dominance argument that quietly doesn't apply to the imprecise case? Someone in class also muttered that the whole thing is circular because the choice of scoring rule "smuggles in" the probability axioms, which sounds wrong to me too but I couldn't articulate why. I'd love it if you could separate out what the accuracy arguments actually prove from what they assume, and explain where my reasoning above goes off the rails.
