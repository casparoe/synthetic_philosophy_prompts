I recently spent fourteen months restoring my late uncle's wooden sailboat, and the yard's invoice has set off an argument I cannot shake: the insurer's assessor says that with so much of the hull, deck, and rigging replaced, what is moored at the marina is "a boat built in 2023," while I maintain it is the same boat my uncle launched in 1968. The dispute has made the philosophical problem of material constitution feel anything but academic.

Construct for me a thought experiment in the statue-and-clay tradition, engineered to the following specifications. The standard argument for coincident objects runs on an asymmetry of persistence conditions: the lump survives squashing, the statue does not, so they differ in modal properties and cannot be identical. Build a case in which this asymmetry is deliberately neutralized -- that is, a scenario in which the "artifact-like" object and the "material-like" object are stipulated, by the natural facts of the story rather than by fiat, to have matching careers: they come into existence together, survive the same changes, and would cease to exist under the same circumstances. I want to know whether the coincidence theorist still has grounds for counting two objects rather than one.

Constraints: the scenario must be realistic -- a conservator's workshop, a boatyard, a foundry, or the like; no time travel, no teletransportation, no perfect duplicators. The case must not assume a theory of persistence (in particular, do not help yourself to temporal parts). And the verdicts must be generated by the theories, not stipulated into the description.

Please structure your answer with the following sections, in this order:

1. The Scenario -- one paragraph, roughly 120 words, stated neutrally enough that it does not presuppose either theory.
2. Verdicts by Theory -- roughly 200 words. State what a coincidence theorist (two objects, spatially overlapping) and what a monist (one object falling under multiple sortals, or one lump and one statue related by constitution without identity) each say about the case, and whether the coincidence theorist's usual modal argument still has purchase here.
3. The Load-Bearing Feature -- roughly 80 words. Identify which feature of the scenario does the work of neutralizing the persistence-condition asymmetry, and say whether removing it returns the case to an ordinary statue-and-clay setup.
4. The Coincidence Theorist's Reply -- roughly 120 words. Give the strongest response available to the coincidence theorist: what, if anything, still distinguishes the two candidate objects once their careers are matched, and does that ground the difference or merely assert it?

Close with one sentence on what this means for my dispute with the assessor, if anything.
