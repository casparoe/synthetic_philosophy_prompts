I spent my career as an experimental physicist and have only recently begun reading moral philosophy in a systematic way. In discussions of existential risk, I repeatedly encounter a simple argument for prioritizing human extinction: extinction forecloses an astronomical number of possible future lives, so even a minute probability of it can dominate almost every other consideration. This seems to neglect a distinction that matters morally. Some catastrophes eliminate future persons, while others preserve or generate sentient beings under conditions of severe and exceptionally durable suffering. Call the latter lock-in suffering catastrophes: outcomes in which coercive institutions, engineered environments, artificial systems, or irreversible biological changes make extreme suffering self-perpetuating and unusually resistant to later correction.

Please argue that, when choosing how to allocate scarce mitigation resources, a sufficiently grave lock-in suffering catastrophe can deserve priority over a more probable extinction risk even if conventional expected-value calculations initially favor the extinction intervention. Your argument should not merely appeal to the slogan that suffering is bad. Develop a principled case based on asymmetries between nonexistence and intensely bad existence, the difference between reversible and locked-in harms, uncertainty about whether future populations would be net-positive, and the possibility that interventions may shift a catastrophe toward worse rather than simply more or less likely outcomes. Address the obvious objection that giving special weight to extreme suffering is either an ad hoc departure from expected-value reasoning or a covert form of negative utilitarianism.
