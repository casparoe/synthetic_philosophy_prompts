i'm leading the discussion when our film club does Jurassic Park and I want to get past the usual "hubris bad" take. Here's what I keep circling: the film's official argument against the park seems to be epistemic -- Malcolm's whole deal is that complex systems are unpredictable, so control will always fail no matter how careful you are. But the live debates around de-extinction and gene drives put the weight somewhere else entirely: the objection is irreversibility, that once you've released something self-replicating into a shared ecosystem there's no recall, and that's a problem even if your predictions are perfect. So does the film actually have the second argument, or does it just dramatize the first while making you *feel* the second? The dinosaurs breeding despite the all-female design and the lysine contingency looks like Malcolm being proved right, but is it also the irreversibility point in disguise? And does the film ever care that nobody downstream consented -- the Costa Ricans who'd end up sharing a country with reintrodued apex predators get no say and barely exist in the movie. Finally, where does the de-extinction-ethics reading just break down? The dinosaurs are theme park IP owned by InGen, not a conservation intervention, so maybe Hammond isn't really the de-extinction advocate at all. I'd like a reading that keeps the epistemic and irreversibility objections distinct, says which scenes carry which, and admits where the film resists the frame.
