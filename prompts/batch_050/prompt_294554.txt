I have recently taken up my first teaching position, as a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in metaphysics, and this week the first batch of exam scripts landed on my desk. The marks I award are subject to student appeal, so I want to be careful: I would rather not wave through fluent prose that contains philosophical errors, nor penalise an answer for imprecisions that do not matter. Below is one of the questions on the paper and one student's answer to it, reproduced in full.

**The exam question**

Explain Locke's account of personal identity over time, including his distinction between the identity of a man and the identity of a person. State Reid's brave officer objection as precisely as you can. Does the objection refute Locke's criterion? Defend your answer, and consider at least one response available to a defender of Locke.

**The student's answer**

Locke distinguishes the identity of a man from the identity of a person. The identity of a man, like that of any living organism, consists in participation in a single continued life through successive particles of matter vitally united to the same organised body. A person, by contrast, Locke defines as a thinking intelligent being with reason and reflection, able to consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places. Personal identity therefore consists in sameness of consciousness: a present person is identical with some past being just in case the present person is conscious of that being's experiences as his own. Identity of person extends exactly as far back as consciousness can be extended, which is why Locke thinks the same person could survive a change of body, and why sameness of human body without sameness of consciousness does not amount to sameness of person.

Reid's brave officer objection runs as follows. A boy is flogged at school for robbing an orchard. Years later, as a young officer, he captures an enemy standard in his first campaign, and at that time he still clearly remembers the flogging. In old age, now a general, he remembers capturing the standard but has completely forgotten the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is identical with the officer, and the officer with the boy, yet the general is not identical with the boy, since he is not conscious of the boy's experiences. This, Reid argues, is absurd, because identity is logically transitive while the memory relation is not. Reid concludes that memory cannot constitute personal identity but is merely our evidence for it, and he takes the case to show in addition that Locke's criterion is circular: being conscious of a past experience as one's own already presupposes that one is the same person who had it, so consciousness cannot be what that sameness consists in.

The objection succeeds. It demonstrates that memory is not necessary for personal identity, since the general is the very person who was flogged even though he cannot remember it; and by the same token it shows that memory is not sufficient either, for the transitivity failure arises precisely from treating remembered connection as identity-conferring. A Lockean can nonetheless salvage the spirit of the view by weakening the criterion: rather than requiring direct memory of the earlier experience, require only an overlapping chain of memory connections, linking the general to the officer and the officer to the boy. This restores transitivity and delivers the intuitively correct verdict that the general is the boy grown old. Reid's case therefore refutes Locke's criterion in its strictest letter but not in its underlying idea.
