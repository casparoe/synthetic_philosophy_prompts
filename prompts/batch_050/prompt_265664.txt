So I've talked myself into a fairly specific version of deontology and I want to stress-test it. My position is that agent-relative constraints (don't kill, don't torture, even when doing so would prevent more killings or torturings) are not actually paradoxical, because I reject the premise that generates the paradox: I deny there's any impersonal standpoint from which "five people being murdered by others" and "me murdering one" sit on a single scale such that the first is five times worse in a way that's action-guiding for me. On my view, moral reasons just aren't rankings of outcomes from nowhere, so the classic Scheffler-style objection — that constraints are irrational if what we care about is minimising constraint-violations — never gets off the ground, and I don't need the fancier defences (Kamm's inviolability stuff, the victim-centred views, the "my agency is special" line) to do any work.

What would count against this? I want a proper map of the view's exposure, in British English please. Specifically:

- Which actual cases put pressure on the "no impersonal scale" move rather than on constraints generally? I'm thinking of cases where refusing to kill one really does seem monstrous (the trolley variants where the numbers get absurd, the terrorist-with-hostages cases, the "you're the only one who can stop a massacre" cases) — but which of these target my move specifically, and which only target side constraints simpliciter and so leave me untouched?
- Are there formal-ish results I should be worried about? E.g. if I accept transitivity of "better than", some weak aggregation principle, and the claim that one death is worse than zero deaths, am I committed to the impersonal scale whether I like it or not? Can the scale-denial be made coherent without giving up comparability elsewhere, or does it collapse into "anything goes" about numbers?
- What would people who agree with me about constraints but take the other routes (agent-relative value, inviolability) say my view gets wrong — i.e., what do I lose by refusing to say that constraint-violations are impersonally bad? If I can't say a world with five murders is worse than a world with one, is that a reductio or a bullet worth biting?
- Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a defender of my view would concede it counts against them or dismiss it as begging the question. Be honest about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability — if "there's no impersonal standpoint" just screens off every consequence-based objection, is that a feature or a bug for a view of this kind?

Don't just give me the generic consequentialist vs deontology survey; I've read it. I want to know where the view bleeds.
