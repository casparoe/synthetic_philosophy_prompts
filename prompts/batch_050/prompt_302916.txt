So I'm a novelist, and last week at our little writers' group two friends of mine got into it and its still bugging me. For context: one of them, Martin, had just read this Hofstadter and Sander book, Surfaces and Essences, where the big claim is that analogy isn't a decoration on thought, it IS thought -- that every time you categorize something you're really just noticing an analogy to things you've filed away before, so analogy-making runs the whole show from word choice up to scientific breakthroughs.

Here's roughly how it went. Martin said novelists should take this seriously and stop treating metaphor as a spice you sprinkle on top of plain literal prose -- since the reader's mind runs on analogy anyway, a novel is just a controlled cascade of analogies, and the "literal" bits are illusions. Dana (she writes very spare, Carver-ish stuff) pushed back hard. She said even if the psychology is right, it proves nothing about writing: the book is about how cognition works under the hood, not about what makes sentences good, and a page crammed with explicit analogies is usually worse than a page of flat exact description. Martin said her flat exact descriptions are only "flat" because the analogies in them have gone stale and conventional -- calling something a chair is already an analogy to past chairs, her plainest noun is a frozen comparison, so shes doing the thing she claims to avoid. Dana said that's a bait and switch: maybe categorization is analogy in some deep sense, but a dead, invisible analogy and a fresh metaphor do completely different work in a reader's experience, and lumping them together erases the only distinction a writer actually cares about. Martin said fine, but then she owes an account of why freshness matters that isn't just a restatement of "good writing good," and the cognitive story gives exactly that account -- fresh analogies force new categorizations, dead ones don't. Dana's last move, before the wine ran out, was to say that by that standard the theory is useless to her, because it can only ever tell her after the fact why a sentence worked, never help her write the next one.

I stayed out of it, mostly because I could feel both of them being right. Can you walk through the exchange point by point and say who's right about what -- including whether Martin is actually entitled to move from the cognitive claim to the writing advice, and whether Dana's dead-metaphor/fresh-metaphor distinction survives his objection? And is there something both of them are missing about what a novelist would actually do with the Hofstadter-Sander picture?
