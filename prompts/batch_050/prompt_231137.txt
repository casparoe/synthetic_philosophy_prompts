So a physics department and a philosophy department share a building, and after years of hallway sniping -- the physicists keep citing that line about the philosophy of science being about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds, the philosophers keep muttering that every physicist does philosophy, just badly -- the dean has made them run an adversarial collaboration before she'll renew funding for their joint foundations-of-physics seminar. Draft the joint statement they come back with.

It should have: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps will actually sign, covering things like what the historical record does and doesn't show about philosophers influencing real physics (think Bell, or Einstein reading empiricists), whether the "philosophy is dead" crowd is attacking a strawman or a real practice, and what each field is even for; (2) a disagreements table where each row states one surviving disagreement, with each side's position phrased so that side would sign its own wording -- the physicists' rows shouldn't sneer, the philosophers' rows shouldn't be defensive; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions where an answer would genuinely force someone to update, not just reframe. I'd guess one crux is whether philosophy's contribution should be measured by discoveries it caused or by bad ideas it cleared away, and another is what "useful" even means when the useful work eventually stops being called philosophy (nobody credits philosophy with inventing logic anymore, which is sort of the philosophers' point and sort of the physicists').

For each crux, specify something concrete both sides agree would bear on it -- a historical episode to dig into, a survey of working physicists, a look at where foundations-of-physics grants went and what came out -- and write down each side's prediction in advance, adversarial-collaboration style. If you conclude that part of their fight is really about words (e.g. they're using "philosophy" to mean different things), say which part and show how the two readings map onto each other.

The tone should be a real document both sides could sign, not a debate transcript and not a verdict from you about who's right. It's fine if the final list of agreed claims is disappointingly short -- that's honest -- but don't pad it with vacuous stuff like "both fields value rigor" unless you can make it say something.
