I've been volunteering with a group that prepares continuing-education materials for trial judges, and I keep getting stuck on a feature of American evidence law that seems to smuggle in a whole philosophy of science without admitting it. In the United States, since the Supreme Court's 1993 Daubert decision, federal trial judges act as gatekeepers for expert testimony: before a jury is allowed to hear from a scientific expert, the judge must independently assess whether the expert's reasoning and methodology are reliable. The decision itself and the standard judicial bench books list several factors for the judge to weigh, and the first one on every list is whether the expert's theory or technique can be and has been tested -- which everyone involved reads, often explicitly, as asking whether the theory is falsifiable. Sitting right alongside it are other factors: whether the work has been peer-reviewed and published, whether the technique has a known error rate, and whether it enjoys general acceptance in the relevant scientific community (a holdover from the older Frye standard that Daubert replaced). So the courtroom practice is, concretely: a judge with no scientific training decides what counts as science, guided by a checklist whose first item is falsifiability and whose later items are publication venues, error rates, and consensus.

My question is what this practice presupposes. The falsifiability prong looks like it presupposes something close to Popper's solution to the demarcation problem -- that what makes a claim scientific is its exposure to genuine risk of refutation -- but the general-acceptance and peer-review prongs look like they presuppose something almost opposite: that the relevant community's settled practices define good science, which is much closer to a sociological or Kuhnian picture in which normal science is whatever the paradigm's practitioners do. Meanwhile the error-rate prong sounds like a third thing again, a kind of reliabilism about methods. Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of science here: is this a coherent composite view, three theories stapled together, or one theory wearing three costumes? I want to know which accounts of demarcation the practice strictly presupposes and which it merely fits comfortably, which rival views it quietly rules out -- would a straightforwardly Bayesian picture of confirmation license any of these prongs, or does it imply that the whole checklist is a category error, since confirmation comes in degrees and nothing is ever crisply falsified given that any test presupposes auxiliary hypotheses? And tell me concretely what gatekeeping would have to look like if the falsifiability prong were dropped: my chemist friend insists no working scientist she knows ever talks about falsifying anything, and that fields like epidemiology or forensic pattern-matching would either all pass or all fail depending on how charitably the judge reads them, which suggests the prong does no real work except in the hands of judges who already know what conclusion they want.

I don't want a survey of positions with the verdict left to me. Commit to an answer: tell me which single philosophy of science, if any, best rationalizes the checklist as it actually operates, whether you think the practice is coherent as it stands, and -- taking a definite position -- which prong you would cut or rewrite first if you were redrafting the standard, with reasons. If part of your answer is that the practice is underdetermined and several philosophies of science would license it, that's fine, but then commit to a view about which of those the judges themselves are implicitly relying on, and why.
