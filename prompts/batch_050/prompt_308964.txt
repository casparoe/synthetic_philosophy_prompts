I teach a seminar on distributive justice, and a student recently pressed me on something I realized I couldn't answer cleanly. The jurisdiction we live in, like most wealthy democracies, taxes large transfers of wealth at death only lightly and only above a threshold that exempts the overwhelming majority of estates, while treating transfers to one's children as a paradigm of legitimate private choice. The same society, however, organizes much of its policy around equal opportunity: it funds public education precisely to offset differences in family background, it prohibits employers from favoring applicants on grounds of lineage, and it treats the sale of jobs, grades, or public offices as corrupt. So we forbid a parent from buying a child a position outright, yet permit a parent to hand that child a fortune whose purchasing power over positions, credentials, and security is enormous. In the second book of his Principles of Political Economy, in the chapter on property, John Stuart Mill tries to split this difference: he argues that the owner's power to dispose of property by gift, including by bequest after death, belongs to the very idea of ownership, whereas the heir's claim to receive is not grounded in the owner's rights at all and may therefore be limited by the state — and he in fact favored capping what any person could acquire by the mere favor of others, so that anyone wanting more would have to work for it. I'd like you to treat our actual inheritance practice, not Mill's proposal, as the object of analysis.

My question is what philosophical commitments that practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory that would make the pattern coherent: does it only make sense if the family rather than the individual is the real unit of property holding, if the dead retain authority over what they owned, if recipients have some natural claim on parental resources, or if equality of opportunity was never meant to reach the transmission of advantage between generations at all? For each candidate, say whether the practice strictly requires it or is merely compatible with it, and flag where the practice is genuinely underdetermined — where several rival theories would license exactly the same rules, so that the pattern of exemptions and rates tells us less than it appears to. I'd also like you to stress-test Mill's own way out: if the liberty to give is an incident of ownership, does limiting what the receiver may take actually leave that liberty intact, or does it merely relocate the interference? And if his distinction holds, does it dissolve the tension I described or sharpen it, given that the giver's power to transfer advantage is precisely what equal opportunity seems to condemn? Finally, tell me concretely what a policy built from the start on the equal-opportunity commitment would have to change — not just rates and thresholds, but which of the surrounding institutions (family privacy, lifetime gifts, trusts, the treatment of the family home) would have to be reconceived, and which commitments we would discover we hold more dearly than equal opportunity itself.
