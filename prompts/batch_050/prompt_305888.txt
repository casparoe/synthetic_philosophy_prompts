I run a mixed philosophy-and-math club at a community college, about 18 people, and we have 75 minutes. A few of us got into an argument about the two-envelope paradox after trivia night, and I realized that even the people who could state the usual reasoning didn’t really feel why it is tempting. Design an in-class game that makes the pull of the switching argument show up in the players’ own decisions before anyone names the paradox. It should use envelopes or cards, tokens instead of real money, and some mechanism for repeated rounds so participants can test strategies and recieve feedback. Ideally, the game should distinguish at least two situations: one where switching is genuinely rational and one where the naive expected-value argument seems to recommend switching even though something has gone wrong.

Please give me the full setup, materials, hidden rules, payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The players should not just vote abstractly on whether they would switch; they should make choices under uncertainty, see outcomes, maybe revise their strategies, and discover where the symmetry intuition breaks. Include a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto conditional expectation, prior probabilities, and the difference between knowing that one envelope contains twice the other and knowing the probability distribution over possible amounts. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to prevent the mathematically confident students from immediately turning it into a lecture, and which feature of the original paradox the activity cannot fully reproduce.
