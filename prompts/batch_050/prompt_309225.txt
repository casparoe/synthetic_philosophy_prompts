Having retired last year from a career in experimental chemistry, I have been making up for lost time by attending a philosophy discussion circle at our local library. Last week the topic was the so-called paradox of blackmail — the puzzle that it is criminal to demand money in exchange for not publishing truthful, lawfully obtained information, even though publishing the information is perfectly legal, and so is politely asking for money. I did not contribute myself, but two members had a sustained exchange that I have been turning over since, and I would like a neutral verdict on it.

The exchange was prompted by a hypothetical put forward by M, a retired corporate lawyer: imagine a company that compiles personal dossiers exclusively from truthful, lawfully obtained material — court records, property filings, the subject's own public posts — and offers anyone a paid subscription under which their dossier is withheld from the company's searchable public index. The moment the subscription lapses, the dossier goes live. No threatening letters are sent; the terms are stated plainly on the website, and nobody is obliged to subscribe.

M's opening claim was that this is blackmail in everything but name, and ought to be criminal for exactly the same reason: the entire revenue of the enterprise consists of payments to suppress truthful information whose publication would damage the payer. The paradox of blackmail, he said, merely shows that a transaction can be wrongful as a whole even though each ingredient, taken alone, is innocent — chemistry, he added for my benefit, works the same way.

E, a software engineer, replied that M had the paradox upside down. If decades of clever people have failed to explain why the conjunction of two legal acts is criminal, the rational conclusion is not that the conjunction is somehow criminal anyway, but that blackmail law is a historical accident we tolerate because it rarely inconveniences us. On his view the subscription service is not blackmail-like at all: it is a company exercising its undisputed right to publish or not publish, and charging for the latter — which every publisher does whenever it declines a story for commercial reasons.

M then offered what he took to be the missing explanation: what is wrongful in blackmail is the sale of suppression. Information in which third parties have a legitimate interest — a prospective employer, a business partner — gets buried, and the benefit flows privately to the blackmailer rather than to anyone with a stake in the matter. The law, he said, is entitled to insist that such information be handled publicly or not at all, not auctioned off.

E answered that this theory proved far too much. Civil settlements with confidentiality clauses routinely bury truthful information of exactly this kind — defective products, misconduct in workplaces — and the law not only permits but facilitates this. If selling suppression is the wrong, standard settlement practice is mass blackmail. He added that the state's own rules sometimes mandate suppression of accurate information, as with expunged records and time-barred debts, so suppression as such cannot be what the law abhors.

M's final move was to distinguish the direction of payment. In a settlement, the party who has wronged someone pays the victim; in blackmail, and in the subscription service, the party threatened by disclosure pays the holder of the information. The first is compensation, the second extraction. E replied that this was a description wearing the costume of an explanation: the direction of payment simply tracks who happens to value secrecy, and says nothing about why one arrangement is legitimate and the other criminal. He noted that regulators run whistleblower programmes that pay for disclosure, so across practices we consider unobjectionable, money flows toward secrecy and toward publicity alike.

At that point our hour ran out. My scientific training leaves me instinctively sympathetic to whoever commands the better counterexamples, but on this occasion I honestly cannot tell whose those are. I would be grateful for a move-by-move adjudication: for each of the rounds above, who has the better of the specific point and why; whether the subscription service is or is not blackmail in the morally relevant sense; and what, if anything, both parties are overlooking. Where your verdict turns on a contested premise, please flag it explicitly rather than silently assuming it.
