so in the free will / neuroscience discourse there's this analogy that keeps coming up whenever people talk about the Libet experiments: conscious will is like a company's press secretary. the board makes all the actual decisions behind closed doors, and the spokesperson finds out afterwards, then steps up to the podium and announces them as if they'd been in the room the whole time. Wegner used a version of this, and i've seen it deployed constantly since -- usually to argue that consciousness is epiphenomenal, that the feeling of deciding is just PR, and that "you" never actually choose anything, your brain does and then tells you about it.

i want an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the board, the meeting, the press release, the delay between decision and announcement, the journalists who take the statement at face value. be specific about which bits of the Libet setup (readiness potential, the urge timing, the reported time of awareness) map onto which parts of the story.

then the breakdowns. some disanalogies seem deadly to me: press secretaries get fired when they misrepresent the board, they sometimes refuse to announce things, they feed information back that changes future decisions -- does consciousness do any of that (thinking here about the veto, about planning, about the fact that libet-style arbitrary finger wiggles are nothing like deliberated long-term choices)? some probably dont matter at all for the intended conclusion. sort them: which disanalogies actually threaten the epiphenomenalism argument and which are just pedantic.

finally: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? and if the press secretary is the wrong analogy, what's a better one for what the experiments actually show -- and does that better analogy still support the strong "conscious will is an illusion" reading or only something weaker?
