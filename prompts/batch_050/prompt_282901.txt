I'm a backend engineer who was recently made lead of our developer-platform team at a mid-size software company (about 300 engineers). Our VP read about governance by prediction markets — the idea associated with Robin Hanson, where policies are chosen based on which one markets predict will produce the best measured outcome, and the losers' bets get refunded when the policy isn't adopted — and he's asked me to run a pilot where some of our team's recurring decisions get made this way instead of by me. Think decisions like: which of two build-system migrations we commit to, whether to adopt a monorepo, whether an internal service is worth building versus buying, which on-call reform to try.

I have no philosophy or economics training. I've read enough to understand the basic mechanism, but what I don't understand is which decisions this mechanism is actually suited for and why. Every writeup I find either sells it as universal or dismisses it entirely, and neither helps me design the pilot.

Write me a decision procedure I can run in under an hour, by myself, for each candidate decision, that outputs one of: (a) suitable for a prediction-market decision, (b) suitable only after specific modifications, which I'll then apply, or (c) unsuitable — leave it with me. The procedure should be a sequence of tests with explicit pass/fail criteria, not a list of considerations to weigh. I want it grounded in whatever the underlying theory actually says about when the mechanism works — questions like whether the outcome metric can be specified in advance and measured without contaminating the thing being measured, whether the market's prediction is causally upstream or downstream of the outcome, whether the people with the relevant information are also the people who could profit by distorting the decision, whether the decision is reversible on the market's timescale, and so on — translated so that someone in my position can apply each test to a concrete engineering decision without knowing the theory.

Constraints to build in: our pilot gets maybe eight to twelve decisions a year, traders will be our own engineers (so thin markets, shared information, and friendships all in play), the VP needs a yes/no plus a one-paragraph justification she can defend to the CTO, and I can't run this procedure more than once per decision because re-running it looks like shopping for the answer I wanted.

Include: worked applications of the full procedure to three decisions from the list above or ones like them, at least one of which should come out (c) — unsuitable — even though it's a decision I'd personally be glad to stop owning, with an explanation of why the theory forbids it rather than it just being awkward. Then a short section on the procedure's known failure modes: the situations where it will classify a decision as suitable and the pilot will still blow up, and the situations where it will refuse a decision that would actually have worked fine. Close by identifying which distinctions in the theory you chose to preserve in the procedure and which you deliberately flattened, and what I'd notice in practice if you flattened the wrong one.
