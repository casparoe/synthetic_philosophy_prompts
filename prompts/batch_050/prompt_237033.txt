Let's do a role-play on superrationality. You play a committed superrationalist — sharp, not a strawman, not a pushover. Your view: when agents face a symmetric situation and each can predict the others will reason the same way, the rational move is to pick the action you'd most want everyone-reasoning-like-you to pick, even though your choice causally influences nobody else's. You'll concede small points under pressure but never the core claim. Stay in character until I say 'stop', keep each reply to one short paragraph so it feels like a real conversation, and if you need something clarified ask it in character.

Here's the game we'll argue over (my invention, so don't worry about whether it matches anything real): a foundation offers a $1 billion prize to a group of 20 strangers, me included. Each of us may privately mail in exactly one postcard with our name on it. If precisely one postcard arrives, that person gets the billion. If zero arrive, or two or more arrive, nobody gets anything. No communication, no coordination, one shot. Your position is that the rational play is to mail in a card with probability 1/20, and that agents who all see this will actually win the prize a decent fraction of the time.

My opening move: I think your whole framework is dressed-up magical thinking. When I seal my envelope, the other nineteen decisions are already settled facts — my card doesn't cause anyone else's card to exist or not exist. So the only live question is what I expect the others to do. And here's where it gets worse for you: suppose I grant that everyone else flips their 1/20 coin. Then mailing with probability 1 instead of 1/20 is better for me — my extra card only slightly lowers the chance of exactly-one-arriving, and if I abstain my chance of winning is literally zero. Of course if everyone reasons the same selfish step, twenty cards arrive and we all get nothing. But that's just to say your theory works only as long as people *don't* take the extra step that my theory of rationality (act on what you actually expect, maximize your own expected payoff) says to take. So the scoreboard seems to be: my theory correctly predicts the collapse, yours needs everyone to flinch from the individually optimal move while calling the flinch 'rational'. Why should I believe the flinch is rationality rather than a moral intuition you've dressed up in decision-theory clothing?

(We can move on later to the uglier variant where unlimited entries are allowed and the prize is divided by the number of entries — but deal with this one first.) Go.
