For my high school philosophy club, we have been discussing Daniel Dennett’s distinction between the “Cartesian theater” and the “multiple drafts” model of consciousness. One member defends a modest Cartesian-theater view: not that there is a little observer or one anatomical screen in the brain, but that at each moment there is a single settled sequence of conscious experiences, so it is always determinate what the subject was experiencing before any later report or revision. Another member defends multiple drafts: the brain contains many partly independent processes that revise and compete with one another, and asking what was really experienced before a report is settled may have no further answer beyond the available discriminations, memories, and verbal judgments. We kept returning to this case: a person briefly hears an ambiguous sound, initially takes it to be one syllable, and then, after receiving a visual cue a fraction of a second later, confidently remembers having heard a different syllable from the beginning. The first student says either the original auditory experience or the later memory must misrepresent what consciousness actually contained; the second says the case shows why the demand for one pre-revision “actual content” is misguided.

Draft the joint statement these two students could sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. Use a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed claims should include points about neural processing, memory, verbal reports, temporal delay, and what the ambiguous-syllable case does and does not establish; they must be substantive rather than merely saying that consciousness is complicated. In the table, state each remaining disagreement neutrally, then give a formulation of each side that its defender could accept, the crux on which it turns, and a possible experimental result, thought experiment, or conceptual argument that both sides agree would bear on that crux. Record in advance what each side predicts or concludes in every proposed test. Do not let the multiple-drafts side caricature the theater view as requiring a homunculus, and do not let the theater side caricature multiple drafts as denying that people have experiences or can accurately report them. Commit to definite formulations instead of offering several optional versions, and identify explicitly which disagreements are empirical, which are conceptual, and which—if any—are merely verbal. End with a brief chair’s note taking a definite position on which single crux would do the most to move the debate and why, while making clear that this final judgment is not itself one of the jointly signed claims.
