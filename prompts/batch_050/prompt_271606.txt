I teach a seminar on the philosophy of probability, and every year the same fight breaks out when we reach the question of whether a finite sequence can be random. One faction of my students insists that randomness is an intrinsic, mathematical feature of a string of symbols itself: roughly, that a sequence is random to the extent that it is incompressible, fails every computable pattern-detection test, and has no description shorter than itself. The other faction insists with equal confidence that randomness attaches only to the process that produced a sequence, so that the string one hundred heads in a row is exactly as random as any other outcome of fair coin flips, while a string churned out by a deterministic pseudorandom number generator is not random at all no matter how lawless it looks. I would like you to draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration: a committed complexity theorist of randomness, in the tradition running from von Mises through Kolmogorov and Martin-Löf, and a committed proceduralist who takes randomness to be a causal-cum-epistemic property of generation methods rather than of outputs.

Structure the statement in three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can actually endorse, and I want these to be substantive rather than diplomatic: for instance, whatever each side makes of the fact that Kolmogorov complexity is defined only relative to a choice of universal machine and hence only up to an additive constant; the fact that the cleanest results in the area concern infinite sequences, with finite-sequence randomness at best a matter of degree; and the fact that in statistical practice the word random does double duty for properties of samples and properties of sampling procedures. Second, a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in terms its own side would accept as fair, with an explicit note wherever a disagreement turns out, on inspection, to be about words rather than the world. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp, and for each crux a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's verdict recorded in advance. The statement must commit both parties to classifications of at least these test cases: a run of one hundred heads produced by genuinely fair coin flips; a string of all zeros produced by a quantum device whose physics made each outcome equiprobable; and a string produced by a deterministic algorithm that passes every statistical randomness test anyone has so far devised. If the two camps give the same verdict on any of these, say so and say why it is not a concession; where they differ, state precisely what each would have to learn in order to change its classification.

Two further instructions, since I plan to use your draft as the seed document for the seminar's final session. Do not let the complexity side hide behind the infinity of the ideal theory: make it say what, if anything, the Martin-Löf-style results about infinite sequences entitle us to claim about a thousand-bit string on my desk. And do not let the procedural side hide behind intentions: make it say whether randomness of process is an empirical hypothesis about the world, an epistemic judgment about our information, or a blend, and how it handles the uncomfortable consequence that whether a given string is random could then depend on facts about its causal history that are in principle undiscoverable from the string itself. Where the collaboration reaches bedrock, mark it as such rather than manufacturing agreement.
