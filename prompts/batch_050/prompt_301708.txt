For the final project in my high school's comparative philosophy elective, each of us has to respond creatively to one of the texts we studied this term, and I've chosen the butterfly dream from the end of chapter two of the Zhuangzi. Rather than write a reflection, I want to submit a poem, because I think the form of poetry can do something the passage itself does. Specifically, I'd like you to write me a villanelle about the dream, one where the two refrains do real philosophical work: the first refrain should give the perspective in which Zhuang Zhou is a man who dreamt he was a butterfly, and the second should give the inverted perspective in which the butterfly is now dreaming that it is a man. As the refrains alternate from stanza to stanza, the poem should enact the way the question of which state is real keeps reversing itself and can never be settled from inside either one, and when the two refrains finally stand side by side in the closing quatrain, their meeting should gesture at what the passage calls the transformation of things -- the idea that there genuinely is a distinction between man and butterfly, yet each passes over into the other without a fixed boundary.

One thing matters a lot to me, because it's the mistake I almost made in my own first reading: the poem should not turn this into Cartesian-style dream skepticism. Zhuangzi is not anxious that he might be deceived about reality, and the point is not that life is an illusion or that we can never know anything. The butterfly in the passage is carefree, flitting about, perfectly content to be itself, and I want some of that lightness in the poem's tone -- wonder rather than dread. It would also be easy to fall back on the cliche that life is but a dream, which flattens the passage into something it isn't saying; the interesting claim is that the boundary between waking and dreaming, self and other, is itself unstable, not that one side of the boundary wins.

I'd like the result to be accurate enough that someone who knows the Qiwulun chapter would recognize the passage in the poem and find it faithful, while someone who has never read Zhuangzi would still find it a good, self-contained villanelle. Please keep to the standard villanelle structure -- nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with the refrains and rhyme scheme handled properly. If any line has to choose between the rhyme and the philosophy, choose the philosophy and rework the line.
