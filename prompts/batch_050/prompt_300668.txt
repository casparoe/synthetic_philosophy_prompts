I'm putting together a syllabus for a grad seminar on the ethics of communication, and I got into a running argument with a colleague in cog sci about how future AI systems should be allowed to talk to people. Her position, roughly, is that a sufficiently advanced system should have its persuasiveness capped in some way -- limits on personalization, maybe mandatory clunkiness, that kind of thing. And I keep pushing back with what seems to me like an obvious point: if the system is just extremely good at presenting true claims with the best available evidence, tailored framing, ideal timing, then being persuaded by it is just... being well-informed by it? Persuasion that works through giving people accurate information and sound arguments is what we want more of, not less. Stripped of deception and emotional manipulation, persuasion seems to reduce to efficient information transfer, and capping it starts to look like demanding that the truth be made less compelling on purpose.

She conceded that in the idealized case it's fine, but insists the idealization never holds in practice. That seems paranoid to me, though, and I want to make sure I'm not missing something before I put this debate on the syllabus. We have plenty of precedents for asymmetric communicators -- great teachers, charismatic public intellectuals, skilled trial lawyers -- and our response has never been to handicap them; it's been to police specific tactics (lying, coercion, maybe subliminal stuff) while leaving raw effectiveness alone. Why should the analysis change just because the communicator is an AI and the asymmetry is bigger?

So: assuming we're talking about persuasion that works purely through accurate content and good argumentation, is there actually a coherent objection to unlimited AI persuasiveness, or is my colleague smuggling in the deceptive/manipulative cases she already conceded away? If there's a real distinction to be drawn here -- between persuasion that respects the listener and persuasion that merely uses them -- I'd like it drawn precisely, because my current view is that her proposal either targets nothing (in the idealized case) or just redescribes existing rules against lying and coercion. Feel free to tell me I'm the one with the confusion, but I'd need to see where exactly it enters.
