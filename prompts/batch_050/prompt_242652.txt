My epistemology reading group has two people who've been circling the Meno problem all semester and keep talking past each other. One holds the swamping view: once you've got true belief, anything you add -- justification, reliability, being non-lucky -- adds no extra value, so knowledge can't be worth more than mere true belief. The other thinks knowledge has a distinctive value that survives this argument. Their fight always comes back to the bit near the end of Plato's Meno where Socrates says true opinions are like the statues of Daedalus: fine as long as they stay put, but they tend to run off, so they're not worth much until you tie them down by working out the reason why -- and that tethering, he says, is what makes knowledge more valuable than true opinion. (That's my paraphrase of the Jowett translation, so don't treat it as exact wording.)

Write the document they'd produce if they ran an actual adversarial collaboration instead of bickering: (1) the claims both can sign about what Socrates' tethering argument does and doesn't establish (e.g., does it show knowledge is more valuable, or only more stable, and does stability even count as extra value?); (2) the exact remaining disagreements, each worded so both sides accept the formulation of their own position; (3) the cruxes -- I suspect one is whether the value at stake has to be practical/instrumental for the swamping argument to bite, and another is whether credit for getting things right is a genuine kind of value or just bookkeeping -- and for each crux, a case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If part of their dispute turns out to be verbal, flag which part and why.

American English please, and start directly with the joint statement itself -- no preface, no restating my question.
