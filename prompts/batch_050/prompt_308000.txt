The assignment was on Keynes's 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren. The question I set was roughly this: Keynes predicts that within about a century the standard of life in "progressive countries" could be something like four to eight times higher, and that what he calls the economic problem — humanity's age-old struggle for subsistence — might actually be solved, leaving people with drastically shorter working weeks and the brand-new problem of what to do with all their leisure. He also says some fairly rude things about the love of money and about people who live for distant future ends instead of the present. So: explain what Keynes means by the economic problem and why he thinks it can be solved, and then explain what he thinks life will (and should) look like once it is — including anything he thinks we'll find hard about that.

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Keynes argues that the economic problem — by which he means the recurring cycle of slumps, unemployment, and scarcity that plagued capitalism in his day — can be solved through continued capital accumulation and technical progress rather than through any political rupture. Writing at the onset of the Depression, he treats the current crisis as a painful but temporary adjustment, not evidence that growth is finished, and predicts that compound growth will multiply living standards several times over within a hundred years. Once the problem is solved, work will no longer be necessary, and Keynes welcomes this as straightforward liberation: humanity will be free of the drudgery of toil and can enjoy a society of pure leisure. The good life, on his view, consists in freedom from work and from caring about money. He regards the love of money as a pathological trait that future psychiatrists will treat, and he rejects concern for the future altogether — living for distant ends is a kind of sickness, so rational people will simply live in the moment once scarcity is gone."
