I co-run an interdisciplinary reading group at my university — twelve participants, a mix of philosophy and computer science graduate students, plus two advanced undergraduates — meeting for a single 100-minute session. Our term theme is whether large language models are best conceived as agents with beliefs and goals, as simulators that merely enact characters, or as something for which neither category is adequate. Everyone has read a bit on this and most have firm intuitions from chatting with these systems, which is exactly the problem: I want an activity that destabilizes those intuitions experientially before we argue about them.

Design a role-play game, requiring no actual computers, in which one participant plays "the engine": a responder bound by strict rules to continue any conversation as whatever persona card they are currently holding, switching instantly and without comment when the facilitator hands them a new card. The other participants interact with the engine as interlocutors, and — this is the part I care most about — they must make real, scored commitments about the interlocutor they think they're talking to: staking tokens on predictions about whether it will keep a promise made three turns ago, whether it still holds a belief it asserted earlier, whether it will pursue a goal it stated across a topic change. The rules should be arranged so that persona swaps happen mid-commitment, so that players feel the specific cost of having trusted an agent that, in some sense, was never there — and then, in a second phase, the engine receives a hidden standing instruction that persists across all personas (steering every conversation toward some fixed end), so the group must confront whether the agency they were looking for was simply located at the wrong level.

The prompt for you: give me a complete, runnable design. Include the engine's exact rules (including how to handle interlocutor questions about the engine's own nature), at least four sample persona cards and two sample standing instructions, a token-staking and scoring scheme that makes misplaced attributions hurt a little, a timeline with checkpoints where the group must publicly commit to attributions before learning they were wrong, and facilitation guidance for predictable failure modes (an engine player who can't let go of a persona, interlocutors who collude to expose the mechanic early, swaps that are too conspicuous or too subtle). Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto the positions in the literature — persona-level agency, the simulator picture, system-level or base-model agency — showing where each attribution broke down in play, without lecturing or naming any of these views beforehand. Finally, be frank about the deepest disanalogy: my human engine genuinely understands the conversation and has real intentions it is suppressing, so the game can demonstrate the fragility of persona-level attribution but cannot by itself show that the underlying system lacks a point of view. Tell me how to surface that gap honestly in discussion rather than letting participants think the activity settled it.
