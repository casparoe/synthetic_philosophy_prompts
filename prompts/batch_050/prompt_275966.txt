Two researchers designing a household robot disagree about what its inverse-reinforcement-learning system can infer. The robot observes a parent repeatedly giving up leisure time to care for a child. Mara says the robot can infer that the parent values the child’s welfare more than some leisure. Colin objects that the behavior could instead reflect habit, social pressure, a mistaken belief about the child’s needs, or a rule the parent follows without endorsing it.

Mara proposes budget probes: vary the costs of caring, leisure, and paying for assistance, then observe which trade-offs remain stable. Colin replies that such choices reveal only a context-sensitive decision policy, not an underlying preference; exhaustion or framing may change the choices without changing what the parent values. Mara answers that stable responses across several controlled interventions deserve more weight than momentary verbal reports. Colin says reports are also evidence, especially when the parent can explain conflicting desires and anticipated regret.

Finally, Mara argues that when several reward functions fit the observations equally well, the robot should choose the simplest one that generalizes across interventions. Colin calls that an external modeling bias rather than something inferred from behavior, warning that the simplest stable rule may merely be the one easiest for the robot to represent.

Adjudicate the exchange point by point. Who is right about revealed preference, the epistemic role of verbal reports, and simplicity as a tiebreaker? What distinction between preferences, values, norms, and decision policies would most clarify the disagreement, and what should the robot remain uncertain about?
