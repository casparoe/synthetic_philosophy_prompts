I help run a small film discussion group, and next week we're screening Christopher Nolan's Memento. I want to frame the evening around a distinction I've been turning over on my own: the difference between knowing how to do something and knowing that something is the case. Leonard has lost the ability to form new factual memories but keeps his skills — he can drive, handle a gun, work a routine — so the film seems to dramatize a clean split between the two kinds of knowing, with one destroyed and the other intact. But I'm not sure the film endorses that split. It strikes me that Leonard's habits are exactly what the people around him exploit, because a practiced skill can't be corrected by a new fact the way a belief can; his knowing-how runs on rails regardless of what the world is like. Meanwhile the tattoos and notes raise the reverse worry: declarative information can be manufactured, planted, and manipulated, so that what gets stored as fact was never really known by anyone. Read the film through this distinction. Where does it treat knowing-how as more trustworthy than knowing-that, and where does it complicate or resist that preference? In particular, what does the Sammy Jankis storyline — his wife's repeated insulin test, and the question of whether his compliance was conditioned habit or hidden recognition — show about whether reliable performance, by itself, amounts to knowledge? Finally, if you think the film takes a position on which kind of knowing is more fundamental or more dependable, say what that position is, and whether a philosopher of mind would find it defensible or whether the film oversimplifies.
