I'm co-teaching a second-year seminar on philosophy of fiction next term, and my segment is imaginative resistance — the phenomenon where readers balk at imagining something a fiction explicitly invites them to imagine, especially when the invitation concerns moral or evaluative matters (a story that simply treats the systematic cruelty of a nursery-rhyme world as admirable, say, rather than a world with extra moons). My students will have read no philosophy of mind and only a little metaethics, and I have one ninety-minute session. What I want is not the explanation itself but a plan for delivering it: I keep finding that students either collapse the problem into ordinary moral disagreement with the narrator, or inflate it into a general skepticism about whether fiction can teach us anything, and I want a sequence that threads between those two failures. I'd like guidance on the order in which the components must be introduced and why (for instance, whether the puzzle should be set up before or after the contrast with straightforward factual impossibilities in fiction), the one carrying case I should build the session around and what features it needs to have and to avoid, and where in the sequence the predictable misconceptions should be headed off rather than corrected afterward. I'd also like the plan to note what I should deliberately leave for later sessions or openly bracket, and a check I can run at the end to see whether the distinction has actually landed rather than been parroted.

Please give the plan as exactly eight numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
