Some backround: I spent 35 years as an experimental chemist (polymers, mostly), retired a while back, and last year I joined a philosophy reading group at our public library. This month we're doing puzzles about infinity and rational choice, and I've gotten into a running argument with Carl, a retired electrical engineer in the group who plays serious poker and has very firm opinions about which bets are worth taking. Before our next meeting I want to predict what he'll say about a new game, and figure out what rule he's actually running.

His calls so far, from the last few sessions:

1. The classic game where you flip a coin until it lands heads and win 2^n dollars if it takes n flips. Carl says the textbook answer -- that it's worth any price since the expected value is infinite -- is baloney. He wouldn't pay $25 for it. He said he might toss in $5 "for the entertainment," but that the infinite expectation is broken math, since you basically always walk away with two or four bucks.

2. Same game, but capped: if you somehow get past 20 flips you just collect 2^20 (about a million) and stop, so the expectation is an ordinary number, around $21. Offered the game at $8, he took it instantly, no hesitation.

3. A game going the other direction: probability 3/4 you lose $2, probability 3/16 you lose $4, and so on -- probability 3/4^n of losing 2^n dollars, so the losses are unbounded but the expected loss works out to exactly $3. Somebody offered him $10 free and clear if he'd take this game on the side, and he took it. His words: "The monster losses don't scare me, they're make-believe. The average is what it is."

4. A symmetric one: flip until heads, and if it took n flips, a second coin flip decides whether you win 2^n dollars or lose 2^n dollars. He refused to play even for free -- said a game whose books never settle isn't a game at all.

Now the new game I want to spring on him: flip a coin until it lands heads, say it takes n flips. If n is odd, you win 2^n/n dollars; if n is even, you lose 2^n/n dollars. So half the time you win $2 right away, a quarter of the time you lose $2, an eighth of the time you win about $2.67, a sixteenth of the time you lose $4, and so on forever. Playing with pencil and paper, the term-by-term expectation comes out to 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which I'm told sums to about 69 cents -- but the win terms and loss terms each blow up to infinity on their own, which apparently means you can reorder the sum to make it come out to whatever you want. That last part makes my head hurt, if I'm honest.

The game costs 50 cents to play, once. Does Carl pay?

What I'd like from you: your best guess on Carl's call and your reasons; the rule that best explains his four verdicts, plus at least one or two rival rules that fit those same four verdicts just as well; what each candidate rule predicts about the new game; and, if the top rules disagree, one more bet I could offer him at the next meeting that would tell them apart.

One more thing: when you get into how philosophers or decision theorists handle games like this, please seperate what's genuinely settled -- I gather the math about rearranging the sum is not in dispute -- from what's still up for grabs among people who actually work on this stuff. Don't present one camp's position as the consensus if it isn't.

There's a beer riding on this, so don't let me down.
