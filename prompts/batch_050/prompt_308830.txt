So background: I drive a delivery truck and listen to a ton of podcasts, and this year I fell hard down the rabbit hole of how future AI systems with weird goals are supposed to share a world with each other and with us. Not an academic, so forgive the clumsy wording.

One idea I can't stop chewing on came from a blog post I now can't find again, about "safe Pareto improvements." As I understood it: when parties with totally different values have to cooperate, you restrict yourself to moves that make things better by everyone's values at once, so nobody ever has to win the argument about whose values are correct — you just harvest the wins everyone agrees on. And the "safe" part (I think) was that the move also has to look acceptable under every halfway-plausible moral theory, so you don't do something monstrous by one theory's lights while helping by another's.

The blog treated this like the great hope: a future full of strange minds doesn't need a solved moral philosophy, it just needs free wins. My brother-in-law (accountant, very confident) says it's hopeless, because once you demand that literally everyone and every plausible theory signs off, the set of approved moves shrinks to basically nothing — like planning a dinner for ten people with ten different food rules.

Here's what I actually want from you: your credence in a specific claim, and the reasoning decomposed. The claim: in a world where AI systems (and humans) with genuinely different goals have to divide resources and make deals, a rule of "safe Pareto improvements only" would capture MOST of the morally important value on the table. Say: whatever the best imaginable negotiator could achieve, the restricted version achieves at least 80% of it. Give me a number.

Things I suspect the number depends on, but correct me or add your own:

- how often agents with different terminal goals still have overlapping instrumental wants (staying around, keeping options open, predictability, not getting lied to)
- whether the every-plausible-theory-must-sign-off condition shrinks the safe set to a rounding error, or whether decent theories mostly agree more than they fight
- whether deals like this survive the commitment problem — if you can't verify the other side will hold up their end, the pareto frontier might be unreachable in practice even where it exists
- whether "improvement" is so theory-laden that the whole concept is mush and my question doesn't even have a truth value

Rough probability for each, one line of justification, then how they combine into your final number, and which term the answer is most sensitive to. Also: I'm sitting at maybe 0.5, my brother-in-law is at like 0.05. Whose side do you land closer to, and which sub-question do you think our disagreement is actually hiding in?
