So I'm a novelist — mostly literary fiction, one midlist book out, second one stalling — and I keep hitting the same piece of advice in totally different mouths. My mentor said it about my antagonist: "everyone is the hero of their own story," meaning the guy reads flat because he doesn't seem to believe he's right. Fine, that genuinely helped the draft. But then my sister said the exact same sentence about our dad, who rewrites every family argument so he's the wronged party, and she meant it as an explanation of why apologizing to him is pointless. And last month a friend who's a therapist-ish person used it to mean something like: be patient with people, they're all stuck inside their own perspective. Same words, three different... somethings. Claims? Observations? Excuses?

Here's what I want: lay out the different precise things that sentence could be asserting. Is it a claim about how people experience themselves (everyone narrates from the inside, first-person, motivated reasoning doing the lighting and camera work)? A claim about moral self-justification (nobody does what they take to be simply wrong; everyone has a story where they're at least decent)? A metaphysical claim that selves just ARE stories, that there's no person underneath the narration? A claim about self-knowledge (you're the final authority on your own story)? Or is it just craft advice about characterization wearing a trench coat? Because I suspect some of those readings are near-platitudes, some are interesting but probably false — I can think of people who seem to experience themselves as the villain, or as a minor character in someone elses story, or as no kind of character at all — and at least one is doing something sneakier, like laundering an excuse into an explanation.

For each reading, tell me: is it plausible, and does it actually justify the use it was being put to in the conversation where I heard it? Specifically — my mentor's version: does "write your villain as if he thinks he's right" actually follow from any true reading of the slogan, or is it just good advice dressed up as a fact about persons? My sister's version: does any plausible reading support "he can't help it, that's just how selves work," or is she smuggling in a fatalism the slogan doesn't contain? And my dad, if he ever read this, would say the sentence proves his grievances are as valid as anyone's, since he's the authority on his own story — which reading is he leaning on, and is it the worst one on the menu?

One more wrinkle, since this is my job: if some reading of the slogan is true, does it constrain fiction? Like, if selves really are narratively constructed all the way down, are certain ways of writing consciousness on the page just phenomenologically wrong — stream of consciousness as false advertising — or is that a category error? I honestly can't tell whether this sentence is a deep fact about personhood or a bumper sticker my whole profession has agreed to treat as scripture, and I'd like to know before I put it in a character's mouth.
