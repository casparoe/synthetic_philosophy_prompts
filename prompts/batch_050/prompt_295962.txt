I serve on the ethics advisory committee of a regional hospital, and at our last meeting two members -- Daniel, a health economist, and Marta, an intensivist -- had an extended argument about revising our crisis standards of care, specifically how to allocate ICU beds and ventilators when demand exceeds supply. Daniel argued that triage should maximize expected quality-adjusted life-years saved: among patients with comparable short-term survival prospects, priority should go to those with more expected years of good life ahead, since a bed that buys a forty-year-old forty more years does more good than one that buys an eighty-year-old five. Lotteries or first-come-first-served, he said, treat equality of *chance* as if it were equality of *outcome*, and knowingly waste lives that could have been saved. Marta replied that QALY-based triage systematically devalues the elderly and people with disabilities, and that once survival probability is accounted for, every patient has an equal claim on the bed; a lottery is not a failure to rank people but a refusal to rank their lives. Daniel then invoked the fair innings argument: prioritizing the young does not value their lives more highly, it merely recognizes that the old have already received what the young have not yet had, so everyone is treated equally when considered over a complete life. Marta answered that this concedes her point -- fair innings is a distributive principle, not an efficiency one, so Daniel had quietly abandoned pure maximization -- and that in any case the framework still discounts the futures of disabled patients based on quality-of-life judgments that those patients themselves typically reject.

The argument then took a turn I found harder to follow. Marta brought up the rule of rescue: societies routinely spend enormous sums to save identifiable people in immediate peril -- trapped miners, sailors lost at sea -- even when a QALY calculation would say the money saves far more life-years elsewhere, and she insisted this is not mere sentimentality but an expression of the commitment that no member of society is simply written off. Daniel responded that this is just the identifiable victim effect, a well-documented cognitive bias: statistical lives are real lives, and rational policy must be made from a standpoint where all lives are statistical, which is precisely what a triage protocol does. Marta's rebuttal was that this standpoint cuts against him -- people deliberating about what policies to endorse, not knowing which patient they will turn out to be, might reasonably fear being abandoned in a crisis more than they value marginal gains in efficiency, and so might choose rescue norms and equal-chance rules over QALY maximization. Daniel's final move was to note that safety regulators already use the value of a statistical life, a figure that does not decline with age the way remaining QALYs do, so even economists do not consistently maximize life-years -- suggesting, he said, that each tool fits its domain, and QALYs fit triage. Marta called that ad hoc, saying either the young and old count equally or they do not, and one cannot switch principles depending on which answer one wants.

The committee is supposed to issue a recommendation, and I genuinely cannot adjudicate this. Please go through the exchange step by step and say who is right about each specific point: whether QALY-maximizing triage counts as discrimination; whether the fair innings argument really does abandon pure efficiency, and whether that is a concession or a clarification; whether the rule of rescue expresses a defensible principle or merely a bias; whether the veil-of-ignorance reasoning supports Marta as she claims or Daniel as he claims; and whether Daniel's appeal to the value of a statistical life is a legitimate division of labor between tools or an inconsistency. If both sides are missing something -- some consideration neither raised that would break the deadlock -- say what it is, and give an overall verdict on whose position is closer to correct.
