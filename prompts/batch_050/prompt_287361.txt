You are a philosopher of technology who also knows the AI safety literature well. Think carefully about this one, and maybe spend a paragraph brainstorming candidate answers before you settle on the strongest reconstruction -- I'd rather you consider a few readings and rank them than jump straight to one.

Here's the practice I want you to analyze. Frontier AI labs (and the governance frameworks around them, like responsible scaling policies) mostly handle catastrophic-risk assessment the same way: before deploying a model, they run a battery of behavioral evaluations -- controlled settings designed to catch the model scheming, sandbagging, seeking power, trying to exfiltrate its weights, manipulating its overseers, etc. If the model fails the tests, you don't deploy; if it passes, you deploy, maybe with mitigations. The whole apparatus is built around discrete probes with pass/fail thresholds.

What does that practice presuppose about what AI failure actually looks like? As best I can tell it assumes failure is *behaviorally manifest* (you can catch it in a sandbox), *static* (the model you tested is the model you deployed), and *threshold-crossing* (there's a line between safe and dangerous, and you can find it). All three of those fit the classic picture of an influence-seeking system defecting -- scheming, reward hacking, a treacherous turn.

But there's a rival picture floating around where the failure mode is slow value drift: no single detectable defection, just a system (or a society increasingly routed through systems) gradually sliding -- small shifts in what's optimized, what's reported, what humans stop checking -- until the world is somewhere nobody chose and nobody can point to the moment it went wrong. On that picture, pass/fail evals are arguably testing for the wrong thing entirely, like screening for burglars while the house slowly floods.

So: (1) Reconstruct the implicit theory of failure the evals regime presupposes, and say which parts are strictly presupposed vs. just fit well. (2) Could someone fully accept the slow-drift picture and still think the current practice is basically right, or does the practice really rule it out? (3) Is the practice internally consistent -- e.g., does the "we test the model before deployment" step sit awkwardly with the same labs' acknowledgment that models learn from post-deployment feedback and fine-tuning? (4) If you rebuilt the assessment regime around slow value drift instead of acute scheming, what would concretely have to change -- what would you even measure, given that drift might live in the sociotechnical system rather than the model?

Be honest about where the practice is underdetermined between the two pictures. I'd rather have a sharp "this part is genuinely ambiguous" than forced certainty.
