I am a novelist, and for the past four years I have also served as one of three jurors for a mid-sized national fiction prize: one winner a year, a shortlist of six, roughly forty submitted novels for the three of us to read between September and January, unpaid, with the jury's decision final and the foundation's board merely administering it. Our charter says, in its entirety on the matter, that the prize is awarded to "the best novel." Almost every year, at least one candidate raises the difficulty I want help with, and it is never plagiarism or fabrication — our rules already cover those. It is the other kind of trouble: something about the author, or about what the book itself seems to endorse, that makes us uneasy about putting the foundation's money and name behind it.

For years my settled answer to scruples of this kind has been the preface Oscar Wilde wrote for the 1891 edition of The Picture of Dorian Gray, and one aphorism from it in particular:

"The moral life of man forms part of the subject-matter of the artist, but the morality of art consists in the perfect use of an imperfect medium."

I have quoted this often and only recently admitted to myself that I do not know what work it can actually do in the judging room. It seems to grant the moral life a place in fiction as material while relocating the morality of art to the handling of the medium — but our hardest cases are not about handling, and the aphorism appears to say nothing at all about what the artist has done away from the desk, unless "subject-matter" is meant to stretch further than I have been assuming. I want you to take the aphorism seriously as a piece of theory and tell me how much of my job it can bear.

Concretely, I want a procedure the three of us can run — a fixed sequence of stages, each stage answering one question — that converts whatever the philosophy of art genuinely knows about the relation between a work's moral character and its artistic value into something usable by three working writers across two afternoons of meetings. Please observe the following constraints:

1. Keep three things apart from one another and from the final verdict: the moral character of the book (what it depicts, what it endorses, whether those can even be separated), the foreseeable effects of honoring it, and the facts of the author's conduct. A failure at one stage must not be allowed to contaminate another without our noticing that it has happened.
2. For each stage, say what counts as an answer and what evidence we may admit. We can establish convictions, court findings, and an author's own admissions; we cannot investigate allegations, and I want the procedure to say explicitly what we do when the most serious thing on the table is an allegation we cannot adjudicate. Mark every point where a stage ends not in an answer but in a judgment call, rather than letting the procedure launder our instincts into findings.
3. Tell me which distinctions must survive the translation into practice — I suspect the distinction between depicting cruelty and endorsing it is one, and the distinction between judging a book and rewarding a person is another — and which can be dropped without loss by people who are not philosophers.
4. Use Wilde's aphorism to settle whichever stage it genuinely settles, and state plainly where it runs out. If a jury that adopted it as its entire policy would be making a mistake, name the mistake. I would rather be told the aphorism is a writer's credo with no jurisdictional authority over juries than have it stretched to cover my whole problem.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases, all drawn from our recent history with details changed:

(a) By general agreement, including mine, the strongest book on the shortlist is a quiet domestic novel by an author who, after shortlisting, was convicted of a serious violent offense unrelated to anything in the book.
(b) A beautifully written novel narrated from the perspective of a man grooming a teenager, which never once signals disapproval; the author's own life is unimpeachable. I find this book morally worse than (a), and I want the procedure to tell me whether that finding of mine is even relevant, and at which stage.
(c) A longlisted writer died between submission and judging; a week later her private letters were published, showing her to have been a vicious and energetic bigot. The award money would go to her estate, and she could never be embarrassed by a refusal.

I expect the procedure to return at least one verdict I will not like. Identify which case you believe that is and do not soften it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — which decisions will remain irreducibly ours, in the room, no matter how well the stages are designed. The version that gets pinned to the wall of the judging room should fit on one page; the reasoning behind it can take as long as it needs.
