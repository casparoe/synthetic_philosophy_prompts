So last night I got into a genuinely three-hour argument with my friend Tomas about moral responsibility, kicked off by a mutual acquaintance who lied on a grant application and got caught, and whether he's really *to blame* or just a product of a pressure-cooker lab culture plus being the kind of guy who cuts corners. Tomas's whole position basically reduced to one line he kept repeating like a drumbeat: "he could have done otherwise. nobody held a gun to his head." At some point I pulled up Galen Strawson's book Freedom and Belief (1986) on my phone and read him this bit from chapter 1:

"Perhaps the best answer to this question at this juncture is the one that draws the present chain of definition — of freedom in terms of true responsibility and of true responsibility in terms of desert — into a firmly closed circle: given that an agent is a moral agent, it is capable of being truly deserving of praise and blame for its choices and actions when and only when it is capable of free choice and free action. Freedom is now defined in terms of true responsibility, true responsibility in terms of desert, and desert in terms of freedom. Circles like this are usually frowned upon; but this one seems to be just what is needed, at this early stage. The terms 'desert', 'responsibility', and 'freedom' just are related in this way given the ordinary, strong sense of the word 'freedom'."

Tomas's response was that this proves his point: Strawson has just rigged the game by defining freedom, responsibility, and desert in terms of each other, so of course ordinary blame comes out impossible on his picture, and meanwhile normal people like Tomas use "free" and "responsible" in the ordinary way, where the guy just could have done otherwise. We went in circles (ha) and I couldn't articulate what was wrong with either his comeback or his slogan.

So two asks. First: take "he could have done otherwise, nobody held a gun to his head" and unpack the different precise claims it could actually be making. I can gesture at candidates — like a conditional thing about what he'd have done if he'd chosen differently, or a categorical thing about the future being genuinely open at the moment of action, or something about him being the real source of the act rather than it tracing back to his upbringing — but I want them laid out as a menu: for each reading, say what exactly it claims, whether it's a platitude, defensible-but-substantive, or just false, and crucially whether it's a reading that survives Strawson's basic regress (you act as you do because of how you are, and to be responsible for how you act you'd have to be responsible for how you are, and so on). And tell me which reading Tomas actually needs for blame to land, because I suspect he's sliding between a weak reading that's obviously true and a strong one that does the blaming work.

Second: is Tomas right that Strawson's passage is just circular reasoning, or is there a legitimate difference between defining terms in a circle and *rigging* a conclusion? What work is the passage actually doing, and does it concede something to Tomas that I should have noticed? I want to go back to him with this sorted, so be concrete about who owes who a concession.
