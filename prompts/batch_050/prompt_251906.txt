Two Thursdays ago I spent election night the way I always do, squeezed into a corner of a newsroom watching the pollsters and forecasters I cover argue about misses, weights, and likely-voter screens, and somewhere around three in the morning a thought I'd been circling for weeks finally crystallized: the philosophical dispute between epistocracy and democracy, which I'd been reading up on for a possible feature, sounds from inside that newsroom like a dispute about estimator design. I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, not a philosopher; I'm fluent in the vocabulary of survey methodology and statistical estimation, while my philosophy is that of a careful generalist. What I'd like is for you to translate the debate into my working vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then to tell me what didn't survive the translation.

Concretely: I want the democrat's best case restated as a claim about aggregation — the electorate as a very large collection of noisy instruments, each vote a fallible measurement, with something like Condorcet's jury theorem or the wisdom-of-crowds literature as the formal backbone showing under what conditions the unweighted average outperforms its own members. I want those conditions made explicit as claims about a data-generating process: independence of errors (which everyone on my side of the room immediately doubts, since voters drink from shared media ecosystems), individual competence better than chance (together with the theorem's unsettling mirror image, that competence below chance makes the majority converge on the wrong answer with the same serenity), and a well-posed question with a correct answer to converge on. Then I want the epistocrat's counterproposal — in the spirit of contemporary defenders such as Jason Brennan, though you needn't reconstruct any particular text — restated as a weighting scheme: precision-weight the reliable instruments, screen out the uninformative ones the way my pollster friends screen for likely voters. And I want the standard objections translated as well: the worry that the knowledgeable are demographically unrepresentative looks to me exactly like coverage bias introduced by restricting the sampling frame, and the question of who gets to certify the competence test looks like the problem of estimating your instruments' precisions without any outside standard.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually commissioned the translation for. Which moves in the debate survive intact, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in the statistical vocabulary at all? My own suspicion — please test it rather than confirm it — is that the biggest casualty is that estimation presupposes an estimand, some quantity the measurements are measurements of, whereas much of what elections decide arguably has no fact of the matter to be estimated, since what an election produces is not accuracy but authorization. A second candidate for the residue is political equality: I cannot see how to express one-person-one-vote as anything other than a stipulation that all instruments count equally, which a statistician would treat as an assumption to be justified rather than a principle to be honored. If I'm right about either of these, does that indict the statistical vocabulary as the wrong instrument for this debate, or does it indict parts of the philosophical debate as trading on notions less substantive than they appear? I eventually have to write this up for readers who currently trust neither pollsters nor philosophers, so I'd like to know which framework, if either, is doing the real work.
