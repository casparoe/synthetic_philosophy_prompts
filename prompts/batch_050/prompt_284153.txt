A friend of mine mediates partnership disputes for a living -- mostly two people who ran a business together, fell out, and need to split whatever's left. He has no training in economics or game theory, he just goes by what seems fair, and honestly his instincts wierdly consistent. I've watched three of his rulings:

1. Two partners, $100k left in the pot, any split is legally fine. He ruled 50/50.

2. Same setup, $100k pot, any split fine, but one of the partners (call her B) had an outside donor who had pledged to match whatever she walked away with, dollar for dollar. So a dollar from the pot was worth two dollars to her. He still ruled $50k each from the pot -- meaning B effectively got twice as much value as A, and he knew that.

3. Same pot, but this time each partner had a guaranteed fallback: if no deal was reached, a prior contract meant each would still collect $25k. He ruled 50/50 again.

Now he's got a new case: $100k pot, any split allowed in principle, except that A has creditors with a claim that claws back anything above $60k in A's hands -- so the most A can actually end up with is $60k, no matter what. B has no such limit.

I want to predict his ruling before he makes it. As I see it, two of his past rulings are compatible with at least a couple of different fairness rules: you could read case 2 as him splitting the actual cash evenly and treating B's matching pledge as her own business, or you could read it as something cleverer. So: (a) what are the plausible rules he might be running, stated precisely enough that they could disagree with each other, (b) what does each one predict for the new case, (c) which prediction is your best bet and why, and (d) if the top two candidate rules can't be told apart by his track record, what future case would settle which one he's actually following? If any of the rules you consider happen to be known named solutions in bargaining theory, tell me which ones they are.
