Below is a draft of a short essay I'm working on for a blog. I want comments, but of specific kinds: (1) am I using Dennett's tower of creatures accurately, or have I distorted one of the floors? (2) is the "tool debt" idea in the middle actually doing work, or is it just a redescription of bad learning that a Skinnerian creature could also suffer from? (3) the strong claim at the end feels too strong -- what's the most defensible version of it? Don't rewrite the essay for me, just comment.

Draft:

Dennett sorts minds into four kinds of creatures, stacked like floors of a tower. Darwinian creatures get all their behaviors fixed by natural selection and that's it. Skinnerian creatures can also learn from reward and punishment within their own lifetime. Popperian creatures can simulate candidate actions in an inner environment, so that their hypotheses can die instead of them. Gregorian creatures can import mind-tools from their culture -- words, concepts, methods -- which lets each generation start roughly where the last one left off instead of from scratch.

Here's my claim: the Gregorian floor introduces a failure mode that the lower floors structurally cannot have. A Skinnerian creature can only retain behaviors that actually paid off for it, and a Popperian creature can only act on hypotheses its inner simulator can actually run. But a Gregorian creature can download a tool -- a concept, a framework, a slogan -- without posessing any of the simulational machinery needed to check it, rebuild it, or notice when it's being misapplied. Call this gap tool debt: the distance between the mind-tools a creature can wield and the ones it could reconstruct on its own if the cultural scaffolding were suddenly removed.

My stronger claim, which the essay builds toward: most recognizable intellectual dysfunction -- people deploying concepts they can't cash out, fields running on vocabulary nobody can operationalize, smart people confidently wrong in identical ways -- is best understood as tool debt coming due, rather than as ordinary error or ordinary bias.
