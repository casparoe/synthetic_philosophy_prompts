I'm TAing a freshman seminar on evil and responsibility, and for the last unit I'm pairing Schlink's The Reader with the Browning–Goldhagen fight over how to explain the Holocaust's perpetrators — Browning's Ordinary Men, where mostly middle-aged, non-ideological reservists become mass killers through conformity, deference to authority, careerism, and gradual habituation, versus Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners, where the killing is better explained by a deeply rooted eliminationist antisemitism in German culture that made it feel righteous to the people doing it. I want to use the novel to put pressure on both sides of that debate, and I need help working out the reading before I get in front of the class.

On the surface Hanna Schmitz looks like a Browning case study: not a fanatic, not shown as especially antisemitic, just a woman who takes a guard job because it's a job, carries out procedures conscientiously, and whose most fateful decisions are driven by shame about her illiteracy rather than by ideology — she'd rather take the blame at trial for a report she couldn't have written than admit she can't read. But I'm not sure the novel lets that reading stand unchallenged. There's her habit of singling out the weak, frail prisoners to read to her before they're sent off — tenderness and selection-for-death in the same gesture — and above all the church fire, where keeping the prisoners locked in trumps everything. Does that scene break the situational reading, or does it actually show the darkest version of it, with dutifulness and rule-following as the entire explanation? What would Goldhagen say the novel is hiding — that making illiteracy the explanatory key lets "ordinary person in a bad situation" stand in for motives the book never actually examines, and so quietly exculpates her? And then there's the reception problem: the book (and the film) have been accused of making the perpetrator the sympathetic figure while the victims stay faceless. Is that a fair hit on the novel itself, or only on readers who take Hanna's story as the whole story?

Finally, supposing neither Browning nor Goldhagen fully wins the historiographical argument: what, if anything, does a novel like this add to that debate that the archival work can't — or is the honest answer "nothing, it just aestheticizes and anesthetizes"? I'd like a reading that takes the book seriously as literature rather than using it as an illustration of a thesis, that takes both historians seriously rather than flattening their disagreement, and that says plainly where the reading strains or breaks down.
