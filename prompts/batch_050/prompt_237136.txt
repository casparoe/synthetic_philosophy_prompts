I'm currently taking a second-year philosophy of mind seminar, and we've just finished the personal-identity unit: psychological continuity theories, the Ship of Theseus, fission cases, and the dispute over whether gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement preserves you or quietly kills you. For the end-of-term creative-response assignment we may submit a satire, provided it demonstrates real command of the material. Here's what I'd like you to write.

A satirical Terms of Service agreement, roughly 600-700 words, for a fictional company (call it something like Continuity, Inc.) that sells gradual mind uploading: nanobots replace one biological neuron with a functionally identical synthetic neuron every hour until the brain is entirely artificial, at which point the customer may be transferred to a server. The document should be deadpan legalese throughout, with numbered clauses and definitions.

The humor must come from the contract's scrupulous refusal to settle the metaphysical questions the customer most cares about. Include clauses that, without ever breaking legal voice, engage at least the following: (1) the Ship of Theseus problem — whether a brain whose parts have all been swapped remains the same brain, and whether the marketing department's favorite line about ordinary cellular turnover actually helps the company's case; (2) the gradualist intuition that slow, piecemeal replacement preserves identity where instantaneous scan-and-copy arguably does not, together with the fading-qualia style worry that consciousness might dim imperceptibly during the transition without any behavioral sign the company could be held liable for missing; (3) the fission problem — what happens if the discarded biological neurons are retained, reassembled into a second functioning brain, and two individuals now claim the same bank account; (4) the gap between metaphysical identity and legal identity, with the company expressly warranting only the latter; (5) an indemnity clause covering the possibility that the customer ceased to exist at the very first neuron and everything since has been a flawless impersonation.

Constraints: no clause may name philosophical positions, thought experiments, or philosophers; the jokes must live entirely in the content, so that a reader who knows the literature can identify exactly which argument each clause is dodging, exploiting, or quietly conceding; and the irony has to stay legible but never wink — the contract itself must behave as though nothing is amiss. Close with an arbitration clause and a signature line.

After the piece, add a short note of 100-150 words explaining which arguments in the personal-identity literature each of the five sections corresponds to and why the satire is fair to each side.
