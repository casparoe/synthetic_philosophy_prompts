Two philosophers of war have argued for two years without converging. One defends the traditionalist view associated with the Walzerian tradition: combatants on opposing sides are moral equals, so an unjust combatant who fights in good faith does nothing wrong merely by fighting, and a just combatant has no special moral complaint against those who try to kill him. The other defends the revisionist view associated with McMahan and the liability tradition: the permissions and liabilities of combatants track the justice of their cause, so combatants fighting an unjust war are typically liable to attack while combatants fighting a just war are not.

Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should have produced. Include: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign -- about what the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants actually asserts and what it does not, about the distinction between liability to defensive harm and desert of punishment, and about which cases (conscripts, combatants under duress, those who reasonably but falsely believe their cause just) are genuinely hard for both sides; (2) a table of precise disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own position; (3) the cruxes -- I suspect these include whether liability requires culpability or mere responsibility for an unjust threat, and whether any principled line separates the rifleman from the munitions worker -- each stated as a question such that settling it would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, at least one case or consideration both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about what it shows written down in advance. If you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal -- say, over what "permission" or "innocence" should mean in this context -- identify which part and argue for that diagnosis. Present only the statement itself, roughly 800-1,200 words.
