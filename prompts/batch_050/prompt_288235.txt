I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on the moral distinction between doing and allowing harm. To anticipate objections before my defense, I need a philosophical dialogue that stress-tests the distinction harder than the standard textbook material does. Please act as a professional philosopher with deep expertise in normative ethics and the philosophy of action, who is also an experienced writer of philosophical dialogues. Think carefully about the strongest version of each side before composing; weak or strawman arguments are worse than useless to me.

First, give me a brief strategy sketch (no more than 150 words): for each of the two interlocutors, state their position in one sentence and identify the two or three moves where you expect the dialectic to turn. Then write the dialogue itself, between 900 and 1300 words.

The setup. Two philosophers disagree about the doing/allowing distinction. The first defends the view that doing harm is, in itself, morally harder to justify than merely allowing harm — that the distinction carries genuine moral weight even when intention, outcome, and context are held fixed. The second is a skeptic: she holds that once intention, outcome, expectations, and role-based obligations are properly accounted for, no residual moral difference between doing and allowing remains, and that the felt difference is an artifact of convention, salience, or cognitive bias. Give them names and distinct intellectual temperaments, and make both of them genuinely sharp — each must respond to the other's actual arguments, each must concede at least one real point, and at least once one of them should refine their position under pressure rather than simply repeat it.

The dialogue must be anchored in the following two cases rather than the usual stock examples (the drowning-child-in-the-bathtub and trolley cases may be mentioned only in passing, if at all):

Case 1, The Two Consoles. A flood-control facility protects a worker stationed in a downstream culvert. Engineer A's console holds a safety valve shut only while she presses a button every sixty seconds; if she stops pressing, the valve opens and the worker drowns. Engineer B's console, on an identical system elsewhere in the plant, holds the valve shut by default; the valve opens only if she presses the button. Each engineer abandons her post to take a break, and in each case the worker drowns. The plant's safety officer notes that the choice between the two console designs was arbitrary — a purchasing decision that could have gone either way, and could still be reversed with ten minutes of rewiring. The skeptic will press the question of whether the moral gravity of what each engineer did can really track a wiring diagram. The defender needs an answer that does not simply collapse into talk of expectations and job descriptions, or must explain why such a collapse would not be a defeat.

Case 2, The Interrupted Rescue. A swimmer is drowning. An automated rescue drone is already descending to drop a flotation ring. X, who stands to inherit from the swimmer, jams the drone's signal; the ring is never dropped and the swimmer drowns. Y, a bystander with the same inheritance motive and no drone to contend with, simply watches and does nothing. Same intention, same outcome, same foresight. The defender will insist there is a morally crucial difference between X and Y; the skeptic will ask whether X has really done harm rather than merely prevented a prevention — whether the sea, and not X, killed the swimmer — and will press the defender to say where, exactly, the category boundary runs and why it should matter there.

Beyond working through these cases, make sure the dialogue engages all of the following pressure points: the challenge that the distinction cannot even be drawn in a morally neutral way, since any course of conduct admits of redescription as either an action or an omission; the question of whether withdrawing aid differs morally from withholding it, and whether that difference can survive the observation that both depend on institutionally fixed baselines; and the deflationary hypothesis that everything the distinction seems to explain is actually explained by agents' intentions, others' legitimate expectations, and special obligations — plus the defender's best reply, whether that reply is to resist the deflation or to absorb it and claim the distinction survives in transformed form.

Keep the dialogue case-driven and self-contained: the characters may gesture at familiar argumentative moves, but do not have them cite authors, papers, or books. End without a forced reconciliation; if the two converge on anything, let it be an honest diagnosis of where their disagreement actually lies — and if they cannot even manage that, let the disagreement stand, sharply stated.
