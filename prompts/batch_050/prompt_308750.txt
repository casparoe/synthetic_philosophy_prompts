I serve on the Ethics and Conduct Committee of a mid-sized engineering consultancy, and I need help drafting the written opinion I will circulate before we vote on a disciplinary matter next week. The facts are not in dispute; the disagreement is entirely about what follows from them.

A senior project manager, Mr. Aldan, processed and countersigned the internal paperwork for a contract he had good reason to believe was secured through bribes paid by our firm's regional director. He raised no objection and asked no questions. As it turned out, his paperwork played no role whatsoever: the client's procurement office had already finalized the contract using a parallel documentation package prepared by another department, and Aldan's file was never transmitted, never consulted, and was redundant from the outset. The bribery scheme would have unfolded in exactly the same way, on exactly the same timeline, had Aldan refused to sign a single page. A junior colleague reported the scheme; the regional director has been dismissed and the matter referred to prosecutors. The committee must now decide what, if anything, Aldan is culpable for and what sanction to recommend, from a formal reprimand to termination.

The committee is split. One faction argues that discipline for complicity in wrongdoing requires that the person actually made a difference to the wrong -- that since Aldan's contribution was causally inert, punishing him for the bribery scheme amounts to punishing him for his state of mind alone, which they regard as both unfair and a dangerous precedent, since whether his paperwork mattered was outside his knowledge and control. The other faction argues that intentional participation in a wrongful venture is culpable in itself, regardless of whether one's contribution turns out to be necessary or even operative, and that the firm cannot maintain an integrity policy if willing participants escape sanction merely because their help happened not to be needed.

Please draft the committee's written opinion. It should: (1) state a clear bottom-line recommendation on culpability and sanction, with a concrete outcome the board can vote to adopt; (2) take a position on the underlying question -- whether culpable complicity requires causal contribution to the wrong, or whether intentional participation suffices -- in terms general enough to serve as precedent for future cases, including cases where a contribution is useful but not necessary; (3) give the strongest available version of the losing side's argument and explain why it should nonetheless not control the outcome, or, if you judge it should control, explain what the majority's intuition gets wrong; and (4) address whether the firm should distinguish, in its conduct policy, between complicity that makes a difference and complicity that merely expresses willingness, and if so, where that distinction should and should not matter.
