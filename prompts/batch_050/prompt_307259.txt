Ok so I'm in a 20th century Europe historiography seminar and we've just spent three weeks on the Fischer controversy, and honestly I'm stuck on something meta rather than on any actual claim. Here's the thing that bugs me. Fischer drops his bombshell in the 60s, the German historical establishment loses its mind, there's this huge fight, then decades of archive work, then Clark's Sleepwalkers book comes out in 2012 and suddenly everyone is talking about shared responsibility and how all the great powers kind of stumbled into it. A century of scholarship, basically all the archives open by now, and yet smart historians who've read the same documents still split into "Germany bears the main blame" vs "everyone sleepwalked" camps. It doesn't seem to converge the way, idk, debates about whether the Lusitania was carrying munitions did.

So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Like, is there still a real factual dispute underneath (about Bethmann Hollweg's intentions, the blank cheque, the Septemberprogramm, whatever), or is the fight really about what "blame" or "responsibility" even means when applied to a whole country -- like whether responsibility has to sum to 100%, whether you can blame a state the way you blame a person, whether the counterfactual question (would war have happened anyway without German actions) is even answerable? Or is it something else, like the two sides just weighting different kinds of evidence, or asking different questions under one label?

My hypothesis, which might be totally wrong, is that the empirical work mostly settled a while ago and the residual disagreement is basically conceptual/moral, dressed up as archival. But then I don't get why Clark's book landed as a *historical* contribution rather than a philosophy-of-responsibility contribution.

Can you diagnose the structure of the disagreement, and tell me what would actually count as progress from here -- a new distinction, a different question, some hypothetical document that would settle it if found? Or is this just the kind of debate that can't be resolved because it's really about present-day politics (Germany's self-image, EU stuff, etc.) wearing a 1914 costume?
