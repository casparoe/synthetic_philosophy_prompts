I'm TAing an intro philosophy seminar this semester and next Friday I get one 50-minute section to teach the sorites paradox to first-years, most of whom have never taken philosophy before. The prof's lecture will have already covered valid argument form in a very basic way (modus ponens, chaining conditionals), but nothing about vagueness. I don't want you to explain the paradox to me — I want a plan for how I should teach it. Tell me the order the pieces have to come in and why: e.g., do the students need to feel the pull of each premise separately before they see the contradiction, and if so which premise do I make them endorse first? My worry is that if I present the whole argument at once they'll just shrug and say "so define your terms better," which kills the whole session.

For the carrying example: I was arguing with my roommate about this and she said just use the heap of sand like everyone does, but I suspect the heap fails because students can picture a three-grain heap and start haggling over whether four grains is a heap, which drags them into exactly the "just stipulate a cutoff" response I want to postpone. I'm considering something temporal instead — a timelapse video of a kid growing up, or slowly turning down the volume of a song — where there's no way to even gesture at a precise threshold. Tell me what the example has to have and has to lack, and whether a dynamic example actually helps or smuggles in distractions of its own.

Then the misconception map: I expect at least (a) "this is just a problem about our words, not the world," (b) "we could solve it by decreeing that 10,000 grains = heap," and (c) confusing vagueness with ambiguity or with mere ignorance of a fact someone could look up. Tell me which of these the students will actually raise, in what order they'll come up as the session proceeds, and exactly where in my sequence I should head each one off rather than letting it fester. Also: should I mention higher-order vagueness (borderline cases of borderline cases) at all, or explicitly tell them I'm bracketing it — and if I do mention it, is it a weapon against the degree-theorist-style "truth comes in degrees" response some sharp student will propose? End with one question I can pose in the last five minutes whose answers would show me whether they got that the paradox is a conflict between premises we each independently want, and not just a puzzle about the word "heap."
