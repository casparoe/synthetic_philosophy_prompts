so I recently reported something at work that probably counts as whistleblowing (long story, it worked out, but the months of "isn't this just betraying your team" from a few people really stuck with me). Somehow this has led to me agreeing to run a session at my local library's ethics discussion group on loyalty and whistleblowing. About ten people, mixed ages, zero philosophy background, 90 minutes, and several of them will definately show up thinking whistleblowers are either heroes or snitches with no third option.

I want to get one specific idea across: that loyalty attaches to what a person or institution is *for* -- its purposes, its best self -- rather than to its immediate wishes, so whistleblowing can be an act of loyalty rather than the betrayal everyone frames it as. And I want to build toward a rough test I've been chewing on: the loyal whistleblower is the one the institution *would* thank if it could see the situation without defensiveness.

Plan the session rather than giving me a lecture script: what has to be in place before the central claim doesn't just sound like a self-serving rationalization (I suspect "loyalty isn't obedience" has to land first, but tell me if I'm wrong about the order), the one concrete case to build the whole thing around -- I want an invented mid-sized case, not a famous one where everyone's verdict comes pre-loaded -- and what that case needs to include and leave out so the counterfactual test actually does work on it. Then the misconceptions in the order they'll surface (loyalty as unconditional standing-by-someone, whistleblowers as disgruntled or self-dramatizing, "real loyalty means keeping it internal forever", the test as unfalsifiable since you can't ask the institution), with where in the sequence each one gets headed off and which one I'd better not leave until questions. Also: how do I check at the end that they've actually got the idea rather than just enjoyed a dramatic story, and what should I openly say I'm leaving out at this level?

End with a last line starting "Summary:" that gives the one-sentence version of the whole plan.
