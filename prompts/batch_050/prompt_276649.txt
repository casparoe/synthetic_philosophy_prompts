You are a philosopher with joint expertise in logic, cognitive science, and the theory of self-referential systems, designing a diagnostic instrument for a graduate seminar on strange loops. The seminar's organizing question is this: when Escher's Drawing Hands shows each hand sketching the other into existence, everyone agrees the depicted situation is impossible — but people disagree sharply about where exactly the impossibility lives. Your task is to turn that disagreement into a decision tree.

Before drafting anything, spend a paragraph brainstorming the logical space: list the distinct claims someone could make about what goes wrong (or surprisingly fails to go wrong) in tangled hierarchies, and only then design the tree. Do not start from a list of named theories and retrofit questions onto them; start from the questions and locate the positions afterward.

Specifications for the tree:

1. Root node. The tree begins at a shared commitment, not at a name: the student accepts that some form of hierarchy among levels of a system (hardware/software, object-language/metalanguage, organism/self-model) is real, and that Drawing Hands-style tangles at least appear to violate it. Every subsequent node sorts diagnoses of what the appearance amounts to.

2. Branch nodes as neutral questions. Each node must be a single question a student could answer from their own intuitions about a concrete case, without knowing which position it funnels them toward. Use concrete probes: a camera feeding its own monitor, a thermostat, a brain constructing a self-model, a program printing its own source code, the sentence "this sentence is false." At least two nodes should be anchored to such cases rather than to abstract formulations.

3. Required leaves. Whatever route the tree takes, these positions must each appear as exactly one leaf: (a) strict hierarchicalism, on which levels are inviolate and apparent tangles rest on equivocation between them; (b) the view associated with Hofstadter, on which tangles are genuine at the level of symbols or representations but bottom out in an inviolate causal substrate that the tangle never touches; (c) the dialethic view that some loops yield true contradictions; (d) a truth-gap or ungroundedness view; (e) a revision-theoretic view on which the loop never stabilizes because the evaluation itself keeps oscillating; (f) a dissolutionist or deflationary view that the paradox is an artifact of an overambitious concept (of truth, of reference, of levels) rather than a fact about the world. You may add further leaves if the space demands them.

4. Unoccupied combinations. After the tree, list every answer-combination that corresponds to no named position, and for each give a one-sentence diagnosis: is the slot empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

5. Indistinguishable leaves. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate (I suspect, for example, that (d) and (e) will be hard to pry apart with yes/no questions about the Liar alone, and that (a) and (b) may collapse into each other depending on how one node is phrased), and say what further question — or what further kind of case — would be needed to separate them.

6. Size and audience. Aim for seven to ten branch nodes. The reader is a bright graduate student who knows the Liar paradox and has read a little about self-reference, but has not studied formal theories of truth. Phrase accordingly: no symbolic notation in the node questions, though you may use it sparingly in your commentary.

Close with two or three sentences of meta-commentary: which node in your tree you judge to carry the most classificatory weight — the one where the largest number of serious positions part company — and why.
