Two coworkers of mine got into it over lunch and I'd like a referee. One of them called generational labels — Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z — "astrology for people who read thinkpieces": the birth-year cutoffs are arbitrary (why does a Millennial start in 1981 and not 1979 or 1983?), nobody can agree on where the lines are, and the stereotype attached to each label fits maybe a third of the actual people in it. The other pushed back: cohorts are causally real because big events hit people differently depending on how old they were — graduating into the 2008 crash, being in high school when smartphones arrived, starting a career remote during covid — so a generation is less like a star sign and more like "people who were teenagers during the war," which is obviously a real group. First guy replied that within-generation variation swamps between-generation variation on basically every attitude researchers measure, so the label carries almost no information, and besides, the shared-events logic proves too much: it would make "people whose first job was during covid" a generation, which nobody believes. She said fine, maybe that *is* a real cohort, and he's conflating "the category isn't very predictive" with "the category doesn't exist" — plenty of real kinds are noisy. He said a kind whose boundaries are conventional and which predicts almost nothing is just media shorthand that's been reified. She said by that standard neither "adolescence" nor "the middle class" is real either. Can you go through this move by move — who's right about the arbitrary cutoffs, the within-vs-between variance point, the covid-cohort reductio, and that last analogy — and is there anything both of them are missing?
