I work in regulatory affairs for a mid-size pharma company (won't say which), and there's a class of decision that keeps coming up in our risk modeling that nobody on my team has the vocabulary for, and I'm starting to wonder if philosophy does. Here's the setup. We sometimes compare two formulations of a drug where option A imposes a tiny, certain loss on a huge number of patients — say each of ten million people loses on average a few hours of healthy life expectancy, an amount no individual could ever notice, detect, or even in principle distinguish from the noise of normal life — and option B imposes a statistically equivalent total loss but concentrated, so a handful of identifiable people die young and everyone else loses nothing. The weird part: everyone I work with, including me, has a strong gut feeling that A is the obviously better choice, possibly even a non-event, even when the models say the total expected harm is the same or slightly larger under A. And I get why, sort of. But it bothers me that I can't tell whether that intuition is tracking something real or just a failure of imagination about numbers.

Here's the specific thing I want a name for, if there is one. I've read a bit around this and the famous cases I keep finding don't quite fit. There's the dust specks vs. torture thing, but dust specks are at least *felt* — everyone who gets one has a mildly bad moment. There's Parfit's case with the torturers where each person's contribution is imperceptible but the victim definitely feels the total. My cases are different in a way that seems important: the harms are sub-experiential all the way down AND all the way up. No individual ever suffers, and there is no aggregate suffering either, because the "total" is just a number in a model — a sum of losses that no conscious being anywhere ever experiences, even in principle. If you interviewed every affected person for a thousand years nobody would ever report anything. Yet we treat the sum as real for regulatory purposes (or at least some people do). So: is there an established term for harms that are imperceptible both individually and in aggregate — losses that exist only as expected values, never as anyone's experience? And is there literature on whether such things can even be aggregated coherently, since it seems like you'd be adding up zeroes of experience to get a nonzero moral quantity?

To be clear about what I'm not asking: I'm not asking whether tiny risks should be regulated (that's a policy question), and I'm not asking about cases where one person's imperceptible contribution combines with others into something someone *does* feel. I want the carving for the strictly invisible case: what are the nearest named concepts, how does this configuration differ from each of them, does anything in the literature actually address the both-directions-invisible structure, and if nothing does, what would you call it and where exactly are its edges — e.g., does it still count if the victim would notice under ideal conditions nobody ever meets, and does it matter whether the loss is hours of life vs. money vs. capability? A bare name with no analysis won't help me. Please answer in American English.
