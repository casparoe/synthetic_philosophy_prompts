My office-mate is convinced he's found a fatal problem for the whole Lewis-style scorekeeping picture of conversation, and he laid it out for me like this. (1) When a speaker presupposes something that isn't already common ground, hearers just accommodate it -- if I say "I have to go feed my sister's cat," you don't stop me to establish that I have a sister, you quietly adjust the record and we move on. (2) Accommodation is silent, automatic, and works by a rule roughly like: accept whatever needs to be true for the utterance to be appropriate in the first place. (3) So presupposed content gets onto the conversational record just as reliably as asserted-and-agreed content does. (4) Which means presupposing is a strictly better move than asserting: an assertion proposes something the hearer can deny, but a presupposition just loads it in behind their back. (5) And that means no utterance can ever be out of line because of what it presupposes -- worst case, the hearer accommodates. (6) So there can't be any real difference between saying "sorry I'm late, my parole officer kept me waiting" to colleagues who know about your record, and saying the same thing to colleagues who have no idea, banking on them just absorbing it. He says since (6) is obviously false, accommodation as a mechanism is a myth and the scorekeeping model has to go.

I don't buy his conclusion but I can't locate the bad step. Each of (1) through (5) looked fine when he said them, and I'm especially attached to keeping (1) and (2) since "hey, wait a minute, you have a sister?" moments seem like the exception rather than the rule. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what do you have to give up to reject it -- e.g., if accommodation isn't as automatic as he says, what actually governs when hearers push back versus when they absorb, and does that distinction have to be doing real work in the theory or can it stay vague?
