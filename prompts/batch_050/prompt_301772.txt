I'm a junior in high school, and I need advice about a disagreement I'm having with my friend Marcus. It started at our school's spring fundraiser, where the student council ran a raffle-style game to raise money for the senior class trip. For a $5 entry fee, you picked one of two envelopes. Envelope 1 contained a guaranteed prize: a $40 gift card to a local bookstore, no strings attached. Envelope 2 contained a slip of paper that gave you a 10 percent chance of winning a $500 gift card and a 90 percent chance of winning nothing at all. I chose Envelope 1, and honestly the choice felt easy. Marcus pointed out that if you averaged things out, Envelope 2 was worth more -- a 10 percent shot at $500 comes out to $50 on average, which beats a certain $40. I agreed with his math but still felt my choice was reasonable. Walking away with something real seemed better than a small chance at a bigger prize when the likely outcome was going home empty-handed.

Here's where things got complicated. The next weekend, the student council ran the game again at a community event, but with a twist: this time, both envelopes contained a slip that gave you only a 10 percent chance of your prize being honored at all (with a 90 percent chance of nothing either way), and if the prize was honored, Envelope 1 paid the guaranteed $40 while Envelope 2 gave you a 90 percent chance at the $500 and a 10 percent chance of nothing. So effectively, Envelope 1 offered a 10 percent chance at $40 and Envelope 2 offered a 9 percent chance at $500. This time I chose Envelope 2 without hesitating, since the odds were nearly identical and the prize was so much bigger.

Marcus says my two choices contradict each other. His argument is that in the second game, both prizes were simply scaled down by the same 10 percent chance of being honored, so if I truly preferred the certain $40 over the $500 gamble in the first game, I should have preferred the scaled-down version of the $40 in the second game too. He says I can't have it both ways: either I go with the higher average payout, or I go with the safer option, but switching depending on how the choice is framed is just muddled thinking.

I have a nagging feeling he's missing something. In the first game, the $40 was a sure thing, and giving up a certainty feels different from giving up a slightly better chance when both options are long shots anyway. Once everything is unlikely, it seems natural to chase the bigger prize. But I can't articulate why the presence of a sure thing should change what counts as the rational choice, and I also can't tell whether Marcus is right that any consistent way of weighing risk should treat the two games the same.

So my questions are: Is my pair of choices actually irrational, or is there a coherent way of valuing outcomes under uncertainty that makes room for treating guaranteed outcomes as special? If such a way exists, what exactly does it say is going wrong with the simple average-payout reasoning Marcus is using? And practically speaking, how should I think about similar choices in the future -- ones where a modest certainty competes against a small chance at something much better -- so that I'm being thoughtful about risk rather than just going with my gut?
