Next month I am presenting a paper to a political science department's theory workshop. My thesis is that Arrow's impossibility theorem is habitually misread in debates about electoral reform. The theorem does not show that we have yet to find the right voting rule; it shows that there is no collective preference waiting to be found that a better rule could reveal more faithfully. The will of the people is not an input to aggregation but an artifact of whichever aggregation rule we use, and since any outcome a rule produces is partly constituted by the rule itself, the familiar disputes among plurality, instant-runoff, and approval voting are, philosophically, arguments about which artifact to manufacture rather than about which instrument measures the electorate more accurately.

The audience contains two formal social choice theorists who know the theorem's conditions and the surrounding results (Gibbard--Satterthwaite, the median voter theorem, the Condorcet literature) better than I do; a deliberative democrat who believes preferences are formed and structured in public reasoning rather than merely aggregated; an empirical comparativist who studies actual ranked-choice elections and is impatient with armchair impossibility results; and several graduate students who will ask the questions everyone else considers too naive to ask.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the voice of the person most likely to raise each, and ranked by how damaging they are to my thesis rather than by how interesting they are. For each objection, tell me what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in under two minutes. Flag where the artifact-rather-than-measurement framing is weakest: for instance, the claim that Arrow's theorem constrains social welfare functions rather than voting procedures, that the ordinalism assumption does all the work and cardinal methods escape the result entirely, or that single-peaked preferences show the incoherence is pathological rather than constitutive. Finally, name the objection I am privately hoping nobody raises, and be candid about whether my thesis survives it.
