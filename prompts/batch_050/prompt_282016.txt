Compare two ways of deciding what to do under moral uncertainty. First, the standard maximize-expected-choiceworthiness approach, where you basically treat each moral theory's verdicts as coming with intertheoretically comparable cardinal scores and then take a credence-weighted average. Second, something I'm gonna call the "conscience parliament": every theory you give non-negligible credence to gets seats proportional to your credence, any option that a majority of the chamber considers flat-out impermissible (not just suboptimal, actually wrong) gets vetoed, and among the survivors you pick via a Borda count where each theory ranks options by how choiceworthy it takes them to be. Where do these two come apart in practice? Which one handles better the case where a theory you barely credit says some act is a catastrophically grave wrong while all your other theories shrug? And is the parliament really anything more than MEC in disguise with extra steps, or is there a genuine structural difference? Justify your answer.
