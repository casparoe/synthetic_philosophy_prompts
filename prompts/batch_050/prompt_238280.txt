For my course on the ethics of war and emerging technology, our seminar has spent the term acting as a mock review panel ruling on proposed autonomous weapon systems, and I have to draft the panel's opinion on the final case. Our rulings so far: we approved a shipboard defensive system that, once activated by its crew, detects and fires on incoming missiles far faster than any human could intervene; we approved a loitering munition that, after launch toward a designated area, searches for and destroys radar installations for up to two hours with no further human input; we rejected a ground robot that generates lists of human targets for an operator to confirm, on the grounds that in practice operators simply rubber-stamp the list; and we rejected an autonomous sentry gun for a border zone that fires on anything crossing a publicly marked line, even though its behavior is entirely predictable. The new proposal is a drone swarm that, once released over a declared combat zone during an ongoing battle, selects and attacks enemy combatants on its own: individual engagements happen too fast for human veto, but officers set the swarm's geographic boundaries, target class, and a two-hour operating window, and can recall it at any time. Recommend the ruling that best coheres with our own precedents, name the closest analog among them, and say which principle we would be committing ourselves to if we approve and which if we reject. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say so and describe the further hypothetical ruling that would reveal which principle we have actually been applying. Please also separate what is genuinely settled -- in the law of armed conflict and in the ethics literature on meaningful human control -- from what remains contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Lead with the recommended ruling in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble.
