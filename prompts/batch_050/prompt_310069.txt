I teach an introductory philosophy of language seminar, and next week I have one seventy-five-minute session on generic generalizations — sentences like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" — for second-year students who have mastered introductory predicate logic but have no semantics beyond it. Plan the session rather than delivering it: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what (my instinct is that the failure of the universal reading has to precede anything positive, but I want this argued), the single example that should carry the session — I am torn between "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria," so tell me which to build around and what the carrier must have and must lack — and whether a case like "sharks attack bathers" belongs in the core arc or would only muddy it. Then give me the misconception map: I expect students to treat generics as universals with tolerated exceptions, as covert statistical claims about most members of the kind, or as loose speech not worth analyzing; tell me the order in which these will surface and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. Finally, specify one question I can pose at the end that would show they have understood that exceptions to a true generic can be the statistical norm rather than noise, and say what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I suspect the formal operator semantics and normative generics like "boys don't cry" should be deferred, but confirm or correct me.
