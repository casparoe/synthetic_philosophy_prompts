I am a novelist at work on a book narrated in part by a historian writing several millennia from now, looking back on our century. In the reading I have done to prepare, I keep encountering a particular analogy in the circles that concern themselves with humanity's long-term future: that our species today is like an adolescent. Humanity, on this picture, has come into adult powers — it can poison its own habitat, split the atom, and may soon build machines more capable than itself — without yet having come into adult judgment. And, like a teenager choosing a career, experimenting with things that can addict or kill, or deciding whom to trust, the species is making choices right now, about how to govern its most powerful technologies and which institutions and trajectories to entrench, that will shape and perhaps irreversibly fix the whole of its later life. The analogy is routinely made to carry an argumentative conclusion: because this is supposedly our civilizational coming-of-age, the present century deserves a disproportionate share of our attention and resources, and actions taken now matter far more than they would in ordinary times.

Before I let my narrator lean on this image, I want to know whether it can bear the weight. Treat the analogy as an argument and audit it. First, give me the correspondence table, worked out with some care: what plays the role of the adolescent's half-formed judgment, of the newly acquired physical powers, of the choices that lock in a life, of the long adult lifespan presumed to stretch ahead, and of the parents or guardians whose restraining hand the teenager resents? Note any element of the source that has no plausible counterpart in the target at all.

Second, catalogue the disanalogies, and — this is the part I care about most — separate the ones that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. For instance: an individual who survives adolescence matures more or less by default, along a developmental arc laid down in advance, whereas a civilization has no such script; does that difference touch the conclusion, or is it decorative? Likewise: the teenager's remaining lifespan is roughly known and the formative window genuinely short, while the claim about our century's unique pivotedness is precisely what the analogy is supposed to establish rather than assume.

Third, render a verdict. Does the conclusion that the present is disproportionately consequential survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing that work by itself? If some version of the conclusion stands on independent grounds — for example, that certain risks are simply time-indexed and certain choices genuinely hard to reverse — say how much of the original force those grounds preserve, and whether the developmental metaphor adds anything beyond rhetoric.

Finally, if the adolescent analogy fails or misleads, propose a better one and show that it fits more closely: would the species be better figured as an infant than a teenager, as a patient self-administering a treatment of unknown dosage, as a crew sailing out of harbor in a storm, or as something else entirely? Tell me which image, if any, my narrator is entitled to.
