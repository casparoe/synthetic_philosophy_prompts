I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and for the past few months I've been building a side project: a public, longitudinal dataset of lethal violence — war deaths, homicides, and similar categories — stretching back several centuries, plus a small dashboard that summarizes the long-run trend. I assumed the hard part would be sourcing and cleaning historical data. It isn't. The hard part is that whether the trend line goes up or down appears to depend on conceptual choices I have to make before I touch a single record, and I can't tell which of those choices genuinely matter and which are distractions.

Here are the open questions I've identified so far:

1. The denominator problem. Whether violence is declining looks very different measured per capita versus in absolute terms, and both seem defensible: per capita tracks an individual person's risk of dying violently, while absolute numbers track the total amount of violent death in the world. I can't tell whether one of these is the correct measure and the other a confusion, or whether the question "is violence declining?" is really two different questions that happen to share a sentence.

2. The category-boundary problem. Where I draw the line between violence and its near neighbors — coercion, neglect, preventable deaths under a foreseeably lethal institutional arrangement — changes the dataset more than any archival research I could possibly do. Worse, every principle I've come up with for drawing the line feels circular, because I notice I keep drawing it wherever it produces a cleaner trend.

3. The aggregation problem. Different categories of violence can move in opposite directions over the same period. If battle deaths fall while homicide rises, does "violence" have a trend at all, or only its components do? And if I do aggregate, I'm implicitly setting exchange rates between kinds of death — one war death against one homicide against one death by state neglect — and I have no idea what could justify those rates.

4. The fact-of-the-matter problem. Suppose every measurement choice above were made fully explicit and agreed upon. Is the remaining disagreement about whether violence has declined an empirical disagreement, or a purely verbal one? Is there anything left to be right about, or does the question dissolve once the parameters are fixed?

What I'd like from you: rank these four questions — plus at most one additional question you think I've missed that belongs on the list — by how much they matter for my project, where "mattering" has to mean something concrete. My proposed criterion is verdict-sensitivity: how much does the dashboard's final answer flip depending on how the question is resolved? If you think a different criterion should outrank or supplement that one — say, tractability for a lone non-specialist, or how many downstream design decisions depend on the question — argue for it rather than assuming it. Defend the ordering, state explicitly what consideration or evidence would change it, and for your top-ranked question, say what settling it would even look like: what would count as an answer rather than an arbitrary decision. The deliverable I want is a build order — which question I have to settle before writing another line of code, which I can defer with a toggle, and which, if any, turn out on inspection to matter much less than they appear to.
