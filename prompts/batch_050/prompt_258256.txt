I'm preparing to TA a seminar on the philosophy of skill and expertise, and in my preparation I came across a chapter in an unnamed introductory textbook on the philosophy of mind that summarizes Gilbert Ryle's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that. The summary runs roughly as follows: Ryle argued that knowing how to do something — ride a bicycle, play chess, speak a language — is a fundamentally different kind of thing from knowing that something is the case, and not a species of it. His central argument is a regress: if performing an action intelligently required first consulting a proposition about how to perform it, then consulting that proposition would itself be an intelligent action requiring a prior act of consultation, and so on forever, so no one could ever act at all. This, the textbook says, decisively refutes the 'intellectualist' view that intelligent action is guided by propositional knowledge, and establishes that knowing-how is non-propositional — a matter of trained disposition or muscle memory rather than belief — which is why expert performers so often can't put their knowledge into words, and why you can't learn to swim from a book. The chapter closes by suggesting that this has consequences for education: since genuine skill can't be transmitted propositionally, instruction that relies on explicit rules and verbal explanation is at best a preliminary crutch and at worst an obstacle to real mastery.

Something about the chapter bothers me, though I can't put my finger on exactly where it overreaches. The regress argument seems to show *something*, but I'm not sure it shows everything the textbook takes it to show, and the slide from 'experts often can't articulate their knowledge' to 'the knowledge is non-propositional' feels quick. I'm also suspicious of the move from 'can't be learned from a book alone' to 'verbal instruction is an obstacle,' since those seem compatible with very different pictures of what knowing-how is.

Could you audit the summary for me? Which parts would Ryle himself, or a careful contemporary defender of the distinction, endorse; which parts are common simplifications that the literature has complicated (for instance, do all parties agree that the regress refutes intellectualism, and are there serious recent attempts to reduce knowing-how to knowing-that); and which parts are misrepresentations — say, conflating the *ability* to do something with *knowing how* to do it, or inferring a claim about the nature of knowledge from observations about what experts can verbalize? Finally, does the closing claim about education follow from the philosophically defensible core of the view, or does it depend on the shakier parts?
