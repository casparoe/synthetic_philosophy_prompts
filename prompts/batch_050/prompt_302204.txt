In Book IV of The Methods of Ethics (1874; I am working from the seventh edition of 1907), in the chapter titled 'Proof of Utilitarianism' (p. 498), Henry Sidgwick makes a concession that he takes to block his own attempted proof of utilitarianism. Paraphrasing: he grants that the distinction between any one individual and any other is real and fundamental; that he is therefore concerned with the quality of his own existence in a fundamentally important sense in which he is not concerned with the quality of the existence of other individuals; and that he cannot see how it could be demonstrated that this distinction ought not to be treated as fundamental in determining the ultimate end of rational action for an individual. This concession is the seed of what he comes to call, in the book's final chapter, the dualism of practical reason: his conclusion that rational egoism and universalistic hedonism are coordinate, self-evident, and irreconcilable deliverances of reason, unless some further and empirically unverifiable postulate (in his view, a religious one) harmonises duty with interest.

I am a welfare economist by training, fluent in the vocabulary of utility theory and social choice but with only a secondhand acquaintance with the Sidgwick literature. I would like the position expressed in this passage, and the dualism it motivates, restated as precisely as possible in the vocabulary I actually use: agent-relative versus agent-neutral orderings, utility functions, separability across persons, anonymity and impartiality axioms, social welfare functionals, and Harsanyi-style aggregation. Then I want the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Which elements of Sidgwick's claim translate cleanly? Which translate only under distortion? My suspicion is that the so-called contradiction evaporates in my framework: an agent-relative ordering and an agent-neutral ordering are simply two different orderings, and rational choice theory has no machinery for saying that both bind the same agent simultaneously, so what Sidgwick experienced as an intolerable conflict of reason looks, from here, like an unproblematic indexicality. If that suspicion is right, say so and explain what has been lost. If something in the passage resists it -- for instance, whether his premise is a metaphysical claim about the separateness of persons or a claim about the rational authority of partiality, and whether agent-relative value in the modern sense captures either -- identify exactly where the translation fails.

Finally, adjudicate: does the residue show that my vocabulary is missing something Sidgwick saw clearly, or does it show that his dualism is an artefact of a vocabulary of self-evident principles that decision theory rightly discarded? Test your answer on a concrete case -- a one-shot choice in which an agent can secure a strictly larger total good for strangers at small cost to herself -- and state how each framework diagnoses it, and what would have to be true for the two diagnoses genuinely to diverge.
