I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with an old friend about classical Chinese ethics, and we reached no resolution. She defends Xunzi's view that human nature is bad and that goodness is an artifact of conscious activity; I side with Mencius, for whom the heart contains inborn sprouts of virtue that flourish if they are tended. The argument kept circling through the same moves — the child at the well against the potter's clay, Ox Mountain against the warped wood that must be steamed straight — and it ended, as these arguments do, with each of us restating our opening position. As a kind of peace offering, I want to send her a poem. Write it for me.

The poem should be a villanelle, in roughly iambic pentameter, and its two refrains should be the two positions: one line that states Mencius's claim, one line that states Xunzi's. Each refrain must be accurate enough that a reader of both texts could tell immediately which philosopher it belongs to — the Mencian line should carry the sense that the capacity for goodness is already present and merely needs cultivation rather than construction, and the Xunzi line should carry the sense that nature unshaped leads to contention, and that what is good in us is made, not found. Neither refrain may caricature the other side: Xunzi does not hold that people are wicked, and Mencius does not hold that people are finished products, and the lines should not pretend otherwise.

The form should do the philosophical work. Across the five tercets, the intervening lines should stage the actual points of friction — the status of desire, what moral education is for, whether ritual reshapes us or merely expresses what is already there — so that each return of a refrain lands slightly differently than the time before. In the closing quatrain, where the villanelle's two refrains finally stand side by side, the poem should make visible what my friend and I discovered at midnight: that the two lines do not refute each other and do not merge, but that the difference between tending a sprout and straightening a board may be smaller, or stranger, than either of us admitted. Do not resolve the disagreement on my behalf; let the form say what the argument could not.
