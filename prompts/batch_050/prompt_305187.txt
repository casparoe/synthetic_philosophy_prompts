I'm in a grad reading group on the gender-equality paradox (the finding that countries scoring highest on gender equality, like the Nordics, often have *more* gender-segregated career choices, especially fewer women in STEM). One member of the group, Mara, has very confident intuitions about what would and wouldn't count as evidence that these preferences are "natural" rather than "cultural," and over the semester I've been collecting her verdicts on hypothetical findings. Her track record so far: (1) if the STEM gap turned up in literally every society ever studied, including forager groups, she'd call that strong evidence it's natural; (2) if the gap showed up in preverbal infants' toy and activity preferences, she'd call that evidence for nature; (3) if the gap vanished completely just by rewording job ads and course descriptions, with the jobs themselves unchanged, she'd say that proves it was cultural all along; (4) if the gap persisted even in kids raised in deliberately gender-neutral communes, she'd call that natural.

Next week I want to present her with a trickier hypothetical and I'm trying to predict her reaction in advance. Suppose the gap is universal across all known societies AND shows up in toddlers, BUT it turns out to be surprisingly fragile: a light-touch intervention, say one semester of revised career materials in schools, eliminates it almost entirely within a single cohort, and it stays gone. Predict her verdict and tell me which implicit rule she's most plausibly running -- "natural = universal/early-appearing," "natural = resistant to intervention," "natural = what develops without social influence," or something else. I suspect her four verdicts are jointly consistent with at least two of these rules, and that the new hypothetical is exactly where they come apart. Say which earlier verdict is the closest analog and which ones pull the other direction, and if her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, tell me what principle she'd be committing to with each possible verdict, and what further hypothetical finding would force her to choose between the candidate rules.

One more thing: Mara tends to slide from "it's natural" to "so we should stop trying to change it" and from "it's cultural" to "so it's fair game for policy." If you can, say a bit about whether her verdict on my hypothetical would actually settle anything about justice or policy, or whether natural-vs-cultural is doing less work in that debate than she assumes.
