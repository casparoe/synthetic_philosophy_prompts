At a workshop last month I ended up at dinner next to two statisticians -- one does ML theory, one does epidemiology -- who spent the entire meal arguing about whether rational disagreement can survive a lot of shared evidence.

The ML guy opened with: it's basically a theorem that two Bayesians watching the same growing stream of data end up agreeing, whatever priors they started with. So when two experts still disagree after years of reading the same literature, someone must be doing motivated reasoning. Priors wash out; what doesn't wash out is bias.

The epi said the theorem only kicks in if both sides put nonzero prior weight in the region where the truth actually lives, and even then it says nothing about *rates* -- "eventually" can outlast any human deadline, so the result is inert for real disputes.

ML guy: putting literally zero probability on a live possibility is dogmatism, not a legitimate prior, so the antecedent he needs is one any rational agent satisfies anyway. And slow convergence cuts both ways -- if it's slow for everyone, durable expert consensus is still the best evidence available.

Epi: real inference happens inside simplified model classes, and you can have two perfectly careful modelers whose classes don't overlap -- data settles lots of questions within each class but can't force their conclusions toward each other. That's not a pathological edge case, it's the normal situation in something like nutrition science.

They both turned to me to settle it and I honestly couldn't. Can you say who wins each round and why, flag anywhere they're talking past each other (I suspect the phrase "the same evidence" is doing some smuggling), and give an overall verdict on the practical question: should long-lived expert disagreement lower my confidence that *anyone* involved is reasoning well, or not?
