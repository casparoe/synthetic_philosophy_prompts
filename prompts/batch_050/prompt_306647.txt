I coordinate a small research group — three philosophers and two psychologists — that is about to commit three years to a program on moral self-evaluation: how people come to judge their own conduct, when those judgments track anything beyond local opinion, and whether the capacity can be deliberately shaped. We are organizing the program around Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and in particular around this passage from Part III, Chapter I:

"Were it possible that a human creature could grow up to manhood in some solitary place, without any communication with his own species, he could no more think of his own character, of the propriety or demerit of his own sentiments and conduct, of the beauty or deformity of his own mind, than of the beauty or deformity of his own face. All these are objects which he cannot easily see, which naturally he does not look at, and with regard to which he is provided with no mirror which can present them to his view. Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before. It is placed in the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with, which always mark when they enter into, and when they disapprove of his sentiments; and it is here that he first views the propriety and impropriety of his own passions, the beauty and deformity of his own mind."

Our working reading is that this passage commits Smith to the view that the standards by which a person judges himself are imported from the society around him — conscience begins as borrowed eyes. If that is right, it generates more open questions than we can pursue, and we must choose. Which two or three open questions about Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator do you judge most worth our three years, in what order, and why? The selection must turn on the passage: on what it actually commits Smith to, and on what it leaves unresolved.

To anchor the choice, here are directions we have discussed — but argue for your own selection rather than treating this list as exhaustive: (i) how, on Smith's account, the internalized impartial spectator can ever correct rather than merely replicate the judgments of one's actual community, if its materials are drawn from that community; (ii) whether the "fair and impartial spectator" Smith invokes elsewhere is meant as a fiction with real causal power or as a summary of accumulated social feedback, and what turns on the difference; (iii) whether a framework in which moral standards are socially mirrored can license cross-community moral criticism, or quietly forecloses it; (iv) what the mirror mechanism predicts about environments in which the "society" supplying the mirror is engineered — curated feeds, niche online communities — and whether that is a test of Smith's theory or merely an application of it.
