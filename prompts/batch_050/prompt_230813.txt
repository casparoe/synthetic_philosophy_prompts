I'm giving a twenty-minute talk next month at a cognitive science seminar defending the Hofstadter and Sander line from Surfaces and Essences: that analogy-making isn't one reasoning skill among others but the very core of cognition, and that categorisation and analogy are the same process seen at different levels of abstraction. The wrinkle I want to defend, which goes a bit beyond the book, is that large language models are evidence for this view rather than a counterexample to it: they got good at analogy not by having an analogy module bolted on but by doing categorisation-at-scale, i.e. by learning to recognise situations as instances of previously encountered situations. The audience is mostly connectionist-leaning ML researchers plus a couple of old-school cognitive psychologists, and I expect at least one person who cut their teeth on Gentner's structure-mapping work and thinks Hofstadter's group never engaged with the quantitative literature properly. Prepare me for the question period: the six or seven objections this lot will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are to come up and how much damage each would do, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them (so think benchmark results, compositional generalisation failures, whether "the core of cognition" is even an empirical claim or just a reframing, and the difference between retrieving a single nearest neighbour and mapping relational structure). For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and note where the reply is weakest or where I'd basically be hoping they don't follow up. Tell me which objections have real force against my specific wrinkle about language models rather than against Hofstadter generally, what I should concede up front to disarm the room, and what the one question is that I'm dreading. Write it as continuous prose, like a long email from a friend who knows the field: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
