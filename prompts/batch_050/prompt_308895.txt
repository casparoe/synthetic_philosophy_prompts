So a couple of grad students and me run this little interview series with faculty, and in two weeks we're sitting down with a metaphysician who defends resemblance class nominalism -- the view that there are no universals and no property-entities, just concrete particulars bundled into maximal classes of things that resemble one another. She's very sharp and has heard every intro-textbook objection a hundred times, so "but what do all red things have in common??" style openers will just bore her.

Act as a professional metaphysician who's reviewed journal submissions on properties and the problem of universals. Think carefully about this. Before drafting anything, brainstorm in bullet points where resemblance class nominalism is genuinely soft (not where the textbooks say it's soft), and then turn that into 8-10 interview questions.

Concretely, I want at least:

- one question on the fact that resemblance is always resemblance *in some respect*, and whether "respects" smuggle the universals right back in through the side door
- one on the old Russellian move that resemblance itself has to be a universal -- when she invokes the regress of resemblances resembling resemblances, the question should force her to say whether that regress is supposed to be vicious or benign and by what criterion we decide
- one on Goodman's imperfect community problem: classes where everything resembles something else in the class but there's nothing they all share, so the class-membership test and the intuitive property come apart
- one on coextensive but intuitively distinct properties (renate vs. cordate): same class of particulars, seemingly different properties. She'll almost certainly go modal here and talk about resemblance across possible worlds, so the follow-up needs to press what entitles a austere nominalist to all those otherworldly particulars in the first place
- and I'd like the last question to be about what she'd actually have to be shown, conceptually or empirically, to abandon the veiw

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, and where there's an obvious retreat position (e.g. "resemblance is just a primitive internal relation," or "the predicates do all the work"), a second follow-up waiting for that too. Keep the main questions short enough to say out loud without losing her -- save the machinery for the follow-ups. No softballs, no "what first drew you to metaphysics." And if two of my requested lines of attack turn out to collapse into the same objection, tell me which to merge instead of padding the list.
