I keep going around in circles on the nonidentity problem, so let me just dump my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and you tell me what principle, if any, I'm actually commited to.

Case 1 — a couple's doctor tells them: if you conceive this month, your kid will have a mild lifelong condition, say limited mobility in one arm, because of some temporary medication in the mother's system. Wait three months and it clears, and you'd have a healthy kid. They conceive now anyway because waiting is annoying. Verdict: wrong. Not monstrous, but wrong, and I even feel like the kid could legitimately complain about it later.

Case 2 — same couple, but the condition is permanent, any child they ever have will have it. They conceive, kid has a decent life. Verdict: totally fine, nobody is wronged.

Case 3 — a government picks between two energy policies. The cheaper one degrades things enough that, two centuries out, the people who then exist live notably worse lives than the different people who would have existed under the other policy (since the policy shifts who meets whom and which kids ever get conceived). They pick the cheap one to save a bit of money now. Verdict: clearly wrong. Wronger than Case 1, even though no specific person is worse off.

Case 4 — two public health programs, same budget. One treats 1,000 pregnant women so their fetuses don't develop a condition. The other screens women before conception and has them delay, so 1,000 different healthy children get born instead of the 1,000 who'd have had the condition. Verdict: the programs are equally good, funding one over the other is a wash. (I know some people think the second one is worse since nobody is cured of anything, but that just doesn't move me.)

Case 5 — a fertility clinic negligently mixes up embryos and a child is born with a condition that the properly screened embryo wouldn't have had. The parents are furious when they find out. Verdict: the parents' anger makes sense, but about the kid himself I genuinely can't decide whether he was wronged, since he wouldn't exist otherwise.

Case 6 — a very young woman knows that conceiving now means a kid raised with a checked-out partner and tight money, measurably harder life; waiting four years means a different kid with a much better life (this is basically Parfit's case). She conceives anyway. Verdict: she does something wrong, though I feel weird saying it because who exactly got harmed?

So: Cases 1, 3, 6 wrong; Case 2 fine; Case 4 a wash; Case 5 genuinely up in the air. The tension I can't resolve: in Cases 3 and 6 I want to say "wrong" even though the people who end up existing can't claim they'd have been better off — but in Case 2 I lean on exactly that kind of reasoning (the kid can't complain, so it's fine) to get my verdict. If "they can't complain" excuses Case 2, why doesn't it excuse Case 3? And if it doesn't excuse Case 3, am I forced to say Case 2 is wrong after all, which I really don't want to say?

Is there one principle that delivers all of these? Maybe something like: when the same number of people would exist either way you're required to pick the option where they're better off, but when different people or different numbers exist the person-affecting intuition just dissolves? Does that actually sort Case 4 (different kids, same number, wash) from Case 3 (different people, wrong)? Tell me which verdict I'd have to sacrifice for the cleanest overall theory, and what that theory then says about a case I haven't listed: choosing between having one child with a great life or twins with slightly-less-great-but-still-good lives, where the twins are different people from the one child.
