So I'm a PhD student (philosophy of science, but I hang out with the stats people) and I keep circling back to Bertrand's paradox and what it's actually supposed to have shown. Quick recap of where I think the debate sits: you ask for the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and you get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on whether you pick random endpoints, a random radius and point on it, or a random midpoint in the disk. Each parametrization looks maximally uninformative in its own terms. From this people drew wildly different conclusions: Keynes-era people said the principle of indifference is fine but only safe in discrete cases where the options come pre-individuated; others said it shows the principle is just inconsistent, full stop, and probability-as-logical-ignorance is dead; and then the Jaynes-ish camp says the original problem is simply underspecified and once you say HOW the chord is generated (like literally tossing straws at a circle drawn on the floor) the symmetry of that mechanism pins down one answer, so the paradox dissolves rather than refuting anything. And then there are frequentists who shrug and say the whole thing is a category error because probability isn't about ignorance anyway.

Here's what bugs me: this dispute is well over a century old and it doesn't feel closer to settled, and my working hypothesis is that that's because the participants are actually disagreeing about what probabilities attach to (physical generating mechanisms vs states of knowledge vs nothing at all), and the chord problem is just a proxy battlefield where that deeper disagreement can never be adjudicated because each side counts different things as a legitimate "solution". But I'm not confident in this. Maybe there IS a real mathematical question here — like whether requiring invariance under all and only the symmetries implicit in a problem statement yields uniqueness in general, or whether Jaynes's method smuggles in physical assumptions that the logic-of-ignorance view was trying to avoid — and the philosophy is the proxy.

And given your diagnosis, what would even count as progress — a theorem, a distinction everyone accepts, an experiment — or should we expect it to grind on forever?
