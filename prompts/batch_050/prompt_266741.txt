Ok so I just spent like three hours at the pub arguing with my friend Marcus about the money pump argument and I'm now convinced he accidentally proved that nobody is ever allowed to change their mind about anything. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it (we were two beers in):

(1) If you strictly prefer A to B right now, it's rational for you to pay some tiny amount, a cent say, to trade B for A.

(2) Whether a sequence of choices is rational is settled choice by choice: if each individual choice is rational at the moment you make it, then the overall pattern they add up to can't be irrational. There's no extra spooky "diachronic" rationality on top of that.

(3) Now suppose on Monday I prefer coffee to tea, so I pay a cent to swap my tea for coffee. Over the week my tastes drift, as tastes do, and by Friday I prefer tea again, so I pay a cent to swap back. Repeat for a year.

(4) Every single trade in (3) is rational by (1), so by (2) the whole pattern is rational.

(5) But the net result is I've paid 50-odd cents to end up with exactly the cup of tea I started with. And predictably paying money to end up where you started is the textbook case of irrationality, it's literally what the money pump argument is designed to rule out.

(6) Contradiction. Since (3) was just a description of ordinary harmless taste-change, Marcus concludes the problem must be with the taste-change itself: rational preferences don't drift.

(7) Generalizing: it's irrational to acquire a taste for wine, to stop liking the bands you liked at 15, to have your mind changed by a good argument. Personal growth is a coherence violation.

Obviously I want to deny (6) and (7). But here's the trap Marcus has laid: he says (2) and (5) are basically the foundations of decision theory. (5) is just the money pump intuition itself, and (2) is what lets us evaluate choices one at a time, which is the whole expected utility way of doing things. He says if I give up (5) then the original argument against cyclic preferences dies along with my coffee case, and if I give up (2) I've invented a weird new kind of rationality that judges whole lives instead of choices, and good luck formalizing that.

So: which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? And be specific about the damage: whatever fix you propose for the coffee/tea case, does the standard money pump argument against intransitive preferences survive it, or does that go down with the ship? I'd really like to keep the result that a rock-bottom cyclical preferrer is irrational, if that's at all salvageable.
