I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and at our annual reader festival I've been voluntold to run a 60-minute session called something like "Why your beliefs should add up." Room of about 25 attendees, smart general audience, mostly not quant people, nobody signed up for a lecture. I want to build the whole session around the Dutch book idea -- the fact that if your degrees of belief don't hang together probabilistically, someone can offer you a package of bets you'd happily accept one by one that together lose you money no matter how the world turns out -- but I want them to feel it happen to them, not hear me explain it at a whiteboard.

So: design me the game. The arc I'm imagining is that participants put their own odds on ordinary claims (sports, weather, celebrity stuff, things where nobody has expert knowledge to hide behind), a bookie -- me or a volunteer -- offers them small-stakes bets priced off thier own stated odds, and at some point at least a few of them realize that the bets they individually welcomed guarantee them a loss come what may. The trap needs to spring from their own numbers, not from me being slick or moving goalposts. Give me the actual rules, the materials, a payoff scheme that works without real money (or with stakes on the order of a fancy coffee), a rough timeline for the hour, and what I do in the moment when the penny drops.

Then the debrief: map what just happened to the structure of the Dutch book result, including whether there's a clean way to run a second round that gestures at the diachronic version where someone's beliefs shift over time and they get picked off by trading with themselves across rounds. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- people refusing to bet at all, everyone hiding behind 50/50, somebody deciding I've rigged the whole thing -- and how to handle each. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce and I'll have to just say in words: my sense is it shows you're exploitable in principle, not that anyone will actually bother exploiting you, and the reverse direction of the theorem (coherent credences mean no such book exists against you) seems basically unstageable. Correct me if I'm wrong on either.

Lead with the bottom line of the design in the first sentence, then the details -- no preamble, no restating my ask back to me.
