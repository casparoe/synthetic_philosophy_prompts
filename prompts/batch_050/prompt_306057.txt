When we settle a question by voting separately on each of the underlying reasons and then combining the results, we can end up collectively committed to a conclusion that a majority of us actually reject, or that contradicts what the individual votes logically imply. I've since gathered that this is a studied problem in how groups aggregate judgments, but I don't want to give my board a lecture; I want them to collide with it. Design an activity for our next meeting, about 40 minutes, that makes eleven people with no philosophy background feel the force of this: the rules should get them to produce the incoherent collective verdict with their own votes before anyone names what went wrong. Give me the setup and framing, the concrete propositions to vote on (drawn from a plausible cooperative decision, like whether to take on a major renovation loan, not an abstract example), the exact sequence of steps, and a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the difference between deciding by aggregating reasons and deciding by aggregating conclusions. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, members sensing the trap and coordinating around it, or insisting we just vote on the bottom line — and be honest about which feature of the real problem the game cannot reproduce and I would have to supply in words. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
