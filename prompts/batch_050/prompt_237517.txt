I work in public economics, mostly on mechanism design and optimal transfer programs, and I recently got into a long argument with a friend who studies nineteenth-century social policy. She claims the 1834 report of the Royal Commission into the Operation of the Poor Laws -- the document behind the New Poor Law, with its twin doctrines of less eligibility and the workhouse test -- was, whether its authors knew it or not, a piece of applied mechanism design, and that the moral language it is notorious for was just decorative wrapping on a screening device. I am not so sure, and I want to test her claim seriously rather than let it stand as a cocktail-party analogy.

The passage we kept circling is this one, where the report explains why relief should be offered only inside the workhouse:

"Into such a house none will enter voluntarily; work, confinement, and discipline, will deter the indolent and vicious; and nothing but extreme necessity will induce any to accept the comfort which must be obtained by the surrender of their free agency, and the sacrifice of their accustomed habits and gratifications."

Here is what I would like. First, restate the report's position, including the specific logic of that passage, in the vocabulary I actually work in: a principal, a population of agents with unobservable types, participation constraints, incentive compatibility, separating versus pooling allocations, self-selection through deliberately imposed non-pecuniary costs, single-crossing, and the outside option as a constraint on what the principal can offer. Be as precise as you can: say whether less eligibility is best understood as a participation constraint, an incentive-compatibility constraint, or something else; whether the workhouse is a screening technology and, if so, what property of it does the screening work; and whether the passage is making a selection claim (only the genuinely destitute will apply) or a treatment claim (the regime changes who people are), since those are very different objects in my framework. Second, and this is the part I care about most, give me the residue report: which elements of the 1834 position translate cleanly, which survive only under distortion, and which have no expression at all in the mechanism-design vocabulary -- I have in mind things like the moral taxonomy of "the indolent and vicious," the commissioners' evident belief that deterrence benefits the deterred by reforming their character, and the framing of confinement and loss of "free agency" as part of the price rather than a harm to be weighed. For each residue item, say whether the gap reveals a limitation of my framework (something a welfare economist genuinely cannot say and should want to) or a confusion in the original that the translation usefully exposes. Finally, tell me whether you think my friend's claim survives the exercise: is the report a screening device in moral dress, or does what fails to translate show that the moral theory was load-bearing all along?
