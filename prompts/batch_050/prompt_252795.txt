I'm a PhD student in theoretical computer science with a side interest in logic, and our reading group has been working through self-reference and formal models of agents that modify or extend themselves. Last week a colleague walked us into an argument that seems to show that no reasoning system can ever be justified in trusting its own outputs — not just a proposed self-improving AI, but any system whatsoever, including us. The conclusion strikes me as proving too much: if it's right, then a mathematician cannot justifiably rely on a lemma she proved last month, a proof assistant cannot rely on the kernel it already checked, and no one can sensibly precommit to following the conclusions they expect to reach tomorrow. But I've poked at it for days and can't find the step I'm willing to deny. Here it is:

(1) An agent is justified in relying on its own reasoning — or on the reasoning of a successor system it might design — only if it is in a position to assert the relevant reflection principle: if my system proves φ, then φ. Trust without such a principle, on this view, is just blind faith. (2) By Löb's theorem, any consistent formal system containing a modest amount of arithmetic that proves its own reflection principle for a given φ already proves φ outright; so such a system cannot consistently adopt the general schema "whatever I prove is true" — if it could, it would prove everything, including falsehoods. (3) Therefore no agent whose reasoning is captured by a sufficiently strong formal system can consistently satisfy the condition in (1) for its own system. (4) Any successor the agent might build, being at least as strong, faces the same obstacle, and the agent itself cannot certify the successor without already trusting its own reasoning; so there is no internal route to justified trust at any point in a chain of self-extensions. (5) All justification of reasoning is internal in this sense — there is no external vantage point an agent can step into without simply using its own reasoning again — so the obstacle is inescapable rather than a limitation of particular designs. (6) Hence justified self-trust is impossible in principle, and every apparent instance of it — in mathematics, in software verification, in everyday planning — is rationally ungrounded.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please explain why the rejected step looked plausible in the first place, and be concrete about the trade-offs. In particular, I'd like to know whether the argument equivocates between different senses of trust (say, uniform reflection versus reliance on particular instances), whether restricting reflection in some principled way (to a bounded number of levels, or to a weaker fragment of the system) is a genuine escape or just a relabeling of the problem, and whether treating self-trust as an empirical or inductive matter rather than a proof-theoretic one dissolves the obstacle or merely changes the subject. If you think the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what an agent built on the surviving premises would and would not be able to do — for instance, whether it could still rationally build a successor, follow its own earlier theorems, or precommit to a plan. If forced to choose, I'd rather keep (5) than (6), but I want to know what I'm paying either way.
