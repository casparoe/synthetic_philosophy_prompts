I’m a philosophy professor organizing a small roundtable on whether philosophy makes progress, and I keep noticing that the discussion stalls in the same way every time.

One group says the answer is obviously no: Plato’s basic problems are still with us, there’s no disciplinary convergence on free will, dualism, moral realism, utilitarianism, external-world skepticism, and so on, and unlike physics or biology there’s no settled body of results that later philosophers can simply build on.

The other group says this uses the wrong standard. On their view, philosophy makes plenty of progress by making distinctions, ruling out bad arguments, mapping logical space, identifying costs and benefits of views, and producing locally settled claims even when the big questions stay open. They point to things like the decline of naive verificationism, refinements in accounts of knowledge after Gettier-style cases, formal work on modality, and better taxonomies of positions. But their opponents respond that “better maps of options” are not the same as answers, and that philosophy can churn out distinctions endlessly without ever settling which distinction matters.

A third faction says both sides are being misled by the word progress. They think philosophy is more like mathematics or logic than empirical science: it can make real advances without producing consensus on first-order philosophical theories, because the advances often consist in new tools, impossibility results, or improved formulations of problems. The first group replies that this changes the subject, since tools are not philosophical knowledge unless they help us discover philosophical truths.

My tentative diagnosis is that this is partly a factual disagreement about how much convergence philosophy actually has, but mostly a disagreement about what counts as progress: truth-convergence, problem-transformation, increased precision, elimination of error, or explanatory usefulness. I also suspect the sides may be evaluating different units—individual claims, whole research programs, or the discipline as a whole—and then talking past each other. But I’m not sure whether that fully explains the impasse, or whether there’s a deeper disagreement about what philosophical inquiry is even supposed to deliver.

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement rather than just defending one side. Use the positions above concretely, identify which parts are substantive, verbal, methodological, or driven by different baseline expectations, and say what kind of evidence or argument could actually move each side. Also tell me what would count as progress in this debate itself: a distinction that needs to be introduced, a historical/empirical question that could be checked, a formal result, a change in the question, or maybe an argument that the dispute cannot be resolved because “progress” is unavoidably discipline-relative. I’d rather have a sharp diagnosis than a balanced survey of familiar opinions.
