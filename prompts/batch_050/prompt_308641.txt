I'm a second-year doctoral student, and my dissertation leans heavily on the notion of biological function. My advisor has taken to policing my use of the word: whenever I write that some trait "has the function of doing X," she tells me whether the attribution stands. I have a chapter draft riding on a case she hasn't ruled on yet, and I'd like to anticipate her verdict before our meeting.

Her rulings so far:

1. A healthy mammalian heart: pumping blood is a function; producing its rhythmic thump is not — the sound is a byproduct.
2. A fetal heart that, owing to a developmental defect, has never pumped and never will: it still has the function of pumping — that, she said, is precisely what is defective about it.
3. The vestigial pelvic bones of a whale: no function at all now. She called it a mistake to say their function is supporting locomotion, even though that is why whales have them.
4. A pebble that happened to lodge in a frog's nostril and, purely by luck, traps fungal spores, keeping this particular frog healthier than its neighbors: no function, however beneficial.
5. A synthetic heart grown from scratch in a laboratory to an engineer's blueprint, with no evolutionary provenance whatsoever: it does have the function of pumping blood.

The unrated case: a freak electrochemical accident in a marsh assembles, molecule for molecule, a duplicate of a living frog — a cousin of the familiar swampman scenario. The duplicate hops, catches flies, and its heart pumps. Does its heart have the function of pumping blood?

Please respond as a philosopher of biology fluent in the function debate — etiological or selected-effects accounts, causal-role and organizational accounts, and the various hybrids. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate rules that could be generating her five verdicts before committing to anything. Then: (a) state at least two rules consistent with all five rulings and what each predicts for the marsh duplicate; (b) give your best guess at her actual verdict, with reasons; (c) describe one further case, realistic or imagined, whose ruling would discriminate between your leading candidates.
