I've been arguing with a friend about what makes the "real" version of a thing matter, and I want to figure out what I actually think by going by my gut on cases. Here are my verdicts:

A perfect copy of a Vermeer, painted today with the same pigments and techniques, good enough to fool any expert -- I'd pay maybe a few thousand for it, while the original is worth tens of millions, and I don't think that's just snobbery on my part. A lossless digital copy of a master recording vs. the master tape itself: genuinely don't care, theyr'e the same thing as far as I'm concerned. A forged Civil War letter, content all fabricated: worthless to a museum, but totally fine as a prop in a high school history class, and I don't feel the kids are being cheated. A famous painting where over the centuries restorers have repainted more than half of what's now visible: still feels like the original to me, and I'd be upset if it burned down in a way I wouldn't be about the modern Vermeer copy. A bronze cast from the sculptor's original mold, but made thirty years after her death by her estate: feels like not-quite-the-real-thing, even though the mold is literally hers. A modern violin that beats Stradivarius violins in blind listening tests: I'd still rather own the Strad, and I don't think that's irrational. Buying a fake designer bag that's indistinguishable from the real one: feels slightly sleazy to me, like the buyer is doing something wrong, not just getting a deal.

So here's my problem. I think the copy-vs-master-tape case and the copy-vs-Vermeer case are pulling me in opposite directions -- in one I'm happy to say identical means identical value, in the other I'm clearly not. And the restoration case seems in tension with the bronze cast case: in one I accept that most of the physical stuff can be replaced and the thing survives, in the other I deny it even when the causal link to the artist is arguably tighter.

Is there a single principle that captures most of these verdicts -- something about the right kind of causal or historical connection to the maker, or about what kind of object the thing is meant to be -- and if so, state it precisely enough that I can see why the recording and the painting come out differently? If no clean principle gets all of them, tell me which verdict or two I should give up, and why those are the weakest. And whatever account you land on, tell me what it predicts about two cases I haven't listed: an AI-generated image that an artist trained entirely on her own work and then curated, and a ship built as an exact replica of a famous historical ship, plank by plank, and sailed as a museum piece.
