My roommate just started law school and we've been arguing about the blackmail puzzle for like a week now — the thing where it's perfectly legal to tell your friend's husband about the affair, and perfectly legal to ask people for money, but somehow it's a serious crime to offer to stay quiet in exchange for money. He keeps saying "it's just obviously coercive" and I keep pointing out that his own examples don't work, and we've both realized we don't actually know what a *good* answer to this would even have to look like. Everyone writing on it seems to start from whatever theory they already like and then declare victory.

So before I read any more proposed solutions, I want the spec first: write the desiderata that any adequate account of what's wrong (or criminalizable) about blackmail has to meet. For each requirement give the case or intuition behind it. Some things I'm pretty sure need to be on the list, but tell me what I'm missing: it obviously has to explain how two individually permissible acts combine into an impermissible one, which is the core puzzle; it has to not condemn the legal lookalikes, like a prosecutor threatening to charge you unless you plead guilty, a union threatening to strike unless wages go up, or me saying I'll stop lending you my car unless you chip in for gas; it should say something about cases where the victim would actually *prefer* to pay rather than have the secret out, which makes the harm story weird; and it probably has to handle the symmetry with NDAs, where being paid to stay quiet is a normal legal contract but demanding payment for the same silence is extortion. Also worth saying whether the theory needs to treat true-information blackmail and lies the same or differently, and whether "warnings" vs "threats" is a distinction the account has to respect or can dissolve.

The part I most want: flag which of these desiderata are genuine constraints that any theory must satisfy and which are really just prejudices of one camp (e.g. people who think the law should track moral wrongness vs. people who think criminalization is purely about preventing harm). And if some subset of the requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — like maybe you can't both condemn classic blackmail and license plea bargaining without smuggling in a distinction about who has a right to the information — say so explicitly, because that would itself be a result. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out existing accounts (I suspect the lookalikes requirement murders a lot of them). You can briefly run a couple of the standard moves — coercion accounts, rights-based accounts, efficiency arguments — against the list, but keep that short; the requirements and their justifications are the deliverable.
