I'm a grad student who helps run my department's little philosophy-and-technology colloquium series, and I've been roped into moderating the Q&A portion this semester. Our next guest is a philosopher who defends so-called griefbots — chatbots trained on a dead person's texts, emails, voice notes and so on, which grieving relatives can keep "talking to". Her rough position is that conversing with such a simulation isn't inherently morbid or deceptive and can be a genuinely valuable continuation of the relationship, morally on a par with rereading someone's letters or replaying their voicemails, just more interactive. I want to push her properly rather than let her restate the view, and the discussion after a recent talk got pretty heated (someone called the whole industry a séance with a subscription fee), so I'd like to be ready.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I'd like the set to cover at least these pressure points: the consent problem (the dead person never agreed to be simulated, and can't be asked now), whether an informed user is nonetheless engaged in a kind of self-deception, the fact that the bot can say things the deceased never would have — including, say, "I forgive you" — and who is responsible for that, the worry that engagement-maximizing companies will deliberately stretch out grief rather than help resolve it, and what happens to the "relationship" if the startup folds or the model gets updated and the simulated personality drifts. For the follow-ups, anticipate the obvious replies (e.g., "we don't ask consent for biographies or memoirs either", or "the user knows it's not really them") and press past them to the second move. Avoid generic softballs about how she got interested in the topic — every question should turn on the actual content of the position. Also, one definitional question somewhere would help, since she slides between "tool for mourning" and "continuation of the person" and I suspect those imply very different ethical constraints.
