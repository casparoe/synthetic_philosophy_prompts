I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on philosophy of language, and I've been roped into TA-ing an undergraduate course on philosophy of mind next term. In preparing a week on Ryle, I've become genuinely stuck on a prior question that I need to sort out for myself before I can teach it: what exactly makes something a category mistake, as opposed to merely false, merely odd, or merely metaphorical? Ryle clearly thought the Cartesian picture -- the mind as a ghostly inner thing steering the bodily machine -- rested on one, on a par with the visitor who is shown the colleges, libraries, and playing fields and then asks where the university is. But when I try to state the demarcation principle myself, my own verdicts on cases won't sit still. So here they are, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

My verdicts: "The number four is drowsy" is a category mistake, not a falsehood -- it isn't even a candidate for truth. "Saturday is in bed" is likewise a category mistake, and notably its negation seems just as bad. "My headache weighs two kilograms" strikes me as a category mistake, even though "my headache lasted two hours" is perfectly fine, which suggests duration belongs to headaches in a way weight doesn't. "The theory of evolution is jealous" is a category mistake; "the theory of evolution is widely accepted" is not. But now the trouble starts. "The city sleeps" predicates of a city something only animals can literally do, yet nobody would call it a category mistake -- it's a live, almost dead, metaphor -- while "the number four is drowsy" seems to differ from it only in how charitable we're willing to be, and that can't be the right criterion. Worse: "my belief that it will rain is located three inches behind my eyes" strikes me as a category mistake, yet "the pain is located in my left foot" is fine, and I can't see why spatial location should apply to one mental item and not the other without begging the very question Ryle is raising. And finally, "the average taxpayer has 2.4 children" seems perfectly acceptable to me on its natural statistical reading, while "the average taxpayer is expecting a baby" is absurd -- but it's the same subject term, so either the principle must be sensitive to predicates rather than category-frames, or I have to give up one of these.

What I'd like from you: a principle (or a couple of serious candidates, stated precisely) that sorts category mistakes from plain falsehoods, from live metaphors, and from fine sentences, with each clause motivated by one of the cases above; an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which one you'd advise me to drop if forced -- I suspect the belief-location case is the weak link, but I'm open to being told it's the metaphor one; and the principle's verdict on two cases I haven't listed, one of your choosing and this one: "the committee changed its mind." Lastly, the payoff question: does your best principle actually vindicate Ryle's diagnosis that Cartesian dualism rests on a category mistake, or does it reveal that his claim was itself looser than the phenomenon he named? If the principle can't deliver his conclusion without also convicting ordinary talk of beliefs and pains, I'd like to know that too.
