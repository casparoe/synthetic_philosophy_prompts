I am drafting a campus novel set at a university that has adopted an unusually strict rule on AI-assisted writing. Students may use language models to brainstorm, translate, summarize sources, or suggest revisions, but before submitting an essay they must sign an attestation stating, in effect, that they understand every sentence in the final text and could explain or defend it if questioned. The disciplinary board’s guidance says that using a model is not itself misconduct; misconduct begins when the student becomes, in the board’s phrase, a conduit for prose they could not inhabit. In one scene I am planning, a brilliant but lazy student uses an AI system to produce a flawless paper in formal semantics. Under questioning, however, he can reproduce the argument only because he has memorized a decision tree for responding to likely objections. He answers correctly every time, yet the board is divided over whether he understands what he is saying.

What philosophical commitments does this policy presuppose? In particular, does it assume that understanding is a matter of counterfactual abilities—being able to answer novel questions, notice implications, recognize counterexamples, and revise the text under pressure—or does it quietly require something more interior, such as semantic grasp, intentionality, or the right kind of causal connection between symbols and what they mean? If the student’s memorized decision tree became comprehensive enough that no examiner could distinguish him from a genuine expert, would the board have to concede understanding, or would it be committed to a Chinese Room–style distinction between merely operating a rule-following interface and actually meaning what one says? I am especially interested in whether the policy can avoid taking sides on consciousness while still making sense of its distinction between assistance and substitution.

I would also like help making the policy internally consistent. The same university permits spell-checkers, calculators, citation managers, statistical software, and heavy line-editing by mentors, yet treats generative AI as categorically different. What theory of authorship would justify that boundary: is the relevant variable understanding, causal responsibility, answerability, effort, or the preservation of a particular relationship between mind and sentence? Does the policy presuppose that meaning must originate in the student, or only that the student must be able to appropriate and stand behind meaning wherever it originated? Finally, if the board replaced its current test with a rigorous oral examination in which the student could defend the paper perfectly, which parts of its implicit theory would have to be abandoned—and which could remain?
