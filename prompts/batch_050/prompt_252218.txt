Two friends of mine got into a surprisingly heated argument at a board game night last weekend and it's been living rent-free in my head since, so I want an outside verdict. Some context: I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and my brain keeps reaching for a distributed systems analogy here (no shared randomness, no unique node IDs, how do you break symmetry?), though I can't tell if that analogy clarifies things or just imports my biases. The setup: one of them described this game, I think originally from some Hofstadter column? 25 strangers each get taken to a seperate room, and each can press a button once. If exactly one person presses, that person gets $25,000. If zero press, or two or more press, nobody gets anything. No communication, and everyone is told the full rules, including that all 25 players heard the exact same explanation.

Maya's position: the rational move is to use your own randomness and press with probability 1/25. Her reasoning was that everyone is in an identical epistemic situation with no way to coordinate, so whatever reasoning leads you to your decision probably leads the others to theirs, and the best symmetric policy is p = 1/25, since that's what maximizes the chance that exactly one person presses. Dev's position: this eats itself the moment you take it seriously, because *given* that the other 24 are each rolling with p = 1/25, just pressing for sure gives you something like a 37% shot at the full prize, which crushes the expected value of mixing, so the "rational" policy instructs you to abandon it the instant it becomes common knowledge that others follow it. Maya shot back that if his reasoning compells him to deviate, it compells everyone to deviate, and then all 25 press and everyone gets nothing, so the right question is "what should *we* all do," not "what should I do holding everyone else fixed." Dev called that cheating: you're assuming the other 24 are perfect copies of you, but they're random strangers, some will press out of greed or spite, some will never press, and symmetry is an empirical bet about the population, not a law of logic. He also mentioned that in the actual lottery thing Hofstadter ran, people mailed in absurd numbers of entries instead of cooperating, which suggests the symmetry assumption is empirically false anyway. Maya's comeback, and this is where they started talking over each other, was that she doesn't need to assume everyone's a copy of her, just that some non-trivial fraction might reason the same way, and since her only realistic hope of winning runs through that fraction, she might as well optimize for the world where it exists. Dev said that makes it religion, not decision theory, and that the honest answer is the game has no rational solution for an isolated player, like asking 25 people to name the same city with no communication: the only equilibria are asymmetric and unreachable, so both of them were pretending a broken question had a clean answer.

Who's actually right here? I genuinely couldn't tell, they both seemed to land real hits. I'd like a point-by-point verdict: is Maya's 1/25 policy defensible as "the rational move," or is Dev right that it's self-undermining? Is Dev right that the game simply has no answer, or is that giving up too early? And is there anything both of them are missing, e.g. whether the right move changes if you expect a mix of agent types in the pool, or whether the "optimize for the world where others reason like me" move is doing more work than Maya admits?
