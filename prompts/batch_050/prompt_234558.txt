As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I have repeatedly seen discussions of ergodicity economics run into the same impasse. One side argues that conventional expected-value reasoning evaluates an opportunity as though its outcomes were averaged across parallel copies of the decision-maker, while an individual living through a multiplicative process should instead care about long-run growth along a single trajectory. On this view, a gamble can have positive expected value and still be ruinous in the relevant sense, especially when losses compound or eliminate future participation. The other side replies that ordinary expected utility theory already captures aversion to ruin through the shape of the utility function, that growth-optimal betting is a special case rather than a replacement for decision theory, and that nothing follows about what an agent should value merely from the difference between an ensemble average and a time average. A third position treats the whole dispute as mainly model selection: sometimes wealth is additive, sometimes multiplicative; sometimes losses are pooled, sometimes not; sometimes ruined agents are replaced, sometimes they are not.

My tentative diagnosis is that the debate has stalled because participants combine three separable questions. The first is mathematical: under a specified process, which quantity determines long-run performance? The second is empirical and institutional: which real decisions resemble repeated multiplicative gambles with absorbing barriers, and which are effectively pooled across people, projects, or independent trials? The third is normative: whose continuation matters—the individual, a family, an organization, a generation, or some larger population—and what losses count as irreversible for that entity?

To make the second and third questions more precise, I have been using a notion I call continuation structure: the rules determining whether an actor remains in the game after a bad outcome and whether lost capacity can be restored through savings, insurance, transfers, new entrants, institutional support, or future opportunities. A casino bankroll, a person risking permanent disability, a venture fund backing many startups, and a government choosing policies for successive cohorts all have different continuation structures. My hypothesis is that many apparent disagreements about ergodicity are actually disagreements about continuation structure, with the mathematics doing less normative work than either side admits.

Please assess that hypothesis and diagnose the disagreement. I am not asking for a survey of ergodicity economics or a history of its terminology. Which parts of the dispute are substantive, which are verbal, and which conceal an empirical question that neither mathematics nor introspection can settle? Where does each side make an unjustified transition from a formal result to a practical prescription, or from an appealing practical case to a general decision principle? What would the opposing sides each count as evidence against their position?

Finally, say what genuine progress would look like. Would it require a theorem stating more exact sufficient conditions, a taxonomy of decision environments, empirical evidence about ruin and recovery, better models of utility over survival and agency, or a replacement of the original question with several narrower ones? If you think no resolution should be expected because the camps are optimizing for different units of persistence, explain why—and give the strongest reason for thinking that this pluralism is not merely an evasion.
