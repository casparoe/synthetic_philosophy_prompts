I’m a journalist who covers science and big ideas, and my newsroom keeps running into claims about heritability: intelligence is 50% heritable, political orientation is partly inherited, depression has a genetic component, and so on. Editors love these lines because they sound precise, but they also invite the usual disasters: treating heritability as if it means a trait is fixed, using a within-population estimate to explain differences between populations, or acting as though a high heritability estimate means changing the environment will not matter. I understand the textbook definition well enough, but I need something I can actually use under deadline, not another conceptual overview.

Please turn the concept of heritability into a practical desk procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes whenever a press release, researcher, or op-ed makes a heritability claim. Assume I usually cannot inspect the original study in depth and often cannot get a numerical answer to every question. Make it a numbered checklist or flowchart in words, with branching questions that distinguish at least: whether a heritability estimate is being reported at all; what population and environment it was estimated in; whether the claim concerns variation, individual destiny, group differences, or responsiveness to intervention; whether the estimate comes from twin, adoption, family, or molecular-genetic methods; and whether assumptions such as shared environments, assortative mating, measurement error, gene-environment correlation, or gene-environment interaction could change the interpretation. Include simple rules for what wording is safe in copy, what wording needs qualification, and what claims should not survive editing.

I’d like the procedure to force an answer even when the evidence is incomplete, while clearly marking where it has merely converted uncertainty into an editorial judgment. Apply it to three worked cases: a twin study saying a common mental-health condition is 40% heritable; a genome-wide association study explaining 8% of variation in educational attainment; and an advocacy piece arguing that because intelligence is substantially heritable, school funding differences probably do not matter. Make at least one application produce an editorial decision I will not like, such as killing or heavily qualifying an attractive statistic. End with a short list of questions the procedure cannot settle and a few headline/dek formulas that avoid the standard mistakes. Keep it usable by a smart generalist editor who knows basic statistics but is definately not a behavioral geneticist.
