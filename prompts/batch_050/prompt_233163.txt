I'm a high school student in a philosophy club, and there's a move I keep seeing in our debates that I don't have a word for. When we test a moral principle against a case — say, the principle that lying is always wrong, set against lying to protect a friend from harm — whichever side of the conflict someone likes less is the side they revise. A person attached to the principle will say the gut reaction to the case is unreliable; a person who trusts the gut reaction will carve an exception into the principle. Each individual revision looks reasonable, but the choice of *what* to revise seems to track the conclusion the person wanted all along, and after several rounds everyone ends up roughly where they started, now equipped with a tidy theory that agrees with them. My question has three parts. First, does the back-and-forth method itself — adjusting general principles and case-by-case reactions against each other until they fit — have an established name, and how is the legitimate version of it supposed to work? Second, does the corrupted version, where the direction of revision is quietly set by the desired conclusion, have a name of its own, and how does it differ from ordinary stubbornness or confirmation bias? Third, if the corrupted version has no name, what would you call it, how would you define it, and what would count as a borderline case? Please answer in American English.
