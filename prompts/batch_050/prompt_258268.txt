My reading group just finished a popular book on global inequality, and one member circulated her notes summarizing the book's central argument as follows: "For most of history, people explained national wealth by geography: tropical countries are poor because heat and disease sap the energy and health of their populations. Modern social science has effectively settled the debate against this view in favor of institutions. The decisive evidence is the reversal of fortune — the civilizations that were richest in 1500, like the Aztec and Inca empires, are poor today, while the sparse settler colonies of North America and Australia became rich, and since geography is fixed, only a change in institutions can explain the flip. The natural experiment of the two Koreas then clinches it: an identical people, with an identical culture and climate, split by a border, with one side rich and one side destitute — which proves that culture and geography are causally inert. The practical upshot is that development is a solvable engineering problem: replace extractive institutions with inclusive ones that protect property rights and enforce contracts, and growth follows more or less automatically, which is why the authors are optimistic that institutional reform is a reliable recipe for ending poverty."

She's inclined to find this convincing, and I have to admit it has a tidy logic to it. But I suspect it's tidier than the actual state of knowledge. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence? For each claim: what would a careful development economist or economic historian accept as accurate; what would they flag as a misrepresentation — for instance, is the geography camp being described the way its serious modern defenders would describe it, and does the reversal of fortune do the logical work the summary assigns it; does the Korea comparison show what it's said to show, or something weaker; and is the leap from "institutions matter enormously" to "institutional reform is a reliable recipe" one the evidence actually supports? Finally, setting the summary aside: given that history gives us one unrepeatable run of the world, with institutions, geography, and culture all entangled and plausibly causing each other, how much confidence should we have in *any* answer to why some nations are rich — and what does responsible causal reasoning about this even look like?
