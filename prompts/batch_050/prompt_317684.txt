At a recent department meeting about whether philosophers have any obligation to engage in political work, a senior colleague closed the discussion by remarking that the arc of the moral universe is long but bends toward justice -- his apparent conclusion being that we can trust matters to come right in the end and need not agonize over strategy. The sentence has bothered me ever since, because it seems to be doing several jobs at once, and I doubt all of them survive scrutiny. What I would like is a careful disambiguation of the slogan as it is commonly repeated.

Could you lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims this one sentence might be expressing? I have in mind, at a minimum, the following candidate readings, though you should feel free to add or subdivide: an empirical generalization about the long-run historical record; a probabilistic claim that justice tends to prevail without any guarantee; a teleological claim that history has a direction or destination built into it, whether providential or dialectical; a causal-structural claim that certain institutions or social mechanisms reliably generate moral progress under the right conditions; a purely normative exhortation that is really an imperative in predictive clothing; and a consolatory or motivational device whose value lies in sustaining activists rather than in being true.

For each reading, please tell me: whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false; what evidence or argument bears on it; and -- this is what I most care about -- whether it would actually license the quietist use my colleague made of it. My suspicion is that only the strongest teleological reading supports his inference, and that this is precisely the reading no one is prepared to defend explicitly, but I would like to know if I am wrong.

Two further points interest me. First, what work is the word 'long' doing? It strikes me that the length qualifier may insulate the claim against counterevidence -- every setback can be redescribed as a local fluctuation on the way to the bend -- and I would like to know whether that makes the slogan unfalsifiable on any of its readings, and whether that matters. Second, if the slogan functions best as an exhortation rather than a prediction, is there anything intellectually dishonest about continuing to utter it in its predictive form, given that the predictive form is what rhetorically settles arguments?
