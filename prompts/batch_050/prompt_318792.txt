I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator is a man in the early stages of a progressive dementia, and the whole architecture of the plot — whether he is still bound by a marriage vow, still guilty of an old wrong, still the father his daughter visits — depends on taking seriously the thought that a person is, at bottom, a thread of remembered experience. A philosophically trained friend, who read the draft, told me the thought cannot be taken seriously because it collapses on its own terms, and he wrote out the collapse for me as follows. (1) A later person is the same person as an earlier one only if the later can remember, from the inside, some experience of the earlier, or is joined to the earlier by an unbroken chain of such rememberings — the chain being the standard repair, since the old general who has forgotten the boy he was is still the boy, because the young officer he once was remembered the boy and the general remembers the officer. (2) A chain is only as strong as its links, and each link, once lost, is lost forever: if you have forgotten your seventh birthday by thirty, you cannot newly remember it at forty. (3) Every day, some memories of the distant past are permanently lost, and none are formed to replace them. (4) So the number of links joining me now to me at seven strictly decreases over time, and since it began finite, it eventually reaches zero. (5) When it reaches zero, the chain is broken and, by (1), a new person begins. (6) Hence every ordinary human life is a relay of distinct persons handing off a body, and long marriages, old debts, and belated punishments attach to persons who no longer exist. (7) That is absurd, so the memory criterion in (1) — and with it any psychological account of personal identity — must be false.

His conclusion would gut my book, and I suspect the argument proves too much rather than too little, but I have reread it a dozen times and cannot locate the slip. Each premise looks modest: (1) is just the memory criterion with the standard repair for the brave-officer case already built in, so it cannot be dismissed as the naive version; (2) and (3) seem like plain facts about how forgetting actually works; and the arithmetic of (4) seems beyond reproach. Something has gone wrong — I would like to keep the memory criterion, since my narrator's predicament only has dramatic weight if his fear of ceasing to be himself is intelligible on its own terms — but I do not want to keep it by cheating. Tell me which step you reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs: in particular, whether the repair you offer merely saves the letter of the memory criterion while conceding that my narrator, at the end of the book, is no longer the man who made the vow — and if so, whether the friend has won the point that matters even while losing the argument.
