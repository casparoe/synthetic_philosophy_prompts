I am testing whether pessimism about moral testimony — the view that there is something defective about forming moral beliefs simply by taking someone's word for it — can be defended on grounds that survive the standard optimist rejoinders. Construct a thought experiment in which a hearer arrives at a moral belief purely by deference to a testifier, the resulting belief is true, the testifier is sincere and reliable, and yet the deference strikes the reader as clearly objectionable — subject to the following constraints.

1. The defect must not be a failure of understanding. The hearer should grasp why the moral claim is true — the reasons behind it, the trade-offs involved, the kinds of cases to which it does and does not extend — as well as anyone could. So the case cannot be a variant of the standard complaint that testimony delivers belief without the ability to explain, justify, or apply the belief flexibly to new situations.

2. The defect must not be epistemic risk. Make the testifier as reliable as you like and give the hearer overwhelming evidence of her track record; keep the stakes such that being wrong would not be catastrophic.

3. The defect must not be second-personal in the obvious way: the testifier's claim should not concern what the hearer owes to the testifier herself, and the case should not trade on the idea that certain moral demands lose their force unless made in person by the person wronged.

4. It must not be a role-obligation case: no judges, jurors, referees, or others who are institutionally required to reach the verdict by their own lights.

5. One paragraph, realistic setting — a family, a hospital, a workplace; no science fiction, and preferably not a philosophy classroom. The judgment that the deference is objectionable should fall out of the situation as described, not be asserted in a closing sentence.

After the scenario: identify which feature of the case is generating the intuition; state the general principle that this feature instantiates; and show that the principle is doing work the standard explanations — understanding-deficit, unreliability, second-personal authority, institutional role — cannot do, since all of those have been controlled for. Then say what happens to the intuition if the feature is removed while everything else is held fixed, and how an optimist about moral testimony would most plausibly attack the case. Finally, be honest about whether the case shows something about testimony per se, or only about the particular moral relationship you have built into the scenario.
