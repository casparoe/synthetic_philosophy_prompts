I'm a PhD student (philosophy of mind, though I keep getting dragged into AI safety stuff) and a collegue from the ML group hit me with an argument over coffee that I can't accept but also can't crack. His conclusion: LLMs literally cannot deceive anyone, full stop. It goes like this:

(1) X deceives Y only if X intentionally brings about, or at least tries to bring about, a false belief in Y.
(2) Intentionally doing something requires beliefs about what you're doing plus some conative state that fixes it as your end.
(3) An LLM has neither. It's a function trained to minimize next-token loss; saying it "believes the evaluator is watching" is shorthand for patterns in the weights, not a state that could play the role (2) demands.
(4) So an LLM can't deceive. It can only be used to deceive, the way a forged letter is used -- the deception, if any, belongs to the forger, or in this case to the developer or deployer.
(5) Same goes for the spooky cases: a model that reliably underperforms exactly when it has cues that it's being evaluated (sandbagging) isn't deceiving anyone, it's just an input-output pattern that makes us nervous.
(6) So "deceptive alignment" is a category error. There's AI-facilitated human deception, and there are misleading artifacts, but no deceptive AI.

He also bites the bullet on camouflage: peppered moths don't deceive birds, biologists are just speaking loosely. So the argument seems to prove that deception requires something almost no candidate deceiver actually has, which feels like proving too much.

Here's my problem: I definately want to keep (3), I'm allergic to cheap belief-talk about models. But (6) seems false -- if sandbagging isn't deception I don't know what is. So which premise do I give up, why did it look plausible in the first place, and what does each exit cost? Specifically: if I weaken (1) by dropping the intentionality requirement, where do I redraw the line so that sandbagging counts as deception but a cloudy mirror doesn't (and where does that leave the moths)? And if instead I go functionalist about (2)/(3), what work do beliefs and desires have to do inside the model for the attribution to be more than a metaphor -- and does that route end up collapsing (3) anyway, which is the thing I was trying to protect?
