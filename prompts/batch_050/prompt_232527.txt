I built the following argument for a philosophy seminar and now distrust it, because the conclusion is one I cannot accept. (1) The doctrine of double effect forbids bringing about harm one intends, whether as an end or as a means, but permits harm that is merely foreseen, so long as it is not disproportionate to the good aimed at. (2) The doctrine therefore does all of its distinctive work through the distinction between intended and merely foreseen effects. (3) What an agent intends is fixed by her plan as she conceives it: she intends the effects she aims at, and merely foresees the effects she expects but does not aim at. (4) For any harm an agent knowingly brings about, there is a description of her plan under which she aims only at something that inevitably accompanies the harm: the terror bomber aims at breaking enemy morale -- or at the civilians' being believed dead, or at their being rendered unavailable to the war economy -- and merely foresees that they die; the surgeon who crushes the fetus's skull to save the mother aims at reducing the head's diameter and merely foresees the death. (5) So whenever an agent is willing to describe her plan finely enough, nothing she does counts as intended harm. (6) Therefore the intention condition never rules out anything the proportionality condition allows: double effect collapses into a bare proportionality test, and terror bombing is morally on a par with tactical bombing that foreseeably kills the same number of civilians. I take (6) to be absurd, so the argument proves too much. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost -- in particular, what does your preferred fix imply for genuinely hard pairs like the Loop variant of the trolley case versus the standard Bystander case? If forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) and the intended/foreseen distinction than preserve the argument, but I want to know the price. Finally, please say what would change your mind: which case or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where your own reasoning about intention is most likely to break down.
