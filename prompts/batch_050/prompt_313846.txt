I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of biology course, and our unit on biological individuality has ended up being the one I can't stop thinking about. Early in the unit we read Julian Huxley's 1912 book The Individual in the Animal Kingdom, and the passage that stuck with me is his treatment of colonial forms like the siphonophores: colonies whose members have become so specialized and so integrated that the whole thing swims, feeds, and reproduces like a single animal, while the members themselves have degenerated into something closer to organs. Huxley's point, as our seminar took it, is that neither the members nor the colony comfortably claims the title of the individual, and that our ordinary criteria (one continuous body, one life, one reproductive career) pull apart exactly here. Later in the unit we read recent work on holobionts and the hologenome, and I was struck by how the same puzzle reappears in the mirror: instead of parts fusing into a whole, we have a whole -- me -- dissolving into a consortium of host plus microbes, where the "I" that gets sick, digests, and possibly even craves things is arguably a many rather than a one.

Our instructor closes each unit with an informal session where someone can bring in a creative response instead of a discussion question, and I'd like to write a sestina. I chose the sestina because of its machinery: the same six end-words recur in a fixed rotating order across six stanzas, and then all six must appear together in the three-line envoi. I want that machinery to do the philosophical work. The six end-words should be something like I, we, host, guest, boundary, and one (feel free to adjust if a near-synonym scans better), so that the form keeps forcing the parts back into relation whether the poem wants them there or not. The middle stanzas should move through the two mirror-image cases: the siphonophore whose members are becoming organs, and the human holobiont whose body is coming apart into partners, and they should get the biology right enough that someone who has read Huxley or the holobiont literature would recognize the cases rather than just the vocabulary. Then the envoi -- the one place in the form where all six words must coexist in three lines -- should be where the poem faces the question head-on: does gathering all these terms into a single utterance make them one individual, or does it just show that grammar can force what nature leaves undecided? I'd like the envoi to take a stand or to refuse one on purpose, not to drift off into vagueness.

A few constraints: strict sestina form (six sestets plus the envoi, correct end-word rotation), no rhyme requirement, and no explicitly naming Huxley, siphonophores, or microbiomes in the poem itself -- I want a reader who knows the debate to be able to tell which stanzas belong to which case purely from how the imagery treats the end-words. If you think the envoi is the wrong place for the verdict and the poem's structure suggests a better one, do that instead and tell me why.
