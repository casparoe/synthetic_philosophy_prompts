After years of reading the rationalist blogs and forums, I keep encountering the line that elegance is evidence of truth — usually phrased as "beauty is a guide to truth" — deployed as though it were an established theorem rather than a slogan. The latest instance was a forum argument about two rival theories in a mature scientific field. I will keep the details generic, since the specific field is not doing any work: Theory A is a patchwork of fitted parameters, special cases, and ad hoc corrections, but it reproduces all the existing data; Theory B is short, symmetric, and derives most of the same results from two principles, yet makes no newly confirmed predictions. A commenter argued that B should receive most of our credence on the grounds that elegance is evidence, and the conversation effectively ended there, as if that settled it.

I suspect the slogan conceals several quite different claims of wildly varying strength, and I would like them separated. Please enumerate the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered from most trivial to most radical. To gesture at the range I have in mind: at one end, something like the claim that simplicity is a legitimate term in one's prior, formalizable along minimum-description-length lines; somewhere in the middle, the claim that our sense of mathematical beauty is a compressed record of which structural patterns have repeatedly turned out to be explanatory, so that the aesthetic reaction functions as a kind of cached evidence; further out, the claim that deep mathematical structures recur across successful physics in a way that makes structural beauty an independent signal even where past track record is thin; and at the far end, something almost mystical, where aesthetic pleasure is treated as near-perceptual access to truth itself. Do not feel bound by my candidate readings — replace or subdivide them if you think I have carved things wrongly.

For each reading, give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, genuinely contentious, or actually false. Then answer the question that matters for the argument I witnessed: which of these readings, if any, is strong enough to license the commenter's move of assigning B most of the credence despite its lack of new confirmations? In doing so, take seriously the standard counterweight — the graveyard of beautiful theories that turned out false, and the ugly theories that turned out true — and say whether the reading the move requires survives that counterweight or quietly assumes that this case is an exception. Finally, give your best guess as to what people who reach for this slogan actually mean when they use it to close an argument, since I suspect the operative meaning is rarely the strongest defensible one.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — no preamble, and do not restate the question — and then lay out the readings and verdicts as a list. Wherever your judgments involve real epistemic uncertainty, say how confident you are, and at the end single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
