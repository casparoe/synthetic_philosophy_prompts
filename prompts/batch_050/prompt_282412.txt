As a novelist, I am sketching a character whose ruin arrives in installments: a device pays him a handsome sum for each notch he advances on a dial, where adjacent settings differ imperceptibly in the pain they deliver but the far end is agony. Each turn is trivially defensible; the sequence is catastrophic. I want the book's moral architecture to rest on a genuine philosophical puzzle rather than a literary conceit, so before I commit to a plot, I want the specification. Write the desiderata for a theory of rational choice adequate to this predicament: must it locate an irrational step somewhere in the sequence, or may it indict the whole while absolving every part? Must it preserve transitivity of preference, and what does it owe us if it doesn't? Must it handle the vagueness — that no notch is the one where comfort becomes suffering? Must it distinguish what it's rational to prefer from what it's rational to do at each moment, and say what a rational agent does midway down the dial? Give the consideration behind each requirement, flag any pair you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, and tell me which single requirement eliminates the most contenders. A brief scorecard of the main positions is welcome but secondary.
