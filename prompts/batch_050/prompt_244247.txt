Exam question: State the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism about normative language. Explain why it is a problem about the meaning of normative sentences rather than their truth-value, and assess whether it amounts to a refutation of expressivism or merely a challenge expressivists must meet.

Student's answer: Expressivism holds that uttering a normative sentence like "Stealing is wrong" is not stating a belief but expressing a con-attitude, such as disapproval of stealing. Frege's original objection to this view, later developed by Geach, concerns contexts where normative sentences occur unasserted. Consider: (1) If stealing is wrong, then getting one's little brother to steal is wrong; (2) Stealing is wrong; (3) so, getting one's little brother to steal is wrong. In (2), on the expressivist account, the speaker expresses disapproval. But in the antecedent of (1), no one asserts that stealing is wrong or disapproves of it; the conditional is compatible with thinking stealing permissible. So "stealing is wrong" cannot express disapproval there. Yet unless it means the same thing in both occurrences, the argument equivocates and is invalid. Because the difficulty arises from embedding, it concerns what the sentence means across contexts, not whether it is true. The problem generalizes to negation: in "Stealing is not wrong" no disapproval is expressed either, so on the expressivist account this sentence must express approval of stealing, which misrepresents someone who is merely undecided or tolerant. Hence negation recreates the same difficulty as the conditional. Frege–Geach is therefore a genuine refutation, not a mere challenge: a theory that renders ordinary modus ponens invalid is false of our practice, and the usual replies—treating embedded clauses as expressing attitudes anyway, or invoking deflationary truth—either abandon the theory's core claim or change the subject, since deflating truth does nothing to explain how "wrong" makes the same semantic contribution in asserted and unasserted contexts.
