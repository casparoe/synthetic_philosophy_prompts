I'm a PhD student in comparative philosophy, working on a dissertation about moral emotions in East Asian thought, and I help run our department's graduate colloquium series. Next month we're hosting a scholar whose new monograph defends a reconstruction of Yi Hwang's (T'oegye's) side of the Four-Seven debate: she argues that his mature doctrine of mutual issuance should be read not as the incoherent claim that principle (li) literally acts or moves, but as a thesis about two distinct generative pathways through which feelings can arise — one in which a moral emotion is occasioned directly by one's apprehension of a morally salient situation, and one in which an emotion wells up from the psycho-physical endowment — and that this preserves a real distinction between the Four Beginnings and the Seven Emotions without violating the Cheng-Zhu commitment that principle is inert. I'd like you to act as an expert in Korean neo-Confucianism and in contemporary philosophy of emotion, and help me prepare a rigorous question set. Before drafting, please brainstorm the main pressure points a well-prepared interlocutor would raise against this reconstruction, then distill them into the final list.

I'm looking for eight to ten questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. Please make sure the set covers at least the following: first, the textual problem that Yi Hwang revised his formulations repeatedly over the course of his correspondence with Ki Daeseung, and whether her reconstruction reads a stable position into what may be a thinker working things out in real time; second, Yi I's (Yulgok's) classic objection that any dual-issuance picture multiplies the sources of feeling and leaves principle doing causal work it cannot do on his alternative picture in which material force issues and principle rides along — I want a question that forces her to say what normative or explanatory work the two-pathway picture does that Yulgok's single-pathway picture cannot; third, the moral-psychological stakes, namely what difference the reconstruction makes for self-cultivation — if the Four have a distinct genesis, is the task of the sage to cultivate one pathway, to suppress the other, or something else; and fourth, a question on how her reading interfaces with the contemporary debate between perceptualist and judgmentalist accounts of emotion, since her description of the Four sometimes sounds like emotions as perceptions of value and sometimes like rational responsiveness, which are not the same thing.

For each question, please anticipate the answer she is most likely to give given her published position, and supply a pointed follow-up keyed to that answer — ideally a follow-up that raises the cost of her most probable escape route. I'd also like the sequence to build: open with a question that lets her present the view in its strongest form, tighten progressively through the textual and metaphysical objections, and close with a question about what textual or philosophical evidence would lead her to abandon the reconstruction in favor of the Yulgokian reading. Avoid anything generic about her career or how she became interested in Korean philosophy; every question should turn on the content of the position itself.
