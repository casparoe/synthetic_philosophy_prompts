So here's something I've been chewing on for months and I want an essay that actually takes a side on it, not one of those "on one hand, on the other hand" things that ends with "welp, it's complicated."

When a huge moral norm collapses -- a practice everyone thought was normal becomes unthinkable within a generation or two -- what actually does the killing? I've run into three explanations and I can't figure out which one is the real engine.

1. Arguments. Moral reformers make the case, the case spreads, people get persuaded, and eventually the practice becomes indefensible in public.
2. Interests. I half-remember a historian (Williams, I think?) arguing that slavery didn't end because people got nicer but because it stopped paying -- the economics shifted and the morality trailed along behind it.
3. Honor. I read a summary of a book by Kwame Anthony Appiah claiming dueling in England didn't die because anyone lost the argument -- the arguments against it were ancient -- but because it stopped conferring status. Once gentleman started seeing it as ridiculous rather than noble, it collapsed almost overnight.

To keep this from being just history homework, here's the part I actually care about. Take some practice that's totally normal now but plausibly won't be in 60 years -- factory farming is my candidate but pick another if you think it makes the point better. Write an essay that (a) figures out which of the three mechanisms best explains what happened with slavery and dueling, (b) uses that verdict to lay out how the modern practice would actually die -- what the sequence of events would look like, which audience has to flip first, and (c) reaches a genuine conclusion about which mechanism is the engine and which ones are just the exhaust fumes, i.e. the after-the-fact story people tell so it feels like the change was about morality all along.

One objection you have to deal with head-on: the three stories aren't obviously rivals. Maybe arguments soften a norm up, shifting interests change the incentives, and honor delivers the killing blow. If you think that division-of-labor picture is right, defend it as an actual thesis rather than using it as a way to dodge the question -- and tell me which stage a movement should spend its money on if it can only pick one. If instead you think one mechanism genuinely dominates, explain why the division-of-labor story is too tidy and what evidence from the historical cases rules it out.
