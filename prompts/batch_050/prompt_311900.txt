I am writing a long-form essay on a puzzle about how we explain political history: retrospective accounts of why a country's institutions are the way they are almost always feel more illuminating than they should, and I want to understand the cognitive machinery behind that feeling. I found a useful foil in the opening chapter of John Stuart Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, where Mill sketches two rival schools of thought about political institutions. Of the first, the "practical art" school, he writes that its adherents look upon a constitution "in the same light (difference of scale being allowed for) as they would upon a steam plow, or a threshing machine." Of the second, the school he is more concerned to resist, he writes:

"The fundamental political institutions of a people are considered by this school as a sort of organic growth from the nature and life of that people; a product of their habits, instincts, and unconscious wants and desires, scarcely at all of their deliberate purposes."

Here is what I would like from you, in roughly 900–1,200 words of polished, jargon-light prose suitable for an educated general reader.

First, use the concepts of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy to explain why the "organic growth" style of explanation Mill describes is so seductive. Be specific about the mechanism: once we know which institutions actually survived and consolidated, what happens to our sense of how likely that outcome was, and how does a coherent story about national character or historical fit exploit that distortion? I want an account of why this kind of explanation can feel deep precisely when it is evidentially thin.

Second, the part I think most treatments would miss: argue that Mill's rival "steam plow" school does not escape the same trap but merely inverts it. If the organic school commits a narrative fallacy of inevitability (the outcome was bound to grow from the people's character), what is the mirror-image fallacy committed by those who see institutions as deliberately contrived artifacts (the outcome was someone's design)? Show that knowing the outcome distorts both schools' reasoning in opposite directions, and give a compact, realistic illustration of each distortion at work on the same institutional fact.

Third, adjudicate. Since both schools are prey to hindsight-driven storytelling, what would genuine evidence for or against an "organic" explanation of some institution actually look like? Give me two or three concrete marks by which a reader could tell real path-dependence from retrofitted narrative coherence.

Finally, turn the lens on Mill himself: his confident insistence, against the organic school, that institutions are made and could have been made otherwise—is that confidence itself a candidate hindsight artifact, the retrospective self-assurance of a reform tradition narrating its own successes? Give me your honest verdict on whether Mill's chapter is a diagnosis of narrative fallacy or an instance of one.
