I work at a wildlife agency and we've just had our mandate quietly expanded: the law protects "individuals" of listed species rather than populations, so every protection decision now turns on how many individuals we're looking at. That was fine for bears and owls, but I'm drowning in edge cases. A clonal aspen stand connected by one root system -- one individual or thousands? A coral head? A lichen, which is a fungus plus an alga doing everything together? An Argentine ant supercolony where workers from nests hundreds of miles apart don't fight each other? The team needs a written procedure we can run in the field or at a desk review, something like a series of tests in order (genetic sameness? physical connection? shared reproduction? whether the parts can survive alone?) that tells us what the law should count as one individual.

Build that procedure, but grounded in whatever philosophers and biologists actually say about what makes something one organism rather than a group or a pile of parts -- I don't want us reinventing this badly. Keep it to something a biologist (not a philosopher) can apply in an afternoon. Show it working on at least three of my cases, including one where the procedure gives an answer that's legally awkward (e.g. it says the whole ant supercolony is one individual and thus the law protects a pest). And be honest about where the tests stop settling things and someone just has to make a call, because we'll have to defend those judgment-call cases in court.
