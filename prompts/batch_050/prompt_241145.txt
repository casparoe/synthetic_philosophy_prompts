I recently enrolled in a clinical trial testing a memory-blocking drug, and I've run into a puzzle about what I should do during the trial itself. The setup, which the researchers explained to me in advance and which I fully trust them to carry out, is this. On Sunday night I'm put to sleep, and a fair coin is flipped. If it lands heads, I'm woken once, on Monday, interviewed briefly, and the trial ends. If it lands tails, I'm woken on Monday, interviewed, then given the drug, which reliably erases my memory of that awakening, and woken again on Tuesday for an identical interview. Either way I'm released on Wednesday. Whenever I'm awake during the trial, everything feels completely normal; I have no way of telling which day it is or whether I've been woken before.

The interview consists of two things. First, I'm asked how confident I am, right now, that the coin landed heads. Second, I'm offered a small wager, which I may accept or decline: it costs $50 and pays out $130 if the coin landed tails. The same offer is made at every awakening.

Here's what's bothering me. Before Sunday, I can compute everything cleanly: the coin is fair, so heads is a 50-50 proposition. But once I'm actually awake in the trial room, the situation seems to shift. If the coin landed tails, this awakening is one of two; if heads, it's the only one. It seems that this should affect how confident I should be, but I can't settle whether it should be a third or remain a half, and arguments for both feel compelling.

The wager makes things stranger. I worked out that if I decide in advance, on Sunday, to accept the wager whenever offered, the plan is clearly profitable, because a tails result means I get the offer twice. But sitting in the interview room, reasoning only about this awakening, the wager looks attractive under one assignment of confidence and much less attractive under the other. So my pre-commitment and my in-the-moment reasoning seem to pull apart, and I'm not sure whether that reveals a mistake in one of them or something deeper about how confidence and rational choice interact when an agent can't locate herself among her own possible awakenings.

So my questions are: When I'm awake in the trial, how confident should I be that the coin landed heads, and why? Should I accept the wager in the room, given that I wasn't able to pre-commit, and does the answer depend on my answer to the first question? And if my Sunday planning and my in-the-room reasoning genuinely conflict, which one should govern, and what does the conflict show?
