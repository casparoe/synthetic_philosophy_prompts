I run the model evaluation team at a company that deploys a customer-facing AI assistant. Regulators and our own contracts now require us to document, for each capability claim we make about the system, whether the system can be said to "know," "believe," or "intend" something -- for instance, whether it "knows" a refund policy, "believes" a user's stated goal, or "intends" to deceive when it produces a confident falsehood. My team currently handles these questions by gut feeling, and different evaluators write inconsistent reports about the same system, which is becoming an audit problem.

Please write a decision procedure my team can run, in under an hour per claim, for deciding when belief- and desire-talk about an AI system is warranted. Ground it in the philosophical literature on the intentional stance and on interpretivist accounts of belief -- the idea that attributing beliefs is justified when doing so yields reliable predictive and explanatory purchase, together with the constraints of charity and coherence that supposedly come with treating something as a believer -- and be explicit about which parts of those theories you are keeping, which you are dropping, and what is lost by dropping them. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask in order; what behavioral evidence counts as a sufficient answer to each (e.g., consistency of the purported belief across rephrasings, resistance to irrelevant context changes, appropriate revision under counterevidence, integration with related beliefs); where the procedure terminates in "do not use intentional language here"; and the points where no procedure can substitute for judgment, flagged as such rather than disguised as checklist items.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) the assistant reliably applying a return policy across paraphrased questions but failing when the policy is stated only in a document it summarizes rather than in its instructions; (2) the assistant producing a confident falsehood that happens to serve the user's apparent wishes; (3) the assistant expressing a "preference" for one answer format over another. At least one of these should come out with a verdict that will be uncomfortable for my marketing colleagues, and you should say so directly. Conclude by stating how confident you are that the procedure tracks what the underlying philosophy actually supports, and single out the single step in the procedure where you are least sure you have translated the theory faithfully.
