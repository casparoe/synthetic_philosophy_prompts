I sit on the five-member integration board of a mid-sized European city. Next month the council votes on a Welcome Fund that would provide housing stipends and intensive language courses to asylum seekers whose claims are still pending — people who may yet be ordered to leave. The budget is genuinely tight: covering pending claimants means cutting evening hours at job centers used by long-settled residents, and the largest opposition party has pledged to move for the fund's abolition if it extends to people without secure status. A colleague on the board frames our choice as one between scope and scale: a city can welcome many newcomers with modest support or fewer newcomers with generous support, but attempting both at once invites the political backlash that destroys both. Another colleague replies that a person's need, and her claim on the city she actually lives in, does not vary with the bureaucratic status of her file.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board before the vote. It should recommend a concrete decision — extend the fund, restrict it to recognized refugees, or a specific hybrid — state the reasoning in a form the council can act on, and engage seriously with the strongest argument for whichever option you reject, including, if you recommend exclusion, the claim that residence and need, rather than paperwork, ground the city's obligation.
