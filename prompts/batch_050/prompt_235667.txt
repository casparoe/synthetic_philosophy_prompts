So I've been going down the higher-order evidence rabbit hole again (it started with the old LessWrong disagreement stuff, but now I'm in the actual academic literature), and I got into a fight on a Discord server about epistemic akrasia -- states like believing p while also believing your evidence doesn't really support p, or holding credence 0.9 in something while judging that 0.5 is the credence your evidence actually warrants. My friend says these states are obviously always irrational; I'm genuinely unsure. But that's not what I'm asking about. What I want is to map out the anti-akrasia side of the terrain properly before I argue with him, because right now I only have the greatest hits.

Here's my existing list of arguments that epistemic akrasia is irrational: (1) the fixed-point argument -- rational credence should be a fixed point where what you believe matches what you take the evidence to support, and a state that fails this is unstable or incoherent in something like the way Moore-paradoxical beliefs are; (2) the self-trust argument -- the akratic agent has to regard her own belief-forming process as having gone off track, but if she really internalizes that, she undercuts the basis for her belief in p itself, so the state eats its own foundations; (3) the action argument -- you can't rationally act on a belief you yourself deem irrational, so akrasia either paralyzes you or transmits irrationality downstream into everything you do on the basis of that belief; (4) the "it's all just evidence" argument -- higher-order evidence (that you're sleep-deprived, biased, outnumbered by epistemic peers) is just more evidence to conditionalize on, and once you've fully taken it into account there's no gap left between your credence and what you judge to be supported, so anyone maintaining the gap is either double-counting or ignoring part of their total evidence.

Is there a fifth argument I'm missing? It has to be genuinely distinct -- not a rewording of one of these four, not a special case of one of them, and not just two of them stapled together. And once you've given it: tell me honestly how well it holds up against the level-splitting response, i.e. the view that your credence should answer to your first-order evidence while your beliefs about what your evidence supports should answer to the higher-order evidence, so the two can rationally come apart. If your proposed fifth argument collapses under that response, or turns out to secretly be one of the four in a trenchcoat, just say so.
