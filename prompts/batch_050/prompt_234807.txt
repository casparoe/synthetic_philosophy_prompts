I've been working through Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics off and on for a year now -- it keeps coming up in my circles whenever someone wants to litigate the foundations of effective altruism or argue about whether pure altruism is rationally required -- and the part I keep getting stuck on is the dualism of practical reason: Sidgwick's conclusion, after all his careful taxonomizing, that the axioms of rational egoism and of universal benevolence are both self-evident, that they can genuinely conflict, and that no purely philosophical argument can force a rational agent to privilege one over the other. He treats this as a deep and unresolved problem rather than a reductio of his method, and modern commentators seem split into camps: those who think Parfit's later convergence arguments (the attempt in On What Matters to get Kantianism, contractualism, and consequentialism to climb the same mountain, and his efforts to defuse the egoism side) dissolve the dualism; those who think the dualism is simply correct and that admitting an ineliminable conflict within reason is the mature position; and those who think the whole thing is an artifact of Sidgwick's contested metaethical machinery -- intuitionism about self-evident axioms, the distinction between personal and impartial points of view -- and evaporates once you stop granting those premises.

My question is not who is right but what kind of disagreement this is and why it hasn't moved in 150 years. Is the dualism debate a substantive standoff between incompatible starting intuitions that neither side can argue for (which would make Sidgwick's own agnosticism look like the only honest position), a verbal or framing dispute about what "reason requires" means, or two groups answering different questions -- one about what morality demands, one about what practical rationality as such demands -- under a single heading? Please diagnose the impasse concretely, engaging what the positions actually claim rather than surveying the literature: identify which premise or intuition each camp treats as non-negotiable, say what would count as genuine progress given your diagnosis (a sharper distinction, an argument that egoism is rationally optional rather than required, a deflation of the self-evidence framework, something else), and give an honest assessment of whether Parfit-style convergence projects actually address the dualism or just relocate it to a different corner of the map. If your conclusion is that no resolution should be expected because the question itself is ill-posed, argue for that rather than hedging.
