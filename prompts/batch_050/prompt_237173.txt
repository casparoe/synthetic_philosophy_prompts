I teach a seminar on the foundations of welfare economics, and every year the same fight breaks out between two factions in my classroom. One side defends a strict revealed-preference position in the spirit of Samuelson: utility is nothing over and above an index that rationalizes choice, talk of what is "good for" people beyond what they choose is metaphysical excess, and the only scientifically respectable evidence about welfare is what agents actually pick given a budget. The other side insists that utility, to do the normative work economists want it to do, must connect to something like felt wellbeing or genuine interest-satisfaction, and that choices can systematically fail to track welfare -- as with addiction, framing effects, or preferences deformed by deprivation (the classic case of the downtrodden person who has adapted and now genuinely "wants" very little). I have asked both groups to stop re-litigating the same slogans and instead produce, as an adversarial collaboration, a single joint statement both could sign.

Please draft that document. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims -- e.g., about what the representation theorems do and do not establish, about which consistency axioms are actually being tested in lab violations, and about what each camp concedes the other's position gets right; (2) a disagreements table stating their remaining disputes in wording each side would accept as a fair description of its own view; (3) a cruxes section identifying three to five sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party -- I suspect the real cruxes include whether there are any choice patterns both sides agree count as welfare-revealing failures rather than mere preferences, and whether non-choice evidence (self-reported wellbeing, physiological measures) can bear on utility at all; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case, experiment, or class of evidence both sides agree in advance would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded beforehand. Use my seeded examples if you find them apt, but feel free to propose better ones.

The hard part is the drafting: formulations must be neither vacuous nor slanted, and at least one of the apparent disagreements should turn out, on careful phrasing, to be verbal -- if so, identify which and explain why. Do not adjudicate a winner; the deliverable is the collaborative text itself, with residual disagreement made exact. Roughly 900-1400 words, in American English.
