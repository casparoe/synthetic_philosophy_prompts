So I'm running a book-club-meets-survey-course thing this fall (community ed, mix of retirees and undergrads, long story) and I've paired Zora Neale Hurston's Their Eyes Were Watching God with Du Bois's 1926 essay where he argues that all art is propaganda and that Black artists in particular shouldn't apologize for making their art serve the cause of racial justice. I picked the pairing partly because it seems like a fight waiting to happen -- Wright's famous takedown of Hurston for writing a novel with no real message is sort of the Du Boisian criticism avant la lettre -- but the more I prep the less sure I am that the fight is real. Help me work out the reading before I go in and improvise something misleading.

Specifically: where does Janie's story actually satisfy Du Bois's criteria, given that his notion of propaganda is broader than "novel with a thesis" -- a self-governing Black town rendered from the inside, Black speech treated as literature, a Black woman granted a full interior life at a time when that was itself a claim? Where does the novel flatly resist him (I'm thinking of the hurricane, the trial, the fact that Janie's liberation is personal and not communal, and the ending where she tells Pheoby you have to go to God and find out about living for yourself)? And -- this is the part I most want pushed on -- does Du Bois's essay even say what I've implied it says, since he also insists on Beauty and artistic freedom and rails against white critics policing Black art from the other side? If I'm flattening his position into "art must have the right politics" when the essay is more conflicted than that, say so plainly rather than giving me the cleaner version I asked for.

End with the thing I couldn't get from just rereading the essay: what, if anything, does the novel add to this debate that Du Bois's argument by itself can't -- e.g., does Hurston show that depicting an apolitical inner life is doing political work, or is that just a neat move book club moderators make to reconcile texts they like? If the honest answer is that the pairing is contrived and the two texts aren't really in dialogue, I'd rather hear that than a forced synthesis.
