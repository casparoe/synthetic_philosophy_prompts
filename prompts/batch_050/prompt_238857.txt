I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on imprecise credences, and two colleagues in my reading group got into a dispute I now have to referee. The setup they argued about is this. A fair coin is about to be flipped. Separately, a sealed envelope contains a single bit, 0 or 1, produced by a mechanism about which the agent knows nothing whatsoever; her credence that the bit is 1 is the full interval [0, 1]. Define X as the proposition that the coin outcome and the bit agree (heads and 1, or tails and 0). Before the flip, her credence that X is true is a perfectly precise 1/2: whatever the bit is, a fair coin matches it with probability one-half, so every probability in her representor assigns X one-half. The coin is flipped, and she is told only that it landed heads. Now X is true if and only if the bit is 1, so her credence in X dilates to the vacuous interval [0, 1]. Learning the outcome of a fair coin, which seems irrelevant to X, has destroyed everything she appeared to know about X.

Elena argued that this dilation is a reductio of the whole framework. No rational agent becomes maximally ignorant about a proposition by learning something irrelevant to it; precise Bayesianism never dilates, so the pathology must come from the intervals. Moreover, the precise pre-flip credence of 1/2 was endorsed by the imprecise framework itself, not imposed from outside, so the collapse can't be blamed on a bad precisification.

Marcus replied that dilation is the right verdict. The pre-flip 1/2 was spurious precision: it arose from two uncertainties canceling out, not from any evidence bearing on X, and the interval representation exposes what the point value concealed. Conditioning on the coin reveals that she never had grounds about X itself.

Elena pushed back that imprecise agents are then dynamically defective. Before the flip she would happily pay 45 cents for a ticket that returns a dollar if X, since her credence is exactly 1/2. After hearing the free information about the coin, she refuses that bet at any price. So free evidence leaves her strictly less willing to act, violating the principle that rational agents never pay to avoid information.

Marcus answered that this begs the question in favor of point-valued expected utility: under the decision rules that accompany interval credences, her betting dispositions were never fixed by the 1/2 alone. He added that on Elena's view, an agent with a forced precise prior over the bit would be immune to dilation, yet nobody thinks such a prior reflects real knowledge — which shows precision was the problem all along.

They've asked me to adjudicate, and I keep changing my mind. Please go through the exchange point by point, covering: who is right about whether the pre-flip 1/2 was warranted or spurious; whether dilation shows that learning can rationally produce ignorance; whether the betting argument establishes dynamic irrationality; who is more right overall; and what, if anything, both are missing. Present your verdict as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction or conclusion.
