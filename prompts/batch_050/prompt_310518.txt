At my company we have this norm around design reviews and incident calls: if two engineers with roughly equal experience look at the same evidence (same logs, same code, same dashboard) and disagree about whether something is safe to ship, we treat the disagreement itself as a reason to slow down, even if you're one of the two people and you're still privately sure you're right. Separately we have the whole disagree-and-commit thing, where once a decision gets made you're expected to execute on it fully even if you think its the wrong call.

Lately I've been wondering what picture of rationality this all presupposes. Does the first norm assume that a peer's judgment counts as evidence over and above the raw evidence we both already looked at — and if so, why isn't that just double counting? And when I commit to a plan I still believe is wrong, am I being asked to act against my own best judgment? Is that even a coherent thing to ask, or does the practice quietly assume my beliefs will catch up with my behavior? Also, do the two norms even fit together: one says treat disagreement as a reason to lower your confidence, the other says once the org decides, set your remaining doubts aside. Is there one consistent theory hiding under both about whose judgment counts and when, or is it just pragmatism dressed up as principle? And if we swapped whatever the implicit theory is for a strictly first-order one, where only the logs and code count and never other people's opinions, what would concretely have to change about how we run reviews?
