In a final-year seminar on social explanation, I gave students a hypothetical but methodologically strong result: after correcting for translation, response styles and sampling error, adults in country A score one-quarter of a standard deviation higher than adults in country B on a personality trait. I claimed that this licenses the statement that country A has a higher mean score, but not the more familiar generic claim that people from A are more disposed that way than people from B. A student objected that adding “on average” makes the two claims equivalent, and that any further resistance must be social etiquette rather than philosophy.

Is there a principled reply to the student? Please distinguish claims about distributions, generic claims about kinds of people, and explanations of individual conduct. I am particularly interested in whether national character could ever be an explanatory category rather than merely a label for an aggregate difference, and in how the permissibility of such a generalisation might depend on its audience, purpose, stakes and the availability of more informative evidence.
