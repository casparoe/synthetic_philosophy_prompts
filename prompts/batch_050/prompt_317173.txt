At the consciousness reading group that grew out of our local rationality meetup, two regulars — call them Mara and Dev — have been litigating whether epiphenomenalism undermines itself for three sessions running. I've stayed out of it, partly because I genuinely can't tell who's winning. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Mara's opening: epiphenomenalism is self-defeating. If phenomenal properties have no causal effects, then nothing about your qualia explains why you believe, judge, or say that you have them. Your report that you're experiencing red is produced entirely by neurons; the felt redness itself does nothing. So the epiphenomenalist's own assertion of the view floats free of the facts it describes — no one could ever be in a position to know it was true, and a view like that forfeits the right to be asserted.

(2) Dev's reply: knowledge doesn't require causal contact — we know things about numbers without causally interacting with them. And epiphenomenalism doesn't leave judgments unmoored anyway. The brain state that produces the judgment also produces the quale, so judgment and quale reliably co-occur through a common cause, like two hands of the same clock. Reliability, not causation, is what knowledge needs, and the correlation here couldn't be more reliable.

(3) Mara's comeback: reliability requires counterfactual sensitivity, and that's exactly what's missing. Hold the brain fixed and subtract the quale — the judgment comes out identical. Worse, swap the red quale for a green one and the judgment still comes out identical, so the concept expressed doesn't even track which quale is present. A thermometer stuck on 72° in a town where it's always 72° is "reliable" in the cheap sense, but it detects nothing.

(4) Dev's escalation: the sensitivity demand proves too much. On any theory, the proximal cause of saying "I'm in pain" is neural, and the zombie twin of a physicalist produces the same judgments. If judgments had to be sensitive to phenomenal facts as such, no one on any view could know they were conscious, so the objection can't be selectively aimed at epiphenomenalism. And there's a positive account available besides: first-person justification runs through acquaintance, not causation. The causal story explains the utterance; acquaintance justifies the belief. Mara is conflating two different relations.

(5) Mara's response: acquaintance is causation under an assumed name. If the quale does any work in fixing what the belief is about, it's been smuggled back into the causal order; if it does none, the belief's content is coincidence. And even granting Dev that the view isn't strictly self-refuting, a theory of experience whose truth is undetectable by the experiences it posits has sawn off the only branch anyone was sitting on — first-person acquaintance was the entire evidence base for qualia.

(6) Dev's closing: undetectable truths are still truths. Self-stultification arguments confuse what a theory says with what anyone is warranted in asserting, and "no one can know anything" might well be true even if it's peculiar to say.

So, some questions for you. First, go move by move: which of the six moves are sound, which fail, and why? Second, does the whole dispute really reduce to whether sensitivity is a legitimate requirement on knowledge, or is the acquaintance move an independent crux? Third, is there a reconciliation available — say, holding that content is fixed by whatever actually plays the right role in the actual world, so the swap scenarios are beside the point? And finally, who is more right overall, and what consideration is each of them missing that would break the deadlock?
