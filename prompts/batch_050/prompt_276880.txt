Last week I spent the better part of an evening arguing with an old friend about whether psychiatric diagnosis can ever be as objective as diagnosis elsewhere in medicine. I had been defending the fairly naive position that psychiatry, whatever its messy history, is in the business of discovering real disorders, and she countered by invoking Ian Hacking's work on what he calls the looping effect. Neither of us has read Hacking directly — we are each working from secondhand summaries picked up in courses and popular books — and at one point she offered a compressed statement of his thesis that sounded plausible to me but seemed to license a conclusion I strongly suspect Hacking himself would not endorse. I would like an accuracy audit of her summary before I concede the argument.

Her version, as best I can reconstruct it, ran as follows. Psychiatric categories are what Hacking calls human kinds, and human kinds are interactive: once doctors classify people as having a disorder, the classified people absorb the label, reshape their behavior and self-understanding around it, and so the classification ends up producing the very phenomenon it claimed merely to describe. His showcase example was multiple personality disorder, which on his account was essentially created in the clinic — patients learned to perform the symptoms their therapists expected, and when the therapeutic fashion moved on, the diagnosis withered, showing that it was never a real illness in the way pneumonia is a real illness. Since classified people react to their labels in a way that quarks and liver cells do not, Hacking concluded that human kinds can never be stable objects of discovery; psychiatric classification is therefore social construction all the way down, and its categories are artifacts of a particular time and place rather than features of nature that medicine uncovers. From this she drew the further conclusion, which is what our argument was really about, that psychiatry cannot honestly present itself as a value-neutral science, and that a default skepticism toward any given diagnosis is the intellectually respectable position.

My instinct is that she smuggled in at least one step — in particular, the move from a category's being interactive or constructed to its being unreal felt too quick, and I also vaguely recall that the "loop" in Hacking's name for the effect is supposed to run in both directions rather than only from label to patient — but I could not articulate either worry well enough at the time. Could you go through her summary and separate what Hacking would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what misrepresents his actual position? Specifically: is the multiple personality case one he treats as manufactured by clinicians, or is his view subtler than that? Does the looping effect, as he describes it, actually entail that psychiatric categories cannot be studied scientifically, or is that an overstatement? And does her final conclusion follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does it depend precisely on the parts she got wrong?
