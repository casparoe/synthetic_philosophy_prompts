I am a novelist, and last week, after a public reading, I sat mostly silent while two people whose judgment I respect — a literary critic and a scholar of literature — argued about a question that bears directly on my own craft. The immediate occasion was a novel by a long-dead author whose private letters had recently been published. (1) The critic maintained that the novel's ending plainly condemns its protagonist, and that it makes no difference that the letters reveal the author intended sympathy: once a book is published, its meaning resides in the text, and an author who protests that we have misread her is merely one more reader, with no special standing. (2) The scholar replied that marks on a page mean anything at all only because someone meant something by them; erosion might by chance trace out a grammatical sentence on a cliff face, but it would not be a message, and treating a text as authorless reduces reading to finding faces in clouds — so intention is not one piece of evidence among others but the very thing that makes the text a text. (3) The critic conceded that an intender is required for there to be language at all, but insisted this concedes far less than it appears to: once the words are fixed, they can do things the author never foresaw, the way a careless apology can come out as an insult; "that is not what I meant" does not change what was said. Meaning is public; intention is private; interpretation is answerable to the former. (4) The scholar answered with the case of irony: an author can attempt an irony so subtle that no reader catches it, and in such a case we would say the joke failed, not that it never existed; so intention at least fixes what the author did, even where it cannot fix what the text achieves — which suggests that the word "meaning" is doing double duty, and that the two of them may be disputing different questions, one about the public object, the other about the author's act. (5) The critic replied that this distinction simply surrenders her point under another name: if the study of a work is the study of the public object, then the letters belong to biography, however fascinating, and not to criticism; and in any event, intentionalism cannot even say which author we are to consult — the same writer, rereading at seventy a novel she wrote at twenty-five, discovers in it meanings she never planted and warmly endorses them, and it is quite unclear why the young woman's intention should govern rather than the old woman's. At that point both turned to me, and I found, to my embarrassment, that I could not decide, though the stakes for anyone in my position are obvious. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification of each rating; say who is right about which specific point; and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing.
