I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of drug policy, and last week we got into an argument about whether any single principle could justify the mix of policies we each found intuitive. I realized I couldn't state mine. Here are my verdicts on the cases we discussed, as honestly as I can report them. Criminal penalties for personal possession of cannabis: clearly wrong. Criminal penalties for personal possession of heroin: also wrong, though I say it with less confidence — the Portugal-style arrangement, where use is decriminalized and met with referral rather than prosecution, seems right to me. Licensed commercial sale of cannabis to adults, roughly on the Canadian model: fine. Licensed commercial sale of heroin: not fine, full stop. Banning advertising and promotion of legal cannabis: fine. Firing or criminally sanctioning a pilot or surgeon who works while intoxicated: obviously fine. Prohibiting sale to minors: fine. Home cultivation of cannabis: fine, and I can't see how anyone who accepts licensed sale could deny it. And one case where my instinct won't settle: prosecuting a dealer for manslaughter when a customer overdoses on what they were sold.

The text that set off the argument was a passage from Chapter V ("Applications") of Mill's On Liberty, where he discusses restrictions on trade. Mill writes:

"On the other hand, there are questions relating to interference with trade which are essentially questions of liberty; such as the Maine Law, already touched upon; the prohibition of the importation of opium into China; the restriction of the sale of poisons; all cases, in short, where the object of the interference is to make it impossible or difficult to obtain a particular commodity. These interferences are objectionable, not as infringements on the liberty of the producer or seller, but on that of the buyer."

This passage seems to cut against my fourth verdict: if restrictions on sale are objectionable because they infringe the liberty of the buyer, then prohibiting heroin sales wrongs the would-be buyer, and my combination — decriminalize possession of everything, legalize sale of cannabis only — looks unstable. Yet just a page earlier in the same chapter Mill insists that trade is a social act affecting the interests of others and so falls within society's jurisdiction, and later in the chapter he treats it as a genuinely hard boundary-line question whether activities that must be tolerated in themselves (his example is gambling) may nonetheless be prohibited as businesses run for profit (keeping a gambling-house). So I have three candidate readings of what my verdicts amount to: (a) a pure harm-to-others principle, which seems to force me toward legal heroin sale; (b) a use/sale asymmetry, where self-regarding consumption is protected but commerce is regulable on consequentialist grounds; or (c) some threshold view in which the severity and addictiveness of the substance does the work, which feels like paternalism sneaking back in.

Here is what I'd like. State precisely the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures my verdicts, and say which clause each case motivates. Test the candidates against Mill's two maxims as they appear in the passage and its surroundings: is the use/sale asymmetry actually available to a Millian, given that he frames sale restrictions as infringements on the buyer, or does it require a different foundation entirely? Tell me which of my verdicts cannot be saved together, and if one has to go, which it should be and why. Then apply the resulting principle to two cases I haven't listed: supervised consumption sites, where the state facilitates use it refuses to legalize the sale of, and the dealer-manslaughter case I admitted I can't settle — I'd like to know whether my indecision there marks a genuine gap in any principle that fits the rest, or just a failure of nerve.
