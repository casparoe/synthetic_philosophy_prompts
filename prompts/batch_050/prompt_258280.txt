I host a podcast about science and big ideas, and next week I'm interviewing a decision theorist who's just published a book with a deliberately punchy thesis: persistent disagreement among smart, honest, well-informed people is a scandal, and whenever two such people walk away from a conversation still disagreeing, at least one of them is being irrational. Her argument leans heavily on the classic result that rational agents who share a prior, and whose posteriors are common knowledge between them, can't just agree to disagree — plus the empirical observation that actual humans disagree all the time and barely update even when they know exactly what the other side thinks. She extends this to some strong practical claims: you should treat a respected peer's dissent as roughly as weighty as your own judgment, deference to expert consensus should be near-automatic for laypeople, and the fact that disagreement persists everywhere is basically a measure of how irrational we all are.

I want eight to ten questions that make her defend the machinery rather than restate the slogan. A few pressure points I definitely want covered: where the common prior assumption is supposed to come from — people clearly acquire different priors through different upbringings and experiences, and when she says (as she will) that it's an idealization, I want a follow-up on what a theorem about idealized agents actually licenses her to conclude about messy real ones. Also the common knowledge requirement: real conversations don't run long enough to establish that, so why should brief exchanges create any obligation to converge? I also want to push on the symmetry worry — I seem to have direct access to my own reasoning but only indirect access to yours, so is there anything irrational about weighting my own judgment more heavily, and if she says no there isn't, doesn't that license everyone ignoring everyone?

Beyond that, I'd like at least one question on bounded rationality — computing the right update from someone else's opinion may be intractable even for ideal-ish agents — and one on whether her view collapses into pure deference: if disagreement with consensus is irrational, how does science ever revise itself? And please end the sequence with the self-application question: she and her sharpest critics are all smart, informed, and aware of each other's arguments, and they still disagree about her own thesis — does her view commit her to thinking her critics are irrational, or herself, and what would she even do with that verdict? For each question, give me a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, since shes very polished and will otherwise glide past the hard part.
