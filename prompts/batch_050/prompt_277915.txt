I'm trying to determine what principle my intuitions about aggregating harms answer to, and whether that principle can be stated within Scanlon's contractualism or forces me outside it. My verdicts on triage-style cases, all involving strangers where I can help only one side:

- One death versus one case of permanent paralysis: save the one from death.
- One death versus five people each losing a leg: save the five.
- One death versus a million people each suffering a mild headache: save the one. I've read the dust-specks arguments and I hold this verdict anyway — no number of trivial harms aggregates against a death.
- One death versus a hundred cases of permanent paralysis: save the hundred.
- One death versus two cases of permanent paralysis: I oscillate and cannot stabilize a verdict.

My tentative principle: a harm is irrelevant to the choice if no number of people suffering it could outweigh the strongest individual claim at stake, and irrelevant harms simply drop out of consideration; relevant harms are then weighed in the aggregate. This delivers my first four verdicts, but the cutoff seems arbitrary, it makes permissibility depend on which alternatives are in play in a way that threatens cycles, and I doubt it handles risk — does a one-in-a-thousand chance of death for each of two hundred people aggregate against one certain case of paralysis?

Is there a defensible sharpening of this principle, and can Scanlon's complaint model, which evaluates principles one complainant at a time, even express it? What would the best candidate predict for cases I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts would a cleaner theory force me to abandon?
