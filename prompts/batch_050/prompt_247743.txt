My roommate and I got into it last week over whether I "knew" the bus was running on Sunday (it wasn't, I got stranded, long story), and it spiraled into this argument about knowledge and stakes that he's now convinced proves something crazy, and I have to admit I can't find the hole. Here's the cleaned-up version. (1) You should treat p as a reason for acting only if you know p -- the whole point of saying "I know" is that you're done checking and can go ahead. (2) You know p only if your evidence rules out every possibility of error that it's not rational for you to ignore. (3) Whether it's rational to ignore a possibility of error depends on what hangs on being wrong, i.e. on the stakes -- that's the one insight from all the bank-case and train-case stuff that even the skeptics about pragmatic encroachment concede has something to it. (4) But stakes aren't really under your control: for any ordinary p and any evidence, there is always some decision you might face -- a huge bet someone could offer you, a surgery you'd authorize based on p, whatever -- where being wrong about p would be catastrophic, and you can't rule out in advance that such a decision will land on you. (5) So for virtually any p, the possibility that p is false is one you can't rationally ignore, since the potential stakes are unbounded. (6) So you know almost nothing, and by (1) you should treat almost nothing as a basis for action. (7) But you manifestly do act on things all day long, and refusing to act is itself acting, so the argument condemns literally every possible course of life as irrational.

My roommate says this is just what pragmatic encroachment gets you and the lesson is to go back to strict purism: knowledge never depends on stakes, full stop. I suspect something slid between (3) and (5), because encroachment people talk about the stakes you're actually in, not every bet that could hypothetically be offered, but when I said that he asked me why actual stakes should matter if merely possible ones don't, and I didn't have a good answer. I'd most like to keep (1), since it seems like the least technical premise, and I'd grudgingly give up (4) if forced.

Tell me which step you reject and what rejecting it costs, then explain why the bad step looked fine -- especially whether the flaw is an equivocation about whose stakes count, a modal slide, or something else -- and whether the standard fixes (restricting relevance to actual practical situations, or saying knowledge has an anti-luck condition the argument never touches) actually survive my roommate's comeback about why actual stakes should be special. Lead with your verdict on which step fails in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate the argument back at me.
