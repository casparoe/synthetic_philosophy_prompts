I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately taken up philosophy of physics, and I find I have settled—perhaps prematurely—on the position that in gauge theories only the gauge-invariant quantities are physically real. The electromagnetic vector potential, the absolute phase of a quantum state, and any particular choice of gauge I take to be surplus structure: redundancy in the formalism, with even the Aharonov-Bohm effect attributable to invariant holonomies rather than to any reality of the potential itself. What I want is not a survey of the literature but a map of this position's exposure. Which phenomena or theoretical desiderata would it fail to accommodate? For instance: if the locality of interactions can only be represented using gauge-dependent potentials; if the very structure of quantized gauge theories cannot be recovered starting from invariants alone; or if gauge symmetry turns out to have empirical significance for subsystems even where it has none for the universe as a whole. Which of these would a committed defender of my view concede as genuine costs, and which would they wave away? Please rank the candidate defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and assess whether the position survives only by strategic qualification—say, retreating to the claim that gauge-related descriptions are 'physically equivalent'—and if so, whether that insulation is a defect in a thesis of this kind or entirely proper.
