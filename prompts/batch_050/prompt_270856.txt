I'm taking an undergraduate ethics course, and we recently spent a seminar on the question of moral worth — in particular, on whether an action has full moral worth only when it's done for the right kind of reason. The seminar left me more confused than when I started, because two positions I initially assumed were the same turned out to pull apart quite sharply, and I want to see them actually argued out rather than presented side by side.

Please write a dialogue (roughly 800–1,100 words) between two philosophers, both of whom agree on the following starting point: merely doing the right thing isn't enough for moral worth — a shopkeeper who gives correct change purely out of concern for his reputation deserves no moral credit. Where they disagree is on what the right kind of reason is. The first philosopher defends the view that moral worth requires acting from a concern with morality itself: what matters is that the agent does the action because it's right, or because it's their duty, and that considerations of rightness are what move them. The second philosopher argues, by contrast, that this gets things backwards in many central cases: someone who visits their lonely friend in hospital because they've worked out that visiting is their duty is, morally speaking, a worse visitor than someone who goes simply because they care about their friend and want to see them. On this second view, an agent motivated primarily by the thought "this is the right thing to do" suffers from a kind of objectionable one-thing-too-many: their attention is on morality rather than on the person or the situation that actually makes the action right.

The dialogue should be a genuine exchange rather than two alternating speeches. In particular, have the first philosopher press the worry that direct, unreflective concern can be fickle or arbitrarily distributed (we care about some people and not others, and our affections track proximity and familiarity rather than moral relevance), and have the second philosopher press the worry that the first view makes morally worthy agents strangely alienated from the people they help. Both should engage with at least one case of overdetermination: an agent who is moved both by duty and by direct concern, where either motive alone would have sufficed. They should also clarify, somewhere along the way, whether acting for the right reasons requires the agent to have the relevant considerations explicitly before their mind, or whether the reasons can operate in the background. Neither character should be a strawman: give each the strongest version of their position, allow concessions where concessions are genuinely due, and don't feel obliged to have them reach agreement at the end — a well-drawn stalemate, where the remaining source of disagreement is made perspicuous, would be a better ending than an artificial reconciliation.
