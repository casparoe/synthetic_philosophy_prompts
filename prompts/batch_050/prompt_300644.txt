I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a feature about why consciousness research keeps talking past itself. A colleague drafted a sidebar for me summarizing Dennett's "Quining Qualia" paper, and before I let it anywhere near print I want to know if it's fair. Here it is:

"Dennett's target is the everyday idea that experience has a raw feel -- the redness of red -- that science can't capture. He defines qualia as properties that are ineffable, intrinsic, private, and immediately apprehensible. His central case involves two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who both come to dislike their company's coffee after years on the job. Chase says the coffee tastes the same as ever, he's just lost his appreciation for it. Sanborn says it genuinely tastes different to him now. Since nothing -- not their own introspection, not any brain scan or physiological test -- could settle which of them is right, Dennett concludes there's no fact of the matter about qualia, so they don't exist. What people call the qualitative character of experience is an illusion thrown off by the brain's information processing, and once we've explained the processing there's simply nothing left for a theory of experience to explain."

Something about this smells off to me but I can't put my finger on where. So: which parts of this would Dennett actually sign off on, and which are distortions? In particular, is he really arguing that we just can't *tell* who's right about the coffee, or is something else going on? And does he really conclude that experience itself is an illusion and there's nothing left to explain, or is my colleague sliding from a claim about one technical concept to a claim about consciousness as a whole? Also, if the summary needs fixing, give me an accurate version I could print in two or three sentences, because that's definately all the space I have.
