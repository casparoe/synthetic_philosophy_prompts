Alright, here's my situation. I retired a few years back after 35 years as a field biologist (mostly social insects, funnily enough) and now I help run a mixed discussion group at the local community college -- about 20 people, undergrads mostly, a few curious retirees like me. We've been circling around the philosophy of mind, and I want to do a session on whether an ant colony, taken as a whole, could count as having a mind or beliefs or understanding of its own. There's that old dialogue in Hofstadter's book where the ant colony is a character who talks and thinks even though its ants are dumb as posts, and that's the territory I want, but please don't lecture them on it first and don't let anyone say the name until the end.

Design me a 75-minute activity where the participants *become* the colony. My rough idea, which you should feel free to rework or replace: each person gets a card with simple local rules -- respond to what your neighbors do, move tokens, follow or lay markers, whatever -- and nobody is told what the overall setup is for or what the group as a whole is doing. Then you feed the group some input from the outside (a question, a maze, a choice between two things) and the room should settle into an answer or a solution without any single person knowing what the answer was or even that a question had been asked. I want that moment afterwards where someone goes "wait, did WE just solve that? because I certainly didn't."

So I need: the actual rule cards or an exact recipe for generating them (concrete enough that I can print them the night before, not just a sketch), the input the facilitator feeds in, and a timeline. Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments from the game onto the real question -- when the group "noticed" something no individual noticed, when it "changed its mind," etc. -- and gets the students arguing about whether anything in the room understood anything, beyond the individual humans.

Two more things I want from you. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong: people cheating by peeking at the big picture, one loud student turning into a central coordinator and ruining it, the whole thing converging on a wrong answer, that sort of thing, and how to handle it on the fly. Second, be straight with me about what the activity *can't* show -- my guess is it can demonstrate something about functional organization but says nothing about whether there's anything it feels like to be the colony, and also my students will remain conscious rule-followers in a way ants aren't, which might be doing all the work. Tell me how to flag those gaps without deflating the whole session.

One constraint: I don't want to spend money, so everything should run on index cards, sticky notes, and masking tape on the floor.
