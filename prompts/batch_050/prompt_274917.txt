I’m a science-and-ideas journalist working on a piece about why the Church-Turing thesis seems to sit awkwardly between mathematics and empirical science. In interviews I keep getting two analogies. One camp says it is like conservation of energy: not provable from mathematical axioms, but an extremely well-confirmed boundary on what any effective procedure or physical machine can do, and a genuine hypercomputer would overturn it. The other camp says it is more like the proof that there is no largest prime: once the intuitive idea of a mechanical procedure is made precise, the thesis is something we can know by analysis, so waiting around for an experimental refutation misses the point.

Audit both analogies for me. Give the correspondence table first: what is supposed to play the role of the empirical evidence, the axioms, the possible counterexample, the formal model, and the thing being constrained. Then separate the illuminating correspondences from the disanalogies. In particular, explain what the energy-conservation analogy smuggles in about contingency and physical possibility, and what the prime-number analogy smuggles in about whether “effectively calculable” has already been given one uniquely correct formalization.

Please distinguish at least three claims that often get run together: the classical Church-Turing thesis about intuitively effective calculation, a thesis about what human beings can compute, and a physical thesis about what can be computed by processes in our universe. For each one, say whether the appropriate evidence is more like mathematical proof, conceptual analysis, inductive support, or empirical testing. I also want to know what would actually change the debate: a formal theorem, a new model of computation, a physically possible hypercomputer, or simply a better precisification of mechanical procedure. Finally, say whether the common claim that the thesis is unprovable but overwhelmingly confirmed survives the audit, and offer a better analogy if neither of the two above really fits. I’m after the epistemology, not a potted history or a reading list.
