You are a philosophy instructor who also designs pedagogical games for the classroom; think carefully, and before settling on mechanics, reason about which structural feature of the target idea a game can actually recreate and which it cannot.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar (16 students, one 75-minute session). We are doing Bernard Williams's critique of utilitarianism: the integrity objection and the complaint that certain situations demand, as he puts it, one thought too many. My students read the Jim case, nod along, and then compartmentalize the whole thing as a puzzle about remote jungles and imaginary botanists. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the objection from the inside. Each participant should carry some ongoing personal commitment or project through the exercise -- something they have invested in across earlier rounds -- and then face a sequence of choices in which abandoning it produces clearly better outcomes on the game's own aggregate measure, so that the discomfort of treating a deep commitment as one more fungible preference arises in their own deliberation before anyone names the concept.

Give me: complete rules; any materials, tokens, or commitment-tracking devices needed; a small-stakes payoff scheme that works without money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; and a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto Williams's concepts (integrity, ground projects, negative responsibility, the one-thought-too-many complaint) rather than lecturing about them. One design constraint I'd like honored: the objection is not that sacrificing a project is painful, but that being required to deliberate about it in the impartially maximizing way already alienates the agent from herself -- so the activity should make participants notice something about *how the rules made them think*, not merely what they chose.

Finally, tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students gaming the payoffs, collapsing it into a trolley problem, performing anguish for credit, and so on) and how to handle each failure mode; and which feature of Williams's objection the activity structurally cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied in discussion afterward.
