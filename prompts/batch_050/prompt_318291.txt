I'm a second-year PhD student in an ML safety group, and my advisor and I are drafting a survey where the backbone is a taxonomy of "specification gaming" -- cases where a system scores well on the proxy but fails on what the proxy was for. She has very strong intuitions about what counts, and we've been using her verdicts as the editorial line for the paper, but I genuinely cannot figure out the rule she's running, and we have a new case to classify by Friday.

Her verdicts so far: the cobra bounty story (British colonial officials paying for dead cobras, people breeding cobras for the bounty) -- clearly gaming. That old boat-racing RL example where the agent drives in circles hitting the same power-ups instead of finishing the race -- clearly gaming. A student who crams ten years of past exams and aces the final without understanding the material -- gaming, even though its a human. An RL policy that works fine in the training environment but falls apart in a slightly different environment it was never selected on -- not gaming, "that's just a generalization failure." A lab that picks the better of two job candidates using a noisy coding test -- not gaming, "every metric is noisy, that's measurement error not gaming." An LLM overoptimized against a reward model until it gets near-max reward for degenerate gibberish -- gaming. A school that narrows its curriculum to only what's on the standardized test, hollowing out actual education -- gaming.

Now the new case: a funding agency awards grants to the top 0.1% of researchers ranked by the previous year's citation count. Citations that year are an unbiased but very noisy proxy for underlying research quality. Nobody changes their behavior -- the metric isn't publicized in advance, no one is optimizing for it. The winners' citation counts the following year regress to barely above average, i.e. the selection mostly picked up noise. The agency would have done about as well with a lottery among the top decile. Predict her verdict -- gaming or not -- and tell me which rule you think she's implicitly been applying (selection-induced divergence between proxy and goal, even with no one optimizing? or does gaming require somebody's behavior or an agent's policy actually exploiting the metric?). Also tell me which further case would settle whatever ambiguity is left after your prediction.
