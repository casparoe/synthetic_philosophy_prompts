I need help settling a disagreement that ran for three hours last night and ended with neither of us conceding. My friend and I were arguing about Hobbes's philosophy of geometry. Her claim is that Hobbes, because he is a materialist, effectively collapses geometry into a branch of physics: geometrical objects for him are generated by the motion of bodies, so geometrical knowledge is ultimately empirical knowledge about how real bodies move. My claim is that this can't be right, because Hobbes is also famous for treating the sciences as resting on settled definitions — on names we have stipulated — which should make geometry hypothetical and conventional rather than a report about actual matter in motion. She says the conventionalism is superficial, since the relevant definitions smuggle motion in; I say the motion is itself something posited, not observed.

The problem is that neither of us had a copy of De Corpore at hand, and I am working from notes I made years ago. Here is the passage as I reconstructed it in my notebook — this is my paraphrase, not Hobbes's words, from the early chapters of the second part of De Corpore, with the chapter Of Method in the first part hovering in the background:

Philosophy is concerned only with things that have a generation, for we can only reason demonstratively about what we understand the making of. Magnitudes themselves are made by motion: a point, by its motion, produces a line; a line, by its motion, produces a surface; a surface, by its motion, produces a solid. Definitions of figures ought therefore to state the motion by which the figure is generated — a circle, for instance, should be defined through a straight line carried around one of its endpoints which remains fixed — because only a definition that exhibits how a thing comes to be lets us deduce why its properties must belong to it. A definition that merely marks the thing off from others, as by saying all lines from the centre to the circumference are equal, tells us what a circle is called without telling us what makes it, and so leaves its properties without a demonstrated cause.

Three things about this passage defeat me, and I'd like your help with them. First, what exactly is the motion doing in these definitions?
