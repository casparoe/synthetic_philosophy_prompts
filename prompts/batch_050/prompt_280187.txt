After a long and rather circular argument with a friend last night, I realised we were not disagreeing about Kavka’s toxin puzzle so much as about what the puzzle is a puzzle of. The setup, as we both understand it, is familiar enough: a very rich benefactor will pay you a large sum if, at midnight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make you violently ill but not kill you; once the intention has been detected and the money paid, you are perfectly free not to drink, and in fact drinking would be pointless suffering. My friend insists the impasse is practical: rationality cannot ask you to intend an act you already know you will have no reason to perform, so the right response is to refuse the whole arrangement as a trap for agency. I suspect instead that the debate keeps restarting because different camps smuggle in different pictures of intention: intention as a present commitment that binds the future self, intention as a confident forecast about what one will do, and intention as a plan whose rationality is assessed only at the moment of action. My own hypothesis, offered tentatively, is that the case is a mirror trap: the reward is attached to a state of mind whose content is a future act, while the very mechanism that rewards the state also removes the downstream reason that would normally stabilise it.

Please give a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and answer in British English. Is this fundamentally a disagreement about reasons for action versus reasons for intention, about the psychology of whether such an intention is even available to a reflective agent, about whether a person is one agent across time or a succession of locally rational momentary agents, or about the word intention being used to cover several different control relations? Engage the positions concretely: those who say you can win by intending and later not drinking, those who say you cannot form the intention once you foresee the later irrationality, those who distinguish deciding from merely resolving or accepting a policy, and those who think the case exposes a defect in idealised incentive stories rather than in ordinary agency. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper distinction, an argument about deliberative authority, a psychological claim that could in principle be tested, a reformulation of the puzzle, or a reason to think no resolution should be expected because the sides are answering different questions under one name.
