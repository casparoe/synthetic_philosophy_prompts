Read Kazuo Ishiguro's novel The Remains of the Day through the philosophy of incommensurable values and hard choices, and say what the novel has to teach that debate.

Stevens's life looks, at first, like a textbook hard choice between two genuine values: the professional ideal of dignified service, embodied in his career at Darlington Hall, and the possibility of intimacy and a shared life with Miss Kenton. Philosophers such as Joseph Raz and Ruth Chang argue that some options are related by none of the standard comparative relations — not better, not worse, not equal — but are instead, in Chang's phrase, on a par, and that in such cases reasons run out and the agent can only resolve the choice by throwing themselves behind one value and thereby making it matter more. Please test the novel against this framework rather than merely illustrating it.

In particular, address the following. First, take a position on whether Stevens's two options really were on a par, given that one horn of the dilemma is compromised: the greatness Stevens served is tainted by Lord Darlington's political naivety, so it is unclear whether Stevens faced a hard choice between two real values or simply made an ordinary mistake about the worth of one of them. Does the novel itself distinguish these two situations, or does it treat them as indistinguishable from inside a life?

Second, consider what I will call retrospective commensuration: the tendency of a life narrated from its end to convert a choice that was prospectively hard into one that now looks simply right or simply wrong. Stevens's narration performs this in both directions — decades of self-deception present the career as vindicated, until the final scenes let regret break through. If rational regret presupposes that the foregone option was genuinely better, then the regret Stevens finally permits himself seems to count against the on-a-par reading; alternatively, the novel may vindicate Bernard Williams's thought that even justified choices leave a moral remainder that is properly felt as loss. Say which diagnosis the novel supports, and whether retrospective commensuration is a cognitive defect or one of the legitimate ways agents create the comparability that hard-choice theory says reasons alone cannot supply.

Third, identify where the novel resists the philosophical model. Hard-choice theory standardly pictures a discrete decision between specified options; Stevens never faces such a moment. His loss of Miss Kenton is distributed across years of small omissions — doors passed without knocking, banter not returned, a father's death attended between duties at a conference. If the most consequential hard choices in a life are drift rather than decision, what does the theory need to add to accommodate this, and does the novel suggest that the actual phenomenology of on-a-par choice is not anguished deliberation but evasion?

I am preparing a seminar session pairing the novel with the literature on incommensurability and want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not flatten Stevens into an illustration of the theory, and it should not soften the theory to fit the book. End by saying what, if anything, the novel contributes to the debate about hard choices that philosophical argument alone does not.
