At a philosophy of mathematics reading group, two graduate students fell into an argument over whether 2 is an element of 4, and they asked me afterward to referee. I found I couldn't settle it, and I'd like a careful adjudication.

The exchange ran as follows. (1) Ana opened by defending the straightforward view that numbers are sets. On the standard von Neumann identification, 4 is the set {0, 1, 2, 3}, so 2 ∈ 4 is simply a theorem; the identification also delivers the existence of numbers for free, since set theory already guarantees the sets exist, and talk of numbers as mere "positions in a structure" is loose talk that eventually has to be cashed out in terms of objects anyway. (2) Ben replied that the existence of rival reductions refutes the identification: on Zermelo's older coding, 4 is { {{{}}} }, and 2 is not an element of it. Since both reductions deliver exactly the same arithmetic, nothing mathematical can decide between them, so neither can be what the numbers really are. Numbers, he said, are positions in the natural-number structure, and positions have only structural properties; so "2 ∈ 4" is not false but senseless — a category mistake, like asking whether the shortstop is made of wood.

(3) Ana answered that structuralism relocates the problem rather than solving it. Either structures are themselves objects, in which case the same embarrassment recurs — is the position 2 in the natural-number structure identical to the position 2 in the integer structure, or in the rational structure? — or structures are eliminated in favor of quantifying over all suitably ordered systems, in which case arithmetic comes out vacuously true if, as may well be the case, nothing infinite actually exists. (4) Ben conceded the second horn was serious but said it could be handled by modalizing: arithmetic states what would hold in any omega-sequence there could be, not what does hold in some actually existing one. The first horn, he said, is not a question structuralism fails to answer but one it correctly refuses to raise: nothing in the practice of arithmetic requires cross-structure identities, and a theory that declines to settle what practice leaves open is being precise, not evasive.

(5) Ana closed with an analogy she thought defused the original argument entirely. Choosing between von Neumann and Zermelo, she said, is like choosing between meters and feet: the choice is arbitrary, but once made it is harmless, and nobody concludes that lengths aren't real just because 2 meters and 6.56 feet pick out the same distance under different conventions. Claims like 2 ∈ 4 are true-relative-to-a-convention, and that is all they ever needed to be. Ben objected that the analogy proves too much: on her view, whether 2 ∈ 4 is a matter of stipulation, whereas whether 2 + 2 = 4 is not — but the reductionist has no principled account of why the arithmetic truths are convention-independent while the membership truths are convention-dependent, since both are delivered by the same identification.

Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it succeeds against the position it targets, and where it concedes or overlooks something. Then give an overall verdict: who is more nearly right, on which specific points, and what consideration — if any — both parties are missing.
