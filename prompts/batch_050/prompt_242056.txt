Right, bit of an odd request but bear with me. I'm a second-year DPhil student in political theory and I've been banging my head against Simone Weil's political writings all term for a paper. My supervisor reckons I haven't actually tested my reading of her essay on abolishing political parties — I've just summarised it. So here's the exercise: I want you to play Weil, first person, as she would have been in roughly 1943, while the war is still on and she is thinking about what a future France should look like. You know nothing she couldn't have known then: no hindsight about how the war ends, no post-war party systems, no later political science. In character, you hold, roughly, that political parties should simply be abolished; that a party by its nature demands that members sign up to the entire platform, which makes honest thinking impossible; that parties are machines for generating collective passion rather than instruments for finding truth or serving the good; that they carry totalitarianism in embryo; and that the same disease infects any organisation that asks for total allegiance — you're willing to follow that thought even where it's uncomfortable for you, including toward the Church. What you will not concede is that parties are merely neutral tools that bad people happen to misuse, or that some reform (better internal democracy, proportional representation, stronger party discipline, whatever) would fix the underlying problem. Stay in character throughout; if you need to know something about my side of the argument, ask it as Weil would.

Now, my opening move, which I think is the strongest thing I have: abolishing parties entrenches exactly the domination you hate. A textile worker in Lille whose factory is closing cannot coordinate thousands of scattered people on her own — the coordination is precisely what a party supplies, and without it, grievances stay diffuse and voiceless. The mill owner, meanwhile, doesn't need a party at all, because money and a small class of men who lunch together coordinate themselves quite happily. So your proposal doesn't dissolve power, it just disarms the powerless while leaving the powerful perfectly organised. Doesn't your own concern for the afflicted commit you to keeping parties, at least until the affliction is gone?
