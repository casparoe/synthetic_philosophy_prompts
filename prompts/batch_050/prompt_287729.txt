Is there an actual name for this, or do I have to make one up? Everyone I describe it to says "Goodhart's law" and moves on, but I don't think it's quite that, and the difference seems to matter.

The pattern I keep seeing: an organization picks a measurable proxy for the thing it actually wants. The proxy is a good one, and here's the important part -- nobody ever games it. The numbers stay honest the whole time. What happens instead is slower and nobody does it on purpose. The proxy becomes the basis for hiring, promotion, and budget fights, so over ten or fifteen years the people who are great at the underlying thing but mediocre at the proxy leave, or never get hired in the first place, and the institution's actual capacity to produce the real thing quietly atrophies. The dashboard looks fine all the way down. By the time anyone notices, there's nobody left who could even tell you what's missing, because noticing was itself one of the skills that got selected against.

Concrete versions I've watched or heard about from people I trust: a school where the test scores are genuinely earned -- no teaching to the test, the exams really do capture something -- but over a decade every hire is someone whose strength shows up on the scores, and the teachers who ran the music program and the after-school stuff retire and are replaced by more of the scoring kind, and nobody can say when the place stopped being good, because by every official measure it never did. Or a hospital where the measured outcomes stay excellent while the unmeasured skill -- experienced nurses catching that something is off with a patient before the numbers move -- evaporates as scheduling and staffing get optimized around the measured stuff. In both cases the proxy wasn't corrupted. The population around it was.

So: does this have its own name? I get that it's in the neighborhood of Goodhart and Campbell's law, but those are usually told as stories about the measure going bad under pressure, and here the measure stays clean while the thing it pointed at dies of neglect. If the established answer really is "this is just Goodhart," I want to know which part of the mechanism the standard accounts actually cover and which they miss. And if there's genuinely no name, coin one, define it carefully, and give me a couple of cases that fall just outside it, so I can tell the boundries.
