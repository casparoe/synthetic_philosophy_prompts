In my historiography seminar we just finished the unit on cyclical theories of history, and a classmate keeps leaning on an analogy I don't know how to assess. He says a civilization is like an organism: it is born, grows, matures, grows old, and dies on a schedule it cannot choose, so talk of deliberately "renewing" an aging civilization is like talk of making an old man young again -- well-meaning but confused about what kind of thing a civilization is. He deploys this to argue that the West's trajectory is fixed and that projects of cultural or political renewal are category errors rather than merely difficult. Could you audit the analogy for me? Set out the correspondence table -- what plays the role of birth, growth, maturity, senescence, and death, and whether anything in a civilization corresponds to a genome, to cells, or to aging at the cellular level. Then show me where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that actually bear on his fatalist conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic, and tell me whether the conclusion survives once they are all on the table. If there is a better analogy for what he is trying to capture -- something about rigidity, lost adaptability, or institutional aging -- I would like to hear what it is and what it would license instead.
