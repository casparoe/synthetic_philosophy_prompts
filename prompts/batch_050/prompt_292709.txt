I work as a control systems engineer in industrial automation, and I am fluent in that field's working vocabulary: feedback and feedforward loops, setpoints and reference signals, plant models, stability and robustness margins, fault detection and isolation, fail-safe design, safety instrumented systems, runtime monitors, verification and validation, and supervisory control. I have been reading about machine ethics — the project of implementing ethical reasoning in machines — and I keep mentally translating the debate into engineering terms, probably badly. I would like you to do the translation properly and, above all, to tell me where it breaks down.

Please restate the following elements of the machine ethics debate in control-theoretic vocabulary, as faithfully as you can:

1. The standard threefold classification of implementation strategies: top-down approaches, which take an explicit ethical theory and encode it as rules or constraints the machine must follow; bottom-up approaches, which let ethical behavior emerge through learning, development, or evolution in a social environment; and hybrid approaches combining the two.
2. The distinction, common in the literature, between machines whose ethics is merely implicit (harmful outcomes are engineered away at design time, without the machine representing anything ethical at all), machines that function as explicit ethical agents (representing and reasoning over ethical categories at runtime), and the further question of whether any machine could be a full ethical agent, possessing something like moral agency of its own.
3. The standard objections each strategy attracts: that top-down systems inherit the blind spots and brittleness of whichever theory was encoded; that bottom-up systems cannot be verified against any specification and may learn the wrong values; and that hybrids merely relocate the disagreement rather than resolving it.

For each element, give the best control-theoretic rendering, then state explicitly what had to be distorted to fit and what has no expression in that vocabulary at all. Be honest in both directions: do not smuggle in control theory's assumptions — that there is a specifiable objective, that correctness is verifiable against a model of the plant — and present them as part of the original philosophical position.

Then the residue report. Which parts of the machine ethics debate have no control-theoretic counterpart? I suspect that the distinction between a machine that reliably produces ethical outcomes and a machine that is ethical — the whole cluster of questions about normativity, moral uncertainty, and whether correctness can be defined independently of any specification — will land in the residue. If so, say whether that reveals a gap in my engineering vocabulary or instead reveals that the philosophers' distinction is doing less work than it appears to.

One more thing. In my own notes I have been using a distinction of my own devising, and I want to know whether it survives contact with the philosophical debate. I distinguish specification-conforming machines, which behave correctly relative to an ethical specification imposed from outside, from specification-critical machines, which can detect and flag cases in which the specification itself is wrong, incomplete, or underspecified. Is this a genuine addition to the implicit/explicit/full-agent typology, or is it merely the explicit-agent idea restated with engineering cosmetics? Where, if anywhere, does specification-criticism sit on that ladder of agency?

Structure your answer as prose analysis first, organized by the three numbered elements, followed by the residue report and your assessment of my distinction.
