Last weekend a friend and I got into a genuinely heated argument over the St. Petersburg paradox -- the game where a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and the payout doubles with each flip (2 if heads comes immediately, 4 if on the second flip, 8 on the third, and so on). She insisted that since the expected payoff is infinite, any rational person should pay an enormous sum to play once; I said no sane person would pay even fifty dollars, and that this tells us something is wrong with the game or with expected value, not with sane people. We eventually realized we weren't even disagreeing about the same fix -- she wanted to keep expected value and patch utility, I wanted to scrap the decision rule -- and that the published landscape of responses is bigger than either of us knew. So I want to build a decision tree that sorts a person by their actual commitments about the game, not by which resolution they've heard of.

Please write that decision tree. It should start from "I agree the naive expected payoff of the game is infinite and that this seems to demand too high an entry fee" and end at named positions. Build the tree from the logical space where possible: the nodes should be diagnostic questions a person can answer without knowing the literature, such as whether the utility of money must be bounded, whether a real casino's finite bankroll (and hence a truncated payout schedule) dissolves the puzzle or merely hides it, whether the answer should change if the game is played repeatedly rather than once, whether the problem lies in the decision rule (maximizing expectation) rather than in the prizes, and whether the setup is even well-posed as stated. The leaves should include at least: Bernoulli-style diminishing marginal utility (e.g., logarithmic utility, so the expected utility is finite), the bounded-utility response that treats the paradox as showing unbounded utility functions are inadmissible, the finite-bankroll/truncation response, a growth-rate or time-average response for the repeated game, and a response that rejects expectation-maximization in favor of a risk-sensitive or typical-outcome criterion. If there are combinations of answers your questions allow that no actual position occupies, note them and say briefly why you think they're empty (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Phrase every node so someone could answer it without already knowing which leaf it leads to, and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't cleanly separate.

Finally, since my friend will read this and is looking for any excuse to restart the argument: indicate how confident you are that your tree carves the debate at its real joints rather than at superficial ones, and single out the one node or leaf-placement you are least sure of, with a sentence on why.
