I'm putting together the final exam for my upper-level course on the ethics of prediction and I need one essay question on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness (the Chouldechova / Kleinberg-style results that calibration and equalized-error-rate conditions can't all hold at once). The problem is that every year half the class writes some version of "you can't have everything, tradeoffs are hard, so fairness is subjective" and I want a question that blocks that move. Specifically, the question should force students to (a) state precisely what the impossibility theorems actually assume — unequal base rates, imperfect prediction, the metrics being properties of the score or classifier rather than of any decision made downstream of it — (b) explain why the result is a mathematical theorem and not an argument about values, and what follows and doesn't follow from that about whether fairness is "subjective," and (c) take a stand on whether the right response is to pick one metric, question the base rates themselves, or deny that group metrics on risk scores are the right locus of fairness in the first place. Give me the question (something answerable in 75 minutes), a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 broken into five must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a one-line note per point on how the weaker answers typically fumble it (e.g., conflating calibration with accuracy, or treating "the math says you can't have both" as if it settles which one to drop).
