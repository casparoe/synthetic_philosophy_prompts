At a recent colloquium jointly hosted by my university's neuroscience and philosophy departments, a philosopher of mind closed a heated exchange by declaring that mental states are multiply realizable: the same psychological state — pain, a belief, a working-memory representation — can be realized in a human brain, in an octopus's nervous system, or in a suitably organized computer, and that, consequently, the details of neural implementation are irrelevant to psychology as a science. The neuroscientists in the room took this as a dismissal of their discipline; the philosophers appeared to regard it as a near-truism. I suspect both reactions track different sentences wearing the same words, and I would like the ambiguity resolved properly rather than by departmental instinct.

Please disambiguate the slogan. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that 'mental states are multiply realizable' could be expressing. I have in mind a spectrum running from the thin claim that alternative realizations are logically or nomologically possible, through claims about type- versus token-identity and the legitimacy of cross-species psychological generalizations, up to substantive functionalist theses about how mental-state kinds are individuated, methodological theses about the autonomy of psychology from neuroscience, and substrate-independence claims of the sort invoked in debates about machine consciousness. For each reading: state it precisely; classify it as near-platitudinous, substantive-but-defensible, or probably false, with a sentence or two of justification; and indicate what kind of evidence or argument would genuinely bear on it, as opposed to merely being associated with it in the literature.

Then do the diagnostic work the slogan was being made to do at the colloquium. Identify which reading or readings the speaker actually needed in order to derive the conclusion that neural implementation is irrelevant to psychology, and say whether the inference from that reading to that conclusion is valid. If the conclusion requires a stronger thesis than any plausible reading of the slogan, state that stronger thesis explicitly and assess whether anything the neuroscientists present actually do — lesion and stimulation studies, neural decoding, comparative work across species, the observation of degenerate neural implementations of a single function — counts for or against it. Finally, where two readings differ only in how finely the relevant 'functional organization' is individuated, say so explicitly: I suspect a large share of the disagreement lives in the grain of description rather than in any factual dispute, and I would like to know whether that suspicion survives your analysis.
