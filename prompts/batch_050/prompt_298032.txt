In Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead, the play opens with a coin coming up heads dozens of times in a row, and Guildenstern cycles through increasingly strained attempts to explain the streak -- divine intervention, his own willing of the result, a failure in the laws of probability -- before the play settles into its real subject: two characters trapped inside a narrative they cannot see, trying to infer the rules of their world from the evidence available to them. I want to teach the play alongside Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, and I need a reading that holds both sides steady. The connection I see is this: the principle of indifference says to spread probability evenly over outcomes when you have no reason to distinguish them, and Bertrand's paradox shows that what counts as 'no reason to distinguish' depends entirely on how you carve up the space of possibilities -- the same ignorance yields different probabilities under different descriptions. The play seems to dramatize exactly this instability: Guildenstern's predicament is not just that the coin defies his prior, but that he cannot settle on the reference class or the outcome space within which the streak should be evaluated (fair coin flipped many times? a play performed every night? a world where physics is optional?), and every interpretation of the evidence is hostage to a framing he cannot justify from inside. Please give me a careful reading of the play through this lens. Where does the principle of indifference actually do work in the characters' reasoning, and where is Stoppard only gesturing at probability language for comic or existential effect? Does the play present the heroes' inability to choose a description of their situation as a philosophical problem in its own right -- that is, does it understand Bertrand's point that indifference is not a foundation but a choice among parameterizations -- or does it treat their confusion as merely a symptom of being minor characters in someone else's plot? How does the Player, who alone seems at ease with the arbitrariness of roles and outcomes, stand in relation to someone who has simply stopped asking which parameterization is correct? Point me to the scenes and beats (the opening coin sequence, the discussions of death and its unobservability, the repeated failures to act on what they know) that best repay this reading, and be honest about where the analogy strains: probability theory has fixed formalisms and the play has dramatic license, and I don't want to tell my students the fit is tighter than it is. Finally, say what, if anything, the play contributes to the philosophical debate that the formal treatments don't -- whether fiction can make vivid something about lived ignorance under multiple descriptions that a paper on Bertrand's paradox cannot.
