Something about causation I keep chewing on and I want to know if philosophers have a term for it. Say a gardener is hired to water a plant, forgets, and the plant dies. Everyone agrees: his not watering it is what killed it. But it's equally true that I didn't water the plant, and neither did the Queen of Denmark or some random guy in São Paulo, and nobody says our not watering it killed the plant. As far as I can tell the counterfactuals come out the same in every case: had any one of us watered it, it would have lived. So there must be some extra ingredient that singles the gardener out, and it looks like it has to do with what he was supposed to do — if the whole street had a standing arrangement to water it, maybe their failures would count too. My question: does this extra ingredient, and the line it draws between the failures that count as causes and the infinitely many that don't, have an established name in the literature on causation by omission? I know there's a debate about whether omissions can literally cause anything at all, but I'm asking about the downstream question — assuming some omissions do cause, what selects which ones? Tell me what the main proposals are, how each deals with awkward variations (everyone in town was equally obligated to water it and nobody did — so who killed it then? or: nobody was obligated, but watering plants is just what decent people do), and where each proposal strains. And say whether whatever machinery answers this is supposed to be the same machinery that picks out "the" cause among ordinary positive conditions, or whether omissions are genuinely special. I'm not after a bare label, I want the carving. Plain prose please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, just written out like an email.
