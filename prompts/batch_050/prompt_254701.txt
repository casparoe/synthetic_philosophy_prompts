I am finalizing the statement of my own commitments for the opening session of a graduate seminar I teach on skepticism and the analysis of knowledge, and before I circulate it to the students I would like an external consistency check. My seminar tends to attract sharp auditors, and I would rather have the problems found now than in week one. Here, as neutrally as I can state them, are the six things I currently hold. (1) I am convinced that some condition in the neighborhood of Nozick's sensitivity requirement is genuinely necessary for knowledge: S knows that p only if, were p false, S would not have believed p, where the counterfactual is assessed relative to the method S actually employed in forming the belief; I am open to refinements at the margins, but I am wedded to the counterfactual, tracking-style core rather than to plain reliability. (2) I take it as a fixed point that I know perfectly ordinary things — that I have two hands, that there is a teacup on my desk as I write this — and any theory that denies this wholesale strikes me as refuted rather than radical. (3) Just as firmly, I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat, and I hold that global skeptical hypotheses of that kind are not the sort of thing one can know to be false by any means available to me. (4) I accept unrestricted single-premise closure: if S knows that p, and competently deduces q from p while appreciating the entailment, then S knows that q, at least where the deduction is short and transparent. (5) I hold that I do not know that my ticket in next week's state lottery will lose, no matter how long the odds, and I want the theory of knowledge itself to deliver this verdict rather than some bolt-on clause stipulating it. (6) I hold that whether a belief satisfies whatever counterfactual condition we settle on should not turn on how finely or coarsely we describe the believer's method; knowledge attributions that flip depending on whether we call the method "perception" or "visual perception under good lighting in a quiet room" are, in my view, a defect in an account rather than a discovery.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture on the literature. First, tell me which pairs (or larger subsets) of these six commitments are in genuine tension, and rank those tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally grave; for each genuine tension, state precisely what has to give, what the cheapest available repair is, and what that repair costs me in terms of the other commitments. Second, identify any apparent tensions in the set that dissolve on inspection — places where two commitments look as though they pull against each other but in fact do not, or where the appearance of conflict is parasitic on some third commitment of mine — and show the dissolving step explicitly rather than merely asserting it. Third, having mapped the terrain, tell me which single revision to the package would buy the most consistency for the least damage, assuming I want to preserve as much of the counterfactual spirit of (1) as I can without prejudging which of the others I would sacrifice. Please be specific about the mechanics — which counterfactuals come out true or false under which readings — rather than gesturing at familiar dialectical positions. And please write your response in American English.
