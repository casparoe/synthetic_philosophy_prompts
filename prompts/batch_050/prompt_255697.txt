I’m a retired chemist who has fallen down the philosophy hole, and I keep hitting the wrong kind of reasons problem. From lab life I made myself an analogy and now I’m suspicious of it: an attitude like admiration, fear, or desire is a bit like an instrument reading. Right-kind reasons are like features of the sample that should move a properly calibratng detector — cruelty really is a reason not to admire someone, the cliff edge really is a reason to fear it. Wrong-kind reasons are like bribing the technician or threatening to smash the meter: the demon will torture me unless I admire him, or I get paid if I want the nasty toxin. Those give me a reason to bring about the attitude, but not a reason that makes the attitude fitting.

How far does this hold? Give me the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of sample, instrument, calibration, readout, noise, technician, bribe, repair bill, and so on. Then pull apart where it breaks. In particular: instruments have a target quantity independent of the reading, but do admiration and fear have “target quantities” in the same way, or is that already smuggling in the fittingness I’m trying to explain? A mis-set pH meter is still about pH; is terror at a harmless moth still about danger or just a glitch? And the bribe case may be too clean — sometimes incentives, threats, therapy, and self-manipulation seem to change what is worth valuing by changing me, not merely by paying the meter.

Most of all, tell me what work the analogy can do. I want to use it to argue that wrong-kind reasons never count toward whether an attitude is fitting, only toward whether you should cause it, and that this saves a buck-passing line: to be admirable is roughly to have properties that give object-given reasons for admiration. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was my lab picture quietly guaranteeing it? If there is a better analogy than instruments — maybe diagnosis, legal verdicts, aiming, or perception — say what it fixes and what it costs. Also flag any places where my retired-scientist habit of treating norms like measurement error is leading me off a cliff.
