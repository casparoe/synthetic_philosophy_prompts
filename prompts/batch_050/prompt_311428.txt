DRAFT: "Indifference Isn't Ambiguous, You're Ambiguous"

Everyone who teaches probability dusts off Bertrand's chord paradox. Inscribe an equilateral triangle in a circle, pick a chord "at random," and ask for the probability that it's longer than the triangle's side. Depending on how you generate the chord you get three different answers. Pick two random endpoints on the circumference: 1/3. Pick a random radius and then a random point on it to be the chord's midpoint: 1/2. Pick a random point inside the circle to be the midpoint: 1/4. The standard moral is that the principle of indifference is broken: "equally likely" depends on which variable you spread your ignorance over, and nothing picks the right variable.

I want to push back. I think the paradox comes from applying indifference at the wrong level. A chord isn't a primitive object floating in conceptual space; it gets produced by some actual process. Once you tell me the mechanism -- you spun a pointer, you dropped a straw, you threw a dart -- the ambiguity is gone, because the mechanism singles out the variable that should get the uniform distribution. Indifference was never ambiguous. We were ambiguous about what we were randomizing. Jaynes famously defended something in this neighborhood, using invariance under transformations to argue that one of the three answers is privileged for the straw-dropping case, and I take my essay to be a popularization of the spirit of that move, though I don't use his machinery.

The obvious objection, which I spend the middle of the essay on: usually we don't know the mechanism. That's the whole point of wanting a prior. So here's my fallback proposal. Enumerate the simple mechanisms consistent with what you do know, compute the target probability under each, and -- and this is where I think the interesting move is -- don't average them. Take the whole range as your answer. Your rational credence is interval-valued, [1/4, 1/2], and Bertrand's paradox stops being an objection to indifference and becomes the derivation of why credences in hard cases have to be imprecise. The paradox is the solution wearing a disguise.

The last section considers the regress: picking which mechanisms count as "simple," or averaging over mechanisms with equal weight, requires a meta-indifference over the space of mechanisms, and Bertrand's paradox just reappears one floor up. I argue this kills the averaging version of the fallback but spares the interval version, because taking the full range commits you to nothing about the meta-distribution. So the essay ends up endorsing interval credences as the honest residue of the paradox.
