I'm a third-year undergraduate double-majoring in statistics and philosophy, and this term I'm taking a course on causal reasoning while also working at my university's drop-in statistics consulting desk, where other students bring their project data for help. Roughly once a week someone shows up with a version of the same situation: their dataset shows one trend overall (a treatment looks beneficial, one group looks favored, a metric is improving), but when they split the data by some variable (department, age band, hospital, baseline severity), the trend flips in every subgroup, or in most of them. My usual instinct -- "always trust the disaggregated numbers" -- is something our instructor explicitly warned against, since sometimes the right move is to trust the aggregate, sometimes the split, and sometimes neither. I'd like to stop improvising.

Please write me a decision procedure I can run in a thirty-minute consultation with a client who is not a statistics major: an ordered series of questions to ask about their dataset and their causal story, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, and what the procedure outputs at each step -- report the aggregate trend, report the subgroup trends, report both with an explanation, or decline to report either pending more information. The procedure should be grounded in what people who've thought carefully about this phenomenon actually say: that the reversal itself is a purely arithmetical fact and cannot by itself tell you which partition matters, so the decision has to turn on the causal role of the splitting variable. In particular, make sure the procedure forces the client to distinguish at least the case where the variable is a common cause of treatment and outcome (condition on it), the case where the variable lies on the causal path between treatment and outcome (do not condition on it, or you'll hide the very effect being measured), and the case where the variable is a common effect (conditioning introduces a spurious association). Where the client's causal story is missing or contested, the procedure should say so plainly and stop, rather than pretend the arithmetic settles anything -- I want the points where judgment cannot be eliminated marked as such, not smoothed over.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical consults: (1) an admissions dataset where one program admits a higher proportion of applicants overall, but every department within it admits a lower proportion of each applicant group; (2) a drug trial where recovery rates are higher for the drug overall, but lower within both the high-post-treatment-blood-pressure and low-post-treatment-blood-pressure subgroups; and (3) a hiring dataset where the client cannot tell me whether the splitting variable was measured before or after the hiring decision, and the outcome where the procedure gives an answer neither the client nor I will like. Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do -- for instance, whether it can ever settle which causal story is true, or whether that always has to come from outside the data.
