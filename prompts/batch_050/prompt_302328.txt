I teach an upper-level seminar in normative ethics, and each year I watch my students race past the hardest part of deontology. They absorb the slogan that there are agent-relative constraints — that one may not deliberately kill an innocent person even to prevent several other deliberate killings of innocents — and then treat the obvious objection as a knockdown: if such killings are so terrible that the constraint exists, why isn't it permissible, even obligatory, to commit one killing when that is the only way to prevent five? My students call this "the paradox of deontology" and generally conclude that deontologists are squeamish or confused. I want to show them the position defended at full strength, so I would like you to write me a model dialogue (roughly 900–1300 words) between two equally competent moral philosophers who genuinely disagree here.

Give them this shared case as a touchstone, and have them return to it whenever the discussion drifts into pure abstraction: a regional governor learns that an armed group is holding five civilians and will execute them at dawn. The group's leader, whose credibility the scenario stipulates, will release all five on a single condition — that the governor personally order the execution of one specific innocent prisoner, a dissident journalist the leader wants dead. No rescue is possible; no third option exists. One philosopher, a consequentialist, should press the paradox carefully and in its strongest form: the constraint's own rationale seems to demand its violation; the deontologist appears to care more about the cleanliness of her own hands than about the five who will die; and there is a symmetry worry about victims — why should an innocent person's claim not to be killed depend on who is holding the knife? The consequentialist should also offer a fallback the deontologist must explicitly refuse: that constraints can be fully captured as agent-relative values within a consequentialist framework, so that "deontology" is really just consequentialism with a fancier objective function.

The deontologist must give at least two genuinely different responses rather than one response rephrased — for instance, one centered on the victim's status (what it means that persons are not to be used or destroyed, and why the five deaths at the group's hands and the one death at the governor's hands are not five-plus-one tokens of the same bad event) and one centered on the agent's perspective (why one's own wrongdoing is not simply one more state of affairs to be weighed in the cosmic ledger). Crucially, she must explain why these responses answer the paradox rather than merely relabel it, and she must be pressed on threshold deontology: if she concedes that the constraint yields somewhere — five hostages no, five million perhaps yes — has she saved the constraint or quietly conceded the consequentialist's entire framework? Neither character may be a strawman. Each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than reciting a prepared position; the deontologist should acknowledge the five deaths as a genuine and grievous moral loss rather than a technicality, and the consequentialist should acknowledge the intuitive force of the refusal. I would like the dialogue to end not with a winner but with a precise, jointly reached statement of exactly where the remaining disagreement lies — which premise or intuition the two sides cannot share.
