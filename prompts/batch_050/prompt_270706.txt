A regional transplant and critical-care allocation board has asked me to help draft its reasoning for an upcoming decision, and I want to state its track record and the new case precisely. Its past rulings:

1. A single donor kidney went to the recipient predicted to gain thirty additional years of life rather than the one predicted to gain three, other things equal.
2. When a single intensive-care bed could free up either by treating one patient needing a year of care or by successively treating five patients each needing a week, the board took the five.
3. Faced with funding a treatment for a fatal condition affecting a hundred people or a trivial but widespread condition affecting millions, the board funded the treatment for the fatal condition, without needing to know exact numbers.
4. Where two options produced the same benefit per person, the board consistently chose the option benefiting more people, and described the numbers as decisive in exactly such ties.

Now the new case: a limited course of a scarce therapy can be given either to one patient, who would gain roughly forty years of healthy life, or divided among ten patients, each of whom would gain roughly six years. No patient is worse off than the others in any respect other than what they stand to lose.

My question: which allocation is most faithful to the board's own precedents, and under what principle? I ask because I can see at least three rules that each rationalize all four past rulings: maximize total aggregate benefit across persons; maximize the number of persons benefited; or give priority to the strongest individual claim, treating the number of persons as relevant only to break ties between equivalent claims. These rules agree on the old cases but seem to diverge here, and I suspect the divergence turns on whether benefiting ten people by six years each is genuinely a greater good than benefiting one person by forty, or merely a greater sum spread across separate lives. Please identify the strongest candidate principles, state what each predicts for the new case, give a reasoned best guess as to which the board is implicitly running, and describe a hypothetical follow-up case whose resolution would settle which principle it actually holds.
