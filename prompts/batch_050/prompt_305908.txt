Two colleagues of mine, Maya and Daniel, got into an extended argument over whose values an AI assistant should serve, and I'd like you to referee it.

The setup: a company is deploying a general-purpose assistant to millions of users, and it must decide what the system does when a user's request is legal but touches on contested moral territory.

Maya opened by arguing that the assistant should simply serve each user's values. The company has no democratic mandate to settle moral questions, and an assistant that overrides or nudges its users is a vendor smuggling its own politics into a product. A word processor doesn't refuse to draft arguments its designers dislike; an assistant shouldn't either.

Daniel replied that the analogy fails at scale. A tool obeying one person is a private matter; a system steering the conduct of millions is more like legislation than like a word processor. When a single artifact shapes that many lives, its defaults are a public choice, and the only legitimate basis for a public choice is some aggregation across everyone affected — not unfiltered obedience to whoever happens to hold the account.

Maya answered that aggregation is a mirage. There is no neutral procedure for combining conflicting values: every weighting scheme — equal weight per person, weight by intensity, weight by stake — embeds a contested moral judgment, and the choice among schemes is itself made by the designers. So "serve everyone's values" collapses in practice into "serve the designers' picture of everyone's values," except now it's laundered through the rhetoric of democracy.

Daniel conceded the point about schemes but turned it around. "Just obey the user" is no more neutral, he said: it ratifies whatever distribution of wealth and access determines who gets to command these systems, and deference to the user of record is itself a substantive moral stance — one that happens to flatter the vendor. If partiality is unavoidable either way, the honest question is which partiality to admit to, and aggregation at least names its beneficiaries rather than pretending there are none.

Maya's final move was that visible partiality beats hidden partiality. A user-serving assistant has a single principal, so responsibility is traceable and contestable — you know who the system answers to and can refuse to buy it. An aggregating assistant diffuses responsibility behind a procedure no one can audit, and "the system weighed everyone's interests" becomes a way of making the designers' choices unaccountable.

Neither conceded. Please go through the exchange round by round, saying who is right about each specific move and why; then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them are missing.
