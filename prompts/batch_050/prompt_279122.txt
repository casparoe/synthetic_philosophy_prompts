I work for a regional emergency management office, and three months ago we were burned badly: we committed resources -- prepositioned crews, a public announcement, a canceled festival -- on the basis of a seasonal flood outlook that turned out to be wrong, while a short-range forecast from the same modeling center that same season was accurate almost to the hour. My director's takeaway was "the models are unreliable," which is too crude, and the modeling center's takeaway was "probability is probability," which is too convenient. What I've come to understand from reading around is that both are missing the actual distinction that matters: a system can be fully deterministic and still place hard limits on how far ahead anyone can predict it, and those limits vary by system, by question, and by timescale rather than by how good the modelers are.

Write me a triage procedure my office can run on any predictive output we're offered -- weather and flood forecasts, seasonal outlooks, epidemic projections, long-range infrastructure risk assessments -- that sorts it into one of three buckets: (1) treat as a dependable basis for committing resources, (2) treat as scenario input for planning under uncertainty, or (3) decline to act on it at all. The procedure should be grounded in the conceptual distinction between determinism and predictability and in what follows from sensitive dependence on initial conditions, but usable by staff with no background in dynamics: a sequence of questions to ask the modelers (about error growth and ensemble spread, about the ratio of the forecast horizon to the system's characteristic divergence timescale, about whether the uncertainty comes from initial conditions, from model structure, or from inputs that are themselves chaotic), with definite answers that count as sufficient for each bucket. Don't give me a menu of considerations and leave the weighting to me -- commit to thresholds and ordering, give reasons for the choices, and mark explicitly the steps where a number can't be had and what default the procedure applies instead.

Then run the procedure on three cases, in writing: a 72-hour river-level forecast from a well-validated hydrological model; the same center's seasonal flood outlook of the kind that burned us; and a 30-year flood-frequency projection used to justify moving a substation. At least one of these should come out with a verdict my director won't want to hear, and the write-up should say so plainly rather than softening it. Finally, state the procedure's known failure modes: the kinds of predictive claims it will misclassify, and the situations (if any) where the chaos-based framing itself is the wrong tool and the office should be asking a different question entirely.
