In a few weeks I am leading one 75-minute session of a third-year undergraduate epistemology seminar on the dogmatism paradox about knowledge and future evidence: the line of argument that, since knowledge entails truth, anyone who knows that p is thereby in a position to know that any future evidence against p is misleading evidence, and so seems licensed to dismiss such evidence in advance of receiving it — a conclusion that appears to turn knowledge into a warrant for closed-mindedness. The students have worked through Gettier cases, closure-based skeptical arguments, and a short unit on defeaters, but they have no epistemic logic and nothing Bayesian, and I want the paradox to land as a problem about the structure of justification, not as a curiosity.

Rather than presenting the material, plan the session as a teaching arc with the following components, and justify each choice.

1. The dependency order. What has to be securely in place before the paradox registers as a paradox rather than as a triviality or a non sequitur? Candidate components include: the distinction between evidence one possesses and evidence one might later acquire; the claim that misleading evidence is nonetheless evidence and can still justify belief; the difference between being justified in believing p and being justified in disregarding considerations against p; and the closure principle for known entailments. State the order in which you would introduce these, which of them depend on which, and at what point closure should be made explicit rather than left implicit, given that the students' only prior exposure to closure is in skeptical arguments where it is the villain.

2. The carrying example. I want a single example to run through the entire session, revisited at each stage. Specify what such an example must have — an uncontentious case of present knowledge rather than mere justified belief, a concrete and credible source of future counterevidence, stakes low enough that students reason about the structure rather than the prudence — and what it must lack, since I want to keep lottery and preface structure out of the room and to avoid cases where the subject's knowledge would obviously be destroyed by the very possibility of the counterevidence. Give your chosen example, name two plausible alternatives that fail, and explain what each failure would do to the session.

3. The misconception map. Identify the three ways students with this background predictably misread the puzzle — for instance, hearing it as a psychological observation about stubbornness rather than a claim about justification; taking the argument to equivocate on what it is for evidence to be misleading; or conflating being licensed to discount evidence one can antecedently identify as misleading with being licensed to refuse to update at all. For each misreading, state where in your sequence it will arise, why that point in the sequence invites it, and how to head it off without defusing the paradox prematurely — in particular, without letting students conclude too early that the argument is just an obvious fallacy.

4. The check for understanding. Specify what students should be able to do by the end that would show the paradox has landed rather than merely been heard: I want something stronger than "you shouldn't ignore evidence," ideally the ability to identify which premise of the argument they would reject — that knowledge entails truth, that known entailments preserve knowledge, that misleading evidence can be rationally discounted, or something subtler — and to state what rejecting that premise costs elsewhere in epistemology. Describe the exercise or question you would use to elicit this and what a merely verbal understanding would look like so I can recognize it.

5. What to leave out. Indicate which standard responses to the paradox should be named and deferred to later sessions and which should be omitted entirely at this level — considering, for example, Bayesian accounts of evidence and conditionalization, knowledge-first treatments of justification, and contextualism about the paradox's own conclusion — and give a reason for each decision in terms of what the students currently have the tools to understand.

The plan should be concrete enough that a colleague could run the session from it, and every ordering decision should be defended by appeal to what depends on what, not merely to what seems natural.
