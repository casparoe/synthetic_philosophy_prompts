I'm preparing materials for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I'd like a dialogue I can use as a discussion piece. Write a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about how rational agents should update on uncertain evidence. One character, a defender of Jeffrey conditionalization, holds that ordinary Bayesian conditionalization is a special case that only works when evidence arrives with certainty, and that the general rule is probability kinematics: when experience shifts your probabilities directly on a partition without rendering any cell certain, you update by weighting your old conditional probabilities by the new partition probabilities. The other character is an orthodox Bayesian reductionist who insists that there is no genuinely uncertain evidence: any case that looks like one (say, glimpsing a cloth in dim light and coming to believe with probability 0.7 that it's green) is really a case of conditionalizing with certainty on a richer proposition about the character of your experience, and Jeffrey's rule is at best a shortcut, at worst a source of error.

Make sure both characters are equally sharp and actually engage each other's arguments. The reductionist should press the real weaknesses of Jeffrey conditionalization: that the rule takes the new probabilities on the partition as exogenously given and so says nothing about where they rationally come from, and that Jeffrey updates can fail to commute with subsequent conditionalization in ways that pure conditionalization never does. The Jeffrey defender should respond with the real weaknesses of the reductionist move: that it requires an evidential proposition rich enough to fully capture the experience (and the defender should challenge whether agents can even formulate, let alone conditionalize on, such propositions), and that it threatens to make all evidence first-person and private in a way that misdescribes public scientific updating. Neither character should win decisively; the dialogue should end with each having conceded something and with the live options clearly stated. Aim for roughly 600–900 words, with the characters named and the exchanges substantive rather than performative.
