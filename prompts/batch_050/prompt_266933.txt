Longtime reader of LessWrong and adjacent blogs, so I've absorbed the standard line that satisficing is just maximizing once you price in search and deliberation costs — a useful heuristic, not a genuinely rival decision rule. I want a case that stress-tests that reduction. Construct a thought experiment in which a satisficer strictly outperforms an expected-utility maximizer under conditions where the reduction cannot be invoked: search is costless, deliberation is free, and both agents face the same fixed, fully known menu of options. Constraints: the setting must be realistic (hiring, housing, procurement, grant-making — no casinos, genies, or perfect predictors); the scenario itself must be one paragraph; and the satisficer's edge must fall out of the aspiration-level mechanism itself rather than from the maximizer having worse information, worse luck, or higher costs. After the scenario: walk through what each agent does and why the maximizer ends up behind; name the single feature of the case doing the work and describe what happens if it's removed; and give the strongest objection available to someone who holds that rational satisficing is always constrained maximization in disguise, saying whether the case survives it. Close by stating what would change your verdict — which consideration would convince you that the specifications are jointly unsatisfiable — and where your construction is most likely to have smuggled a hidden search cost back in. Begin with the scenario paragraph itself, no preface; then the analysis.
