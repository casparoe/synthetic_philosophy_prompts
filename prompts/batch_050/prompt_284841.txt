I'm a PhD student working on standing to blame, and my supervisor keeps leaning on the legal analogy: a hypocrite who condemns you for something they regularly do themselves "lacks standing" to blame you, the way a plaintiff with no stake in the case lacks standing in court. Around my department this gets used to argue that hypocritical blame is void — not just obnoxious or badly received, but morally out of bounds, like a ruling from a court with no jurisdiction. Audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table — who plays the plaintiff, what plays the injury, who's the court, what's the remedy being sought — then tell me where it breaks. For instance, courts deny standing to manage dockets and police institutional boundaries, but a hypocrite's accusation might still be perfectly true, and blame seems more like a reactive attitude than a filing you can get thrown out. Which of the disanalogies actually matter for the "hypocritical blame is void" conclusion, and does something defensible survive — maybe as a claim about the blamer's entitlement to deliver it rather than the blame's validity? Or was the analogy carrying the whole conclusion the entire time?
