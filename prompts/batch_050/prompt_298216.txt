I'm writing the term paper for my upper-division moral philosophy seminar on Scanlonian contractualism, and I've gotten stuck on the aggregation problem: whether the view can accommodate the seemingly obvious judgment that, all else equal, one ought to save the greater number. As I understand the framework, an act is wrong if it would be forbidden by any principle that no one could reasonably reject, and reasonable rejection is assessed pairwise — each individual's complaint against a principle is weighed on its own, so the fact that five people will each die does not stack into a complaint five times as weighty as one person's. Critics argue that this makes it permissible to save one stranger rather than five in a standard rescue case, which they take to be a reductio; defenders reply that when the complaints on each side are perfectly balanced, the mere presence of further equal claims breaks the tie, so the numbers count without being aggregated.

Last week a classmate proposed what she called the proportionality amendment: a principle is reasonably rejectable just in case the strongest individual complaint against it outweighs the total of the complaints against every alternative, with each complaint weighted by how far the person in question falls below what anyone could reasonably demand. My suspicion is that the amendment either collapses into a maximizing principle in contractualist dress or else quietly reinstates pairwise comparison and therefore solves nothing — but I can't push my credence in that suspicion above about 0.6.

Here is what I'd like. First, give me your own credence in the following proposition: standard Scanlonian contractualism, with no amendments, delivers the verdict that one is morally required to save five strangers rather than one in a rescue case where the complaints at stake on each side are equal and no special obligations are in play. Second, decompose the reasoning behind your number: how much turns on whether pairwise comparison really forbids the numbers from counting at all, how much on whether the tie-breaking response generalizes beyond exact ties to the harder cases, and how much on whether "one must save the five" is a verdict contractualists should even aspire to capture rather than a place to bite the bullet. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, indicate how they combine into your overall credence, and identify the sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive — that is, which one, if resolved the other way, would move it the furthest. Finally, tell me whether the proportionality amendment escapes my suspicion or collapses in one of the two directions I described.
