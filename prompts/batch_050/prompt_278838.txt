Something's been bugging me for a while and I want to know if it has a name, or if it's just obvious to people in some field I dont know about.

Instance one. Someone offers you a coin flip: if it comes up heads your money goes up by 50%, tails it goes down by 40%. On any single flip the expected gain is positive (average of +50 and -40 is +5), so the naive calculation says take the bet. But if you keep playing, flipping again and again with your whole bankroll each time, you almost certainly end up broke: one win and one loss multiply to 0.9, so the typical long run is a steady drain even though each individual bet looks favorable. The average across a huge crowd of people each playing the game once looks great; the history of one person playing it many times looks terrible.

Instance two. Insurance. The premium is more than your expected loss, so buying it is a losing bet in the one-shot sense. But it still seems obviously rational, because the bad outcome isn't something you get to average out across a thousand copies of yourself. You'd just be ruined, once, for real, and there's no second copy of you somewhere enjoying the favorable branch.

Instance three, vaguer: I keep hearing claims like "the average return of this asset class is fine" or "most startups fail but the average founder does okay," and the average keeps quietly coming apart from what a typical person's actual path through the thing looks like.

So: is there an established concept or literature for this gap between the average-across-many-people and the typical-history-of-one-person? I have a hunch it touches risk aversion and diminishing marginal utility, but I don't think that's the same thing, because the coin flip result seems to hold even for someone who's totally indifferent to utility curves. And bet sizing heuristics seem related but are a response to the phenomenon, not the phenomenon itself. What I want is not just a label (though I'll take it if one exists) but the carving: what exactly is the phenomenon, when does the gap appear and when does it vanish — is it about repeated vs one-shot, multiplication vs addition, irreversibility, absorbing states? — and how does it relate to plain expected value. Does it show expected value is wrong, or just that it's answering a different question than the one you face when you only get one life to live?
