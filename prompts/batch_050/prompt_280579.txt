Last week I got into a long, unresolved argument with a friend about C. Lloyd Morgan and the famous canon from his An Introduction to Comparative Psychology -- roughly, the injunction not to explain an animal's behavior by appeal to a "higher" psychological faculty when a "lower" one will do. My friend reads the canon as a stern prohibition: in her view, Morgan was telling us that talk of reasoning, planning, or insight in animals is almost always sentimental over-attribution, and that the honest default is associative learning and instinct. I pushed back that this can't be the whole story, because Morgan himself frames his project in thoroughly evolutionary terms, and an evolutionary outlook seems to pull toward continuity between human and animal minds rather than toward skepticism about them. As evidence I kept returning to a sentence from the book's preface, where Morgan writes:

"A secondary object, subordinate indeed, yet forming an integral part of my plan, is to consider the place of consciousness in nature, the relation of psychical evolution to physical and biological evolution, and the light which comparative psychology throws on certain philosophical problems."

My claim is that this sentence is in real tension with the deflationary reading of the canon: someone who sets out to describe "the place of consciousness in nature" and the relation of "psychical" to "physical" evolution is not trying to explain animal minds away, but to locate them on a continuum with ours -- and the canon is then better understood as a rule of evidential discipline (don't multiply faculties without need) than as a skeptical verdict. My friend replied that the sentence proves nothing of the sort: mapping the evolution of consciousness is perfectly compatible with concluding that genuinely higher cognition is rare and late-arriving, and she thinks I am smuggling the continuity conclusion into the word "psychical." We also got stuck on a further problem neither of us could resolve: the canon only has teeth if there is an independent, non-question-begging way to rank faculties as "higher" and "lower," and it is unclear whether evolutionary ancestry, neural complexity, or something else is supposed to supply that ranking.

Please write a dialogue that stages exactly this disagreement, between two equally competent interlocutors -- call them a "restraint theorist" (my friend's side) and a "continuity theorist" (mine) -- arguing over how the quoted sentence should bear on the interpretation of Morgan's canon. The dialogue should have genuine back-and-forth: each speaker must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than restate prepared positions, and at least one of them should concede a specific point. Have them test their readings against one concrete case they both stipulate the facts of -- say, a dog that limps only when its owner is watching -- and have them explicitly address the ranking problem: what could ground the higher/lower ordering of faculties without circularity, and does the canon collapse into vacuity if nothing can? Neither speaker should be a strawman; the continuity theorist should feel the force of the charge that evolutionary continuity is being assumed rather than shown, and the restraint theorist should feel the force of the charge that excessive parsimony is itself a substantive theoretical commitment, not a neutral default. The dialogue may end in honest disagreement, but it should be clear by the end precisely where the remaining disagreement lies.
