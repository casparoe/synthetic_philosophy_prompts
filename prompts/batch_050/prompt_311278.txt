"Prototype theory, associated with Eleanor Rosch, says we represent a category by a summary of its most typical features, and we judge category membership by how similar an item is to that prototype. This explains typicality effects — people are faster to verify that a robin is a bird than that a penguin is — but it struggles with compositionality, since the prototype of a compound like 'pet fish' resembles a prototypical fish more than a prototypical pet, even though the compound should combine the two. Exemplar theory agrees that categorization is similarity-based, but instead of storing a single average, we store the category's average member plus a few especially vivid individual instances we've encountered, and compare new items to those. Its strength is that it naturally handles within-category variation; its weakness is that storing every exemplar would be computationally costly, which is presumably why the theory only keeps a handful. The theory theory, which grows out of Wittgenstein's idea of family resemblance, holds that concepts are like mini scientific theories: we categorize based on causal-explanatory beliefs about what makes something the kind of thing it is. This explains why category membership can survive radical changes in appearance, as when we still call a three-legged, hairless creature a dog. However, the theory theory denies that similarity plays any role in categorization, which makes it hard for it to explain the very typicality effects that motivated prototype theory in the first place."
