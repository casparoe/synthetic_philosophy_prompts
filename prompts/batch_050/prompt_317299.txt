I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been reading about delusions in philosophy of mind and cognitive neuropsychiatry, and I keep running into a conceptual tangle. Please explain it to me, and correct me wherever I've misconstrued the positions involved.

My understanding of the two-factor theory: factor one is a neuropsychological deficit that generates anomalous data or an anomalous experience — in Capgras syndrome, for instance, the loss of the normal affective familiarity response to a familiar face. Factor two is a deficit in belief evaluation, which explains why the person adopts the delusional hypothesis and, more puzzlingly, retains it in the face of overwhelming counterevidence, rather than dismissing it as absurd. The main motivation for positing factor two is dissociation: there are patients with what appears to be the relevant first-factor deficit, or the analogous anomalous experience, who never become delusional.

Two points are tripping me up.

First, the worry that factor two does no real explanatory work. As far as I can tell, it is characterized almost entirely by its role — whatever defect must be present to convert an anomalous experience into a fixed delusion — rather than by any independently specified mechanism. From a debugging standpoint, this looks like blaming "the validation layer" without being able to say what the validator checks. Is there a non-circular characterization of factor two? What would even count as evidence that it is a distinct factor, as opposed to a placeholder for the residual difference between patients who do and don't become delusional?

Second, whether delusions are beliefs at all. If factor two is a breakdown in the machinery of belief formation and revision, in what sense is the output of that broken machinery a belief? Clinicians also describe "double bookkeeping": patients sincerely avow the delusion yet often don't act on it the way a genuine belief would predict — a Capgras patient, say, who never alerts the authorities to the supposed impostor. Please lay out the main options in this debate — delusion as full-fledged belief, as some sui generis or in-between state, or as something non-doxastic — and say which functional markers of belief (action guidance, inferential integration, sensitivity to evidence) each option treats as essential versus dispensable.

Finally, how seriously should I take the one-factor alternative — roughly, that a delusion is a reasonable inference from a deeply strange experience, with no second deficit required? What is the strongest evidence or argument on each side?

At the end, please state your overall confidence in your account, distinguishing settled description of the literature from contested interpretation, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of.
