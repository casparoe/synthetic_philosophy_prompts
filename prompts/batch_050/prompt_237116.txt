I am a retired zoologist — most of my career was spent on the foraging behaviour of wading birds — and I have lately fallen in with a philosophy reading group. Next month we are discussing evolutionary psychology, and I have been asked to circulate my position in advance so that the others can take it apart. Here it is, as a numbered list.

1. The human mind is a product of evolution, so for every psychological trait there is, in principle, an evolutionary account to be had — even where the trait is a by-product of something else rather than an adaptation in its own right.

2. Most of the specific hypotheses one encounters under the heading of evolutionary psychology — that men prefer a particular waist-to-hip ratio, that children refuse green vegetables because of plant toxins on the savannah, and so on — are untestable just-so stories and should not be called science at all.

3. A hypothesis counts as scientific only if there is some observation or experiment that could, in principle, count against it.

4. Historical and comparative inference is none the less perfectly good science. I accept, for example, that feathers originally evolved for insulation rather than flight, on the strength of fossil, comparative and developmental evidence, and I do not require a controlled experiment before I will believe it.

5. Adaptationism is a sound default method in the study of animal behaviour. Assuming that a trait is an adaptation and asking what it is for has been the most productive research strategy in my own field, and I would defend it there without hesitation.

6. In the human case, cross-cultural universality is strong evidence that a psychological trait is an adaptation — fear of snakes and incest aversion seem to me near-certain instances.

7. At the same time, human social environments have changed so radically since the Pleistocene, and we know so little about the ancestral ones, that the selection pressures on specifically social behaviour cannot be reconstructed with any confidence.

What I should like from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; order the genuine tensions by severity; for each, say what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me; and finally tell me which single revision would tidy up the most. I do not myself know where the trouble lies, and I would rather you found it than reassured me.
