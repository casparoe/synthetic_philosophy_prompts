I'm putting together materials for my phil of mind seminar and we're doing the extended mind stuff next week — Clark and Chalmers, Otto and his notebook, the whole thing. Prepping for it I realized I don't actually know where I think the boundary of the mind falls, so let me just dump my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and you tell me what principle, if any, is doing the work. Verdicts first, hand-wringing after.

1. Classic Otto: memory loss, keeps everything in a notebook he consults automatically and trusts completely. Verdict: the notebook entries are his beliefs. Part of his mind.
2. My phone's contact list. I don't know anyone's number anymore, I just tap their name. Verdict: not part of my mind. It's a tool, full stop.
3. A neural implant that stores memories and is accessed exactly like biological memory — same speed, same felt sense of just remembering something. Verdict: obviously part of the mind.
4. Same implant, but the storage is physically offloaded to a server in another city and the implant streams it over an encrypted link. No detectable difference from the inside, ever. Verdict: still part of the mind, I think? Though I admit this one makes me sweat.
5. My wife of thirty years. I automatically ask her "who's that actor" or "when is the dentist" and she just knows, I trust her answers without checking, I do it without thinking. By any fluency or integration measure she's at least as coupled to me as the notebook is to Otto. Verdict: she is not part of my mind. She's her own person, and calling her part of my mind feels like a category error, maybe even an insult.
6. An old notebook of mine from twenty years ago, in a box in the attic, which I never look at, full of things I once knew. Verdict: not part of my mind. Whatever was in there is just gone from me.
7. A notebook I carry everywhere and consult automatically and trust completely — but it's written in a cipher I invented, and decoding each entry takes me a couple minutes. Verdict: not part of my mind. Too slow, it feels like reading rather than remembering.

Now the problem. My students will inevitably propose the standard trust-and-glue style criteria: roughly, constant availability, automatic endorsement, fluent retrieval. But as far as I can tell those criteria hand me verdicts 1, 3, 4 — and also 5, which I flatly refuse. And 6 is awkward too, since if Otto's dispositional beliefs include notebook entries he's not currently looking at, it's not obvious why my attic notebook fails other than distance and dust.

So here's what I want. Give me the most precise principle (or two rival candidate principles) that saves as many of my verdicts as possible, and be explicit about which verdict has to be sacrificed if the simpler version of the principle is to survive — my money is on 4 or 5 being the casualty but I want to see the argument. Then tell me what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: an AI assistant on an earpiece that I consult constantly, trust implicitly, and whose outputs I automatically treat as my own thoughts — except that its contents get silently updated overnight by the manufacturer. Part of my mind or not?
