You know the literature on idealized-preference theories of well-being and value pretty well — full-information accounts, ideal-advisor views, and more recently the "extrapolated volition" family of ideas that show up in AI alignment, where the proposal is roughly: figure out what a person would want if they were smarter, knew all the relevant facts, and had time to think it through, and treat *that* as what they really value. Act as a professional philosopher who's also fluent in the alignment discussions and have seen every standard objection a hundred times.

Here's my situation. I'm writing a paper and before I evaluate any particular theory I want to nail down what a theory in this space even has to *do* to count as adequate. So write me the desiderata: a numbered list of requirements any adequate theory of idealized or extrapolated preference must meet, with the case or consideration motivating each one. Start by brainstorming freely (you can ramble a bit in a scratchpad section), then distill into the final list. Think carefully about which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp.

Some half-baked candidate requirements I've been chewing on, to give you the flavor — feel free to endorse, sharpen, merge, or reject any of these, and add your own:

1. It has to get the easy cases right: the addict who hates his addiction, the person who wants X only because of a straightforwardly false belief, the preference formed under obvious manipulation. Any theory that fails these is dead on arrival.
2. It shouldn't launder manipulated preferences. If I was raised in a cult and fully informed-me would still be a cult member (because the values that would do the evaluating are themselves cult values), the theory shouldn't just shrug and call that my real good. But it also can't just declare all deeply socialized preferences suspect, or it collapses into the theorist imposing her own values.
3. The extrapolation can't be a different person. There's some continuity constraint — if "what I would want if I were smarter and knew everything" describes someone I'd barely recognize as me, in what sense is it *my* good?
4. Here's one I haven't seen discussed much: extrapolation path-dependence. The outcome of "giving someone more information and more intelligence" plausibly depends on the order in which they learn things and the route their thinking takes, and there's no obviously privileged path. If different reasonable extrapolation procedures give wildly different answers for the same person, does the theory have to say something about that, or is it allowed to just bite a bullet?
5. Convergence can't be assumed. A lot of the appeal of these views (especially in the alignment context) rests on hoping that extrapolated preferences converge, at least within a person and maybe across people. A good theory should say what it does if that hope fails — does it aggregate? pick a coalition? admit indeterminacy?
6. Something about values that are partly *constituted by* ignorance. Some people seem to value not-knowing, or value things whose value depends on uncertainty (surprise, faith, some kinds of trust). Full information might destroy the value rather than reveal it. Does the theory need a clause for this?
7. There's the weird self-referential problem that the extrapolation process itself would change the person's values — being made smarter and more knowledgeable isn't a neutral lens on your current values, it's an intervention on them. The theory has to distinguish "the values you'd end up with" from "your values, illuminated," and I'm not sure that distinction survives scrutiny. Maybe that's a desideratum: the theory must either draw this distinction non-circularly or explain why it doesn't need to.

For each item on your final list, give the motivating case in a sentence or two, and mark it as non-negotiable or camp-specific. The part I care most about: flag any subsets of the desiderata that look *jointly unsatisfiable* — my hunch is that the anti-laundering requirement and the continuity requirement are in real tension (anything strong enough to filter out cult values will also filter out values the person would regard as authentically theirs), and that path-dependence plus faithfulness-to-actual-self might be another collision. Tell me if I'm right or if there's a way to have both.

Then, briefly — this part can be short, a few sentences each — say how three broad families do against your list: the classic full-information view, an ideal-advisor view where the standard is what an idealized version of you would *want you* to want (not what you'd want for yourself), and the extrapolated-volition approach from the alignment literature. Don't write a whole scorecard, just note which desiderata each one most obviously fails.

One more thing: I've noticed people slip between talking about idealized preferences as a theory of *individual well-being* and as a proposal for *what a powerful AI should aim at*, as if these were the same problem. If some of the desiderata differ between those two projects, split the list and say which requirements belong to which project.
