I chair the grants commitee of a small community charity (five of us) and I've landed myself in a procedural mess I'd like a second opinion on.

Background: our constitution says we fund a project only if it meets all three of our criteria — (1) it mainly benefits people in the borough, (2) it can realistically be delivered within a year on the budget requested, and (3) it doesn't just duplicate something that already exists locally. Two years ago, after a shouting match about a different application, we agreed we'd vote on each criterion separately and fund only if all three get a majority. The constitution itself just says "decisions are by majority vote", without saying majority of what.

This year's one serious application is a repair café / tool library. Here are the votes:

- Me: benefits locals yes, deliverable yes, duplicates no (the actual library already lends tools) → so I'd reject
- Priya: yes, no (their costings are a fantasy), yes → reject
- Tom: no (thinks it serves a tiny clique), yes, yes → reject
- Anne: yes, yes, yes → fund
- Chris: yes, yes, yes → fund

So every criterion passes 4–1, but only two of the five of us think the project should actually be funded. Under our vote-each-criterion rule, we fund it. Under a single up/down vote, we reject it 3–2. Same five people, same views, opposite outcomes.

Everyone's now dug in. Anne says a rule's a rule, and we adopted criterion-by-criterion voting precisely to stop decisions turning on gut feeling. Tom says the only honest question is "do we fund this or not" and the criterion votes were just us thinking out loud. Priya wants to re-vote as a single question; Anne says you can't relitigate the procedure just because you dislike the result. To make it worse, Priya muttered that she reckons Anne voted yes on deliverability despite privately agreeing the costings were dodgy, because she knew a no there would kill the project under the criterion rule — which, if true, feels like it should matter, but I'm not sure why. And meanwhile I have to write to the applicant either way, and here's the bit that really gets me: if we reject, no single ground for rejecting commands a majority. A majority thought it benefits locals, a majority thought it deliverable, a majority thought it non-duplicative. What do I even put in the letter?

Also, full disclosure: I voted reject myself, so I have a dog in this fight even though I'm supposedly the neutral chair.

What I want to know: is there actually a fact of the matter about what "the majority" decided here, or have we stumbled into proof that majority voting only means something when there's exactly one question on the table? Is criterion-by-criterion voting better in principle — it forces us to own our reasons, Anne says — or does it just hide disagreement and invite gaming? Does it matter that the criteria aren't really independent (my "duplicates the library" and Tom's "serves a tiny clique" might be the same worry in different clothes)? And practically, what do I do at Tuesday's meeting — honour the old rule, put the procedure itself to a vote (under what procedure do we vote on *that*?), or something cleverer, like everyone having to state their reasons before any vote happens?

Please answer in British English.
