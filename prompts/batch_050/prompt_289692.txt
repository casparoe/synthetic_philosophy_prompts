A friend who once took a philosophy of mind course summarized Daniel Dennett's work for me like this: Dennett holds that there are three stances we can take toward any system. The physical stance explains it in terms of its material constitution, the design stance in terms of what it was built to do, and the intentional stance in terms of what it believes and wants. The three stances are rival theories of what is really going on inside a system, and which one applies depends on the kind of system it is: thermostats and chess computers get the design stance, while only humans and perhaps some animals genuinely merit the intentional stance, because only they really have beliefs. But Dennett also thinks that belief-and-desire talk is ultimately a convenient fiction: since treating something as if it had beliefs is just a predictive trick, mental states are not part of the real furniture of the world the way atoms are, and Dennett is therefore an eliminativist about the mind.

The first part sounded right to me, but the rest struck me as off, and I couldn't say exactly why. Which parts of this summary would a careful reader of Dennett accept, and which are misrepresentations? In particular: Is it right that the stances are competitors among which only one can be correct for a given system? Is it right that taking the intentional stance toward a system settles nothing about whether its attributed beliefs are real? And does the eliminativist conclusion actually follow from anything Dennett claims -- or does he explicitly resist it? Please correct the summary where it goes wrong, explain what the accurate version commits Dennett to, and tell me whether a genuine controversy remains about how real beliefs and desires are on his view.
