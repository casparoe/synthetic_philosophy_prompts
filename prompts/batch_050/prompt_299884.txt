In a reading group on AI safety, we recently worked through Alan Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and I was struck by the section near the end where, rather than trying to replicate an adult mind directly, Turing proposes constructing something analogous to a human child and then educating it, with rewards and punishments playing roughly the role they play in the schooling of children. To me this looked like the modern alignment problem in embryo, and it has made me suspect that the field has overcomplicated things since. Turing's sketch seems to imply that the whole difficulty is a training problem: get the reward signals right, shape the system as you would a pupil, and the machine will end up doing what its teachers intend.

On that basis, I've been defending the following position to my reading group, and I'd like it stress-tested. Suppose we succeed at intent alignment in the narrow sense: the system reliably tries to do, and in fact does, what its operators actually intend, rather than what they literally said or what some proxy metric rewards. Then, it seems to me, any catastrophic outcome must by definition trace back to a failure of that training — the system wasn't really intent-aligned after all — since a fully intent-aligned system would have done what we wanted, and we presumably don't want catastrophe. Value alignment, on this view, is either just intent alignment done properly, or else an attempt to smuggle the designer's contested moral opinions into the system under a technical-sounding name. So why do safety researchers insist on treating value alignment as a further problem? Shouldn't we say that once a machine faithfully executes our intentions, the alignment problem is solved by definition, and that any residual worry about what we ought to intend belongs to ordinary moral and political philosophy rather than to AI safety?

I suspect there is a mistake somewhere in this reasoning — perhaps in what faithfully executing our intentions actually guarantees, perhaps in the assumption that our intentions are coherent, informed, and jointly satisfiable enough to be executed, perhaps in the move from the system doing what we intend to things going well — but I can't locate it cleanly. Please identify where the argument goes wrong, if it does; explain the standard distinction between intent alignment and value alignment and why the narrow definition of the alignment problem is usually adopted, whether as a substantive claim that intentions are all that matter or merely as a deliberate scoping choice; and assess whether my deflationary position has any serious defenders or is simply an error. Throughout, please separate what is broadly agreed among people who work on these questions from what remains genuinely contested — in particular, do not present either the sufficiency or the insufficiency of intent alignment as settled if it isn't — and flag any place where the field itself uses this terminology inconsistently.
