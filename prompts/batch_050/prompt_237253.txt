I'm a TA for an intro philosophy of language course and I've got one 80-minute section to teach Russell's theory of descriptions to second-years. Complication: they've only done propositional logic so far, quantifiers don't come up in their logic class for another three weeks. So I can't just write the formal analysis on the board and call it a day — the whole thing has to work in English paraphrase, or with whatever minimal quantifier scaffolding you think I can get away with.

Don't explain the theory to me, I know it. Plan how I should explain it.

Specifically:

- The order of the pieces. What has to be in place before the analysis of "the F is G" looks like a solution to anything rather than word salad? My instinct is: the puzzle first (how can a sentence about nothing be meaningful and false rather than gibberish), then the idea that grammatical form misleads about logical form, then the three clauses (something is F, at most one thing is F, whatever is F is G). Push back on that ordering if it's wrong — especially on where "grammar misleads about logical form" should go, because I suspect students nod along to it without it doing any work.

- The carrying example. Everyone uses the present king of France and I'd like something else. Give me one example that can carry the whole session and tell me what it has to have: a predicate students have real intuitions about, existence that's genuinely contestable in the room, and enough structure to show the negation ambiguity later. Also what it has to lack — is a purely invented case (a made-up wizard, say) actually worse, and why? And: one example for everything, or one for the puzzle and a second for the scope point?

- The misconception map. My list so far: (a) "Russell says the sentence is meaningless" — conflating his view with a truth-value-gap view; (b) thinking the theory is a quirky claim about the word "the" rather than about quantificational structure, so it has no lessons beyond definite descriptions; (c) thinking it only matters for empty descriptions, and missing why the analysis is still needed when the description is satisfied — the identity-puzzle motivation; (d) reading "the F is not G" the wrong way and accusing Russell of contradiction. For each one: where in the sequence do I head it off, and what's the single move that kills it? If I've missed the misconception that actually wrecks this class, tell me.

- Strawson. The presupposition objection is next week's reading. Do I spend the last ten minutes on it as a hook, or does raising it destroy the landing? If I do mention it, how do I frame it so they don't walk out thinking the theory they just learned is dead?

- A check for understanding: one question I can ask at the end that seperates students who got the theory from students who memorized "existence, uniqueness, predication" as a formula.

And tell me what to openly skip at this level — I'm assuming referential/attributive stays out entirely, but argue with me if a one-line version of it actually helps.
