I work as a data scientist, and three months ago I took a seat on the grant committee of a mid-sized research foundation. Watching our deliberations, I've become convinced we have a structural problem, and I'd like to understand it properly. A proposal gets funded whenever even one member champions it strongly enough: enthusiasm is contagious, nobody wants to be the person who killed a colleague's favorite idea, and the skeptics hold their fire because raising an objection feels costlier than letting the thing through. The predictable result is that we fund projects whose true value is well below the average member's honest private estimate.

I've since learned that philosophers have analyzed a version of this dynamic under the name the unilateralist's curse: when any member of a group can unilaterally initiate some action whose value is uncertain, and each member acts on their own estimate, the actions that actually get taken will be disproportionately the ones somebody has overestimated, so a group of individually reasonable agents ends up undertaking more bad initiatives than any of them would have chosen alone, and the problem worsens as the group grows. The standard proposed remedy is a kind of epistemic modesty: each agent should discount their own judgment before acting, precisely because the decision structure selects for overestimates.

Here is what I want from you. Restate this argument in the vocabulary I actually think in: the statistics of selection on noisy estimates. That means, as precisely as you can manage, the common-value auction and the winner's curse; the expected maximum of a collection of unbiased estimators; empirical-Bayes shrinkage and regularization; multiple-comparisons corrections; and selective inference, including the winner's-curse bias in published effect sizes. Translate each component of the philosophical argument — the setup, the mechanism, the conclusion, the remedy — into that machinery, and state the translated version as a formal claim wherever you can, noting what assumptions it needs.

Then give me the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which elements map cleanly? My guess is the core mechanism is just the winner's curse under a threshold rule, and if so I want to know whether the philosophical literature is saying anything the statisticians haven't. Which elements require distortion? For instance, the auction setup assumes bids rather than binary go/no-go decisions; the shrinkage remedy assumes a centralized decision-maker with access to everyone's estimates, whereas the curse arises precisely when no such aggregation occurs; and multiple-testing corrections presuppose a notion of a family of tests that may have no analogue for a loose committee. And which parts have no statistical expression at all — the claim that modesty is a duty rather than merely good policy, the assumption that each member is entitled to act on their own judgment, the treatment of the agents' deliberation as part of the phenomenon rather than as data to be pooled? Wherever there is residue, say whether the gap reveals something the statistical framing misses or something the philosophical argument was doing that doesn't survive formalization.

Close with a verdict on my original suspicion: is my committee's pathology fully explained by selection on noisy estimates, or does the analysis leave something out — and if the latter, what would I need to change in how we actually vote?
