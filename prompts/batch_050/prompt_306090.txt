I'm the best man at my college roommate's wedding next month and I need a toast, maybe four or five minutes. Here's the situation: both he and his wife are physicists, and they actually met in grad school in the same lab working on one of those experimental Maxwell's demon setups — the kind where you use information about a particle's state to extract work from a heat bath through feedback. So roughly a third of the guests are physicists who did this stuff for a living, and the rest are aunts, old friends like me (I'm a highschool teacher), and her dad, a retired plumber who will absolutely heckle me if I get too cute.

I want to build the toast around the standard resolution of the demon paradox: the demon doesn't actually beat the second law, because it has to store a record of each measurement somewhere in its memory, and while writing that record can in principle be done for free, erasing it can't — wiping one bit dumps at least a fixed minimum of heat into the environment, and once you count the erasure costs the books balance and the second law survives. Now, there's an obvious toast move there (marriage = treasure your memories, forgetting is what's expensive, awww), but the physicists in the room will instantly spot the catch: a demon that just keeps every bit forever isn't running a cycle anymore, it's just growing an ever-bigger memory bank, so "never forget anything" isn't really the happily-ever-after the naive version promises. I'd like the speech to earn its sentimental ending by going through that complication rather than around it — the honest version seems to be something like: what makes a shared life different from a data log is precisely that you have to choose what to erase, and every erasure costs you something real, which is why the choosing matters.

Requirements: the physics has to be correct enough that two members of the groom's thesis committee won't wince — get the asymmetry right, recording can be thermodynamically free, erasure is the irreversible step that pays the heat — but the whole erasure-costs-something idea needs to come across in one or two sentences the plumber dad genuinely follows. One good joke that lands for both halves of the room, and the ending should feel earned rather than like a lecture with a bow tied on it. Also, no names of physicists or papers in the toast itself — nobody wants citations at a wedding.
