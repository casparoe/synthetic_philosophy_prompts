I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long piece on creativity that takes its spine from Douglas Hofstadter's suggestion that variation on a theme is the crux of creativity. On this view, every creative act, from humming a tune in a minor key to founding a new physics, consists of twiddling knobs on something that already exists: there is a continuum running from trivial parameter-adjustment all the way up to world-historical invention, and the apparent leaps are simply very many twiddles compressed together, guided by analogies that let one concept slip into a neighboring one. Crucially, the thesis denies any discontinuity in kind between the doodler and Einstein.

I want your credence that this continuum thesis is substantially correct as a general account of human creativity, and I want it as a number, not as a verdict about the thesis being illuminating or influential. But what the piece really needs is the decomposition, so please assign rough probabilities to each of the following sub-questions and show how they combine into your final figure. First, the historical question: do celebrated discontinuities, such as the move from classical to relativistic physics or from figuration to cubism, dissolve into accumulated small variations once you look closely at the notebooks, influences, and dead ends, or do some survive scrutiny as genuine jumps? Second, the recursion question: can the choice of which knobs to twiddle, and the invention of new knobs nobody had thought to attach to the theme, itself be explained by the same twiddling mechanism one level up, or does the model quietly presuppose the very thing it claims to explain? Third, the artificial question: present-day language models look very much like knob-twiddlers operating at enormous scale over human material, so does their creative competence count as evidence for the continuum thesis, or does their widely alleged difficulty with genuinely far-reaching analogies count against it, or are they simply irrelevant to a claim about human minds? Fourth, the kind question: if creativity is not a natural kind but a grab-bag of loosely related capacities, is the continuum thesis even the sort of thing that can be true or false?

Two people in my orbit sit at opposite ends of this. My editor puts the thesis at about 0.85, largely because she thinks the language-model case has already vindicated Hofstadter; a philosopher I interview regularly puts it at about 0.2, largely because of the recursion question about where new knobs come from. After giving your own number, tell me which sub-question that disagreement most likely lives in, which sub-question your own final number is most sensitive to, and what single piece of evidence, empirical or historical, would move your credence the most in either direction.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
