So this happened at a meetup last weekend and it's been bugging me since. Two people from our local group (both smart, both have read their Lewis and their de Finetti) got into a real argument about the Principal Principle, and I'd like an outside referee.

The setup they kept coming back to: imagine a machine that each morning uses some quantum process to set the bias of a coin it then flips once, and right before the flip it prints out the objective chance of heads on a little screen. Say it prints 0.7.

Round 1: Ada said this is the clean case the Principal Principle was built for -- if the printed number is genuinely the objective chance and you have no other relevant info, your credence in heads has to be 0.7, and anyone who sets it differently is just being irrational, same as someone who'd take a losing bet on both sides. Ben said fine, but "no other relevant info" is doing literally all the work in that sentence. Suppose he happens to know the screen sometimes displays yesterday's bias because of a caching bug. Then obviously he shouldn't defer. And in the real world we basically never know we're in the clean case, so the principle tells us almost nothing in practice.

Round 2: Ada said that's just the standard admissibility clause, everyone already knows the principle is conditional on your evidence being admissible, and pointing at a caching bug isn't an objection to the principle, it's an application of it. Ben then shifted ground: his deeper worry is that "objective chance" is a myth outside of fundamental physics. A weather forecast's "70% chance of rain" is just some model's output, i.e. someone's glorified credence. So deferring to "chances" in real life is really deferring to other people's credences, and calling it a principle about objective chance is circular -- you're deferring to whatever a well-informed agent would believe, and defining well-informed as "someone whose credences match the chances."

Round 3: Ada pushed back that the circularity charge proves too much: even if macro-chances arent metaphysically fundamental, you can still make sense of them via something like a best-system analysis -- the chances are whatever the best simple summary of the world's actual frequencies would assign, which is a fact about the world, not about anyone's beliefs. And the quantum coin machine shows the concept of chance is at least coherent, so the principle has real content in at least some cases. Ben's reply, which is where they got stuck: best-system chances depend on the entire actual history of the universe, including the future, which is by definition unavailable to you when you're setting your credence. So the chances you're supposed to defer to are in principle unknowable at the time you'd need them, and a rationality principle you can never know you're applying correctly is definetely not doing the work Ada wants. Ada said this conflates the metaphysics of chance with our epistemic access to it -- the principle says defer to the chances, and our best estimates of them are all we ever have, same as with any fact about the world. Ben said "defer to your best estimate of the chance" is a much weaker and more boring principle than the one that was supposed to ground the epistemology of objective probability.

Neither would concede and honestly I can see both of their points at each step. Please adjudicate: for each move, say who's right about that specific point (or whether it's a draw and why), and then give an overall verdict on who's more right about the Principal Principle's status -- is it a substantive constraint on rational credence, a near-tautology, or something in between? Also tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. And one more thing: say what would change your verdict -- which argument or consideration, if it held up, would flip you to the other side, or where your own reasoning here is most likely to fall apart.
