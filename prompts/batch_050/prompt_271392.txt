I'm a PhD student in economics serving on the reading committee for an interdisciplinary seminar on climate policy, and I've run into a configuration that my training in collective action doesn't seem to have a slot for. The seminar group has had lively arguments about it, but nobody — including the philosopher and the political scientist at the table — could point me to an established treatment, so I'm asking here.

The standard story I know well: cutting greenhouse-gas emissions is expensive for each country individually, while the benefit, a stable climate, accrues to everyone whether or not they contributed. Each country therefore has reason to hang back and let others do the cutting, so the shared benefit is underprovided. This is the familiar free-rider structure, and there are shelves of work on it. But last month we read about proposals to cool the planet directly — spraying reflective particles into the upper atmosphere to bounce a small fraction of sunlight back into space. What struck me was the estimated cost: low enough, apparently, that a single mid-sized state, conceivably even a very wealthy private individual, could run such a program alone, with effects falling on every country on Earth whether they consented or not. Here the problem seems inverted. Instead of a shared benefit nobody wants to pay for, you have a shared intervention that almost anybody can impose. Instead of coaxing people into contributing, you have to find a way to stop people from contributing.

My questions: First, does this inverted configuration have an established name, and is there a literature treating it as a structure in its own right rather than merely the free-rider problem with the sign flipped? I ask because the dynamics seem different in at least three respects. Prevention appears to require something close to universal restraint, whereas provision requires only enough participation; the imposer may sincerely believe they are bestowing a benefit, which makes them unlike the ordinary polluter; and once such a program has run for a while, stopping it abruptly would release the pent-up warming all at once, so the lone actor acquires a strange permanent leverage — the rest of the world could find itself forced to continue a program it never agreed to. I'm also unsure where to file a subtler effect: the mere knowledge that this cheap emergency option exists seems to dull enthusiasm for the expensive work of cutting emissions, even if the option is never used. Second, how does the configuration relate to ordinary negative externalities like industrial pollution, where the imposer at least doesn't claim to be helping? Third, where are its edges: does it still count if the imposer is a coalition rather than an individual? If the imposed outcome genuinely would benefit everyone and the objection is purely that nobody was asked? If it could be done undetectably? I'd rather not be handed a bare label — and if no established term exists, I'd prefer a carefully coined one, with a definition and some boundary cases, over a gesture at adjacent literatures.
