Can you build me a thought experiment showing that easy knowledge and bootstrapping can come apart? Set it somewhere realistic, like a hospital lab, a repair workshop, or a small farm, and keep the scenario itself to one paragraph of under 180 words.

The case has to meet these conditions:

- An agent forms a belief p wholly by trusting one instrument.
- There is a specified sceptical hypothesis h under which the instrument would produce exactly the same reading, so the agent cannot rule h out just by inspecting the reading.
- Even so, the agent can know not-h by inference from p together with ordinary background knowledge about what h would have required.
- The same evidence does not allow the agent to know that the instrument is generally reliable, because establishing that would require illicit bootstrapping from the instrument’s own outputs.
- Don’t give the agent an independent calibration, manufacturer’s certificate, or past test results.
- The difference must not merely be that one conclusion is specific and the other general. It has to arise from the epistemic structure of the two inferences.
- No demons, perfectly deceptive environments, or stipulated miracles.

After the scenario, identify p, h, and the general reliability claim. Explain why the first inference is a case of easy knowledge, why the second is bootstrapping, and which feature of the case is doing the real work. Then say what happens if that feature is removed, and give the strongest objection that the supposedly easy knowledge is still objectionably circular. Please answer in American English, even though I’m writing this in British English.
