A close friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the retrospective judgment of historical figures, and specifically about the "man of his time" defence — the claim that it is unfair to condemn people of earlier eras for failing to meet moral standards their contemporaries did not share. She defends a strong version of that view; I resist it. Late in the evening she changed tactics and claimed that my own position, as I had stated it over the course of the night, is unstable — that my test for when the defence succeeds either excuses virtually everyone in history or convicts virtually everyone, and cannot deliver the mixed verdicts I want. When I pressed her to say which of my commitments I should drop, she only gestured. I still cannot see the problem myself, and before I concede anything I would like an impartial audit of whether my views actually hang together.

Here is my position, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(a) Moral truth is not indexed to time. Chattel slavery was exactly as wrong in 1750 as it is today, and this is precisely what makes moral judgment of the past intelligible rather than a category error.

(b) Blame, however, has an epistemic condition: a historical figure is culpable for participating in a wrongful practice only if the standard condemning it was genuinely available to them — only if they were in a position to know better. This is the kernel of truth in the "man of his time" defence, which I accept in principle.

(c) My test for availability is this: a standard counts as available to a figure if serious contemporaries had articulated it, or if the figure's own writings show they understood the case for it. On this test Jefferson has no excuse. In Query XVIII of his Notes on the State of Virginia, Jefferson himself characterizes the relationship between master and slave as one of unrestrained despotism on the one side and degrading submission on the other, and argues that the institution corrupts the character of slaveholding families, whose children absorb tyranny by watching it practiced daily. A man who could diagnose slavery that lucidly, and who nonetheless continued to hold and sell enslaved people for the rest of his life, knew better. He is blameworthy.

(d) All the same, I want to excuse Aristotle. His defence of natural slavery in the opening chapters of the Politics was written inside a slave society, and demanding that he arrive at abolitionist conclusions strikes me as presentism at its worst — even though, as my friend pointed out, Aristotle himself reports that certain contemporaries considered slavery contrary to nature and therefore unjust, and takes some pains to argue against them.

(e) Figures who were ahead of their time — the early abolitionists, say — deserve special moral praise precisely because they saw further than the moral horizon of their contemporaries.

(f) Decisions about commemoration — statues, the names of schools and streets — are not settled by the verdict on a person's culpability. What a public honor communicates now, and to whom, is a separate and forward-looking question; so blaming Jefferson does not by itself determine whether his name stays on a building.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me on the topic. Cover every pair you consider worth flagging, ordered by severity. After the table, give me a brief verdict naming the single revision that would restore the most coherence at the lowest cost, and state plainly whether my friend is right that the position set is unstable.
