I work in software and have spent the past year reading about AI safety in my spare time, and I keep running into the same move: we cannot write down everything we value, so instead we tell the system to "just avoid side effects" -- pursue its task while otherwise leaving the world as it found it. It is an appealing thought, but the more I poke at it the less I understand what it could precisely mean. A delivery robot that refrains from knocking over a vase has still displaced air, worn down the carpet, and altered what everyone in the building does for the rest of the day; a sufficiently paranoid reading of "side effect" forbids it from moving at all, while a sufficiently lax one forbids nothing. I have seen proposals that penalise the agent for changing measurable features of the environment, others that penalise it for reducing what *other* agents (including us) could go on to do, and others that compare what happened against some counterfactual in which the agent did nothing or behaved in some default way. Each seems to fail on different examples, and I cannot tell whether the failures are engineering details or whether the whole idea is confused.

Before I read any more of these proposals, I want to know what I should actually be asking of them. Write the desiderata: what any adequate formalisation of "low impact" or "avoid side effects" has to deliver, given that the point of the exercise is precisely to get safe behaviour *without* a full specification of human value. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it -- the concrete scenario it must get right, the perverse incentive it must not create, the distinction it must respect. Some desiderata I suspect belong on the list, but I would like them assessed rather than assumed: that the penalty must not punish the agent for effects it did not cause (earthquakes, other people's choices); that it must not punish merely existing, acting, or consuming resources at all; that it must not be gamed by "offsetting" (breaking the vase and then repairing it, or preventing someone from noticing a change rather than preventing the change); that it must handle irreversible changes more harshly than reversible ones; that it must not require superhuman forecasting of distant causal chains; and that it must scale with how capable the agent is rather than only working in toy gridworlds.

For each desideratum, say whether you take it to be genuinely non-negotiable, negotiable given the purpose, or a prejudice of one school of thought. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied and explain the tension, since if the list itself is inconsistent that is worth knowing before anyone builds anything. Then, briefly and without needing any formalism, sketch how the three broad families I mentioned -- penalising change in features of the world, penalising changes to what others could go on to achieve, and penalising divergence from a do-nothing or default-behaviour baseline -- fare against the list, and which single desideratum does the most work in ruling candidates out.
