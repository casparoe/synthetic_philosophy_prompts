In our philosophy of mathematics seminar we have been circling a dispute that strikes me as unresolved but somehow also dead. In 1976 Appel and Haken announced a proof of the four-colour theorem whose central case-check could only be carried out by computer; later, Thomas Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture leaned even more heavily on computation, to the point that journal referees famously reported near-total confidence without being able to certify every step. Philosophers at the time split sharply. One side — Thomas Tymoczko most prominently — argued that such proofs are unsurveyable: no human can check them, so accepting them means trusting empirical facts about hardware and software, which makes mathematical knowledge a posteriori in a way it was never supposed to be. The other side replied that computer assistance differs only in degree, not kind, from trusting long proofs, referees, and the community, and that fallibility is old news in mathematics. What puzzles me is that the debate was never settled; it simply went quiet. Mathematicians accepted both theorems and moved on, and the philosophers stopped writing about it. Then, in the 2000s and 2010s, both proofs were fully machine-verified — the four-colour theorem in Coq, the Kepler conjecture via the Flyspeck project — which some treat as having resolved the philosophical question and others treat as beside it.

So here is what I want: a diagnosis of why this debate stalled, not a verdict on who won. My own hypothesis is that the two sides were never disagreeing about the same thing — one side was asking whether we are justified in believing the theorems, the other was asking what a proof is for, and unsurveyability matters enormously to the second question and barely at all to the first. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually argued, or replace it with a better one: is this a verbal disagreement about the word 'proof', a substantive epistemological disagreement about warrant, or a disguised disagreement about the aims of mathematics? Then address the machine-verification coda directly: did Coq and Flyspeck dissolve the disagreement, settle it in one side's favour, or merely change the subject? Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress now — a conceptual distinction, a formal result about proof-checkers, or an admission that nothing further can be said.
