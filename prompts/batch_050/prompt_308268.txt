I'm a longtime lurker on philosophy forums who's been working through the literature on radical translation this fall — Quine's Word and Object, the essay "Ontological Relativity," and some of the Kripke–Putnam material on reference — and I've put together a list of positions I think I hold. What I want is an audit: do these hang together as a package, or am I quietly committed to something I wouldn't endorse if I saw it stated plainly? Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) The gavagai case succeeds at what it aims at: for radical translation between languages with no shared history, no amount of behavioral evidence — assent patterns, ostension, stimulus conditions — can privilege the manual translating the native term as rabbit over the manual translating it as undetached rabbit part or temporal rabbit-stage; the choice between manuals is underdetermined by everything the field linguist could in principle gather. (2) Nevertheless, in my own mouth the word rabbit determinately refers to rabbits rather than rabbit-stages; homophonic translation within English isn't just a practical convenience but the correct translation, and whatever argument works against the field linguist doesn't touch the home case. (3) Putnam-style externalism is right: reference is fixed partly by causal and environmental facts rather than by anything in the speaker's head, which is what makes the Twin Earth intuitions come out correctly. (4) Facts about meaning are facts about use — there is nothing more to what a speaker means than what shows up, or would show up, in their verbal dispositions and practices; meaning is not a Platonic inner item. (5) Ordinary translation between languages succeeds all the time in practice, and bilingual speakers genuinely translate; the indeterminacy thesis is a philosophical artifact of artificially restricting the admissible evidence, not a threat to anything real translators do.

A few things I'm specifically unsure about, though I can't tell whether they're problems or just places where I haven't thought hard enough. My reasons for accepting (1) were entirely Quinean — the argument from stimulus meaning and the underdetermination of ostension — but I gather Quine himself drew a further moral in "Ontological Relativity," something to the effect that the inscrutability of reference can't be quarantined to the foreign case, and I'd paraphrase his point as saying that once you accept his premises there is no principled way to stop the indeterminacy at the border of one's own language. I haven't fully absorbed that argument, so I'm not sure whether it touches (2) or not. I also have a nagging sense that (3) and (4) might pull against each other, since one leans on causal history and the other on use, but when I try to state the conflict precisely it evaporates, which makes me suspect it's merely apparent. And I'm aware that some writers treat Quine's indeterminacy thesis and Kripke's rule-following skepticism as the same beast, which would matter if I'm relying on a use-based picture in (4) to defuse the latter.

So: please audit the five commitments. Identify the genuine tensions, ordered by severity, and for each one say exactly which commitment has to be weakened or abandoned to restore consistency and what that move costs — including whether Quine's own extension of the argument to the home language is sound and, if so, which of my commitments it forces me to give up. Where two commitments look incompatible but aren't, say so and explain why the appearance dissolves rather than counting it against the set. I'd rather be told precisely which plank to pull than be given a survey of the debate, and I'm not attached to any particular one of the five surviving the audit.
