My roommate does CS and hit me with this argument over dinner, and I've been stuck on it for days. (1) A physical system counts as running a computation only relative to some mapping from its physical states onto computational states. (2) For any sufficiently complex physical system and any computation you like, some mapping exists under which the system performs that computation -- the wall behind me has enough molecular states that it can be read as running Word, or a chess engine, or whatever. (3) So the brain is a computer in exactly the same sense that the wall, or a hurricane, is a computer. (4) A property that brains, walls, and hurricanes all share can't explain what's cognitively special about brains. (5) But computationalism about mind says cognition is explained by the brain's being a computer. (6) So either walls think, or computationalism explains nothing. He says this is basically a refutation of cog-sci as a whole. I suspect something's off around (2) but I can't pin it down, and if I have to choose I'd rather give up almost anything before (1). Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? Also: if the fix is to restrict which mappings count, tell me what restriction does the work without just being defined ad hoc to save brains and exclude walls.
