I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on a governance experiment in a mid-sized European city I'll call Marholm. Two political economists I interviewed disagreed sharply about it, and I'd like you to referee their exchange.

Background: Marholm ran a pilot of "futarchy" — governance by prediction market. The question was whether to introduce a congestion charge in the city center. The city issued two kinds of conditional securities: one paying out according to a measured welfare index (a composite of average commute times, retail footfall, and air quality) if the charge were adopted, the other paying out according to the same index if it were rejected. The announced rule was that the charge would be adopted if the adopt-market priced welfare higher than the reject-market. The city also reserved the right, with small probability, to decide by coin flip instead, so that both markets could in principle resolve to real outcomes rather than being unwound.

Here is the exchange, compressed from my interviews:

(1) Dr. Anders, who advised the pilot, argued the experiment vindicated the core claim of futarchy: markets aggregate dispersed information — from delivery firms, commuters, shop owners — that no consultant's model captures, and the adopt-market's pricing anticipated a footfall decline the city's own forecast had missed entirely. On his telling, the mechanism outperformed conventional policy analysis on its first real test.

(2) Dr. Reyes replied that the pilot cannot have vindicated anything, because of an asymmetry baked into the design: only the branch that actually happens generates observable payoffs. The reject-market's prices were never disciplined by any outcome, so calling the comparison of the two prices "information aggregation" is unfounded — one price was grounded and the other was pure speculation.

(3) Anders responded that this is exactly what the coin-flip provision addresses: because rejection retained positive probability, both markets carried genuine resolution risk, and prices in both had to be backed by real expectations. The design, he said, was specifically engineered to answer her objection.

(4) Reyes countered that the provision answers one problem and creates another. Now a party with a financial stake in the charge — say, a contractor selling the tolling infrastructure — can profitably buy up adopt-market securities, not because they believe the forecast, but to push the price past the decision threshold. The subsidy costs them little compared with the contract, and in a thin municipal market with perhaps a few dozen active traders, moving the price is cheap.

(5) Anders invoked the standard defense: manipulation attempts create profit opportunities. Every dollar a manipulator spends inflating a price is a dollar of expected profit for an informed trader betting it back down. Manipulation, he said, subsidizes its own correction.

(6) Reyes's final move: that defense assumes deep-pocketed informed traders exist and can afford to wait years for resolution while their capital is locked up. In Marholm's market, she claimed, no such traders exist — and worse, the eventual payoff is measured by a welfare index the city itself defines and can quietly reweight, so even honest traders are ultimately betting on municipal politics, not on traffic.

Please adjudicate the dispute move by move. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — who is more right overall — with no preamble and no restating of the question. Then assess each of the six moves individually: for each, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right-with-qualifications, and justify briefly. Close by identifying any consideration both parties overlooked that should bear on whether Marholm's design can produce trustworthy prices.
