Now that I am retired from a career in experimental physics, I have been working my way through the literature on consciousness and moral status, and I keep running into a tension I cannot resolve on my own. Consider two artificial systems with precisely the same input-output profile: the first is a recurrent network with a global workspace, self-monitoring, and rich internal dynamics; the second is a vast, purely feed-forward lookup structure that was mechanically compiled from the first and reproduces its behavior exactly, but whose internal organization contains nothing resembling integration, recurrence, or self-modeling. The question that interests me is whether these two systems have the same moral status — and, more generally, which features of a computation, if any, are the ones that make a system matter morally: its causal-functional organization, its internal structure, or merely what it does.

Please write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers disagreeing about this case. One defends the view that moral status supervenes only on coarse-grained functional or behavioral organization, so that the two systems must be treated alike; the other defends the view that fine-grained internal causal structure matters, so that the compiled system may lack whatever makes the original a subject of experience. Give the exchange some genuine dialectical movement: let the first character press the charge that internal-structure views collapse into an unprincipled form of biological or architectural chauvinism, and let the second press back that pure input-output equivalence is threatened by absurd substitutions (a sufficiently large printed table, a precomputed recording) that would trivialize the notion of a mind. Each should respond to the other's actual arguments, concede points where concession is honestly required, and exploit the concessions they win. Neither should be a caricature. Set the dialogue wherever you like, but keep the focus on the argument.

At the end, append a brief note — outside the dialogue — indicating which moves in the exchange represent broadly settled or widely shared positions in the philosophy of mind and which remain genuinely contested among specialists. I do not want a contested view presented as though it were the consensus, nor a fringe position smuggled in as established doctrine; where the literature is divided, please say so plainly.
