This term I'm running a graduate seminar on collective mentality, and I began by asking each student to submit a one-page position statement for the rest of us to interrogate. Fairness demands that I submit one of my own, but before I circulate it I'd like an audit of whether the package actually hangs together. Our common touchstone has been Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary from Gödel, Escher, Bach: the ant colony that converses with the Anteater, intelligent and (on the natural reading) conscious at the colony level, while the individual ants are mindless signal-carriers whose comings and goings constitute her symbol-level processing. Here is where I've landed:

(a) Functionalism is correct. Mental states are individuated by their functional roles, and any substrate that implements the right organization — neurons, silicon, ants — thereby instantiates those states. Substrate is irrelevant; organization is everything.

(b) Aunt Hillary, as described, is genuinely conscious. There is something it is like to be the colony; she has beliefs, memories, and a stream of experience, and none of this requires any ant to be conscious of colony-level content.

(c) Block's Chinese Nation — the population of China linked by radios, simulating the functional organization of a brain neuron-by-neuron — is not conscious. However perfectly it implements the organization, a nation passing signals around does not add up to a mind; the intuition that something is missing seems to me bedrock.

(d) The individual ants have at most minimal sensorimotor experience, and whatever experience they do have neither contributes to nor detracts from Aunt Hillary's. The two minds coexist in the same patch of matter without rivalry and without causal overdetermination.

(e) Consciousness is not ubiquitous. Rocks, thermostats, and thunderclouds have none, precisely because they lack the requisite organization — so the line between minded and mindless systems is drawn by functional organization alone.

(f) If we replaced the ants one at a time with tiny radio-linked robots duplicating each ant's behavioral dispositions exactly, nothing mental would change; Aunt Hillary would persist, her experience undiminished, throughout the transition.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture on collective minds. Which pairs among (a)–(f) are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each, state precisely what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs me — including whether any repair that saves (c) can avoid collateral damage to (b) or (f). In particular, I would like your honest assessment of the escape routes I suspect I might reach for under pressure — that the Nation is too slow, that its members merely interpret a manual rather than realize neural roles, that the relevant causal grain is finer in the colony than in the nation — and whether each is a principled distinction or a cosmetic relabeling of the problem. Finally, if you could force exactly one revision to my list, which single change would restore the most consistency at the lowest price?
