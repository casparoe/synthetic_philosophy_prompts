I spent my career as an experimental scientist and have taken up philosophy in retirement; I am now trying to get the Peirce–James dispute about truth into a form rigorous enough to satisfy me. Act as an expert on classical American pragmatism and draft the joint statement that a committed Peircean and a committed Jamesian could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the nature of truth.

Before drafting, brainstorm the candidate formulations privately, then produce the final document with three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: I expect these to include that truth is tied to inquiry and experience rather than to a bare mirroring of a mind-independent order, and that both men took their pragmatism to be an improvement on the correspondence theory as commonly stated — but only include what each side would genuinely endorse, not vacuous common ground. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, each side phrased in words its own adherent would accept. My sense is that the deepest one concerns whether truth is fixed by what an ideal, indefinitely prolonged community of inquirers would converge on (Peirce's picture) or by what proves satisfactory and expedient in an individual's actual experience (James's picture), and that a second concerns beliefs no inquiry could ever settle, such as claims about the remote past or events leaving no trace. Third, for each disagreement, identify the crux: a case, argument, or consequence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or verdict recorded in advance.

If you conclude that part of their dispute was verbal — for instance, whether Peirce's complaints about James rest on an uncharitable reading of what James meant by the satisfactory or expedient — say which part, and justify the verdict.
