Someone in my AI safety reading group hit me with this argument last week and I hate where it lands but I can't find the bad step. Quick setup: there's this result, Löb's theorem, which roughly says that any consistent formal system strong enough to do basic arithmetic can't prove its own soundness in full generality -- it can't prove "whatever I prove is true" as a blanket claim. This is apparently why it's famously annoying to design an agent that can sign off on building a successor copy of itself: the parent can't establish that the child's reasoning is trustworthy, since the child runs the same reasoning. Okay, the argument:

(1) You're only justified in relying on a belief-forming method if you're justified in believing the method gets things right.
(2) Being justified in believing that requires being able to show it -- you can't just take the method's reliability on faith, that's not justification.
(3) By Löb's theorem, a reasoner can't show, in full generality, that its own reasoning gets things right.
(4) So no reasoner is justified in relying on its own reasoning in full generality.
(5) But following any plan -- even "I'll finish grading these exams tomorrow" -- means relying on your future self's reasoning, which is your own reasoning in the relevant respect.
(6) So nobody is ever justified in following their own plans, or in trusting anything they concluded yesterday.
(7) That's absurd, so something above has to go, but (3) is literally a math result and (1)-(2) just seem to spell out what justification is.

Which step do I reject? I keep feeling like the action is somewhere between (3) and (4), or that (2) is doing sneaky work, but I can't pin it down. If forced, I'd rather weaken (2) than swallow (6), but I want to know what I'm actually paying. Like, what do the standard escape routes here -- treating self-trust as default entitlement rather than something you earn, distinguishing the global soundness schema from trusting particular inferences one by one, the rule-circularity vs premise-circularity thing -- each give up? And does any of them survive contact with the original successor-agent problem, or do they all just change the subject?
