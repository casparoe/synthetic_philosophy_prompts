I teach a grad seminar on decision theory and every spring the same fight breaks out among my students about how to patch expected utility theory so it doesn't collapse the moment infinite payoffs enter the picture. I've gotten tired of refereeing, so I want an outside verdict. Below are five candidate rules, in my own shorthand, for an agent who might face infinite stakes, plus a test case I invented last term. Please rank the five rules from most to least defensible as general policies, and give each one a sentence or three of justification, in particular how it fares on my test case, which I designed so that each rule embarrasses itself in a different way. If you think two of them tie, or that one of them isn't even wrong but simply falls silent on the case, say so -- a justified tie is a fine answer.

Here are the rules. Rule one, the Cap: treat utility as bounded above by some finite ceiling, so infinities just never arise. Rule two, the Shrug: ignore any outcome whose probability falls below some fixed tiny epsilon, then maximize expected utility with what's left. Rule three, Lexical: any option with a nonzero chance of infinite value strictly beats any option with merely finite stakes, and ties among infinite options are broken by comparing their finite parts. Rule four, the Discount: weight consequences less the further away they are in space or time, steeply enough that any total converges. Rule five, Regions: prefer option A over option B only if, in every sufficiently large finite region of the universe, A's expected value inside that region beats B's.

And the test case. A reliable machine offers you three buttons, exactly one of which will be pressed, your choice. Button A guarantees a modest finite good, say it cures a painful disease for a million people. Button B has a one-in-a-billion chance of bringing about an endless flourishing civilization of infinite total value, and otherwise does nothing. Button C has a fifty percent chance of that same infinite flourishing and a fifty percent chance of infinite misery. Assume the machine is genuinely trustworthy and the probabilities are exact. Rank the five rules by how well they handle this, and where each one cracks, point to the crack. I'm especially curious whether you think the rules that refuse to rank B against C are being honest or being useless.

Format-wise, write it as continuous prose, like a longish email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Put the ranking itself in your opening sentence so I can see the bottom line at a glance, then defend it. Sorry this got so long, and thanks in advance -- the students will definately hear about whatever you say.
