Next month I'm presenting at a comparative ethics workshop and I'm nervous about the Q&A. My talk defends a position I've been calling "justificatory impartialism": I start from Mozi's impartial care (jian'ai) and argue that the *reasons* institutions and individuals give for caring must be impartial -- no one counts for less just because they're a stranger -- but that the actual *delivery* of care can and should be graded, flowing first through family and personal relationships, because that's the only psychologically stable and institutionally cheap way to get everyone cared for. So my claim is that Mozi and the Confucians are both half right: Mozi about justification, the Confucians about execution. The motivating case for me is triage and hospital resource allocation (I used to work in hospital admin), where the system has to be impartial but works by deputizing graded networks -- families, next of kin -- to do the actual caring.

The audience: mostly scholars of Confucian ethics, several of whom hold that graded love (love with distinctions) isn't just a practical concession but is constitutive of ren, so that "impartial concern" is either incoherent or amounts to not loving anyone at all. There's also at least one Mohist scholar who'll think I've sold Mozi out, and a stray utilitarian who'll think I've conceded too much to the Confucians. I expect Mencius's extension-of-the-sprouts argument and the charge that impartiality cuts off the root of benevolence, but I'm less sure how they land against a justification/execution split specifically.

Draft my prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'm actually going to get from *this* crowd, ordered roughly by how much damage they'd do to the talk, each phrased the way a Confucian scholar (or the Mohist, or the utilitarian, where it's theirs) would actually put it. For each: what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply, and a note on where my reply is weakest. And please flag the one question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I suspect it has something to do with whether "justification" and "execution" can even be separated once you take seriously that care is partly constituted by the carer's motives, but I havent been able to make the worry precise enough to answer it.
