I'm putting together a prospectus for a nine-month postdoctoral project in decision theory and would like you to draft it, roughly 800-1,000 words. No bibliography -- I'll attach citations myself -- but you should refer to the relevant positions and standard arguments by name or description so a specialist referee can place the project.

The project is on money pump arguments and what they actually establish. Here is my half-formed idea, which I want you to sharpen rather than simply endorse. The canonical money pump argument purports to show that cyclic preferences are irrational: if you strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, a trader can offer you a sequence of trades, each of which you accept, that returns you to your starting position minus some money. But the argument seems to hinge on how the agent behaves *across time*, not at a time. If the agent chooses myopically -- evaluating each offer in isolation, ignoring what she'll do later -- she's pumped. If she's a sophisticated chooser who foresees the sequence, she may refuse the first trade, in which case there's no pump. And a resolute chooser, one who adopts a plan and sticks to it even when a later preference reversal tempts her to abandon it, is likewise unpumpable. So the argument appears to establish, at most, a conditional: *if* you choose myopically, *then* cyclic preferences make you exploitable. The question I want to pursue is whether this conditional can be leveraged into an unconditional synchronic requirement of transitivity, or whether money pump arguments only ever deliver diachronic conclusions -- norms about how to sequence choices, plan, and bind yourself -- and, if the latter, whether 'don't be exploitable' is even a requirement of rationality as opposed to a contingent prudential fact that depends on your environment happening to contain pumpers.

The proposal should contain: (1) a precise statement of the central question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer -- I'd suggest framing it as whether non-pumpability, defined in terms of what an agent would accept given correct beliefs about her own future choices, is extensionally equivalent to transitivity, or whether there exist coherent diachronic policies that render a cyclic-preference agent unpumpable without converting her preferences into transitive ones; (2) a section explaining why the existing literature leaves this open, including the point that defenders of the money pump argument rarely specify the agent's decision policy explicitly, and that resolute-choice theorists have tended to defend resoluteness on independent grounds rather than asking what the pump argument becomes once the policy is a free variable; (3) the method, month by month -- I expect roughly three months on formalizing pumpability across decision policies, three on the taxonomy question (which policies render which preference structures unpumpable, and at what cost in foregone opportunities), and three on the normative upshot (whether exploitability arguments can ground synchronic coherence requirements at all, or only ever conditional diachronic ones); (4) what a positive result would look like, what a negative result would look like, and what would count as the project failing; and (5) an honest paragraph on scope risks.

Two further requirements. First, the proposal should take seriously the strongest objection to my framing -- that the appeal of money pump arguments was never really about pumps but about the agent's own acknowledged losses, so that dismissing them as 'merely prudential' misses the point -- and should present that objection fairly before explaining how the project engages it. Second, in a short final section, state explicitly what would change the project's verdict: which argument or formal result, if it emerged in the course of the work, would lead to the conclusion that money pump arguments do ground transitivity unconditionally, and identify the step in my framing where the reasoning is most likely to break down.
