I have been arguing with a colleague in the pub about the following set-up, and I would like to settle it with something more rigorous than intuition. Suppose physicists tell us, on the basis of our best confirmed theory, that a particular atomic process has a genuinely objective chance of 0.3 of occurring in the next hour -- not a frequency estimate, not a measure of our ignorance, but a real, theory-grounded propensity built into the world. I know this figure, and I have no other relevant evidence: no crystal ball, no insider knowledge of the microstate, nothing. The natural thought is that my rational credence in the process occurring should then be exactly 0.3, simply because 0.3 is the chance. This is roughly the thought that philosophers of probability formalise as the requirement that rational credence defer to known objective chance. My colleague insists this "defer" principle is either trivial or false; I think it is a substantive, correct norm of rationality.

So here is what I want. Give me your own credence that some suitable principle of deference to known objective chance is a genuine and correct requirement of rationality -- that is, that absent undermining information, one is rationally required to set one's credence equal to the known objective chance. Then decompose that number into the sub-questions it really depends on, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each: first, that there are objective single-case chances at all (as opposed to chance talk merely summarising frequencies or encoding our ignorance); second, that we can in principle come to know what those chances are; third, that knowing the chance rationally constrains credence in the way the principle says, rather than merely advising it; and fourth, whatever further term you think the argument needs -- for instance, whether the principle survives the awkward cases where the future could bear on what the chances themselves are. Tell me how the terms combine into your final figure, which term your number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would shift it most.

Please lead with your headline credence as a number, then give the decomposition. The whole answer must be no more than 300 words, in British English.
