I work in transport analytics for a mid-sized city, and a pattern in my own work has started to bother me enough that I've begun reading about revealed preference theory to see whether anyone has isolated it. The standard pitch, as I understand it, is that we needn't speculate about what people want: we watch what they choose, and if their choices are internally consistent, a utility function can be fitted that summarizes their preferences. My problem is that I keep producing fitted preferences that are, in a sense I can't make precise, artifacts of the menu rather than facts about the chooser.

Three cases from recent projects, all of the same shape. First: from commuting data we infer that residents of the outer districts place a low value on travel time, because they accept ninety-minute commutes rather than paying central-district rents. But the inference runs in a circle: the central rents are what pushed them out, so the choice that supposedly reveals their tolerance for commuting is produced by the very constraint the analysis treats as background. Second: our clinic no-show data suggest that a certain patient population does not value follow-up care, since they reliably decline it; the follow-up slots are only available during working hours, and these patients are hourly workers. Third, from software rather than transport: users "reveal" a preference for the default insurance plan, which nearly everyone keeps, though the plan was chosen by the employer and switching takes an afternoon of paperwork.

In each case the choices are real, free in the thin sense, and perfectly consistent, so a utility representation exists and my models fit beautifully. Yet the attributed preference would evaporate if the feasible set changed. I am aware of several nearby ideas, and I want to know which, if any, actually names this. There is the adaptive preferences literature, but that is about the chooser's desires shrinking to fit deprivation over time, whereas my commuters may want exactly what they wanted before; nothing in them has adapted. There is the econometrician's endogeneity problem, but that is a technical obstacle to estimation, not a claim that the estimated preference is itself a mirage. There is the old point that a destitute person's revealed choices are a poor guide to their welfare, but that point is usually made about welfare, whereas my worry is about the preference attribution itself, one level up.

So my questions: Does this specific failure mode — a preference attribution that re-ratifies the constraints that generated the choice, such that the inferred utility function describes the menu's shape rather than the chooser's dispositions — have an established name and literature? How does it differ, precisely, from adaptive preferences, from endogeneity, and from the general complaint that choices track constraints rather than values? And if nothing in the literature carves exactly here, propose a term with a definition, a test for when it applies, and at least two boundary cases where it should not apply — for instance, presumably not every constraint-sensitive choice is an artifact, or the concept would swallow all of economics. I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know where the phenomenon's edges are.
