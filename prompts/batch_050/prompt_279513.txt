A historian of moral revolutions gives brisk verdicts. On duelling in Britain, she says the practice collapsed when refusing a challenge ceased to damage a man's standing as a gentleman; the moral arguments had been available for centuries. On foot-binding in China, she says the decisive change was the pledge associations that made unbound feet compatible with respectable marriage, again reversing an honour incentive. On the abolition of the British slave trade, however, she sides with the Williams thesis: abolitionist arguments mattered less than the shift in imperial and commercial interests that made suppression useful to powerful actors. On witchcraft prosecutions, she says they ended because legal elites quietly changed their standards of evidence, not because of a mass moral campaign.

Now ask her about the disappearance of smoking in enclosed public places in a country where the medical evidence had been accepted for forty years but where, within one decade, smoking lost its association with sophistication, prestigious employers and restaurants banned it, and the treasury became less dependent on tobacco duties. What would she say did the real work? Identify at least two general rules that fit her earlier verdicts, state what each predicts here, give your best prediction, and name one further case that would best distinguish the rival rules.
