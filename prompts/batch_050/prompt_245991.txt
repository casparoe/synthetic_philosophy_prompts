I'm in Grade 11 and recently listened to a philosophy podcast episode recommended by my history teacher, in which the host summarized Leibniz's famous doctrine that we live in the best of all possible worlds. I wrote down her summary because it struck me as suspiciously easy to mock, and I'd like to know whether it does Leibniz justice. Here is what she said, as I recorded it: "Leibniz reasoned that since God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and perfectly good, He must have created the best world He could, which means our world is the best of all possible worlds. So everything that happens — wars, plagues, earthquakes — is actually for the best, and any world without these things would have been worse overall. Leibniz concluded that we should accept suffering calmly rather than fight against it, since resisting evil would mean trying to make the perfect world worse. Voltaire demolished this philosophy in Candide simply by showing his characters enduring one catastrophe after another, proving that optimism of Leibniz's kind is absurd."

My uncertainty is this: the summary makes Leibniz sound like someone who thinks each individual event is good and who preached doing nothing in the face of injustice, and yet I remember our textbook mentioning that Leibniz was a tireless reformer and diplomat, which hardly fits a passive fatalist. I also wonder whether a satirical novel showing people suffer really counts as an argument against a philosophical position, or whether it only ridicules a caricature of it. On the other hand, the basic inference from God's attributes to the best possible world does seem to be genuinely Leibniz's, and I don't want to dismiss the podcast unfairly just because its conclusion is unpopular.

Could you go through the host's summary and tell me, point by point, what a careful Leibniz scholar would accept, what is a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what misrepresents Leibniz's actual view? In particular, I'd like to know whether "best possible world" means what the host takes it to mean, whether Leibniz really drew the quietist moral she attributes to him, and whether Voltaire's satire, properly understood, refutes anything Leibniz actually claimed — or whether correcting the summary leaves some version of the doctrine standing that Candide never touches.
