Here's a debate that has eaten several evenings of my philosophy of physics reading group, and I want to understand why it's going nowhere rather than get another tour of the positions. The topic is what, if anything, is physically real in a gauge theory beyond the gauge-invariant stuff. Everyone in the room agrees on the formalism: gauge transformations don't change any observable, the vector potential is underdetermined, only gauge-invariant quantities like field strengths and Wilson loops are unambiguous. Nobody disputes the math. The fight is about what to say about it.

Roughly three camps have formed. Camp one says the gauge-dependent parts of the description are pure surplus structure, like coordinate choices, and reality consists only of the gauge-invariant content — so the Aharonov-Bohm effect should be chalked up to the holonomies, which are real but weirdly non-local. Camp two says the gauge potential itself is real and gauge symmetry is just redundancy in our description of it, the way one object can have many names; they think the AB effect shows the invariants are too coarse to capture the local facts. Camp three, the smallest, says the question is malformed because gauge symmetries become physical at boundaries — they keep pointing at edge modes and soft charges at infinity and saying the "redundancy" does observable work, so it was never just redundancy. Whenever camp one replies that edge modes are themselves only defined up to gauge and so can't settle anything, camp three says that's exactly the point and we go in a circle.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't a physics disagreement at all but a disagreement about what a physical theory owes us: camp one thinks a theory must give a one-to-one map between mathematical structure and physical structure, camp two thinks it's fine for nature to come with multiple mathematical presentations, and camp three is really making a point about what counts as an observation (they weight boundary measurements and subsystem talk more heavily). So the same word, "real," is doing different jobs. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a hidden empirical question, or a theorem, lurking under all of it.

Diagnose this for me: is it verbal, substantive-but-conceptual, or is someone actually making a physics claim that could be tested or proven? Use what each camp is actually committed to, not caricatures. And tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result about subsystems or boundaries — or argue that nothing could move it and why that wouldn't make the question worthless.
