I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a column about what makes consent actually valid -- when does a yes count as a yes -- and I want to anchor it in Measure for Measure, which seems bizarrely ahead of the modern debate. The play gives you both failure modes in one plot: Angelo's proposal to spare Claudio's life if Isabella sleeps with him, so a yes extracted by threat, and then the bed trick, where Angelo sleeps with Mariana in the dark believing she's Isabella, so a yes given under deception about who is literally in the bed. Read the play through the philosophy of consent: do the threat and the deception void consent in the same way, or are they different kinds of wrong? And here's the part I keep chewing on: Angelo gets the very act he agreed to, just with a different woman, and the play treats this as a clever trick rather than a violation, ending with him married off to Mariana and roughly forgiven. Modern accounts tend to say deception only invalidates consent when it touches something the person would have treated as a deal-breaker, but who gets to decide what counts as one? Take a stand on whether the play's moral arithmetic is defensible or whether it exposes a hole the consent literature still hasn't closed -- I want a verdict I can argue with in the column, not a menu of positions.
