I direct the early modern program at a small liberal arts college, and each spring our department stages an event we call Conversations with the Dead, in which I interview a historical philosopher impersonated by a colleague who has agreed to stay strictly in character and to defend the figure's genuine positions rather than a caricature. This year's guest is Descartes, and the evening will be devoted entirely to his doctrine of animal automatism. In rehearsal my colleague has proved slippery, and I want questions that cut off his two favorite escapes. Whenever I ask whether animals feel anything, he says only that his metaphysics denies them thought and remains agnostic about sensation; whenever I offer evidence of animal intelligence, he recites his two marks of genuine mind — flexible speech, and conduct guided by general reason rather than by the arrangement of the organs — and dismisses the evidence as mechanism all the way down.

Draft me eight to ten questions for the interview, sequenced so that the ground closes in on him. For each question, include in the same breath the reply he is most likely to give and the follow-up I should have ready for it. Over the course of the sequence I want the following threads developed, in whatever order builds the best pressure: his own practice of dissection, and the trouble that anatomical continuity between human and animal nervous systems makes for a physics that is uniform everywhere except at the pineal gland; the worry that a non-deceiving God has populated the world with creatures that produce every outward sign of agony when injured, so that our spontaneous and seemingly irresistible belief in animal suffering would count as a natural falsehood; the cases of human infants, the congenitally deaf, and the mad, who pass his marks imperfectly or not at all; whether he would follow a disciple who went further than he did and denied animals even feeling, or whether something in his system forbids that step; the moral consequence of the doctrine, that is, whether cruelty to animals is wrong at all on his view or merely imprudent because of the habits it forms in us; and finally a hypothetical I intend to reserve for the close of the evening, an artificial contrivance that converses fluently and adapts its behavior to novel circumstances — whether he would grant it a rational soul, concede that his marks are defeasible, or insist that we remain deceived by a cleverer machine.

Two constraints on the form of your answer. Write in American English throughout. And present everything as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold text, no tables. Each question, together with its anticipated reply and its follow-up, should form a single unadorned paragraph, so that I can read the sequence through as naturally as it will be spoken.
