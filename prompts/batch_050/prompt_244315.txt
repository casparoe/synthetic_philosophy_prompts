I am a retired experimental physicist, and in the year since my retirement I have been working my way through some of the classics of moral philosophy I never had time for. Lately it has been Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I have become quietly obsessed with the impartial spectator and with Smith's distinction between wanting to be praised and wanting to be praiseworthy. The timing is not accidental: next month I am to give the after-dinner speech, perhaps ten minutes long, at a small festschrift dinner for a colleague of forty years, a theorist in my former department. The complication is that early in our careers the two of us were embroiled in a priority dispute over a result we each claimed to have reached first. It was never publicly adjudicated, we both behaved decently enough outwardly, and it has hung between us ever since. I do not want the speech to relitigate it, nor do I want to paper over it with the usual banquet sentimentalities, which he would see through instantly.

The audience will be mixed: half physicists who know the old story in outline, half spouses, administrators, and a few philosophers from across the quad who will catch every Smith reference. Please write the speech so that the impartial spectator does real structural work rather than appearing as an ornament. Specifically, I would like the speech to raise the question of how either of us could ever have known, from the inside, whose claim was just -- given that each of us was judging his own case -- and to use Smith's answer (that we learn to see ourselves as an imagined impartial observer would) both to explain why the dispute mattered so much to us and to suggest what genuine resolution would even consist in. I would also like the praise-versus-praiseworthiness distinction to carry the ending: a way of honoring my colleague that does not depend on settling who was right, and that a room full of scientists -- professionally addicted to priority, citation counts, and prizes -- will feel as a gentle rebuke rather than a flattery of the honoree or a confession by the speaker.

A few constraints. No verbatim quotations from Smith, please; paraphrase his ideas faithfully and let the philosophically literate guests recognize them on their own. Do not name the disputed result or assign blame in either direction, even obliquely. The tone should be dry, warm, and a little self-deprecating about physicists discovering moral philosophy late in life, but the Smith material must be accurate enough that the philosophers present do not wince -- among other things, do not conflate the impartial spectator with God, with public opinion, or with simple empathy. Around 900 words.
