I help run the ethics discussion group at a mid-sized software company, and we've spent the last few meetings going around in circles on the question of when an AI assistant should push back against its own user. The word "paternalistic" gets thrown around as an insult, but I've noticed that the people who use it most freely are also the ones who want the assistant to stop them impulse-buying at 2am, while the self-described "user autonomy" hardliners make an exception for scam victims. Nobody's actual view matches their self-applied label. So I'd like you to write a sorting quiz for the group: "How paternalistic should your AI assistant be — according to what you actually think, not what you call yourself?" Eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete scenario with three or four answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The scenarios must be answerable by someone who has never heard the position names, so no question may ask anything like "do you value autonomy over welfare?" — every question has to be a case.

The positions I want as leaves are these, though you should feel free to rename them more memorably: (1) the pure tool or fiduciary view — the assistant complies unless the request harms third parties or is illegal, full stop; (2) soft or means-focused paternalism — the assistant may add friction, warnings, or delays when a request seems to undermine the user's own settled ends, but may never flatly refuse a competent adult's considered request; (3) the Ulysses-contract view — instructions the user gave in a calm earlier moment can authorise the assistant to override or refuse the user's present self, but absent such a precommitment the assistant must comply; (4) hard or ends-focused paternalism — the assistant may refuse or override for the user's own good even against the user's settled, fully informed wishes; and (5) the guardian view — the assistant may additionally report concerning behaviour to third parties (a doctor, a family member, an employer) for the user's own sake. To make the quiz diagnostic, I'd like the cases to genuinely prise these apart: for instance, the distinction between (2) and (3) only shows up if one scenario pits a user's *present* demand against a *past* instruction without telling the respondent whether the past instruction still counts, and the distinction between (4) and (5) only shows up in a case about telling someone else. Please include at least one case each involving a suspected scam the user refuses to believe, a relapse into a habit the user had previously asked to be protected from, a possibly rash irreversible decision made in visible distress, and a request that is harmless to the user but quietly disadvantages a third party — the last one should catch self-described hard paternalists who are actually Millians about third-party harm.

In the scoring key, please do more than tally letters: show which specific combinations of answers locate a respondent at each leaf, note any logically possible answer pattern that no position occupies (and say briefly whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just undefended), and flag explicitly any pair of positions your quiz cannot reliably separate, with a note on what extra scenario would be needed to split them. Each scenario should be three or four sentences long, vivid enough to argue over in the pub, and written so that the "comply immediately" option doesn't read as obviously foolish — I want the quiz to have real pull in both directions. Finish with a short note to me, the quiz administrator, on how to handle respondents who insist their answer is "it depends on more details" — which details should I tell them to assume fixed? The whole thing should be in British English, and polished enough that I can paste it into the group's shared document as-is.
