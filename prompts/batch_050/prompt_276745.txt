A bit of background on me, since it shapes what I'm after: I'm a retired physiologist (cardiovascular, mostly) who spent fourty years in the lab and has lately fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole. The thing that hooked me is the definition-of-death mess. Back when I was teaching I told students the brain-death criterion was settled science; now that I've actually read some of the philosophical literature I'm a bit embarassed, because it looks to me like neither standard really works on its own terms and both are quietly held in place by the needs of organ transplantation.

So here's the task. Act as a professional philosopher of medicine who also gets the clinical details right -- I don't want to catch you confusing brain stem testing with a CT scan or something. Take your time. Brainstorm both sides properly before you commit to any formulations, and then write the final document.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two people who have argued about this for years without giving an inch:

- Party A defends the neurological standard: death is the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem. The organism is dead even if a ventilator keeps its heart beating and its skin warm.
- Party B holds that only the circulatory-respiratory standard tracks real death, and that "brain death" is at best an extremely reliable prognosis of imminent death, not death itself. On their view, calling a perfused body with a beating heart "dead" is a useful fiction we maintain because we want the organs.

The document needs three parts:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims -- things both parties can honestly sign. Don't stop at trivialities. I want agreements about the biology (what a brain-dead body on support can and cannot still do, what actually happens in controlled donation after circulatory death) and agreements about the logic of the debate (for instance, that no pile of empirical data alone settles which standard is right, since "death" is not a purely empirical term -- if they both accept that, say so explicitly).

2. A disagreements table: each row states one residual disagreement in neutral wording both parties would accept, then one sentence each on how A and B would state their own position. Be careful that the shared wording doesn't smuggle in one side's answer -- for example, don't define "irreversible" one particular way in the supposedly neutral text.

3. Cruxes: the sub-questions such that, if one got settled, at least one side would have to move. For each crux, give a concrete case, observation, or imaginable finding that both sides agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts or concedes about it. Two cruxes I already suspect belong on the list: first, whether "irreversible" should mean cannot be reversed by any currently available means or will not be reversed because nobody is going to attempt it -- this bites hard in controlled DCD, where circulation has stopped for only a few minutes before procurement begins and spontaneous restart after longer intervals is rare but documented; second, whether what makes a human death a death is the loss of the organism as an integrated whole or the loss of the capacity for consciousness, and what to make of brain-dead bodies maintained for months, including pregnant ones that gestated a fetus to viability, which seems like awkward data for the integration story. Find at least one further crux I haven't thought of.

If you conclude that part of their disagreement is really verbal -- that on some sub-question both sides accept all the same facts and are just fighting over who gets to keep the word "dead" -- then say which part and why. But don't pull the cheap philosopher's move of declaring the whole thing a language dispute, because I suspect at least one genuine disagreement survives.

Ground rules: don't strawman B as a religious sentimentalist -- the strongest version of that view is held by people who fully support organ donation and just think we're kidding ourselves about when donors die. Don't let A rest anything on "the law says so." And no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I can track down sources myself, and I'd rather you get the arguments right than namedrop papers you half-remember.
