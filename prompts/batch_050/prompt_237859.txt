In discussions of Davidson's radical interpretation, the situation of the interpreter is standardly presented with an analogy from algebra: from evidence about which sentences a speaker assents to in which circumstances, the interpreter must recover both what the speaker's words mean and what the speaker believes, and this is said to be like being asked to solve a single equation containing two unknowns -- x + y = 7, find x and y. The analogy is then put to work in two directions. Some use it to argue that interpretation is radically underdetermined, so that many rival meaning-belief assignments fit any body of evidence equally well. Others, including readers of Davidson himself, use it to explain why the principle of charity is not an optional courtesy but a constitutive constraint: the only way to make the problem solvable is to fix one unknown by stipulating that the speaker's beliefs are largely true by the interpreter's lights. Audit the analogy. Give the correspondence explicitly -- what plays the role of the equation, the two unknowns, the givens, and the solution -- and then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, consider: whether meaning and belief are independent unknowns in the way x and y are, given that beliefs are individuated partly by the meanings of the sentences used to express them; whether the algebraic case contains anything corresponding to the interpreter's ongoing ability to revise assignments in light of new evidence; and whether the analogy misrepresents charity as an externally imposed constraint rather than something else. Finally, say which of the two conclusions drawn from the analogy, if either, survives once the disanalogies are made explicit, and whether a better analogy for the interpreter's predicament is available.
