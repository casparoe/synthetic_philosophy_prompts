I teach a seminar on moral psychology, and a pattern keeps surfacing in our discussions of criminal responsibility that I have been unable to match to any established concept in the literature I know, though I suspect one exists. The configuration is this: consider a person who passes every verbal test of moral competence we know how to administer. Asked whether an unprovoked assault is wrong, he says yes; asked whether it would still be wrong if the local authorities permitted it or if everyone in his community approved of it, he says yes, and ranks it as serious rather than trivial. By every measure on the questionnaire, his judgments are indistinguishable from those of any healthy subject. Yet the judgments appear to be inert. They produce no hesitation before he acts, no distress afterward, no place in his deliberations, and when asked why he answered as he did, he treats the question the way one might treat trivia about a foreign country's traffic laws -- correct information he happens to possess, connected to nothing. He can also deploy wrongness-talk fluently and strategically when it serves him, which suggests the vocabulary itself is intact.

My students keep reaching for nearby notions and finding them all slightly off. It is not weakness of will, because there is no competing motivation to be overridden; it is not hypocrisy, because there is no gap between his public pronouncements and his private convictions -- he is entirely sincere in both settings; and it is not simple ignorance, since on any standard test of moral judgment he scores normally. What seems missing is some connection between the judgment and everything else a judgment is ordinarily plugged into. My question is twofold. First, does this exact dissociation have a settled name, and if so, which of the candidates in the vicinity -- a distinction between knowing that something is wrong and some richer state sometimes called moral understanding, or the debate about whether moral judgment necessarily carries motivation with it -- actually picks out this phenomenon rather than merely overlapping it? Second, and more important to me than the label: what should we say this person has and lacks? Does he know that what he does is wrong, in the sense relevant to blame, or does the hollowness of his judgments show that the tests were measuring the wrong thing all along? I would rather have a careful carving of the phenomenon and its boundary cases than a bare term, and if nothing established quite fits, I would welcome a coined name with a definition precise enough to exclude akrasia and hypocrisy.
