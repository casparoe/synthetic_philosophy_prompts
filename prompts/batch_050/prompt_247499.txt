For the past three years I have recorded an amateur history-of-science podcast with an old friend. Each episode ends with a segment we call The Verdict, in which we settle who deserves the credit for a famous discovery or invention. I do the reading and present the cases; she delivers the rulings. We are now preparing an episode on which we seem likely to fall out, and I should like to predict how she will rule before we record it.

Her verdicts to date have been these:

1. The calculus: joint credit to Newton and Leibniz. Her reasoning was that the work was genuinely independent on both sides at the decisive stages, so neither man could be demoted; the fact that Leibniz published first while Newton had it earlier in unpublished papers did not, in her view, break the tie.

2. Natural selection: Darwin alone. She accepted that Wallace's essay arrived in 1858 and that the two men's ideas were presented together, but held that the credit belongs to the man who had spent twenty years assembling the evidence and whose account was developed enough actually to change the science. Wallace, in her phrase, guessed right once.

3. The steam engine: Watt. She acknowledged that Newcomen's engines had been pumping mines for decades beforehand, and that still earlier devices stretch back to antiquity, but ruled that the credit belongs to the person whose improvements turned a curiosity into the machine that powered an industrial revolution.

4. The structure of DNA: Watson and Crick, but with a rider that has stayed with me. Because they made crucial use of Franklin's data without her knowledge, she said the episode could not count as a case of independent discovery at all, and she added that had the debt been much greater she would have refused them the credit outright.

The next episode concerns the discovery of oxygen, and the historical record, so far as I have it, is roughly this. Scheele isolated the gas first, in the early 1770s, but did not publish until after the others. Priestley obtained it independently shortly afterwards and was first into print, in the mid-1770s, but interpreted it entirely within the old phlogiston theory, calling it dephlogisticated air and never grasping what he had. Lavoisier came last, learned of the new air directly from Priestley, gave the gas its name and its modern theoretical role, and it was his account that displaced phlogiston and founded modern chemistry.

What I should like from you, in order, is the following. First, a prediction of her verdict, with the reasoning she would most probably offer. Secondly, a statement of the rule her four rulings appear to be running, and of any other rules that fit those four rulings equally well; I note myself that they seem to have killed off both strict priority and first-to-publish, but that leaves more than one candidate standing. Thirdly, an account of which of those candidate rules diverge on the oxygen case, and what each of them delivers. I should particularly like to know whether her DNA rider, which seems to treat independence as a hard constraint, can be squared with her Darwin ruling, which seems to reward depth over origin. Fourthly, the name of a further priority dispute, real or constructed, whose resolution would best distinguish between the two most plausible rules.

I am not asking you to adjudicate the oxygen case yourself, though you are welcome to say where you think the credit lies. What I chiefly want is the prediction and the map of her possible rules, so that I know what I am arguing with before we sit down to record.
