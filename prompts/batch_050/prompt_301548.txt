So I TA a first-year intro epistemology course at my university — about 24 undergrads, mostly non-majors, and I get one 75-minute session to run however I want. We've been talking about disagreement in everyday life, but they haven't seen the words "conciliationism" or "steadfastness" yet, and I'd like to keep it that way until the very end. I want an activity that makes them actually feel the pull of both sides of the peer disagreement debate: the sense in which splitting the difference with an equally smart friend seems obviously rational, and the sense in which backing down just because someone disagrees feels like betraying your own judgment. Pretend you're an experienced philosophy instructor who's run this kind of thing many times, and think carefully about the game design before you write anything up — I'd rather have one well-thought-out mechanic than three half-baked ones.

Here's roughly the shape I'm imagining, and you should design around it: something with real but small stakes (points, candy, whatever) where students make estimates or judgments on questions with objectively right answers, discover that a peer disagrees with them, and then get to revise or stick. The design problem I can't crack is how to arrange the rounds so that in some of them the information really is symmetric (so students learn the hard way that averaging beats stubbornness) while in other rounds one person secretly had worse or better information (so blindly splitting the difference burns them), ideally with at least one round where they know a peer's general track record but not what that peer got to see this time. Crucially, the disagreement should emerge from the the rules and setup, not from me telling them to disagree. So give me: the full rules, materials, the scoring/payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and the actual questions or stimuli you'd use — they need to be generatable without me hunting down obscure trivia facts, because I want answers I'm completely sure of.

Then the debrief: map specific moments of the game onto the conciliationism vs. steadfastness positions and whatever else naturally comes up (higher-order evidence, self-trust, the "spinelessness" worry, whatever fits), tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students colluding, estimates not actually being independent, one loud person anchoring the whole table — and how to head it off, and be honest about which feature of the philosophical problem the game can't reproduce: my hunch is that real peer disagreement involves trusting your own reasoning in a way no estimation game captures, but push back if you think I'm wrong about that. Also please do a bit of brainstorming about the overall arc of the session before commiting to the specifics.
