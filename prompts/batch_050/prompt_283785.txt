Amateur number theory hobbyist here. There's a move I see mathematicians make all the time and I can't figure out what it's called, if anything.

When someone says the Riemann hypothesis is "almost certainly true," they usually don't point to a proof, and not even mainly to the trillions of checked zeros. The argument I hear is more like: the primes behave, as far as we can tell, like a sequence drawn by some random process; in that random process the interesting conjectures hold with probability one; and there's no known mechanism that would push the actual primes away from the random model. So the conjecture wins by default unless a mechanism shows up. Same with Goldbach -- every even number has plenty of prime-pair decompositions, the count keeps growing with size, so for a counterexample the primes would have to activly conspire to avoid each other, and nobody can see any reason why they would.

What interests me is that this "wins by default" reasoning is doing real work -- people openly say they'd be shocked if RH were false -- even though it clearly isn't proof, and it also isn't just "we checked a lot of cases and they all passed." It's a third thing: you build a deliberately fake, randomized stand-in for the object, show the conjecture holds for the stand-in almost surely, and then treat the burden of proof as having flipped onto anyone who thinks the real object behaves differently from the fake one.

Does this style of argument have an established name or a literature around it? I know Pólya wrote about plausible reasoning in mathematics, but I think his thing was much broader than this. I want the specific concept: the step where you substitute a random model and then presume the model and the original agree unless forced to conclude otherwise. Two more things I'd want from a good answer: (a) how is this different from ordinary extrapolation from computed cases, given that computed cases are also just data points, and (b) what are its known failure modes -- I'm thinking of things like the π(x) vs li(x) business, where every computed case goes one way but Littlewood proved the sign flips somewhere out past anything checkable, so the "no conspiracy" default would apparently have been wrong there. Why did the default fail in that case but is trusted in others?

If it turns out there's no clean established name for exactly this move, say so, coin one, and give me its edges: what counts as an instance, what's a near-miss, and when the inference is at its weakest.
