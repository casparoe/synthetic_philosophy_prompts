I have no academic background in philosophy — my training is in accounting — but for the past few years I have been turning over one problem in my spare time. I take myself to know all sorts of ordinary things: that I have two hands, that my car is where I parked it, that the animal at the zoo was a zebra. Yet I cannot rule out far-fetched scenarios in which all of it is false (a dream I cannot detect, a simulation, a painted mule). Either my knowledge somehow reaches far enough to exclude those scenarios, or it does not, in which case something has to give. From my own reading I have learned that this territory is well mapped — closure principles, Robert Nozick's tracking account, Fred Dretske's denial that knowledge transmits across such deductions, David Lewis on how attention changes what counts, contextualists and their bank cases — but every map I have found sorts people by the labels they apply to themselves rather than by what they would actually say about cases.

I would like you to build me a proper decision tree instead. Specifically:

1. Ten to fourteen binary or three-way questions, each built on a concrete scenario or everyday claim — the zebra that might be a painted mule, the lottery ticket you are sure will lose, the bank that will be open Saturday, the countryside dotted with fake barns — phrased so that a reader can answer without knowing a single philosopher's name. No question may simply ask whether one accepts closure or sensitivity; each such principle must be operationalized as a case where endorsing it costs something.

2. The leaves must include at least: Nozick's tracking view; Dretske-style denial of closure on different grounds; a Moorean common-sense position that keeps both ordinary knowledge and closure; outright skepticism; stakes-based contextualism; Lewis-style contextualism driven by which possibilities one is currently attending to; and a safety-based view that preserves closure by requiring only that the belief could not easily have been false. If the answer-space contains coherent combinations that no one actually defends, mark those leaves as unoccupied and briefly diagnose why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

3. Construct the tree from the logical space first: lay out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions within that space, rather than drawing branches backwards from a preset list of -isms.

4. Flag explicitly any pair of positions the tree cannot separate. I suspect Nozick and Dretske will both slide off the same closure-denying branch, and that distinguishing them requires a second-round question about whether knowledge depends on the method by which the belief was formed. If I am wrong about that, show me the question that separates them earlier.

5. At each leaf, one sentence in plain language stating the position's most awkward commitment — for instance, that on some views you can know you have hands while not knowing you are not a handless brain in a vat.

The intended audience is a reading circle of friends, none of whom have studied philosophy; the finished tree will be printed as a single-page flowchart we each walk through to see where we land.
