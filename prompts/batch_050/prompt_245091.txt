So here's a thing that's been bugging me about how the criminal justice system handles psychopathy. The insanity defense (at least in M'Naghten-style jurisdictions) asks whether the defendant knew what he was doing and knew it was wrong, and psychopaths basically always fail that test as an excuse -- they can recite the rules, they know murder is "against the law," so they're held fully responsible. But then at sentencing, the exact same diagnosis gets used against them: prosecutors bring in expert testimony that psychopathy predicts recidivism and untreatability, and it functions as an aggravating factor, sometimes even in death penalty cases. Meanwhile defendants with intellectual disability or severe emotional disturbance get mitigation, partly on the grounds that they didn't really grasp what they were doing in the right way.

What view of moral knowledge does this whole setup presuppose? Like, is the law committed to the idea that knowing right from wrong is purely cognitive -- being able to state the rules and predict sanctions -- and that emotional understanding is irrelevant to responsibility? If so, is that consistent with then treating psychopathic callousness as making someone *more* blameworthy rather than as a deficit that undermines the very knowledge the conviction assumed? Or is the practice actually running two different theories of moral knowledge at different stages? And tell me what would concretely have to change -- in the defense, in sentencing, or both -- if the law instead adopted the view (which some researchers defend) that real knowledge of wrongness requires some capacity to care, so that psychopaths only "know" in a thin, quote-unquote sense. Where's the practice philosophically underdetermined vs. where does it genuinely commit itself?
