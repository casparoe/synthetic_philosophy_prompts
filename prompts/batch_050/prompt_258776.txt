Frederick Douglass spent the 1840s denouncing the Constitution as a proslavery compact, then reversed course around 1851 and argued the opposite: that the text nowhere names slavery, that ambiguous clauses must be construed against slavery rather than for it, that the three-fifths clause actually disfavored slave states relative to the alternative, and—crucially—that the private intentions of the framers are not the law; only the enacted text and its publicly declared objects are. Suppose he were brought to the present and briefed on the modern debate between originalism (in both its original-public-meaning and original-intent variants) and living constitutionalism. Where does his interpretive *method*, as opposed to his substantive politics, actually land him? I'd like the reasoning run on his documented hermeneutic practice rather than on guesses about which modern faction he'd find congenial—in the spirit of asking what an agent's decision procedure outputs, not which outputs he'd applaud. Please work through the hard cases: (1) scholarship establishing that the ratifying public overwhelmingly understood the Fugitive Slave Clause as a concession to slavery—does Douglass have to accept that this fixes its meaning, or is his appeal to the text doing different work entirely?; (2) a modern originalist holding whose outcome he would abhor but whose reasoning—fixed meaning at ratification—mirrors his own stated rule; and (3) his own willingness to cite framers' antislavery statements when convenient, which might suggest the method was outcome-driven all along. I'm less interested in the verdict than in whether Douglass's position contains a stable interpretive algorithm at all, or whether "construe the text against slavery" collapses into living constitutionalism with a nineteenth-century accent.
