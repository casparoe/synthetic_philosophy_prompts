So I'm a philosophy prof and, lucky me, I got voluntold onto our university's student conduct board. We handle academic integrity cases -- plagiarism, cheating, the odd falsified lab data -- maybe 15-20 cases a semester. The other members are admins and one very tired lawyer, and the whole thing runs on vibes. Some cases end with a quiet meeting and a reflective essay, others basically amount to a public dressing-down in front of a panel, and honestly which one you get seems to depend on who's in the room that day.

Here's what I want. There's a real philosophical and psychological distinction between guilt (I did a bad thing) and shame (I am a bad person), and between sanctions aimed at repair and ones aimed at exposure. I want to turn that distinction into an actual decision procedure the board can follow when we design sanctions: which questions to ask about the case and the student, in what order, what counts as an answer to each, and where the procedure says "this kind of sanction is off the table" vs. "this is a judgment call, argue it out." It should be usable by people who have never read a word of moral psychology -- no jargon, or jargon that gets defined in one sentence each.

A few things it has to handle:
- The difference between a sanction meant to make the student feel bad about what they *did* versus one that works by making them feel bad about *who they are* in front of others -- and whether the second kind is ever justified at all.
- Cases where the student shows genuine remorse vs. obviously strategic remorse vs. none, and whether that should even matter.
- Deterrence: the rest of the board LOVES the idea that visible punishment deters, so the procedure needs to say what the guilt/shame framework actually implies about that, even if the board hates the answer.
- Repeat offenders.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a first-year who panicked and copied two paragraphs the night before a deadline, came forward before being caught; (2) a senior who ran a small operation selling essay answers, unrepentant; (3) a grad student who fudged data under pressure from their advisor, where going after the student publicly would feel like punishing the wrong person.

Also be honest about where the procedure breaks down or where the empirical/psychological claims underneath it are shakier than I'd like. And if there's a point where the procedure spits out a recommendation a university lawyer or administrator would balk at, flag that too. Roughly checklist-or-flowchart-in-words format, something I could actually bring to our next meeting.
