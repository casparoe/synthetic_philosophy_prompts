I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a piece about one of the oldest fights in the social sciences: why some nations ended up rich and others poor. Rather than interview the two camps separately and let them talk past each other on my pages, I want to publish the document they would produce if they had to collaborate adversarially. So: write the joint statement that a committed institutions-first scholar (who holds that the wealth of nations is explained primarily by the quality of their political and economic institutions, and that geography and culture matter mostly through their effects on institutions) and a committed geography-first scholar (who holds that deep environmental factors -- climate, disease burden, crop and livestock endowments, continental orientation, access to trade routes -- do most of the causal work, with institutions largely downstream of these) could both sign after a long good-faith collaboration.

The document should contain the claims both sides can genuinely endorse: what the cross-country evidence does and does not show, what the natural experiments and historical episodes (colonization reversals, divided countries, settlement patterns) establish and where they are fragile, and which popular arguments each side agrees are bad ones. It should then state the remaining disagreements in exact terms, each phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording as fair -- for instance, whether institutions are a proximate cause layered over a deeper geographic cause or an independent causal force, and whether the concept of culture does any work that institutions and geography cannot. For each disagreement, identify the crux: the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side, and describe the evidence, historical case, or methodological advance that both sides agree would bear on that crux, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal -- about what counts as an institution, or what counts as an explanation as opposed to a background condition -- say so explicitly and explain why.

Two further requests. First, end with a candid assessment of the statement's own weaknesses: which agreed claim is most likely to be overturned, which crux is least likely ever to be settled by evidence, and what consideration or argument would lead you to redraw the line between genuine and verbal disagreement. Second, present the entire statement as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
