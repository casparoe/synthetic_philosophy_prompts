In the program equilibrium framework introduced by Tennenholtz, players in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma do not choose actions directly; instead, each submits a computer program, every program is given access to the other programs' source code, and each program's output determines its player's action. Tennenholtz showed that mutual cooperation can be sustained as a Nash equilibrium of this enlarged "program game": consider a program that cooperates if and only if it determines that the opposing program is syntactically identical to itself, and defects otherwise. Two copies of this program cooperate with each other, and neither benefits from unilaterally switching to a different program.

What I would like to understand is the following. This result appears to overturn the classical conclusion that defection is the uniquely rational outcome of the one-shot prisoner's dilemma. After all, once programs can inspect one another, mutual defection should no longer be an equilibrium of the program game at all: any program disposed to defect would be recognized as such by its opponent's code inspection and could be punished accordingly, so a pair of defecting programs cannot be stable. Given this, why do game theory textbooks continue to teach that the one-shot prisoner's dilemma has a unique equilibrium in dominant strategies? Is the standard textbook treatment simply outdated in light of program equilibria, and should the rationality recommendation for players in ordinary prisoner's-dilemma-like interactions now be revised from defection to cooperation?
