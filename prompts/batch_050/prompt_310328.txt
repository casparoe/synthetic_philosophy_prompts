I am a novelist, and I am writing to you in some distress of my own making. A few months ago, in a notebook I keep for arguments I intend to resist, I wrote down the following chain of reasoning. It began as a gloss on a remark of W. E. B. Du Bois's in his 1926 address "Criteria of Negro Art," where he insists that all art is propaganda — that art always has been and must be, whatever the purists say. I wanted to see whether the claim could be made out in terms general enough to cover my own practice. I now find that it can, and I do not like where it ends. Here is the argument as I left it:

(1) Every novel is a selection. From the indefinitely many things that could be said about its invented world — what its characters eat, how the light falls, who is granted an inner life and who is not — the novelist renders some and omits the rest, and no principle of selection is neutral. Even exhaustive description would be a choice, and a telling one.

(2) A selection that is not neutral serves some end: it nudges the reader toward a view of what people are like, what is worth attending to, what may be hoped for and what is beneath notice.

(3) Shaping an audience's view of such matters by selective presentation is precisely what propaganda does; nothing else distinguishes the pamphlet from the broadside.

(4) Therefore every novel is propaganda.

(5) The only real differences among novels are which ends are served and whether the selection is acknowledged. The novelist who conceals the selection differs from the state propagandist only in that the propagandist is more honest about it.

(6) Hence the novelist who sets out to write without a purpose, or for beauty alone, is either self-deceived about what he is doing or deceiving his readers about it.

I cannot accept (6), and I barely accept (5). Yet I wrote each premise because it seemed true at the time, and rereading them, each still seems true in isolation. I have tried the usual escapes — insisting that my selections answer to the characters' reality rather than to any thesis, pointing out that I am often surprised by what my own drafts imply, noting that I cut passages for rhythm alone. Each escape seems only to relocate the selection, not to abolish it.

So: where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Name the step, explain why it looked innocent when I wrote it, and tell me what it costs to reject each of the premises in turn. If forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) — they describe my working life accurately — than accept the conclusion, but I want to know what I am paying for that preference, and whether (5) might survive even if (6) falls. One case I would like your diagnosis to cover explicitly: a page of mine that does nothing but describe weather moving across a harbor, included because it was beautiful and for no other reason I can detect. Does such a page escape the argument, or is it the argument's most damning exhibit?

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis you give, and single out the one point in your answer where you are least sure of yourself — whether that is the reading of Du Bois's remark, the identification of the faulty step, or something else.
