I'm a junior and our school's ethics bowl team drew the topic of complicity, so I tried to write out the cleanest argument I could for why ordinary consumers aren't complicit in, say, sweatshop labor. The problem is that my argument seems to prove that almost nobody is ever complicit in anything, which can't be right. Here it is:

(1) You are complicit in a wrong only if the wrong would have been different, or at least less likely, had you not participated.
(2) For any large-scale wrong -- sweatshop labor, factory farming, an unjust war -- no single purchase, vote, or day's work changes anything: if you had stayed home, abstained, or refused, the same things would have happened on the same scale, because the outcome is fixed by what millions of others do.
(3) So no individual consumer, voter, or replaceable employee is complicit in any of these large-scale wrongs.
(4) But nothing in (1) and (2) depends on the scale being large. It depends only on your participation being redundant. So the same reasoning applies wherever your part was unnecessary: a firing squad of ten where any three bullets would have been lethal, a mob of a hundred beating one person, five friends who each shove the same boulder off a cliff when two would have done it. In each case, no single participant made a difference to the outcome.
(5) So no one is complicit in any jointly produced wrong where their part was redundant.
(6) But surely each volunteer on that firing squad is complicit in the execution -- indeed, fully complicit, not one-tenth complicit.
(7) So we have to deny either that complicity requires difference-making (premise 1) or that the firing squad members are complicit (premise 6). Neither seems deniable, so complicity, at least in redundant-participation cases, appears to be impossible.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd -- it would mean that whenever enough people join in a wrong, each of them individually walks free of it, and that the way to do wrong without complicity is to make sure lots of others are doing it too. But I can't locate a false step. Tell me which premise to give up and why that premise looked fine in the first place. If I have to give up (1), I need something to put in its place: please sketch what a positive account of complicity without difference-making would have to say -- about the firing squad, about the lone consumer whose purchase genuinely changes nothing, and about whether those two cases can really be treated the same way. I'd most like to keep (6), and I'd be happiest if the answer also explains why complicity doesn't divide up evenly among participants the way causal responsibility might.
