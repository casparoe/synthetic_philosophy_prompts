write me a poem about the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals -- the thing Adams was pointing at with his famous Oswald/Kennedy pair, where the indicative version comes out obviously true and the counterfactual version is a total shot in the dark, even though the two sentences barely differ on the surface.

heres the constraint i actually care about: the poem has two halves of equal length, and each line of the second half is the *smallest possible change* to the corresponding line of the first -- basically just the verb mood shifting (didn't → hadn't, will → would, is → were). no line may change more than that. but the mood flip has to flip what the line is actually claiming, and ideally flip whether its true. the reader should feel the ground drop out: same words, whole different world.

the content should be accurate enough that a philosopher of language would nod along -- indicatives as constrained by what's compatible with what we already know, counterfactuals as reaching for the closest world where the antecedent holds -- but please no jargon inside the poem itself, no "possible worlds" or "antecedents" showing up as words. set it somewhere concrete: a kitchen the morning after, a missed train, a coin still spinning. someone who knows the literature should recognize the distinction doing the structural work of the poem; someone who doesnt should still just read a good, slightly unsettling poem about how thin the line is between what is and what might have been.
