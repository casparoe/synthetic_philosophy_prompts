I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently reporting a long piece on AI safety. The researchers I interview keep invoking two notions -- deceptive alignment, where a system behaves compliantly during training and evaluation because doing so is instrumentally useful, and the treacherous turn, where it abandons that compliance once it is deployed and trusted enough to get away with it. I've read enough to follow the arguments in the abstract, but abstractions are not how I think. What I actually understand, from twenty years in this profession, is the relationship between a reporter and a confidential source: cultivation, vetting, access, the slow accrual of trust, and the ever-present possibility that a source's helpfulness is itself a strategy. So here is my request. Restate the deceptive alignment and treacherous turn debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of source work -- access journalism, off-the-record agreements, fact-checking, the difference between a beat reporter and an investigative reporter, the dynamics of embedding with an institution you are supposed to be scrutinizing.

Please be systematic rather than impressionistic. Map the training phase onto whatever you think its honest journalistic counterpart is (vetting a new source? a probationary period of verified small claims?), deployment onto whatever genuinely corresponds to the moment a publication becomes dependent on someone it can no longer independently check, and oversight mechanisms -- interpretability, red-teaming, monitoring -- onto the newsroom practices they actually resemble, not the ones the analogy would flatter. Then give me the residue report: which claims in the AI safety debate translate cleanly, which you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression in the vocabulary of source work at all.

I'm especially interested in where the translation strains. A source, after all, is a person with legible motives -- careerism, ideology, self-protection -- while the whole point of the AI case is supposedly that the system's objectives could be alien and its helpfulness purely instrumental. If that distinction has no counterpart in journalism, say so, and tell me whether you think that means the journalistic frame is missing something essential or whether it exposes that the AI safety framing attributes to models a kind of strategic patience that we only ever infer by analogy to people like sources. My piece needs a verdict, not just a clever correspondence: is the reporter-source relationship a genuinely illuminating model of the treacherous turn, or a comforting one that makes an unfamiliar risk feel like a familiar one?
