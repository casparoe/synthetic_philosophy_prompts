Two colleagues of mine, Dana and Marcus, have been arguing since a department dinner, and they have asked me to referee. Dana admits she has cyclic preferences over three apartments she is choosing among: all things considered, she prefers apartment 1 to apartment 2, apartment 2 to apartment 3, and apartment 3 to apartment 1, in each case because a different pair of features (light, space, location) dominates the comparison. Marcus made the standard money pump argument: (1) If she takes apartment 3, someone could offer her apartment 2 for a small fee, then apartment 1 for another small fee, then apartment 3 again for another -- and after paying three fees she would be back where she started, strictly worse off with nothing to show for it. Cyclic preferences, he concluded, are irrational, full stop. (2) Dana replied that the pump only works if she keeps accepting trades, and a rational person who foresees the cycle simply stops: she can adopt a policy of making at most one exchange. Having a preference, she said, does not compel her to act on every pairwise comparison indefinitely, so the argument shows at most that she needs a rule, not that her preferences are defective. (3) Marcus answered that this rescue concedes his point: she can avoid ruin only by refusing trades she herself judges favorable, overriding her own preferences at the moment of choice. A rational agent should not have to manage herself like an unruly subordinate, and her one-trade policy is arbitrary by her own lights, since at the time of the second trade her preferences plainly recommend it. (4) Dana countered that rationality governs extended courses of action, not isolated choices, and that even someone with perfectly transitive preferences can be led into bad outcomes by choosing myopically. So the pump does not target cyclicity specifically; it merely assumes, without argument, that rational preference must guide each choice in isolation, which she denies. (5) Marcus's last move was to ask in what sense she really prefers each apartment to the next if she is committed in advance to thwarting those very preferences whenever they would be acted on repeatedly -- preferences are dispositions to choose, and hers are dispositions she has resolved never to let operate. I genuinely cannot settle it. Please adjudicate move by move: say who is right about each specific point, identify anything both sides are missing, and indicate who is more right overall.
