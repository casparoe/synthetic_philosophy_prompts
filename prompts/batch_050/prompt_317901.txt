In late 2016 and early 2017 I took part in the candlelight vigils in Seoul, where millions gathered week after week to demand President Park Geun-hye's removal over the Choi Soon-sil scandal. What struck me then, and still puzzles me, is how the crowd's anger behaved: it was intense and sustained, yet almost eerily self-disciplined — orderly, largely peaceful, often festive, with participants cleaning up after themselves. I recently encountered the Korean Four-Seven debate and want to use it to understand what I experienced. On Yi Hwang's (Toegye's) side, the four Mencian sprouts (compassion, shame-and-dislike, deference, and approval/disapproval) arise from principle, while the seven ordinary feelings arise from material force, so moral and everyday emotions have different origins. On Ki Dae-seung's (Gobong's) side, the four are simply the seven at their most refined: all feeling issues from material force and principle rides along, so there is one continuous spectrum. Analyze the candlelight protests through this dispute. On each side, what exactly was the protesters' outrage at corruption — the sprout of shame-and-dislike in Mencius's sense, or ordinary anger successfully harmonized? Does the crowd's capacity to sustain moral outrage without tipping into violence count as evidence for Gobong's single-spectrum view, or can Toegye accommodate it equally well? Finally, does the episode expose something the debate never anticipated — for instance, that the original disputants theorized emotion in the individual mind, whereas the vigils involved emotion that was deliberately choreographed and amplified across millions of people?
