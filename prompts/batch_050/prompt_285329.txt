I have no formal training in philosophy, but I keep circling a question about when good behavior deserves moral credit, ever since an argument with my brother about a friend of his who posts every one of his charitable donations on social media. I've been testing my intuitions against cases from my own life, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle underneath them. Here are my verdicts. My aunt visits her elderly neighbor twice a week, entirely because she enjoys the woman's stories and company, and she would stop if she stopped having fun; I say she deserves full credit for those visits anyway. A coworker gives a tenth of his income to carefully researched charities and admits he feels no warmth toward the recipients, doing it solely because he reasoned his way to the conclusion that he ought to; I say he deserves credit too, perhaps the most of anyone here. A man at the lake near my house dove in and pulled a drowning child out before, by his own account, he had time to think anything at all; I say the rescue reflects well on him even though no reasoning was involved. A business owner I know treats his employees scrupulously fairly, but only because he calculated that lawsuits and turnover cost more than decent treatment; I say he gets almost no moral credit, though I'm glad for his workers. A friend volunteered at a shelter every weekend for two years, mostly because it would look good on her graduate school applications, but she kept going for months after she was accepted; I say the pre-acceptance volunteering earns her little, and the post-acceptance volunteering earns her a lot. And a stranger who returned my lost wallet, after checking whether the security camera across the street had seen him pick it up, strikes me as deserving roughly nothing.

The pair I can't reconcile is my aunt and the coworker: if acting from a felt duty is what confers moral worth, my aunt's visits shrink to nothing, which I refuse to accept, yet if enjoying an act or benefiting from it doesn't reduce its worth, I can't explain why the business owner's prudential fairness counts for so little. There's a second fault line between the rescuer and the coworker: if worth requires acting on a principle or a conclusion, the man who never deliberated at all gets no credit, and I find that conclusion absurd. So: is there a principle, or a short list of principles, that sorts all six of my cases the way I've sorted them? Please state it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which case motivates each clause. Then test it against cases I haven't settled: a woman who keeps every promise out of pride in being the sort of person who keeps promises; a man who tells the truth because he believes liars are punished after death; a nurse who is excellent with terminal patients partly because the work makes her feel her life matters. If no reasonably simple principle delivers all my verdicts, tell me honestly which one I should give up and why that one, rather than papering over the conflict with a principle so elaborate it could accommodate anything.
