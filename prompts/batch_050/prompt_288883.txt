Take Ireland's Citizens' Assembly on abortion (2016–2018) and analyze it as a case study for John Dewey's experimentalist theory of democracy. Here are the salient facts as I understand them, in case details differ in your memory — work from these: following decades of constitutional deadlock after the 1983 Eighth Amendment gave the unborn an equal right to life, the Irish government convened an assembly of 99 randomly selected citizens plus a chairperson. The assembly met over a series of weekends across roughly eighteen months, heard testimony from medical, legal, and ethical experts as well as from advocacy groups and women with direct experience, deliberated in small facilitated groups and plenary sessions, and ultimately recommended by large majorities that the constitutional provision be replaced and that abortion be legislated for on grounds far more permissive than most observers had predicted a randomly chosen Irish public would endorse. A referendum in May 2018 then repealed the Eighth Amendment by roughly two to one, closely tracking the assembly's recommendations.

Now do the Deweyan work, in both directions. First, map the episode onto the framework of The Public and Its Problems (1927) and Dewey's broader account of social inquiry: what, on Dewey's view, was the 'public' here — the assembly itself, the electorate, or something else — and does the assembly count as an instance of the organized, articulate public whose eclipse Dewey lamented, or as a workaround for its absence? What counts as the 'experiment' in the Deweyan sense: the assembly, the referendum, the legislation that followed, or the whole sequence? How well does the assembly's design — expert testimony filtered through lay deliberation, with citizens treated as capable inquirers rather than as the uninformed masses of Lippmann's Public Opinion and The Phantom Public — instantiate Dewey's claim that democratic social intelligence is a matter of method rather than of prior expertise? Where did communication, which Dewey treated as the heart of the democratic method, actually happen in this episode, and did it happen where his theory says it must?

Second, be honest about where the mapping strains. Does single-shot random selection fit a theory built on ongoing association and organized publics, or is a minipublic better understood as a device Dewey never anticipated? The assembly deliberated on one issue handed to it by the government and courts, under pressure from European human-rights rulings — does that agenda-setting from above sit badly with Dewey's insistence that publics must form themselves around experienced consequences? And the referendum's result matched the assembly's recommendations: is that a vindication of deliberative inquiry transmitting its conclusions to the wider public, or evidence that the referendum merely ratified what elites and courts had already set in motion?

Finally, let the case bite back on the theory. Does the episode strengthen Dewey's experimentalism against its Lippmann-style critics, expose a gap in it — say, an underdeveloped account of institutional design or of how inquiry gets commissioned — or reveal that experimentalism was never really about constitutionally entrenched moral questions at all? Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
