So my partner and I are drafting a kind of personal giving charter — basically writing down what we actually think about charity instead of freelancing it every december — and before we commit I want to check my philosophical homework. Here's where I've landed:

1. I'm basically a consequentialist. What matters morally is outcomes — suffering reduced, lives improved, flourishing promoted. Intentions, rules, virtues, all of that only matters insofar as it tends to produce good outcomes.
2. I find Singer's drowning child argument pretty much unanswerable. That the child is far away, or that lots of other people could wade in and aren't, doesnt change what I'm required to do.
3. I think the demandingness objection to consequentialism is a dud. "Morality can't ask that much of me" isn't an argument, it's just discomfort dressed up as one.
4. We give 10% of our income and I want the charter to say that this is *enough* — not just "what we can manage psychologically," but enough in the moral sense: anything past it would be supererogatory, genuinely above and beyond the call of duty.
5. I don't think people who give nothing are bad people, and I think blaming or guilt-tripping non-givers is almost always the wrong thing to do.
6. I think there's a real moral difference between killing and letting die — it's not mere bookkeeping. Someone who lets a patient die to harvest their organs does something worse than someone who just declines to donate money.

Before this goes in the charter: does the set actually hang together? If some of these pull against each other, tell me which pairs, rank the tensions by how serious they are, and for each one say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. I also suspect at least one apparent conflict here dissolves if you think about it properly — flag that kind too, rather than treating everything as equally damning. Don't tell me which commitment I should drop overall; just be specific about what each one actually commits me to.
