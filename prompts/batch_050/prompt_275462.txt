I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of language seminar on LLM output, and my professor keeps leaning on an analogy that half the class now repeats: ChatGPT's hallucinations are like the confident student bluffing his way through an oral exam on a book he hasn't read, and this is more or less exactly what Frankfurt meant by bullshit. The model isn't lying, on this picture, because lying requires keeping track of the truth so as to steer away from it, whereas the model simply doesn't care whether what it says is true so long as it sounds plausible. And my professor uses the analogy to argue for a fairly strong conclusion: that calling these failures errors or bugs is a category mistake, and that companies which describe them that way are whitewashing something morally worse, since we've all agreed since Frankfurt that bullshit is the greater enemy of truth than lying. Meanwhile my roommate, who's an engineer, has the opposite analogy: a hallucinating model is like a calculator with a rounding bug, pure mechanical error, and moralizing it with vocabulary stolen from Frankfurt is just anthropomorphizing.

Can you audit the bluffing-student analogy for me, and the calculator one along the way? Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the student's indifference to truth, the professor he's performing for, the unread book, the exam setting, the impression-management motive Frankfurt takes to drive bullshit. Then the places the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for my professor's use of it. For instance: does Frankfurtian bullshit require an attitude toward truth that a language model could even have, or is his account about the character of the utterance rather than the psychology of the utterer? Does it matter that the bluffing student knows he's bluffing, and if so, in which direction? Does it matter that the model also tells the truth constantly, which is not true of the student? And on the other side, what does the calculator analogy smuggle in -- does the calculator have anything like the systematic bias toward plausibility that made Frankfurt's category seem apt?

Then the payoff: does my professor's conclusion survive the disanalogies? Is error really a category mistake here, or was the analogy carrying that verdict the whole time? And if neither bluffing student nor broken calculator quite fits, what's the better analogy -- confabulating patient, intern making up sources, something else -- and what follows from the better one about how we should describe and respond to these failures? I don't want a bibliography, I want to know which analogy deserves to do the argumentative work in my paper.
