For a novel involving an artificially designed ecosystem, I have been reading Aristotle’s *On the Parts of Animals*. He writes:

"For if a house or other such final object is to be realized, it is necessary that such and such material shall exist; and it is necessary that first this then that shall be produced, and first this and then that set in motion, and so on in continuous succession, until the end and final result is reached, for the sake of which each prior thing is produced and exists. As with these productions of art, so also is it with the productions of nature."

My editor has turned the passage into an argument that seems to prove too much:

(1) To identify a biological part’s function is to identify the end for the sake of which that part exists.  
(2) In an artifact, the relevant end exists beforehand as an intention and guides production.  
(3) In nature, where no such intention exists, an effect can explain why a heritable trait now exists only if ancestors possessing the trait were selected because they produced that effect.  
(4) Therefore, every genuine biological function is historical: a trait’s function is the effect for which past tokens of that trait were selected.  
(5) Yet a newly arisen trait can perform an indispensable causal role—for example, allowing an organism to neutralize a poison never before encountered—even though no ancestor was selected for performing that role.  
(6) Such a trait still has a function, because it now operates for the sake of the organism’s survival.  
(7) Hence biological functions both must be historical and can be purely present-causal, so Aristotle’s formula cannot be applied consistently to organisms.

I suspect the contradiction is manufactured somewhere between (1), (3), and (6), but I cannot locate the exact misstep. Which premise or inference should be rejected, why did it initially seem plausible, and what would an etiological account and a causal-role account of function each have to concede in diagnosing the argument differently?
