So I'm midway through a novel (thriller-ish, but booker-prize-adjacent in my head, don't laugh) and I've built the plot around this device: a dying tech billionaire puts up £10 million for anyone who can sincerely intend, at midnight tonight, to drink a vial of his toxin at noon tomorrow. The toxin won't kill you or damage anything permanently, but it will make you properly, wretchedly ill for about 48 hours. His people have a brain scanner that detects genuine intention — not behaviour, not saying the words, the actual state of intending. If you've got the intention at midnight, money's transferred instantly and it's yours no matter what you do at noon.

My problem: I've written down the philosophical commitments I baked into the plot, and my partner (who did PPE twenty years ago and is insufferable about it) says they don't hang together. She won't say which ones. Can you be my consistency auditor? You're a philosopher of action who does script consultancy on the side — please actually think hard about this rather than pattern-matching to a seminar answer, maybe brainstorm the moving parts first and then give me the audit.

Here's what I'm committed to:

1. You can't genuinely intend to do something you're fully convinced you won't do. Intending isn't wishing or vaguely resolving — it has to be hooked up to what you actually expect of yourself.
2. A perfectly rational person can sincerely form the midnight intention, because doing so is what wins the ten million.
3. Come noon, with the money already banked, drinking the toxin would be irrational — a pointless two days of misery for nothing. And my protagonist, being rational, doesn't drink.
4. My protagonist knows herself completely. No self-deception, no convenient blind spots; she can predict her own future behaviour as reliably as she predicts other people's.
5. The scanner only pays out for the real thing, so the plot genuinely needs her midnight intention to be authentic, not performed or half-hearted.
6. Rationality is a matter of what it's best to do at each moment, all things considered at that moment.

Which of these actually conflict, and which just look like they do? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each one tell me exactly what would have to give. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that makes the plot coherent — cheapest in terms of how much of my existing draft I'd have to rewrite. I'd rather fix a premise than my protagonist, but don't let that bias the analysis, tell me straight.
