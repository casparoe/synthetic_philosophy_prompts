I've settled on a deflationary view of money-pump arguments, and I'd like you to stress-test it. The view, as I hold it, runs as follows: a money-pump argument never establishes that a pattern of preferences is irrational. What it establishes, at most, is that a certain *package* is exploitable -- namely, the combination of (i) the preferences, (ii) a particular disposition to act on one's current preferences trade-by-trade, without binding oneself in advance or reconsidering one's overall plan, and (iii) a willingness to keep engaging with a trader who offers the pumping sequence. Strip away any element of the package and the pump fails. An agent with cyclic preferences who is resolute (forms a plan at the outset and holds to it even when her current preferences would prompt deviation), or who simply declines repeated trade offers, or who revises her preferences after noticing the first cycle, never loses a cent. So whatever irrationality the pump reveals lies in the agent's *policy for acting over time*, not in the structure of her preferences. The general moral, on my view: diachronic rationality arguments -- money pumps, and by analogy Dutch-book arguments -- cannot justify synchronic structural constraints like transitivity or probabilism unless supplemented by a contested bridge premise about what rationality demands of agents across time, and once that premise is made explicit it does all the real work, leaving the pump itself as theater.

Here is what I want from you. Tell me what would count against this view -- a map of its exposure, not a defense of the orthodoxy. In particular: Are there cases where *no* diachronic policy rescues the agent, so that the defect has to be located in the preference structure itself rather than in how she implements it? Consider, for instance, one-shot choice situations that seem to reveal incoherence without any sequence of trades, or cases where resoluteness itself looks straightforwardly irrational -- sticking to a plan that is, from one's current standpoint, strictly worse than deviating. Would the latter force me to admit that the deflationary view merely relocates the irrationality rather than dissolving it, and is relocation itself a substantive result or a verbal maneuver? Second: where does the view risk collapsing into its rival? If I concede that a rational agent must adopt *some* anti-pumping policy, have I conceded that cyclic preferences carry a mandatory cost that transitive ones don't -- and if so, is that cost just irrationality under another name? Third: say which of these defeaters a committed deflationist would themselves concede as genuine trouble, and which they would dismiss as question-begging -- for example, does the objection that "the pumped agent ends up with strictly less money and the same objects" already presuppose the synchronic norm at issue? Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the view, suitably qualified, has been insulated to the point of triviality: if any apparent preference-level defect can always be redescribed as a policy-level defect, does the deflationary position actually risk anything, and is that a virtue of the framework or a sign that it has stopped making contact with the debate?

Rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one state what the world -- or the theory of rational choice -- would have to look like for it to land. If in the end you think the deflationary view survives everything, say so, but say what it survives *as*: a claim about preference, a claim about agency, or a claim about what arguments of this form can ever show.
