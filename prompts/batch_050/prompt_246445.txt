Consider a real episode often invoked in discussions of Molyneux's problem. Beginning in 2003, Pawan Sinha's Project Prakash program in India identified a small number of children and adolescents (aged roughly eight to seventeen) who had been blind from birth and whose sight was surgically restored, with the relevant operations performed between 2007 and 2010. Tested within days of first seeing, the subjects could visually discriminate simple shapes but could not reliably say which of two visible objects corresponded to one they had just touched; in follow-up tests about a week later, most could. Analyze this episode as a test of Molyneux's question. Begin by stating Molyneux's question precisely enough to say what an experimental result would have to look like in order to answer it, and then say whether the Prakash pattern — initial cross-modal failure, success within days — amounts to a No, a Yes, or evidence that the original question conflates several distinct questions; if the last, identify them (for instance: whether spatial concepts are modality-specific, whether cross-modal mapping is innate or learned, and whether learning can be rapid enough to be empirically indistinguishable from immediacy). Then run the analysis in the other direction. Molyneux's subject was an adult with fully trained touch and healthy eyes, whereas these were children whose visual systems had been deprived during development and were still maturing. Say what these mismatches do to the evidential weight of the episode, and conclude with a verdict: does the episode vindicate the negative answer given by Locke and Berkeley, or does it instead show that their answer was never about anything a clinic could measure?
