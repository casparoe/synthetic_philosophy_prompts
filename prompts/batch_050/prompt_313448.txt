I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on social epistemology and democratic theory, and I also run the attached undergraduate reading group: about 14 people, 75 minutes, mixed mathematical backgrounds. Next week we're on the Condorcet jury theorem. Everyone has read a statement of it, but when I tried to explain it last term to a different group, they treated it as a trivia fact about coin flips and nothing landed. I want them to run into the phenomenon themselves before anyone names it.

Design an in-class activity that makes participants feel the force of the theorem, and specifically these three things: (1) how majority rule amplifies individual competence that is only slightly better than chance, provided votes are independent; (2) how quickly that amplification collapses once voters' evidence is correlated -- for instance, when everyone has been exposed to the same misleading clue; and (3) the fact that the theorem cuts both ways, so that when individual competence dips below chance the majority does worse than the average voter. Ideally the group should also stumble onto a tension I care about for the seminar: deliberating before voting seems to make each voter more competent while simultaneously destroying the independence the theorem requires. I want that tradeoff to arise from the rules, not from my lecturing.

Constraints: it must work with roughly 14 people in 75 minutes; use only cheap materials (envelopes, beads, coins, slips of paper); include a points or payoff scheme with real but non-monetary stakes; run as multiple short rounds rather than one long one, since with 14 voters a single majority verdict is mostly noise; and use a ground truth the facilitator controls, such as the composition of a hidden urn, rather than a matter of opinion.

Deliverables: complete rules written so I could hand them to a co-facilitator; a timeline; the payoff scheme; a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the theorem's machinery (the competence parameter, independence, majority rule, asymptotic convergence) and onto the epistemic-democracy debate about deliberation; a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- students openly pooling answers, the group anchoring on the first confident speaker, small-group variance making the correct majority lose a round and embarrassing me -- and how to handle each; and an honest note on which features of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce in one room, with genuine independence and asymptotic behavior as my two suspects, and what I therefore have to supply in discussion instead.

One structural requirement: the debrief must not state the theorem in advance. It should reconstruct the theorem out of our own vote tallies, round by round, as the evidence.

Format: begin with the activity design itself, rules first, with no preface, no restatement of the theorem, and no commentary on the pedagogy before the design.
