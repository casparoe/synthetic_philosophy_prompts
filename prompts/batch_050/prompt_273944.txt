I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind with a secondary field in philosophy of economics, and I'm drafting a dissertation chapter that uses the "Flash Crash" of May 6, 2010 as a test case for the concept of sphexishness — the property Dennett made famous via the digger wasp and Hofstadter named: behavior that looks intelligent and purposive until you notice it is a fixed routine incapable of registering its own pointlessness. The standard narrative of that afternoon, as I understand it: a very large sell order in E-mini futures was executed by an algorithm that paced itself to trading volume without regard to price; the high-frequency firms' algorithms responded according to their own routines — some churning the contracts among themselves, others exiting the market entirely once internal anomaly checks tripped — liquidity evaporated, shares of perfectly healthy companies briefly printed at absurd prices, and the whole thing largely reversed within about twenty minutes once automated pauses triggered and humans stepped in. Regulators later placed much of the blame on the interaction of these automated strategies rather than on any single actor. One of my committee members insists this is a story about market regulation and has nothing to teach philosophy of mind; the chapter is my attempt to prove him wrong, or to find out he's right.

Analyze the episode through the lens of sphexishness, and run the analysis in both directions. First, the mapping: which actor, if any, plays the role of the wasp? Be careful here, because the obvious candidates each resist the role in instructive ways. The execution algorithm that kept selling into the collapse looks maximally sphexish, yet it was doing exactly what its designers asked of it; the firms that withdrew liquidity look rigid in one sense (rule-governed exit) but almost anti-sphexish in another, since their routines included meta-rules for detecting that conditions had departed from the assumptions the routines were built for — the wasp has no meta-rule; and the market as a whole, a system of individually "smart" components generating collectively mindless behavior, may be the best candidate of all, which would make sphexishness an emergent property rather than a property of components. Second, where the lens breaks: the wasp never redesigns her own routine, but within months every firm involved had revised its algorithms and the exchanges had installed circuit breakers. Does the capacity for post-hoc redesign dissolve sphexishness, or merely relocate it one level up, into a slower institutional routine? Third, the free-will payoff: sphexishness was introduced as the intuitive antithesis of free agency, so say what the episode suggests about whether anti-sphexishness comes in degrees, whether stacking anomaly-detection rules on top of routines constitutes genuine flexibility or just more elaborate rigidity, and whether the humans who "intervened" were doing anything other than executing their own trained procedures. End with a verdict on my chapter's thesis: is sphexishness, sharpened by this case, a useful concept for describing automated institutions, or does it turn out to fit insects cleanly and everything else only by metaphor?
