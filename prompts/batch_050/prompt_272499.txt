I’m trying to build a tier list of interpretations of Anscombe’s objection to Truman and the atomic bombings, but I want fidelity to her actual argument and philosophical strength kept separate. Please rank these readings from best to worst *as accounts of Anscombe*, while also rating how forceful each is independently:

1. Absolute prohibition: deliberately killing non-combatants as a means is murder, so the bombings can’t be justified by the lives supposedly saved.
2. Necessity: her main complaint is that alternatives short of the bombings were available, especially if the demand for unconditional surrender had been reconsidered; had the bombing genuinely been necessary, she would have accepted it.
3. Mere foresight: because Truman may not have desired civilian deaths for their own sake, Anscombe has no basis for calling the deaths intentional rather than merely foreseen.
4. Symbolic complicity: her protest against Oxford honoring Truman concerned what an institution communicates when it celebrates someone for a gravely wrongful act, not the absurd idea that the degree caused the bombings.
5. Tradeoff exclusion: her point is that some means fall outside permissible cost-benefit reasoning altogether, even when the expected benefits are enormous.
6. Religious authoritarianism: the argument only works if one already accepts Catholic moral authority, so it has no relevance to secular ethics.

Use two diagnostics, even if they’re imperfect. First, my pressure-valve test: if civilians had been evacuated but the same destructive spectacle and pressure on Japan remained, would the attack still have served its intended coercive purpose? If yes, that weakens the claim that civilian death itself was being used as the means. Second, my commander’s-lens test: assess intention and responsibility using what Truman and his advisers could reasonably have believed then, not facts available only in hindsight. Also don’t let Anscombe’s technical use of innocent get flattened into the ordinary claim that every victim was personally blameless; the important distinction is who may legitimately be targeted in war.
