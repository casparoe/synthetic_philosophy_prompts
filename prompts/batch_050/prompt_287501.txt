When I taught engineering, I told students that diagnosing a bridge collapse meant identifying the loads, tracing the failure through the members, and finding the design flaw -- and that no appeal to the story of this particular bridge could substitute for principles that apply to bridges generally. Reading philosophy of history in retirement, I keep encountering the same picture used as an analogy: explaining an event like the French Revolution, some say, is just like explaining a structural failure, and talk of narrative is merely the literary packaging of an underlying causal analysis. Others treat the narrative as the explanation itself, not packaging. Please audit the bridge-collapse analogy for the work it is being made to do here. Lay out explicitly what is supposed to map onto what (loads, members, design flaws, failure), and where the mapping breaks -- for instance, whether historical events have identifiable components independently of the descriptions a narrator chooses, whether anything plays the role of general principles covering all cases, and whether the absence of re-runnable cases matters. Say which disanalogies matter for the conclusion that narrative is dispensable and which are merely decorative, and tell me whether that conclusion survives the audit. Finally, please indicate what consideration or argument would most plausibly change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most exposed to failure.
