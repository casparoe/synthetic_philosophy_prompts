I serve on the planning committee for a new museum of twentieth-century mass violence, and we have reached an impasse I would like your help thinking through. The building has a fixed amount of floor space, and every allocation decision we face — which atrocities receive how many square feet, in what sequence visitors encounter them — implicitly ranks horrors by something like moral gravity. One faction on the committee argues that since the ranking is unavoidable, we should make it explicit and defend it. A second faction insists that any such ranking is conceptually confused: the Holocaust, the Holodomor, the Cambodian genocide, and the Rwandan genocide are, on their view, incomparable in badness, so there is nothing to be explicit about. A third faction grants that comparison might be possible in principle but maintains that actually producing the ranking would be obscene regardless.

Please give your credence in the following proposition, stated as precisely as I can manage: there exists, at least in principle, a defensible all-things-considered ordering of the badness of these four atrocities — one that fully informed and reasonable deliberators would ratify, rather than one merely imposed by a committee under practical pressure. Do not give me the number alone. Decompose it: roughly, the probability that the badness of an atrocity supervenes on features (lives lost, suffering inflicted, deliberateness of intent, destruction of a people or a culture) that are at least roughly commensurable with one another; the probability that incommensurability among the relevant values is genuine and severe enough to defeat even a partial ordering; the probability that reasonable, fully informed deliberators would converge on an ordering given that one exists; and whatever further terms you judge the question actually turns on. Assign each term a number with one line of justification, explain how the terms combine, and report the resulting figure.

Then address three follow-ups. First, identify the term to which your final credence is most sensitive, and say what evidence or argument would move it most. Second, my own credence is around 0.75, while a colleague's is around 0.1: tell me which term our disagreement most plausibly lives in, and why. Third, the committee's third faction keeps sliding between "ranking is impossible" and "ranking is obscene," and I suspect these claims are doing very different work. If the obscenity objection is sound, should it lower, raise, or leave untouched the credence you assigned above? And given that the museum must allocate its floor space one way or another — even a decision to give every atrocity identical space expresses a judgment — what follows for how we should proceed, and for what we should tell ourselves we are doing when we proceed that way?
