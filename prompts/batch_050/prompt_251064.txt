I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set among English gentlemen in the decades when dueling was dying out — roughly the first half of the nineteenth century, when the practice was already widely condemned yet still socially compulsory for men of a certain standing. My central character is a military officer who fights a duel he privately believes to be murder, and I want the novel's deeper architecture to reflect whatever actually kills a norm like dueling, rather than the flattering version in which people simply hear better arguments and repent.

I have been reading around two broad accounts of how moral revolutions happen. On one account, associated with Kwame Anthony Appiah's work on honor, practices like dueling and footbinding end not because new moral arguments appear — the arguments against them are usually old and well known — but because the honor code sustaining them is destabilized: participation stops conferring esteem, or national and collective honor gets recruited onto the side of reform, so that a man can cease dueling without ceasing to be honorable. On the other account, associated with Eric Williams's thesis about abolition, moral change is downstream of material interests: norms collapse when they stop serving the economic position of the classes that uphold them, and the moral rhetoric is mostly decoration on a shift already accomplished.

Please describe the most important implications of these two accounts for how I should construct my novel — in particular, what each account implies about (1) what my officer's inner deliberation should actually look like at the moment he decides whether to fight, (2) which secondary characters and social scenes will carry the real causal weight of the story, and (3) what the ending can and cannot honestly show.

I do not want a balanced survey that leaves the choice to me. Please commit: tell me which account I should build the novel on, what specific dramatic choices that commitment dictates, and where the losing account should still be allowed to appear — as a character's sincere self-misunderstanding, if at all. If you think the two accounts can be genuinely unified rather than one subordinated to the other, argue for that as your committed position instead, but defend one definite construction of the book either way.
