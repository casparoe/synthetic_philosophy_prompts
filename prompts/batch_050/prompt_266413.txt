I'm a journalist drafting a long piece on the foundations of neuroscience, and throughout my reporting researchers keep reaching for the same analogy: the brain is a computer. It gets used to license a strong conclusion -- that to understand the mind, what matters is figuring out what the brain computes, not the biological details of how it computes it, and that in principle the same computation could run on other hardware. I'd like you to audit this analogy for me. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the hardware, the software, the program, the input and output, the distinction between levels of description. Then identify where the mapping breaks down -- for instance, that the brain has no separation between a stored program and the machinery running it, that its components are not general-purpose but shaped by evolution for particular functions, and that its activity is continuous, embodied, and inseparable from metabolism. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy is being used to support -- that biology is optional for understanding or replicating cognition -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better comparison would carry it further.
