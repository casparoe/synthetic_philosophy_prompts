I have recently left a job in corporate finance to take over my late uncle's small community building-and-loan in a town that has seen better decades, and the move has left me brooding over what money-making is actually for. Against that backdrop, I would like a reading of It's a Wonderful Life through Aristotle's account of money and exchange: the distinction in the Politics between household management and the boundless art of acquisition, the idea that wealth has a natural limit set by living well, the treatment in the Nicomachean Ethics of money as a convention that renders unlike goods commensurable, and the notorious claim that lending at interest is the most unnatural mode of getting wealth there is.

On the surface the film seems Aristotelian. Bedford Falls and Pottersville dramatize two relations to money, and the bank-run scene -- George explaining that the depositors' money is not in the safe but in one another's homes and businesses -- looks like a parable of money as convention and mutual trust rather than as a hoardable stuff. But press the lens and things get harder. Aristotle condemns usury without qualification, and George Bailey charges interest. Is the film's moral economy one Aristotle could actually endorse, or does it merely relocate acquisition from the miser's vault to the community without changing its form? Does the Building and Loan count as household management writ large, subordinated to the good life of a town, or is Potter simply the more consistent chrematist and George a sentimental one? And what would Aristotle make of the ending's implied verdict that George turns out to be the richest man in town -- rich in friends rather than in holdings -- given that Aristotle treats wealth as an instrument and friendship as a component of flourishing rather than a stock of it?

I am not after summaries of either Aristotle or the film. I want criticism that is faithful in both directions: where the film genuinely illuminates the natural-versus-unnatural acquisition distinction, where it resists the Aristotelian frame or quietly refutes it -- the film clearly regards some interest-bearing lending as virtuous, which Aristotle could not concede -- and what, if anything, a two-hour piece of popular cinema adds to this debate that treatise argument does not capture. Where the movie does not fit the theory, say so plainly rather than forcing it.
