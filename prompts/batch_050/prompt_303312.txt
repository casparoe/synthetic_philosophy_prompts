As I understand it (mostly from pop treatments, so correct me if I've mangled it): two people walking past each other in opposite directions have different "now" slices through spacetime, and for something far away like the Andromeda galaxy, events that count as happening right now for one of them are days in the future for the other. Say there's an alien fleet over there deciding whether to invade. For one walker the launch is already in their present, for the other it's still in their future. The argument then says: if everything simultaneous-with-me-now is real, and simultaneity is relative, then the future event must already be real too, so the future is fixed and we live in a block universe where everything just... is. I've seen this called the Rietdijk-Putnam argument and the Andromeda paradox, and every version I find ends with some variation of "so the future is already out there," which I find completely wild.

Here's the thing though. Everything I actually understand about "now" I learned from distributed systems, and the physics keeps sounding like my day job. In my world there is no global clock, full stop. Events on different nodes are only ordered relative to each other when one causally influences the other -- the standard happened-before relation gives you a partial order, and events that can't influence each other are just concurrent, meaning there's no fact of the matter about which came first. A "snapshot" of the system is a cut through the event graph, and different observers computing different consistent cuts isn't a paradox, its a Tuesday. So when physicists say there's no absolute simultaneity and spacelike-seperated events can't be ordered, my honest reaction is: yes, obviously, we've known this since the 70s, why is anyone drawing metaphysical conclusions about the future being fixed from it?
