Take the first ten weeks of the COVID-19 pandemic -- roughly January through March 2020, when governments had to decide on border closures, lockdowns, and mask guidance under deep uncertainty about human-to-human transmission, asymptomatic spread, and fatality rates -- and analyze the episode through the precautionary principle. Not the epidemiology, which I know: the decision theory. Distinguish at least two versions of the principle: a weak, Rio-Declaration-style version holding that lack of full scientific certainty is no reason to postpone cost-effective measures against serious threats, and a strong version that mandates preventive action, or forbids the risky activity, whenever harms could be catastrophic even if unproven. Then address the following.

First, what did each version prescribe, and for whom? Be precise about the mapping: who the decision-maker is, what the options were, what counted as evidence at the time. Second, engage the well-known objection, pressed by Cass Sunstein, that the strong principle is paralyzing because precaution can point in both directions at once: the virus posed an uncertain catastrophic risk, but so did lockdowns -- economic collapse, forgone medical care, long-run harm to children. Did the episode show the paralysis objection succeeding in practice, or did decision-makers resolve it? If they resolved it, does their resolution count as applying the principle or quietly abandoning it?

Third, take early mask guidance as a microcosm: major health bodies initially advised against general public masking, citing absence of evidence of benefit, then reversed on broadly precautionary grounds. Which phase confused absence of evidence with evidence of absence, and what should the principle say about asymmetric evidence when the intervention is cheap?

Finally, run the analysis back at the theory: does COVID vindicate the precautionary principle, expose it as rhetoric invoked selectively, or reveal that it was never a decision rule at all but something else -- a rule about burden of proof, or about who bears the cost of scientific uncertainty?
