I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm taping an episode of my podcast with a philosopher who defends a strong reading of Parfit's hitchhiker case: not only should the rescued hitchhiker hand over the money once safely in town, but rationality can require a person to become the kind of agent disposed to act against their own future interests -- a position she calls rational irrationality, which she extends to Kavka's toxin puzzle and to one-boxing in Newcomb's problem. Draft ten interview questions that press past a restatement of the desert case. Include at least one question each on: how she distinguishes rationally acquiring a disposition from rationally performing the later act it calls for; whether her view collapses into rule-consequentialism about dispositions and, if so, what is left of practical rationality as a standard for individual choices; how she handles the objection that a genuinely rational agent would reason, upon arriving in the city, that the driver's evidence about her character is now fixed and so there is nothing left to influence; and whether her framework vindicates or undermines timeless and functional decision theories. For each question, write the follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, and note the answer you expect. Avoid generic questions about her career or how she came to the topic. Write in American English, and end with a single sentence, on its own last line, summarizing the line of questioning you think is most likely to unsettle her position.
