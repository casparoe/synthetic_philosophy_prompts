A guy in my PhD cohort cornered me with this at the pub and it's been bugging me for weeks. (1) Rational choice just means picking the option with the highest expected value, i.e. summing probability × value across all outcomes. (2) Expected value is blind to how the probability is spread out — a one-in-a-million chance of an outcome a billion times better beats a guaranteed merely-good outcome, every time. (3) Value is unbounded: for any outcome you can describe, there's a better one (one more year of happy life, one more person saved, double the pleasure, whatever). (4) Nothing in physics or epistemology lets you assign literally zero probability to weird causal chains, so for any action there's some tiny nonzero chance it leads to one of those astronomical outcomes. (5) So any option with even a remote shot at an astronomical payoff beats every safe option with a merely good certain outcome. (6) So the rational life is lottery tickets, long-shot cryonics, speculative schemes of every kind, and never once taking a sure thing. (7) That's nuts, so the argument proves too much and something above has to give.

He says (1) is the culprit and expected utility theory is dead. I'd honestly rather keep (1), it feels like the one claim in there I'm actually confident about. My gut says the slip is somewhere between (4) and (5) but I can't tell wether it's a math mistake or a philosophy mistake. Which step should I reject, why did that step look fine in the first place, and what do the standard escapes — capping utility, rounding tiny probabilities to zero, refusing to compare options when expectations are undefined — each cost me?
