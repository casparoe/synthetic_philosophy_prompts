I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on well-being, and I'm writing this year's final exam. Draft one essay question on Nozick's experience machine that cannot be answered by reciting the standard lesson that we value more than pleasurable experience. The question should force students to do three things: (1) state precisely which theories of well-being the thought experiment purports to refute and which it merely pressures -- for instance, whether it touches desire-satisfaction theories at all, or only experience-internalist ones like hedonism; (2) engage seriously with the objection that our reluctance to plug in reflects status quo bias rather than insight, on the grounds that most of us would likewise refuse to plug out if told we were already living in a machine; and (3) take a stand on whether the argument, if it survives that objection, supports a specific positive theory of well-being or only constrains the space of acceptable ones. Provide a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (out of 20), and a one-line note on the mistake candidates typically make on each -- for example, treating the thought experiment as decisive against every subjectivist theory, or dismissing the status quo bias objection without explaining what would distinguish a bias-driven intuition from a theory-tracking one.
