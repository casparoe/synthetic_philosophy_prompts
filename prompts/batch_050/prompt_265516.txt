Exam question:
State Bell's theorem and explain what it establishes. In your answer: (a) specify the assumptions that enter the derivation of a Bell inequality; (b) explain what the experimental violation of Bell inequalities does and does not rule out; (c) discuss the bearing of the result on the EPR argument and on the prospects for hidden-variable theories; and (d) state whether the theorem shows that quantum mechanics permits superluminal signaling or stands in conflict with special relativity.

Student's answer:

Bell's theorem, proved by John Bell in 1964, shows that no theory of local hidden variables can reproduce all the statistical predictions of quantum mechanics. Bell considered pairs of particles prepared in an entangled state and sent to two distant measurement stations, where each experimenter chooses a measurement setting, such as the orientation of a spin measurement. The derivation of a Bell inequality assumes three things: (1) locality — the outcome at one station cannot depend on the setting chosen at the distant station; (2) hidden variables — a more complete specification of the pair's state that underlies the statistics of outcomes; and (3) measurement independence — the experimenters' settings can be chosen freely, independently of that underlying state.

In the CHSH version of the inequality, any local hidden-variable model must satisfy |S| ≤ 2, where S is a particular combination of correlation functions. Quantum mechanics predicts values of S up to 2√2 ≈ 2.83 for suitable choices of settings, and experiments beginning with Aspect's in the early 1980s, and more recently the loophole-free tests of 2015, have confirmed the quantum predictions and violated the inequality.

The consequences are far-reaching. First, because the inequality is violated, the hidden-variables assumption is empirically refuted: nature cannot be deterministic at the fundamental level, and the randomness of quantum measurement outcomes is irreducible. Second, the result settles the dispute between Einstein and Bohr in Bohr's favor. The EPR paper of 1935 had argued that quantum mechanics is incomplete because it exhibits nonlocal influences between spacelike-separated events, and that a complete underlying theory would eliminate these influences. Bell showed that no such completion is possible, so Einstein's program failed and Bohr's complementarity is vindicated. Third, although the correlations are nonlocal, the no-signaling theorem shows that they cannot be used to transmit information faster than light, so there is no conflict whatsoever with special relativity.
