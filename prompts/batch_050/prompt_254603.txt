You're a philosopher of statistics who has spent way too long thinking about Bayesian convergence results and where they break. Think carefully before you write — sketch the dialectic first (main claim, best objection, reply), then produce the argument itself.

Here's the setup. A colleague of mine uses "priors wash out" as a conversation-stopper: whenever people disagree about a model or a forecast, she says disagreement is just a finite-data problem, and with enough evidence any two Bayesians converge to the same beliefs, so long as neither started with a dogmatic prior assigning probability zero to the truth. I think this is much weaker than it sounds, and I want you to argue my side: the washing-out story fails in exactly the cases where you'd most want it to hold — namely, when the truth is not in anyone's hypothesis space and the world refuses to sit still.

To make this concrete, anchor the argument in a running example: two forecasters, Ada and Boris, predicting quarterly loan defaults. Both are ideal Bayesians, but they use different model families, neither of which contains the actual data-generating process, and the economy's regime drifts a little after every major policy change.

Your argument needs to do at least these three things:

1. Pin down what the standard results actually deliver — Doob-style consistency and Blackwell–Dubins merging — and show how much weight the absolute-continuity ("grain of truth") condition is carrying. In particular, explain why the condition is not a mild technicality but basically assumes away the problem.

2. Explain what happens under misspecification: the posterior concentrates on whichever model is closest to the truth in the relevant information-theoretic sense, and that "closest" point depends on the data distribution — so when the distribution drifts, the two forecasters' posteriors can chase different targets, and even a single forecaster's posterior can oscillate rather than settle. Argue that in this regime, priors don't wash out so much as get replaced by a dependence on the path of the data.

3. Press the gap between agreeing about long-run frequencies and agreeing about what to do on the next trial. Show that even if Ada and Boris asymptotically agree on some things, their one-step-ahead forecasts and the decisions built on them can diverge in ways that matter.

Also, steelman the other side at some point — roughly the reply "fine, but with stationary data and overlapping support, the prior's influence provably shrinks, so your complaint is about pace, not principle" — and say why it doesn't rescue the conversation-stopper use.
