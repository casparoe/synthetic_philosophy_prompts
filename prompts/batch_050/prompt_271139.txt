You are a philosopher with expertise in value theory and practical rationality, retained as an outside advisor by a small grant-making foundation. Please think carefully before writing: begin with a short, explicit brainstorming pass over the strongest considerations on each side and the structure of the disagreement, and only then compose the deliverable described below.

I sit on the five-person grants board of the Hartwell Trust, which must allocate a one-time, indivisible bequest of $2.4 million to exactly one of two finalists. Option A would fund the complete documentation of Teiu, a language with roughly forty remaining speakers, all elderly, including an archive, grammar, and teaching materials that would otherwise be impossible to produce once the last speakers die. Option B would fund a treatment program in a low-income region that, by conservative projection, would prevent about sixty cases of permanent childhood blindness. The board accepts all the factual projections on both sides. The disagreement is not about facts.

One faction holds that the choice is straightforward: preventable blindness in children is simply the greater evil, and anyone who hesitates is indulging sentimentality. The other faction holds that the permanent extinction of a language is a loss of a kind that cannot be weighed against health benefits on any common scale, and that treating it as if it could be already concedes the argument. Two complications have arisen in discussion:

First, a board member pressed the following argument. Imagine Option A modestly improved — say the archive is expanded slightly at no extra cost, so we have A+. If A+ still does not beat B, then, she argues, A could not have been worse than B in the first place, since a genuine improvement would have closed the gap. She takes this to show that the two options must be comparable after all, one way or the other. Other members were unsure whether this reasoning is sound or whether it smuggles in the very comparability it purports to establish.

Second, a junior member has proposed what she calls the weighted coin procedure: if, after full deliberation, the board finds the options neither equal nor clearly ranked, each member should state how strongly they lean, we should convert those leanings into probabilities, and a lottery should decide. She defends this as fair to the values themselves, since neither value gets to dominate simply by being easier to argue for in committee.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote, roughly 600 to 900 words. It should:

- arrive at a concrete recommendation between A and B, not merely propose further deliberation or deferral;
- engage seriously with whether goods of these two kinds can be compared on a single scale, and — crucially — what follows for rational choice if they cannot: does incomparability make any decision arbitrary, or are there rational ways to choose without a common measure;
- assess the improved-option argument above, identifying exactly where it succeeds or fails;
- evaluate the weighted coin procedure on its merits, including whether it conflates the board's uncertainty with genuine indifference between the outcomes, and whether it could be defended on grounds better than the ones its proposer offered;
- give the losing side an honest account of what is lost, without inflating that loss into a veto or dismissing it as irrational.
