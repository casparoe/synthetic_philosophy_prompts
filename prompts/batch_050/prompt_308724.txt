Quick bit of context: I'm a PhD student in political theory working on secession, and I keep running into the same analogy in the literature and (worse) in op-eds: secession is like divorce. Scotland, Quebec, Catalonia -- someone always says a state is like a marriage, so if one partner wants out, insisting they stay is like forbidding divorce, i.e. keeping someone trapped in a relationship against their will. Unionists fire back with their own version: fine, it's a divorce, but then you don't get to unilaterally walk off with the house, the savings and the kids -- asset division, debts and custody have to be negotiated, and the other spouse gets a say. Both sides seem to think the analogy settles the argument in their favour, which makes me suspicious it's doing no real work at all.

Can you audit it properly? Concretely:

1. Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the spouses, the marriage vows, the marital home, the joint debts, the kids. I suspect the interesting action is in who counts as a spouse (is "Catalonia" a spouse, or is it a member of the household with its own internal disagreements?) and whether territory is house, child, or body part.

2. Where does the mapping break, and which breaks actually matter for the conclusions being drawn? E.g. divorce is between two parties but a state contains the secessionists, the unionists within the seceding region, and everyone else; marriages don't usually have one spouse who is themselves a divided household; the "marital home" is inhabited by people who didn't consent to the analogy. Also the analogy seems to smuggle in that the seceding group is a pre-existing person-like agent with a unified will, which is arguably the very thing at issue.

3. The marriage analogy is often used to argue for a right to unilateral secession (no-fault divorce: you don't need your spouse's permission to leave). But modern divorce still involves courts, waiting periods, and a settlement -- nobody gets to just walk out with half the assets by their own unilateral say-so. So does the analogy, once you take its details seriously, actually support the unilateralists or the "negotiated process only" crowd (think Edinburgh Agreement versus the 2017 Catalan route)?

4. If the divorce analogy can't carry the load, what's the better analogy, if any? Business partnership dissolution? Leaving a club? Amputation? Or is the whole genre of "the state is like X" arguments hopeless here because the state is sui generis, and we should just argue about self-determination directly?

Please separate the disanalogies that are fatal from the ones that are merely cosmetic -- I want to know whether this analogy is a broken crutch or a decent crutch being leaned on too hard, because my supvervisor keeps using it and I'd like to know what I can say back.
