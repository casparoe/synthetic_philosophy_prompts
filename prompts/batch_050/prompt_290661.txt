Three weeks from now, I am giving the best-man speech at the wedding of my closest friend from graduate school. We met in a seminar on Bernard Williams, and the running argument of our friendship — now entering its twelfth year — concerns Williams's well-known objection to impartial moral theories: the man who finds his wife and a stranger both drowning, and who first pauses to ask himself whether morality permits him to prefer his wife, has already had, in Williams's phrase, one thought too many. My friend has always sided with Williams: love, properly understood, is unmediated by justification, and the deliberating husband has misunderstood something fundamental about what he is doing. I have always pushed back: the same line can sanctify favoritism and laziness, and a person who never checks his partiality against principle may simply have failed to notice the moment his love became mere selfishness.

The groom knows exactly where I stand, and at the rehearsal dinner he dared me — in front of everyone — to say it in the speech. So here is the task. Write a best-man speech of roughly five minutes, somewhere around 700 to 800 words, for a mixed audience of about 120 people: both extended families, the groom's eighty-four-year-old grandmother, a handful of colleagues from his engineering firm, and perhaps six philosophy PhDs who will catch every nuance and silently grade me. The speech must land for all of them.

The Williams thought should do real structural work, not decorative work. In particular, it should constrain what the speech can and cannot say in praise of the couple. For example, the speech cannot praise the groom as a man who weighed everything carefully and chose wisely, because that would cast the marriage itself as the conclusion of a deliberation — precisely the one thought too many. But the speech also cannot simply celebrate unthinking devotion, both because I do not believe in it and because standing up at a wedding to announce that the groom never had a single justificatory thought about his bride is, on its face, an insult. The speech has to find the third thing: a way to honor deliberation without convicting the groom of having deliberated about the one thing he shouldn't have, or, alternatively, a way to concede Williams's point while showing why the concession is not the defeat it looks like. I do not know in advance which of these the speech should attempt; that is part of what I am asking you to figure out.

Constraints: the tone must be warm and affectionate throughout; there must be at least one genuine joke that works simultaneously for the grandmother and for the philosophers, ideally one that turns on the drowning-wife scenario without requiring anyone to have heard of it; no philosophical name should be spoken aloud more than once, and any reference must be immediately cashed out in terms a non-philosopher can follow; do not quote Williams or anyone else verbatim — paraphrase only; and the speech must end somewhere a wedding speech is allowed to end, which is to say on the couple, not on the argument.

After the speech itself, add a short note, marked clearly as not to be read aloud, in which you do two things. First, state how confident you are that the speech actually threads the needle — that it neither flatters unreflective partiality nor implicitly accuses the groom of insufficient thought about his own marriage — and explain your confidence level in a sentence or two. Second, single out the specific moment in the speech you are least sure of: the line most likely to misfire in the room, or the place where the speech leans hardest on a contested reading of what Williams was actually claiming about love and justification.
