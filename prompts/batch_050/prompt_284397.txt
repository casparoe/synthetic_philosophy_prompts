I’m a novelist running a one-off workshop for six other fiction writers, mostly people who build fantasy species and alien societies but have only a pop-culture grasp of evolution. Next month I get 75 minutes to teach them the spandrels critique of adaptationism—not the history of the debate, but the habit of not treating every striking trait as existing because it was selected for.

Plan how I should teach it rather than giving me the lecture itself. What conceptual pieces have to come first, and in what order? At some point they need the difference between a trait being an adaptation, a side effect of something else, a developmental or historical constraint, and a feature that originated one way but later became useful, but I suspect introducing all four at once would be fatal. What dependencies should determine the sequence?

Also invent the one carrying example I should build the session around—preferably a fictional organism with a weird trait—and tell me exactly what details it needs, and what tempting details to leave out, so it can support those distinctions without turning into an evolutionary Just So Story itself. I’d rather not use the usual examples like chins or male nipples.

Then map the misconceptions they’ll probably bring: that the critique says adaptation explanations are always bogus, that a good story about usefulness counts as evidence, that byproducts have to be useless, that constraints mean nothing can evolve, or that once a trait becomes useful its origin stops mattering. Tell me where in the sequence to head each one off.

Finish with a quick check for understanding—maybe something involving a trait from one of their own drafts—and what I should probly leave out at this level while admitting that I’m leaving it out.
