I'm giving a single ninety-minute guest lecture on modularity of mind to a reading group of machine-learning researchers -- people fluent in gradient descent and architecture design who have never read Fodor -- and the arc must run from Fodor's original thesis to the massive modularity defended by evolutionary psychologists. Plan the lecture rather than deliver it. Give the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what each depends on: I assume the persistence of the Müller-Lyer illusion in the face of known line-length equality has to precede the formal list of Fodor's marks of modularity, so tell me what has to precede *that*, and where the divide between input systems and central cognition belongs in the sequence. Pick the one example that carries the whole lecture and state what it must demonstrate and what it must not require -- and why a merely fast or automatic phenomenon, such as the Stroop effect, would carry the wrong lesson. Then map the misconceptions this particular audience will form, in the order they'll surface: I expect at least "a module is a brain region," "informationally encapsulated means nothing can influence it," plus their discipline-native confusions, such as "modular means not learnable by gradient descent" and "massive modularity is just a mixture-of-experts architecture," and say exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Include the point where massive modularity must be presented as a rejection of Fodor's pessimism about central cognition rather than as an extension of his view, and the fresh misconception that framing generates. Finish with one closing question that tests whether encapsulation has landed -- for instance, whether they can correctly judge what the McGurk effect does and does not show -- and one topic I should explicitly refuse to cover in ninety minutes, with the refusal itself drafted.
