You are a philosopher with expertise in population ethics and the ethics of procreation. Work carefully; reason through the argument step by step before committing to a diagnosis, and do not settle on the first plausible failure point you find.

A colleague and I assembled the following argument out of Benatar's asymmetry. Each premise seemed acceptable when we wrote it down; the conclusion seems to us clearly false. Either something subtle went wrong in the reasoning, or one of the plausible premises has to go. We want to know which, and what rejecting it costs.

First, the background comparison. To decide whether coming into existence is a harm or a benefit, compare, for any possible person, the world in which they exist with the world in which they never do:

(1) The pains the person would suffer in the existence-world count against bringing them into existence.
(2) The pleasures they would enjoy count in favor of bringing them into existence only if the absence of those pleasures in the never-exist world would be bad for someone; since in that world there is no one to be deprived of anything, the absence of pleasure is not bad.
(3) The absence of pain in the never-exist world is good, even though no one there exists to enjoy that good.
(4) So, for any possible person, the ledger is asymmetric: never existing secures a good (absent pain) at no cost (absent pleasure is not a loss), while existing guarantees at least some pains whose accompanying pleasures purchase nothing that non-existence lacks.
(5) Therefore coming into existence is always, on balance, worse than never coming into existence — worse for the person created, in the only sense of "worse" that comparisons of this kind can support.

Now the extension, which is where we start to feel vertigo:

(6) The reasoning in (1)–(5) makes no reference to population size or to the distribution of pains and pleasures; it applies person by person, so it applies to any world we might choose to create.
(7) Consider two worlds we could actualize. World A: ten billion people with rich, joyful lives, exactly one of whom, on one occasion, feels a single sharp pinprick lasting one second. World B: no one ever exists.
(8) Applying (4) person by person: each of the ten billion would have done better, in the relevant sense, never to exist; the pinprick victim's existence contains a bad that B lacks, and the others' joys are not goods that B is missing. So B is better than A.
(9) But B is obviously not better than A. A near-perfect world does not lose to the void on the strength of one pinprick.

Something has to give. My colleague would rather accept (8) than give up (2) and (3) — she regards the asymmetry as the one firm intuition in the area. I would rather keep (9) and sacrifice whatever needs sacrificing. Before we talk past each other further, I want an outside diagnosis.
