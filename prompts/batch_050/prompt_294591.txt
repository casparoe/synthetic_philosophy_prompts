So I write for a general-audience science/ideas magazine, and I've gotten myself into something: I pitched a talk for a weekend festival-of-ideas thing and they put me on a panel in front of actual academics. My talk argues that the shift to agriculture was, on net, a bad deal for the humans who lived through it and for a long while after — the usual package: people got shorter and sicker, diets narrowed, work hours went up, hierarchy and property and patriarchy show up with grain surpluses and states, and the whole thing locked us onto a treadmill we couldnt get off. Think Diamond's "worst mistake" essay, the Sahlins hunter-gatherer affluence angle, plus the more recent stuff about grain states and legibility.

Problem: the room will have a bunch of archaeologists and biological anthropologists in it, plus maybe an economic historian or two. These are people who've spent careers on osteological data and are sick of pop books flattening it. They will eat me alive if I just repeat the greatest hits.

So prep me for the Q&A. Give me the objections I'll actually get from THIS audience — not the ones a lay audience asks ("but modern medicine!"), the ones specialists ask. Order them by how likely they are to come up and how much damage each does. Phrase each one the way an archaeologist would actually phrase it, jargon and all (I know the lingo well enough to keep up, I just can't produce it natively). For each: what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply that takes an actual position — don't give me "some scholars say X, others say Y" menus, I need replies that commit to a reading of the evidence and defend it, even if the honest reply is "yeah, this part of my thesis is weak and here's the strongest version of it that survives."

Also include: which objections have real force versus which are the kind of thing this audience reflexively says but can't fully back up either, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks (I have my suspicions about what it is — something to do with Göbekli Tepe and what it does to my causal story — but tell me if you think it's something else).

Format-wise: lead with your bottom-line assessment of whether my thesis is defensible in that room at all, first sentence, no preamble, no restating my setup. Then the prep sheet.
