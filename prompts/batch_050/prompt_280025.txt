I am a novelist at work on a book about two former lovers who jointly possess a dangerous secret — say, evidence of a fraud they committed together years ago — and who maintain an uneasy alliance of mutual protection. At any moment, either of them can go to the authorities, trade what they know for immunity, and destroy the other; every year neither does, their shared prosperity and their separate investments in the lie both grow. A friend in economics told me I had reinvented the centipede game, and explained the backward induction argument: since each partner knows the other would defect at the final stage, each should defect one stage earlier, and so on until rationality demands betrayal on the very first day. What fascinates and frustrates me is that this conclusion seems airtight on paper and yet obviously false to life — real people in such arrangements pass and pass and pass, often for decades, and my two characters must do the same for my plot to exist at all.

With that puzzle in mind, I would like a reading of Choderlos de Laclos's Les Liaisons Dangereuses through the lens of backward induction and the centipede game. The alliance between the Marquise de Merteuil and the Vicomte de Valmont strikes me as the great literary instance of the problem: two players who profit enormously by continuing to cooperate in manipulation, who each hold material capable of ruining the other, and whose partnership does in the end collapse into mutual destruction. Where exactly does Laclos locate the defection, and does the novel give a psychologically satisfying account of why it happens at that node rather than earlier or later? Does the book suggest an explanation for why such alliances last as long as they do — trust purchased by shared complicity, reputation among a small circle, genuine attachment, self-deception about the endgame — and is that explanation one a game theorist could formalize, or does it resist formalization? At the same time, I want the reading to be honest in both directions: where does the game-theoretic frame distort the novel, for instance by treating the characters' motives as fixed payoffs when the book's whole point may be that desire, vanity, and love keep rewriting the payoffs mid-game?

Finally, I would like the argument brought back to my own craft problem. Backward induction is, dramatically speaking, poison: it says the collapse should be visible from page one, and a reader who reasons that way will find my characters' long cooperation naive. Yet I suspect the gap between what the logic demands and what people actually do is precisely where the novel lives. So tell me what a novelist can learn from the centipede paradox itself — the fact that a valid argument produces a conclusion everyone rejects — and whether fiction can show something about trust, foresight, and the refusal to reason to the end that the formal analysis cannot state. Please answer in American English, and do not feel bound to preserve either the economics or the novel intact; I would rather be told where my analogy breaks than have it flattered.
