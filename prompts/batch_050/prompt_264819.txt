I'm in my final year of high school and writing a philosophy paper on self-deception, which I'm convinced is real but which several of my classmates insist is impossible -- you can't lie to yourself, they say, because a liar must know the truth while getting the victim to believe the opposite, and one mind can't play both parts. Rather than argue case by case, I want the specification first: write the list of requirements an adequate account of self-deception must meet to count as explaining the phenomenon -- the paradoxes it has to dissolve rather than just restate (how the same person can hold the conflicting belief and intention, and how the process can be motivated without being deliberate), the distinctions it can't collapse (self-deception vs. wishful thinking vs. ordinary bias vs. akrasia), and the everyday cases it has to cover, like the partner who sincerely does not see the affair everyone else sees. For each requirement, give the consideration behind it. Then be committed, not even-handed: tell me which single requirement you think does the most to eliminate the standard families of account (intentionalist, deflationary, and divided-mind), and which commonly demanded requirement you think should be dropped as a prejudice rather than a genuine constraint, with reasons in both cases.
