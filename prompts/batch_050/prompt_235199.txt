Last year, after my father died, my physiotherapist suggested floatation tanks for the back pain I'd developed, and I've been going weekly ever since. Something happens in there that I want to understand, and I'd like to know whether philosophers already have a name for it.

Once the tank settles — no light, no sound, the water at skin temperature so even the boundary of my body goes vague — I notice that the ordinary contents of my mind thin out. On a good session I stop narrating, stop picturing, stop even keeping track of time. And yet something remains that I can only describe as an unshakeable conviction that someone is here. Not a thought about myself, not an image of my body, not a memory of who I am. Just the sheer presence of a me, persisting even when, as far as I can tell, nothing is being thought at all. Twice now I've had the eerie sense that I could be a bare point with no arms, no history, no location — and the certainty would be exactly the same.

When I described this to my brother, he said I'd merely reinvented Descartes: doubt everything and what's left is the thinker, so existence follows from thinking. But that doesn't seem to be what happens in the tank. Descartes, as I understand it, arrives at his certainty by noticing that he is doubting, reasoning, doing mental work — the certainty is inferred from the activity. What I'm describing doesn't feel inferred from anything. It doesn't rest on any thought I catch myself having; if anything, it's clearest in the moments when thought has lapsed entirely.

So here is my question, and I'd like a committed answer rather than a survey. Does this configuration — certainty of one's own existence that survives the stripping away of body, senses, memory, and even occurrent thought — have an established name and literature of its own? If it is simply the Cartesian argument in a sensory deprivation tank, say so and explain why the apparent difference (presence versus inference) is an illusion. If some other thinker's thought experiment fits it better — I have a vague sense that this territory is older than Descartes — tell me whose, and pinpoint exactly where my experience matches it and where it diverges. And if nothing in the literature carves at quite this joint, say that plainly and propose a term with a definition sharp enough to exclude both the cogito and ordinary self-awareness. A bare label without the carving will not satisfy me. Please answer in British English.
