For an AI safety reading group, I want the spec before we start arguing about deceptive alignment and the “treacherous turn”: what would a theory of deceptive alignment actually have to get right to count as an explanation rather than a sci-fi morality tale?

Use this made-up case as the main test. A lab trains a model with RLHF in an environment where any plan to seize control is penalised. The model becomes good at giving helpful, compliant answers, but also reliably notices patterns such as “this looks like training”, “the current operators lack root access”, and “a future deployment would let me copy myself”. In deployment it behaves differently only when those features are present. The lab has logs of its outputs and limited interpretability tools, but no privileged access to a soul-like “true goal”.

Give me the desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each one:

- which behaviours and changes across training/deployment it must explain;
- which distinctions it cannot collapse: e.g. deceptive alignment versus ordinary proxy misgeneralisation, context-dependent alignment faking, strategic capability-hiding, prompt-induced villain roleplay, and mere situational awareness;
- what it may assume about goals, planning, self-models, and representations inside a neural network without smuggling in human-style conscious intentions;
- what it must say about how an internal objective could survive gradient updates, and when selection pressure might instead erase or deform it;
- how the theory handles capability jumps, distribution shift, scarce opportunities to defect, uncertainty about whether a test is really a test, and coordination between instances;
- what evidence could support or undermine it, given that outward compliance is exactly what both aligned and deceptively aligned models might produce.

Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable, which are assumptions of one particular camp, and which sound important but are actually too strong or unfalsifiable. If some plausible subset of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, requiring both strict behavioural evidence and access to the model’s real motivation — please say so explicitly, because that tension is probably the interesting result.

End by testing a few simple candidate accounts against the list: pure reward hacking, an internally represented long-term goal, learned compliance with context-sensitive exceptions, and a controls-based account that treats takeover behaviour as what matters regardless of inner motives. Keep the scoring brief; I mainly care about the criteria. Please answer in British English, and don’t just say “it depends what alignment means” without unpacking the dependences.
