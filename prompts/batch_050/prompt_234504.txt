I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early Chinese philosophy, and I'm writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature that cannot be answered by reciting the slogans "Mencius: nature is good; Xunzi: nature is bad." Students who write that will get a C at best, and I want the question itself to make that obvious.

To force the issue, I'd like the question to confront students with the following constructed case, which they haven't seen: imagine a child raised entirely by a scrupulously fair but completely unemotional caretaker — every need met, every rule consistently enforced, but no affection, no delight in the child, no grief at its distress. The child grows up reliably just but, as an adult, reports never having felt spontaneous compassion, shame, or reverence; she acts well the way one solves equations. The question should ask students to argue, on Mencius's behalf and then on Xunzi's behalf, whether this person is (a) morally complete, (b) morally damaged, or (c) evidence that the very category of "human nature" is doing no work in that thinker's system — and then to adjudicate which philosopher has the more serious problem with the case. The crux I want them to hit: for Mencius the difficulty is that the sprouts are supposed to be universally present and merely in need of extension, so a human apparently lacking them threatens either the empirical claim or the definition of the human; for Xunzi the difficulty is whether his account of transformation through conscious activity (wei) and ritual has the resources to say anything is missing in this person at all, or whether he is forced to call her a success story.

Please deliver three things:

1. The exam question as it would appear on the paper, including a word limit and time allowance you think appropriate, and one sentence of guidance to students about what the question is not asking (to head off the summary-recital answer).

2. A model answer of about 600–700 words written to the standard of your best student, engaging both thinkers' actual conceptual machinery (the sprout and extension picture, the heart-mind, wei and ritual, the role of models and teachers) rather than textbook paraphrase, and reaching a defended verdict on whose problem is worse.

3. A marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer (22+) must contain — e.g., particular distinctions or interpretive risks it must take — and the specific failures that cap an answer at a B (18–21) or below, phrased as concrete missing moves rather than vague quality descriptors.

Finally, since the scholarly readings here are contested (in particular, how strongly to read Xunzi's claim about nature, and whether his position is best understood as nature being bad, merely disordered, or simply unformed), I'd like you to append a short note after the marking scheme stating how confident you are in the reading your model answer commits to, and singling out the one interpretive point in it you are least sure of — the place where a knowledgeable marker could most plausibly say the model answer took a side it shouldn't have.
