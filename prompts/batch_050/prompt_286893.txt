You are a professional philosopher specializing in social ontology and collective intentionality who also teaches a film-and-philosophy seminar. I am preparing this semester's session on group agency, and I would like a careful critical reading of Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957) through the literature on collective intentionality and joint action: Searle's irreducible we-intentions, Bratman's account of shared intention as meshing subplans held with common knowledge, and Gilbert's plural subject theory with its central notion of joint commitment.

Here is the puzzle around which I want the reading organized. A jury is the textbook case of a group agent: twelve people are convened to produce a single verdict, and the law treats that verdict as the jury's act rather than as the sum of twelve individual acts. Yet the film's path to unanimity is strikingly heterogeneous. Some jurors are persuaded by reconstructed evidence (the old man's testimony, the woman's eyesight); one switches his vote out of impatience to leave for a baseball game, openly conceding that the reasons no longer matter to him; the final holdout capitulates only when his private grievance against his estranged son collapses in public. The group thus arrives at a unanimous verdict of not guilty while its members hold that view, if they hold it at all, on wildly different grounds.

Use this as a stress test for the theories. On Gilbert's account, a group can believe something as a body even when few or none of its members personally believe it, since group belief is a matter of joint commitment rather than aggregated individual attitudes. Does the film vindicate that surprising consequence, or does it expose it as a reductio? Is the final verdict a genuine collective belief, a collective action performed in the absence of one, or merely twelve individual acquiescences under institutional pressure? Consider also Juror 8's early maneuver, in which he offers to abstain from a secret ballot and to join a guilty verdict should no one else dissent: is he initiating a joint activity in Bratman's sense, declining one, or engineering the background conditions under which one could form? And can a joint commitment survive the species of consent the film depicts — consent extracted by fatigue, impatience, and social pressure?

Two constraints. First, take both directions of fit seriously: the reading should illuminate the film and test the theories, not reduce one to an illustration of the other. Where the film resists the framework, say so plainly — an honest failure of fit is more useful to my students than a forced synthesis. Second, refer to scenes by description rather than by quotation, since I will be screening clips rather than distributing a text. Before drafting, think carefully about which scenes carry the greatest theoretical weight, and identify the strongest objection to your own reading.

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. Setup (~200 words): the three accounts of shared intention in brief, and why a jury verdict is the natural test case for them.
2. The Film as a Laboratory (~350 words): where 12 Angry Men vindicates the theories — which account best captures the deliberation, and at which specific moments.
3. Where the Film Pushes Back (~350 words): the gap between group verdict and group belief, the indifferent or coerced vote-switchers, and which theorist is most embarrassed by them.
4. The Verdict on the Verdict (~250 words): your final assessment — does this jury end the film as a Gilbertian plural subject, a Bratman-style shared agent, or a hollowed-out Searlean we-intention? Close by stating what, if anything, the film contributes to the philosophical debate that a purely argumentative treatment could not: in particular, whether watching a joint commitment form, deform, and re-form over ninety minutes in a locked room reveals something about the phenomenology and fragility of group agency that the theories themselves leave out.
