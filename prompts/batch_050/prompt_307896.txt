So I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about a lawsuit that's been stuck in my head. A chemical plant operated near a small town for decades; a kid who grew up nearby got a rare cancer; epidemiologists show that exposure to the plant's emissions raises the rate of that cancer in populations, but everyone admits you can't rerun history to check this particular kid. The whole case turns on whether "the plant caused HER cancer" is even a coherent thing to say, as opposed to just shorthand for the population statistics.

I'd like you to write a dialogue I can mine for the piece, between two philosophers who disagree about exactly this. One is a hard-nosed regularity theorist: causation just is patterns of constant conjunction plus the inferential habits they produce in us, so single-case causal talk is at best a loose way of gesturing at population-level regularities (and at worst a confusion the law should stop indulging). The other is a singularist in something like the "New Hume" spirit: there are real causal powers and singular connections in the world, Hume himself arguably believed in them and only denied we could ever know them directly, and the kid's case involves a brute singular fact that regularities don't exhaust.

A few things I want from the dialogue:

- Make them fight over the actual case, not just trade slogans. The regularity theorist should press: what observable difference does a "power" make in the kid's case, and how could a court ever be epistemically answerable to it? The singularist should press back: if causation is only regularities, what do you say about a possible world where each kind of event happens exactly once, or about causes that were interrupted before any pattern could form -- does nothing cause anything there?
- Have them grapple with the awkward fact that Hume seems to offer two different characterizations of cause -- one about regular succession, one about what we would infer counterfactually -- and let each of them try to claim the better reading without either of them treating it as decisive.
- Neither should win cleanly. By the end I want them to have genuinely moved each other on at least one point each, but still disagreeing about the kid.
- Keep them both sharp and charitable -- these are colleagues, not cartoon villians.

Roughly 800-1200 words. Don't have them lecture like encyclopedia entries; I want actual conversation, interruptions and all.
