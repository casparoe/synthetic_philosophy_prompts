A colleague of mine classifies systems using the standard four-level taxonomy of kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures, whose competencies are entirely hardwired by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures, which improve their behavior within their own lifetime through reward and punishment; Popperian creatures, which can represent candidate actions and test them in some inner surrogate environment before committing, so that their hypotheses can fail instead of their bodies; and Gregorian creatures, which amplify their thinking with tools inherited from their culture, such as language, notation, and taught methods.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A fish that performs a fixed courtship display, identical even in individuals raised in isolation: Darwinian.
2. A lab rat that learns to press levers in a particular sequence through food rewards: Skinnerian.
3. An octopus that masters unscrewing a jar only after many attempts: Skinnerian.
4. A chimpanzee that surveys a room and then, on its first attempt, stacks crates to reach a hanging banana: Popperian.
5. A chess engine that explicitly searches a tree of candidate moves against an internal board model before moving: Popperian.
6. A chess engine that is a pure trained policy network with no lookahead, whose weights were shaped entirely by reinforcement during training: Skinnerian.
7. A person doing long division with pencil and paper: Gregorian.
8. A person doing the same long division entirely in their head, using methods they were taught in school: Gregorian.
9. A crow that invents a wire-hook technique entirely on its own, with no other crow or human having demonstrated it, and appears to plan its approach before acting: Popperian, and explicitly not Gregorian.

The new case: a large language model that, before giving its final answer, generates several paragraphs of step-by-step reasoning which it then reads and builds on. Crucially, this behavior was never learned by imitating human-written worked solutions; it emerged purely through reinforcement learning that rewarded correct final answers. The model's operators discovered the scratchpad behavior only after training.
