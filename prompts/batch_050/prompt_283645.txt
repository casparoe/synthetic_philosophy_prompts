I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I run a monthly "ideas night" at work — around 10-12 devs, we get about 90 minutes. I fell down a rabbit hole on Turing's 1950 paper (the can-machines-think one) and got stuck on this bit where he says: don't try to program an adult mind directly, build a "child machine" and educate it, with rewards and punishments, more or less like raising a kid. The tension that grabs me: the teacher only gets crude signals to send, the machine will generalize in weird wrong ways, but if you keep stepping in and overriding what it's figured out, you've defeated the whole point of teaching instead of programming — and if you never step in, it learns nonsense. He also says somewhere that the teaching signal should ideally go through a fairly impersonal channel, which seems to matter.

Design me a game for this group where they *feel* that dilemma instead of hearing about it. I'm imagining a couple people play child machines who can only recieve restricted signals (rewards/punishments, no plain instructions) and the rest have to teach them some task against a clock, but you can pick a better format. Key things: the interference-vs-hands-off problem has to bite them during play, and nobody should hear the phrase "child machine" until afterwards, no mini-lecture up front.

Then give me a debrief plan: which moments in the game map to which parts of the idea, what will predictably go wrong when I actually run this (people smuggling real instructions into the signal channel, the "machine" getting frustrated, etc.) and how to handle it, and be straight with me about which part of Turing's idea the format just can't reproduce and I'd have to fill in myself.
