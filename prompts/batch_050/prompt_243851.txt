I'm TAing an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar this term and the prof has delegated the final exam's long essay question to me. The course spent three weeks on the new mechanists, mostly Machamer, Darden, and Craver's 2000 paper and the constitutive relevance stuff from Craver's book, and past exams have basically just asked students to state the MDC characterization of a mechanism and apply it to the textbook examples (protein synthesis, the action potential). Students can memorize that cold without understanding anything. I want one essay question, answerable in about 45 minutes, that they genuinely can't prep for by rereading their notes.

Here's the seed I've been playing with, feel free to adjust the details if something's off. Suppose researchers studying a fictional alpine moth discover that its wing spots fade every autumn. They propose a mechanism: falling temperatures activate an enzyme they call brumase, brumase binds a pigment precursor in the wing scales, and the bound precursor degrades, bleaching the spots. Interventions check out at both ends -- cooling the moths early makes spots fade early, knocking out brumase keeps spots dark all winter. But here's the twist: it turns out the fading plays no role in the moths' camouflage or survival, nothing downstream depends on it, and some members of the research team argue this means brumase binding isn't really a component *of* a mechanism -- it's just a side-reaction, the way a fever is a byproduct rather than part of the immune response's mechanism. The question should ask students to adjudicate this: does the mutual manipulability / constitutive relevance criterion deliver a verdict here, does the byproduct objection actually have teeth given what MDC say about start-up and termination conditions, and what follows for how permissive the account of mechanism should be.

So please write (1) the exam question itself, in a form I could paste onto the exam paper, (2) a model answer of around 600 words that takes a definite position on whether the brumase case counts as a genuine mechanism and commits to either defending or revising Craver's criterion -- don't give me an on-the-one-hand-on-the-other survey, I need something I can actually hold student essays against -- and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer must contain versus a B answer, and flags the specific mistakes that cap an essay at a C (e.g. treating mutual manipulability as just "the intervention worked," or conflating the byproduct worry with the etiological/functionalist objection).
