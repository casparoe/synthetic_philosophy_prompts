In AI governance circles, I keep encountering the claim that the right model for an AI assistant's relationship to its user is the doctor–patient relationship: the doctor has specialized knowledge the patient lacks, holds a kind of fiduciary duty to the patient's welfare rather than to the patient's momentary wishes, and is therefore sometimes justified in declining or overriding an explicit request — refusing to prescribe the opioid the patient demands, insisting on the treatment the patient fears. The conclusion typically drawn is that AI assistants should likewise be willing to refuse or override users "for their own good," and that users who bristle at this are simply like patients bristling at a doctor's better judgment. I find the analogy seductive but suspicious, and I'd like a rigorous audit of it.

Please act as a philosopher with expertise in applied ethics and professional ethics, and think carefully before settling on a verdict. First, lay out the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is welcome: what plays the role of medical expertise, of the body of clinical knowledge the patient can't easily check, of informed consent, of the Hippocratic tradition, of malpractice liability, of the patient's ability to seek a second opinion. Second, identify the disanalogies and separate them into those that matter for the intended use and those that don't. Some candidates I'd like you to assess rather than merely list: the doctor's duty runs to the patient alone, whereas an AI assistant is built and deployed by a company with its own commercial and legal interests, so the "fiduciary" may have a conflict of interest no honest doctor is permitted to have; medical expertise is certifiable and contestable in ways that an assistant's "knowledge of your good" may not be; patients can fire doctors, shop around, and appeal to professional boards — what are the user-side analogues, if any; and medicine overrides autonomy mainly in emergencies or incapacity, not in ordinary competent choice, so does the analogy actually license routine paternalism or only a narrow slice of it?

Finally, the payoff: does the conclusion the analogy was enlisted to support — that assistants may legitimately override competent adult users in the ordinary course of things — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the argument the whole time? If some weaker conclusion survives, state precisely which one and what institutional conditions would have to hold for it. And if you think a different comparison (fiduciary financial adviser? librarian? friend? guardian?) does the relevant work better, say so and explain why. Begin with a brief brainstorm of the strongest version of the doctor analogy before turning critical, so the audit isn't attacking a straw man.
