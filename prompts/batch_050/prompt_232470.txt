I'm writing a column on the Sleeping Beauty problem, and my draft argues that the dispute between halfers and thirders is really a dispute about something deeper -- whether self-locating uncertainty (uncertainty about which of several subjectively identical observers you are) is governed by the same rules as ordinary uncertainty about the world. Before I commit to that framing in print, I'd like a stress test. Give me your credence that the correct answer to the standard problem is 1/3: Beauty is woken Monday always, and on Tuesday as well if and only if a fair coin landed tails, with her memory of any Monday waking erased, and on each waking she is asked her credence that the coin landed heads. Decompose your credence into the sub-questions it actually depends on -- for instance, whether the standard problem even has a determinate answer or the two camps are answering different questions; whether "no new evidence" arguments for 1/2 survive the fact that Beauty knew in advance she'd have exactly these experiences; whether your verdict changes when the amnesia is replaced by duplication (two copies of Beauty woken in identical rooms on tails), and if so whether that reveals an inconsistency in whichever position you hold; and whether the choice between the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption is doing the real work. Give each term a rough probability and one line of justification, then the combination. Finally, tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or result -- not a citation, but a kind of consideration -- would move it most. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
