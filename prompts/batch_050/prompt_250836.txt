Having retired last year after four decades in experimental particle physics, I have been making my way through the literature on algorithmic fairness, and I keep having the slightly disorienting feeling that I have seen this material before in the laboratory. The result I have in mind is the well-known impossibility theorem about risk scores. In rough terms: suppose an algorithm assigns each defendant a score estimating the probability of reoffense, and suppose we want three things of it. First, calibration: among all defendants assigned a given score, the fraction who actually reoffend should equal that score, and this should hold within each demographic group separately. Second, equal false negative rates across groups: among those who do not reoffend, the proportion mistakenly flagged as high-risk should be the same. Third, equal false positive rates: among those who do reoffend, the proportion mistakenly classified as low-risk should be the same. The theorem says that unless the groups happen to share identical base rates of reoffending, or the predictions are perfect, no score can satisfy all three at once. This became famous when a news organization faulted a commercial recidivism tool for unequal error rates across racial groups and the tool's vendor replied that it was equally well calibrated for everyone — and both sides were, in a sense, right by their own lights.

Here is my request. Restate this theorem, and the controversy around it, in the vocabulary I actually spent my career using: instrument calibration, detection thresholds, sensitivity and specificity, prevalence, base rates, ROC curves, and the trade-offs one accepts when one moves a cut along a curve. I want the translation to be as faithful as you can make it — tell me, in that vocabulary, precisely why the three desiderata conflict when prevalence differs between populations, the way you would explain it to a colleague calibrating a trigger on a detector that must serve two experimental samples with different signal-to-background ratios. Do not sanitize the mathematics for my benefit; I am comfortable with it. What I am not comfortable with is the philosophical framing, and I want to see how much of it survives contact with my own idiom.

Then, and this is the part I care about most, give me an honest residue report. Which elements of the fairness debate translate cleanly into measurement language, which survive only under distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? My suspicion — and I want you to test it rather than indulge it — is that the residue consists of genuinely normative questions: whether fairness is owed to individuals or to groups, whether an asymmetric cost of the two error types is a moral judgment rather than a technical one, and whether calling any one of the three desiderata "fairness" is a choice that no calibration curve can make for you. If that suspicion is right, does it mean my vocabulary is missing something essential, or does it mean the public dispute was never really a mathematical disagreement wearing a mathematical disguise? I would also welcome your judgment on whether the impossibility result tells us something deep about fairness specifically, or whether it is simply the old base-rate lesson in new clothes.
