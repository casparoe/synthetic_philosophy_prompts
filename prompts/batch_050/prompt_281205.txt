At my school's debate club last week, two members got into an argument I've been turning over since, and I'd like help settling it. The motion was hypothetical: suppose a coastal province of a country announces a referendum on becoming an independent state. Who should be entitled to vote in it?

Sofia argued that only the province's residents should vote. Her reasoning was that political legitimacy rests on the consent of the governed, and the people whose citizenship, taxes, and laws are actually at stake are the ones whose consent matters. She added that if the whole country votes, a region could be held inside a state against the clear wishes of nearly everyone living there, which she said would defeat the point of democracy.

Marcus countered that secession would seriously affect everyone in the country — the national budget, the military, shared infrastructure, and control of the ports — so on the principle that everyone affected by a decision deserves a say in it, the rest of the country must be given votes as well.

Sofia replied that this principle proves too much. Neighboring countries would feel economic aftershocks too, and by Marcus's logic they'd deserve votes; more generally, since almost every political decision affects someone somewhere, the principle gives no non-arbitrary way to draw any boundary at all.

Marcus then shifted ground: the real criterion, he said, isn't being affected but being subject to the laws. On that basis, long-term residents of the province who lack citizenship should get ballots, while citizens who emigrated abroad years ago should not.

Sofia objected that subjection is also a matter of degree: commuters who work in the province, children who will inherit the outcome but can't yet vote, and people living near military installations are all "subjected" in different senses, so his criterion is no sharper than the one he criticized. Finally she raised a regress worry: any rule for deciding who gets to vote must itself be chosen by somebody, so the boundary question can never itself be settled democratically. Marcus answered that this regress is just how all constitutions begin and isn't a genuine problem.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: say who has the better of each move, note where a reply fails to actually answer the argument it targets, and give an overall verdict on who is more nearly right about how the demos for such a referendum should be drawn — including anything important that both of them are missing.
