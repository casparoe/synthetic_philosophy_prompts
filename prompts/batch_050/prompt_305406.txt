EXAMINATION QUESTION

England, Germany, France and the United States are often placed on a single spectrum running from "establishment" to "separation". Critically assess this practice. Your answer should:

(a) distinguish at least two dimensions along which a church–state arrangement can vary, and explain why a single spectrum may obscure more than it reveals;

(b) describe, with respect to at least three of the four countries, how money flows between taxpayers, the state and religious bodies;

(c) consider whether one country can be more "separationist" than another on one dimension while being less so on another, using at least one concrete pair of countries.

CANDIDATE'S ANSWER

The practice of placing England, Germany, France and the United States on a single spectrum from establishment to separation is misleading, because it compresses several independent variables into one ranking. I will distinguish two dimensions: constitutional status, meaning whether a church is established by law, with the state playing a role in its governance or its officers playing a role in the state's; and fiscal arrangement, meaning who pays for religion and who collects the money.

England is the clearest case of establishment. The Church of England is established by law, the monarch is its supreme governor, and twenty-six of its bishops sit as of right in the House of Lords. Yet, strikingly, England has no church tax. The Church is funded largely from its historic endowments, its investments, and voluntary giving by its members. England therefore shows that establishment in the constitutional sense does not entail state-collected funding.

Germany is the mirror image. It has no established church; its constitution recognises religious bodies as corporations under public law without privileging one of them. However, the German state collects a church tax through the income tax system: a surcharge of roughly eight or nine per cent is levied on every taxpayer's income tax and passed on to the recognised churches, with the state acting as collector and retaining a fee for the service. Individuals may avoid the tax only by formally leaving their church. Because this makes religious funding a matter of personal, reversible choice, Germany is arguably the most separationist of the four systems, since no citizen is locked into supporting a church.

France represents separation in a stricter sense. Since the law of 1905, the French state neither recognises nor subsidises any religion, and the principle of laïcité keeps religious bodies at arm's length from public institutions. Money for religion comes wholly from private sources.

The United States likewise has no established church and no church tax. The First Amendment forbids Congress from establishing a religion, and American congregations are funded entirely by voluntary contributions, which receive favourable treatment through the general law of tax exemption.

These facts show why the single spectrum fails. On the constitutional dimension, England is the least separationist; but on the fiscal dimension, it is arguably more separationist than Germany, since no English taxpayer is ever billed for a church, whereas every German taxpayer contributes through the state tax apparatus. Conversely, France and the United States rank together on both dimensions, while Germany combines constitutional non-establishment with fiscal entanglement. A single ranking would have to place these countries in one order, and any such order would hide these cross-cutting differences.
