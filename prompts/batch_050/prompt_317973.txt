So I retired from experimental physics about two years ago and have been falling down the philosophy rabbit hole ever since, mostly via a reading group of other retirees (a chemist, two engineers, and a very patient former GP). Last month we did decision theory and I got properly stuck on Buridan's ass. What got me is that in physics this situation isn't remotely puzzling: balance a pencil on its tip and it falls in some direction, not because anything favors that direction but because perfect symmetry is unstable and any tiny fluctuation breaks it. So my first instinct was that the ass problem just dissolves — the agent needs a bit of noise and that's that. But when I tried to write up for the group why the philosophical version still bothers people, I produced the following argument instead, and now it seems to prove far more than I want. I'd like to know where it goes wrong.

(1) A rational agent selects A over B only if she has some reason favoring A over B. (2) Whenever a rational agent is actually offered A and B, she eventually selects one of them — real agents don't starve between the haystacks. (3) So whenever a rational agent faces two options, there is a reason favoring one of them. (4) But having a reason favoring A over B just is preferring A to B. (5) So a rational agent is never genuinely indifferent between her options; every apparent tie conceals a real preference. (6) But then indifference does no work at all: every time I say I'm indifferent between the two soups on the shelf there is supposedly a genuine preference lurking in me, and my reaching for the left one proves it. That makes preference something an agent can never be wrong about and never fail to have, and it makes "she chose it because she preferred it" completely empty, since the choosing would be the only evidence the preference could ever have. Wich can't be right.

The step I most want to keep is (2), since it seems like plain observation of how people behave, and I'd also hate to give up the idea that choosing-because-you-prefer explains anything. So tell me which premise to reject, why it looked so innocent in the first place, and what it costs to reject the steps on either side of it instead. And as a separate thing I'm curious about: does my pencil analogy do any real work here, or is a symmetry broken by random fluctuation just the ass picking at random — wich is the very thing the argument above is suspicious of?
