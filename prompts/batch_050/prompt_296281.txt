So I'm a PhD student in aesthetics and I've been stuck in the imaginative resistance literature for months now for my diss, and I genuinely can't tell whether this debate is going anywhere. Let me lay out how I see it and you tell me what kind of disagreement this even is.

The setup (as I understand it): since Hume, people have noticed that readers will happily go along with fiction that wildly violates physics or biology -- time travel, talking animals, whatever -- but balk when a story flatly presents some clearly appalling moral claim as true, like a story that just states, as part of the fictional world, that a cruel act was right and good. Gendler called this the puzzle of imaginative resistance and there's been a huge debate since. Roughly, I see two camps. The "can't" camp says we literally cannot imagine morally deviant worlds the way we imagine physically deviant ones -- because imagining is somehow belief-like, or because moral claims in fiction export to the real world in a way descriptive claims don't, or because we can't grasp what a world with inverted morality would even be. The "won't" camp says we could imagine it fine, we just refuse, out of discomfort or because going along with the fiction would feel like endorsing something. Then there's a third cluster saying the whole thing is about genre conventions, story authority, or what the author is allowed to "establish" as true in the fiction, not about imagination at all.

My working hypothesis, which I want stress-tested: the camps are mostly talking past each other because "imagining" is doing different work in each. The can't camp seems to mean something thin, like entertaining or supposing within the fiction. The won't camp seems to mean something thick, like fully immersing yourself, simulating it, feeling it from the inside. And the third camp has left the psychology entirely and is doing theory of fiction. If that's right, the disagreement is at least partly verbal and the empirical studies people have run don't settle it because they don't control for which sense of imagining subjects are using.

So my questions: (1) Is that diagnosis right, based on what the sides actually argue -- or is there a real substantive disagreement underneath, and if so is it about the nature of imagination, about the normativity of fiction, or about something else? (2) What would progress even look like here? A sharper distinction between kinds of imagining? Better experimental design? Or should we just split the puzzle into three separate questions and drop the unified label? Please don't give me a lit review, I have that. I want the diagnosis and an honest verdict on whether this debate is stuck because it's confused or stuck because it's hard.
