In March I am giving a single fifty-minute lecture introducing Zorn's lemma to second-year mathematics undergraduates. They are at ease with partial orders, functions, and proof by induction, but they have never seen the axiom of choice stated formally, and every vector space they have met has been finite-dimensional. Plan the lecture for me; do not deliver it. I want the order in which the pieces must be introduced — chain, upper bound, maximal element, the statement itself, the first application — with the dependency reasons for that order, and an explicit decision on whether to let a naive greedy construction be attempted first and seen to stall, and if so exactly where it should stall. Choose the one application that will carry the session, adjudicating between every vector space having a basis and every commutative ring with unity having a maximal ideal, bearing in mind that the basis result is trivial in all the vector spaces they know, whereas a maximal ideal can be exhibited by hand in the few rings they know, so that either example risks making the lemma look superfluous unless the plan handles this directly. The application should also teach the one technical habit I care about: forming the union of a chain of partial objects and verifying that the union remains a partial object. Map the misconceptions I should expect — that the lemma constructs the maximal element, that maximal means greatest, that the chain condition comes for free, that the word lemma signals a minor technicality — and indicate the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Finally, give me one closing question that tests whether they can verify the chain condition in an unfamiliar poset rather than recite the statement, and tell me what I should openly defer, and say that I am deferring: the proof from the axiom of choice, and the equivalence with the well-ordering theorem.
