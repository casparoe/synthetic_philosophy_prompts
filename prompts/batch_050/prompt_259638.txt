A colleague in my department has been pressing an argument that the ordinary concept of truth is beyond repair, and although the conclusion strikes me as manifestly false, I have been unable to identify the step I reject. I would like a precise diagnosis. The argument runs as follows.

(1) The English predicate "is true" is governed by the unrestricted T-schema: for any declarative sentence S, "S" is true if and only if S. This is not an empirical hypothesis about truth; it is what the predicate means. Giving it up would leave us without any account of what speakers are doing when they call something true.

(2) English permits self-referential truth ascriptions without any special apparatus. Indexicals, definite descriptions, and ordinary quantification suffice: someone can write "the sole sentence on the seminar room board is not true," and it can be a contingent empirical fact that this is the sole sentence so written. No diagonalization machinery is required.

(3) Given (1) and (2), classical reasoning yields a contradiction: the board sentence is true if and only if it is not true.

(4) From a contradiction, anything follows. So the semantic theory constituted by (1) and (2) — our best theory of what English speakers mean — is trivial: it classifies every sentence as true.

(5) A practice whose constitutive theory is trivial is itself bankrupt. If our best account of assertion, denial, and truth-ascription collapses into triviality, then there is no fact of the matter about what anyone has ever asserted or denied, and the apparent successes of science, law, and ordinary communication are, strictly speaking, illusory.

(6) But those successes are not illusory. So the argument must fail somewhere.

(7) The standard repairs do not locate the failure. Hierarchical approaches deny (2), but self-reference of the relevant kind arises contingently, through perfectly ordinary devices, so banning it means legislating against sentences whose status depends on empirical facts no speaker can verify in advance — a point associated with Kripke. Gap approaches say the board sentence is neither true nor false; but then it is, in particular, not true, which is exactly what it says of itself, so it is true after all, and the contradiction returns. Glut approaches accept the contradiction and block (4), but only by abandoning the inference from inconsistency to triviality that ordinary mathematical and scientific reasoning takes for granted.

(8) So one of (1), (4), or (5) must go, and each looks non-negotiable: (1) is definitional, (4) is logic, and (5) merely states that a theory is answerable to the practice it theorizes.

I accept that the argument fails — I do not believe (5)'s conclusion — but I cannot tell where the break is: whether something slips in the move from a trivial theory to a bankrupt practice, whether the derivation itself misuses the T-schema, or whether (7) dismisses one of the repairs too quickly. If a repair survives scrutiny, I want to know which one and what its advocates quietly concede. My preference, if I must rank the premises, is to keep (6) and the ordinary usefulness of truth-talk at any cost; beyond that I am open. Please name the step you reject, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives.
