So I've just inherited a small research program — three people, two years, purely theoretical work, no experiments — on the problem of agents that can trust their own reasoning: Löb's theorem, the tiling-agents stuff, everything around it. The person who ran it before me left a note claiming the whole problem has been mis-carved. Her split is: (a) trusting your own future reasoning, where you stay roughly the same kind of reasoner over time, (b) trusting a successor you deliberately built to be smarter than you, and (c) trusting reasoning you can check but not follow — where you can verify each step of a proof without being able to survey why the proof as a whole exists or whether the conclusion is one you'd endorse. Her claim is that (c) is where the proof-theoretic results actually bite, and that (a) and (b) only look impossible because people keep confusing them with (c). I genuinely don't know if that decomposition is right, and honestly figuring that out is part of what I'd be paying you for.

So: what are the real open questions in this area, and which two or three should the team prioritize, in what order? For each one, I want to know what's downstream of it — which other questions get easier, or dissolve, if it's answered — and how I'd know within the first six months whether it's tractable or a dead end. There are also a bunch of proposed escape routes floating around: bound the length of proofs the agent is allowed to check, switch to probabilistic or approximate notions of belief instead of proof, or just refuse to let the agent model itself precisely. Which of these actually engage the hard case and which are quietly changing the subject? If your honest answer is that the whole obstacle dissolves once you give up exact self-modeling, that's fine, but argue for it — don't just assert it and move on.

One constraint on the prioritization: I care about this because someone will eventually want to build systems that can safely be modified, copied, or replaced by their own successors, so weight "does answering this matter for actually constructing an artifact" above "is this a pretty result in proof theory." But if you think that weighting is itself the mistake — that the pure, seemingly useless questions are where the leverage is — then say so and defend that instead. Also flag any question on your list where you suspect the literature has already implicitly answered it but nobody has written down the argument cleanly, since those might be three-month paper-writing jobs rather than two-year research jobs.
