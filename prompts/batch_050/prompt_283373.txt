I've gotten pretty attached to a strong reading of Kant's antinomies of pure reason, and I want to stress-test it. Roughly my position: when reason tries to settle the big cosmological questions by pure argument alone -- did the world have a beginning in time and a boundary in space, is matter infinitely divisible or composed of simples, is there any causality other than the kind governed by natural law, does anything exist necessarily -- it can prove both sides of each question with apparently equal force. Kant takes this not as a problem with one of the proofs, but as a symptom: both arguments share the hidden assumption that the world as it is in itself is a determinate, complete totality that these questions are genuinely *about*. Drop that assumption, treat the world as it appears as something constituted by our forms of experience rather than as a mind-independent whole, and the contradictions dissolve -- some questions turn out to have no fact of the matter, and the freedom one turns out to be resolvable once appearances and things in themselves are distinguished. My position is basically: this argument works, the antinomies are real contradictions and not just clever sophisms, and transcendental idealism (or something structurally like it) is the *only* way out, not one option among several.

What would count against that? I don't want a summary of Kant scholarship, I want a map of where this view is actually exposed. Which specific steps in the thesis and antithesis proofs could fail, and what would mathematics or physics have to look like for them to fail (e.g., would a discrete model of spacetime just hand the antithesis of the second antinomy a win, or does Kant not care about empirical results here)? Could a straight realist -- someone who thinks the world in itself is fully determinate -- resolve one or more antinomies without idealism, and if so, which ones are the soft targets? Is the resolution of the freedom antinomy doing real work or just relocating the contradiction into the appearance/in-itself distinction? And on the meta-level: if transcendental idealism itself turns out to be incoherent or unstable (the old objection that Kant can't say the subject *in itself* does the constituting), does the whole antinomy-based argument for it collapse, or does it survive as an independent puzzle about reason?

Please sort the defeaters from fatal to merely annoying, and for each say whether a committed defender of my position would concede it as a genuine threat or dismiss it as a misunderstanding -- I'm interested in what the view *itself* risks, not just what its critics throw at it. Also: if it turns out the position only survives by making the antinomies into artifacts of Kant's 18th-century assumptions (Euclidean space, Newtonian absolute time, pre-modern logic), tell me that plainly, because that's the defeat I'd find hardest to swallow but probably the most important.
