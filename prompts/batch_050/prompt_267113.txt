I'm writing a term paper for an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of belief — we've spent the term on the James–Clifford dispute, and my thesis was going to be a qualified defense of Clifford. A classmate who enjoys playing devil's advocate showed me the following argument, and I've been unable to find the exit. It seems to show that Clifford's whole project is empty, which can't be right, since cases like his negligent shipowner — the man who stifles his doubts and sincerely convinces himself his vessel is seaworthy before it goes down with all aboard — still look like genuine moral failures. Here is the argument:

(1) A person is morally responsible only for what is under her voluntary control.
(2) Believing is not under our direct voluntary control: you cannot simply decide to believe the ship is seaworthy, the way you can decide to raise your arm.
(3) So a belief is morally assessable only insofar as it traces back to voluntary conduct that produced or sustained it — choosing to investigate, choosing to stop investigating, choosing which sources to trust.
(4) But every such choice is itself guided by beliefs: the choice to stop investigating rests on a belief that one has looked hard enough, or that further evidence is unlikely to matter.
(5) By (1)–(3), those guiding beliefs are morally assessable only insofar as they trace back to still earlier voluntary choices, which were guided by still earlier beliefs.
(6) Tracing any belief's pedigree in this way, the chain terminates only in beliefs formed in childhood or by brute psychology — things no one chose and for which, by (1), no one is responsible.
(7) Therefore no belief is ever a fit object of moral assessment: Clifford's principle imposes duties that no one can be responsible for violating, and his shipowner did nothing morally wrong in believing as he did.

The conclusion has to be wrong — or so I keep insisting — but each premise seems like something I already accept. (1) is just the control condition on responsibility that I thought everyone granted; (2) seems like a plain psychological fact; and the bookkeeping in (4)–(5) strikes me as honest rather than tricky. My classmate says the argument is valid and I must simply pay the price and abandon the ethics of belief. I would rather keep the intuition about the shipowner than keep any particular premise, but I don't want to win by fiat. Which step should I reject, why did it look innocuous, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I'd like to know how the main ways out — denying the control condition itself, claiming that indirect doxastic control is enough for responsibility, or holding that responsibility for a belief can be inherited from an earlier culpable act or omission even when the agent never adverted to the risk — differ in what they commit me to, and whether any of them preserves enough of Clifford to make my paper worth writing as planned.
