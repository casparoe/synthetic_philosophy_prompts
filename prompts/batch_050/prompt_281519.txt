I've been keeping score on how moral norms actually die, and I want to know what theory my case-by-case verdicts commit me to. The Atlantic slave trade: I think abolition is a genuine win for moral argument -- decades of pamphlets, parliamentary testimony, and sugar boycotts really did move people who had no self-interested reason to budge. Dueling among English gentlemen: the arguments against it -- that it is irrational, unchristian, and no test of anyone's innocence -- were stated eloquently for two centuries and accomplished nothing; the practice collapsed within a generation once killing a man in a duel stopped counting as proof of honor. My verdict there is that argument was impotent and only the honor code mattered. Footbinding in China: resisted for a millennium, ended in a few decades once reformers recast it as a national humiliation rather than a local custom -- honor again, not argument. Smoking in America: declined mainly on health information, price, and regulation -- interests, not morality -- and I suspect explicit moralizing about smokers slightly backfired. And here is the pair I cannot reconcile: I insist the anti-slavery arguments mattered, yet the anti-dueling arguments were older, cleaner, and aimed at a practice with far weaker interests behind it, and they failed completely. My tentative repair is that moral argument works only as an enabling condition -- it gives wavering elites a respectable reason to switch sides once honor or interest has loosened the norm, but it never loosens the norm itself. Test that principle against my five verdicts, repair it if it cannot save them all, and say which verdict you would sacrifice if forced. Then apply the repaired principle to two cases I haven't listed: the ongoing decline of corporal punishment of children, and the future of boxing and mixed martial arts -- for each, should reformers invest in moral argument, in shifting honor, or in interests?
