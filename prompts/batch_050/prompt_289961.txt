Write a formal cease-and-desist letter, roughly 600–700 words, from an invented law firm representing a client's earlier self. The facts: a man of seventy, recently diagnosed with early-stage dementia, has announced his intention to revoke the advance directive he signed at forty-five, which refuses certain life-prolonging treatments and appoints his daughter as attorney-in-fact once he can no longer manage his affairs. His forty-five-year-old self, through counsel, objects. The letter is addressed to the man's present self, demands that he cease all steps toward revocation, and is signed by the firm both selves retained decades ago -- which means the letter must also contain a stiff paragraph addressing the firm's obvious conflict of interest and why the earlier self's retainer takes priority.

The letter should press the strongest available case for the earlier self's authority and should press it seriously: the doctrine of precedent autonomy; the Ulysses logic of binding oneself in advance against a future one can foresee but not trust; the claim that the present self's sudden warmth toward continued treatment is itself exactly the symptom the directive anticipated, and therefore evidence for the directive rather than against it; the distinction between the critical interests a person has in the shape of their whole life and the merely experiential comforts of whoever happens to be living it now. The humor should arise from the collision of genre and metaphysics -- deadpan legalese wielded in a dispute between two tenants of one body, an enclosure of the original 1998 directive as Exhibit A, a discovery request for the present self's recent diary entries as proof of changed values, a schedule of proposed remedies that no court could grant.

The satire must land, but never state, the standard objections: whether the signer and the current patient are the same person in the sense that matters, whose interests are actually at stake when the person who must live with the consequences now wants something different, and what it means for a self to litigate against itself. Stay in register throughout -- no winking, no aside acknowledging the absurdity. A reader who knows the advance-directive literature should recognize the dispute faithfully rendered; a reader who doesn't should still find the letter funny and come away feeling the genuine pull of both sides.
