So my reading group has spent like three sessions on the frame problem and we've settled into two camps that keep talking past each other. Camp one (the engineers, roughly) says the frame problem was only ever a practical challenge: figuring out what stays the same and what's relevant is a matter of clever indexing, default assumptions, learned models of the world, good heuristics for noticing what changed, and modern AI systems — including LLM-based agents that chug along fine without anyone hand-coding axioms about what doesn't change — show the problem is basically licked, or at least dissolves with scale. Camp two (the philosophers) says the engineering successes are beside the point: the frame problem was never about search speed or storage, it's about what it *is* for a system to grasp relevance at all, and any system that works by manipulating explicit representations will hit the wall eventually — the new systems just hide the wall better because they're trained on human text, which smuggles in human relevance judgments.

One more wrinkle: someone in the group proposed a concrete test they call the "silent Tuesday test" — you let an autonomous agent run in a rich simulated world for months of subjective time, you change exactly one thing in the world every day (sometimes causally relevant to the agent's current goals, sometimes not), and you measure whether the agent's downstream behavior tracks only the relevant changes. Camp one says passing this test would be strong evidence the frame problem is an engineering matter; camp two says the test is rigged from the start because the designers decided in advance which changes count as relevant, so the agent is being graded against someone else's relevance judgments rather than demonstrating its own.
