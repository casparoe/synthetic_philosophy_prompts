A senior colleague on my team keeps shutting down arguments about modeling choices with "relax, the data will swamp the prior." Most recently it was to wave off my worry that the prior in our demand-forecasting model basically encodes his gut feeling that demand is seasonal, when I think the effect might be mostly noise. His attitude seems to be that once we've collected enough observations, whatever prior we started with becomes irrelevant, so arguing about priors is a waste of time.

The thing is, I'm pretty sure "the data swamps the prior" is doing a lot more work than any single theorem actually licenses. Can you unpack what precise claims that slogan could be making? I can think of a few candidate readings -- e.g., something about the likelihood dominating the posterior as n grows, something about two agents with different priors converging to the same beliefs, something about the posterior concentrating on the truth -- but I suspect these are genuinely different theses with different conditions attached, and I want them seperated out properly.

For each reading, I'd like: (a) the claim stated carefully, (b) whether it's a triviality, a real theorem, a heuristic that's roughly right in nice cases, or just false, (c) what conditions it secretly needs -- I'm thinking things like whether the true model has to be in your model class, whether both people's priors have to assign nonzero probability to the same stuff, whether the data has to be iid, whether "eventually" means anything about the next 50 datapoints or the next 50 million, and (d) whether that reading, if true, would actually justify my colleague's shrug in our specific dispute, where we're working with maybe a few thousand observations and a model neither of us thinks is literally correct.

Please also be upfront about which parts of this are settled mathematics (like, actual theorems with proofs) versus which parts are genuinely contested among statisticians -- e.g. whether asymptotic convergence results say anything useful about real finite-sample practice, or whether the subjectivist "priors don't matter in the limit" line is something everyone accepts or mostly a talking point. Don't present the contested stuff as consensus just because it's repeated confidently at standup meetings.
