I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, conceived through anonymous sperm donation, discovers in middle age that the clinic's records were unreliable: her mother selected one donor, but a paperwork error may mean the actual donor was a different man entirely. The emotional architecture of the novel turns on a scene in which she asks herself: if the mistake had run the other way — if the technician had reached for a different vial that morning — would I still be here, or would someone else exist in my place? I want her interior monologue to be philosophically literate rather than a seductive muddle, so before I draft it I need to know whether her question has a determinate answer, and which way the answer leans.

You are a professional philosopher with expertise in the metaphysics of modality and personal identity, thoroughly familiar with Kripke's argument in Naming and Necessity that biological origins are essential to the organisms that have them, and with the objections and refinements that argument has attracted since. Think carefully before answering. Begin by brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side of the question, and only then settle on numbers.

Here is the proposition I want your credence on: it is metaphysically impossible for an actual human person — the very person now reading this sentence, say — to have originated from a numerically different sperm-and-egg pair than the pair from which that person in fact originated. Give me your credence that this is true, and decompose the reasoning rather than offering a bare number. At minimum, assign a rough probability to each of the following sub-questions, with a line of justification for each:

1. The probability that Kripke-style necessity-of-origins reasoning succeeds for organisms generally — that the appeal to how we would describe a counterfactual situation, rather than any observation of the thing itself, really does secure an essentialist conclusion rather than trading on an intuition that could be read either way.
2. The probability that a person is identical to a human organism whose history traces back to a particular zygote, as opposed to a psychological continuant or some other entity whose modal profile might differ — since origins essentialism for organisms transfers to persons only if persons are organisms in the relevant sense.
3. The probability that the standard trouble cases — monozygotic twinning, chimerism, and the common intuition that one could have been born earlier or later to the same parents — genuinely threaten the thesis rather than merely complicating its formulation.
4. The probability that a de re modal claim of this kind has a determinate truth value at all, as opposed to suffering from semantic indecision, in which case the sensible credence would be something like the proportion of legitimate precisifications on which it comes out true.

Then show me how these combine — whether roughly multiplicatively, whether you treat any of them as dependent on others — and tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or thought experiment would move that term most. Finally, the practical payoff: given your number, advise me whether the character's settled conviction that a different vial would simply have meant her nonexistence should be written as a genuine insight she arrives at, or as an understandable error the novel quietly corrects.
