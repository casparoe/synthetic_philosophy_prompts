I teach intermediate logic alongside philosophy of language, and I am preparing a lecture that presents Brandom's inferentialism to students who are fluent in natural deduction but have read no pragmatist philosophy of language. I want a genuine translation, not a suggestive analogy, so I would like your help constructing it.

Restate the inferentialist account of conceptual content — the thesis that an expression's meaning is constituted by its role in inference, by the commitments one undertakes in applying it and the entitlements those commitments inherit, rather than by any representational relation to the world — in the vocabulary my students command: introduction and elimination rules, harmony and stability between them, conservative extension, cut elimination, and the idea that the rules of a system fix the meanings of its connectives rather than the other way around. The translation should address at least the following: (i) the inferentialist reading of the logical constants, where the proof-theoretic gloss seems most natural; (ii) the extension of the doctrine beyond logical vocabulary to ordinary empirical and observational predicates — what plays the role of introduction and elimination rules for a term like "acid" or "crimson," and is the proof-theoretic notion of a rule even the right kind of thing here; (iii) Arthur Prior's tonk as the consistency problem internal to this whole approach, and what constraint on admissible rule-sets corresponds to whatever prevents inferentialism about empirical vocabulary from collapsing into a similar anarchy.

Then give me the residue report. Which components of the position translate cleanly into proof-theoretic vocabulary; which translate only with distortion — state the distortion explicitly; and which have no proof-theoretic counterpart at all? As candidates for the last two categories, consider at minimum: the distinction between material and formal inference (natural deduction only recognizes formally valid steps, whereas the inferentialist takes material inferences like the move from "the coin is copper" to "the coin conducts electricity" to be content-constituting); the social and normative dimension of the view (scorekeeping, the interlocking structure of commitment and entitlement between speakers); and the order-of-explanation claim that representational locutions should be understood in terms of inferential practice rather than conversely. For each item of residue, I want a verdict: does its untranslatability expose a limitation of proof-theoretic semantics as a framework, or does it instead suggest that this part of the inferentialist position was doing less work than its rhetoric suggests?

Assume my students know the statement of the cut-elimination theorem and the tonk example, but nothing about scorekeeping or the commitment/entitlement distinction. The lecture is fifty minutes; flag anything in your translation that you expect them to resist, and tell me which objection I should invite rather than preempt.
