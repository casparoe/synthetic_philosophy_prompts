I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about the longevity movement -- cryonics people, life-extension researchers, death-positivity hospice folks, the whole scene. In interview after interview some version of the same ancient one-liner keeps getting deployed at me, and I've realized I can't tell whether my sources are all saying the same thing or like four different things. It's from Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus: "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not."

Here's where I've seen it do work. A cryonics skeptic quoted it to argue that wanting more time is itself a kind of confusion, so freezing yourself is not just expensive but philosophically misguided. A hospice doctor used it to argue that her patients shouldn't fear being dead, though she conspicuously did not say they shouldn't mind dying sooner rather than later. A philosopher friend says it strictly proves only that fearing the state of being dead is irrational, and proves nothing at all about whether dying young is bad for you. And a stoicism-adjacent influencer used it to mean something like "death is only bad if you experience it as bad, so don't."

What I want is a menu, not an essay. What are the distinct precise theses hiding in that one sentence? I'm guessing they range from something nearly tautological (you can't feel anything once you're dead, so death isn't painful) up through the timing thing (there's no moment at which death is bad for you, since before it you're alive and after it you don't exist) to the really strong claim (being deprived of future life cannot be a harm at all, full stop). For each reading I want to know: what does it assume (in particular, does it quietly depend on the idea that only experienced sensations can be good or bad, and does it survive if you drop that and accept that you can be harmed by things you never experience, like being defamed after your death)? Is it a platitude, genuinely defensible, or just false? Which of my interviewees' uses does it actually support, if any? And does any version of the argument get the cryonics skeptic his conclusion, or is he smuggling in extra premises about what makes life worth continuing? If you think the strongest defensible reading still leaves deprivationism (dying early is bad because of the good life it cuts off) standing, say so plainly.

Please give me the output as a JSON object with this exact shape: a "readings" array where each entry has fields "reading_name" (a short label), "precise_claim" (the thesis stated as a proposition), "key_assumption" (what it needs to be true, especially any dependence on experience-requirement stuff), "verdict" (one of "platitude", "defensible", "defensible-but-narrow", or "false", plus a sentence of justification), "supports_which_use" (which of my four interviewees it would actually vindicate, or "none"), and "weakest_point" (where an objector should push). Then a field "best_case_for_the_line" summarizing the strongest single reading in two sentences, a field "overall_confidence" (low/medium/high with a sentence on why), and a field "least_certain_point" naming the one claim in your answer you're least sure of and why. Don't soften anything to protect Epicurus -- I'd rather have a harsh verdict than a diplomatic one, and I'd rather you flag genuine uncertainty than paper over it.
