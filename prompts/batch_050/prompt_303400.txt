Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about grief. She had recently lost her father, and she accused me of treating sadness at a death as a kind of intellectual failure -- as if understanding that everything transforms should dissolve mourning altogether. I insisted I was only describing an attitude I aspire to, not one I think is obligatory. Neither of us could get the other to budge, and it made me realize how badly I want to put the question to someone who has actually thought about it for centuries. So: my university's philosophy society is staging one of its "living interviews," in which a scholar portrays a historical philosopher and answers questions in character, and this term's subject is Zhuangzi. I have been asked to draft the interviewer's questions. Please write eight to ten questions, in the order I should ask them, aimed not at getting him to retell familiar stories but at pressing on the tensions in his views on death. I want at least one question on why he sang and drummed on a basin when his wife died, and whether he thinks that reaction is a demand on everyone or merely a report of where he happened to arrive; at least one on why, if death is just one more transformation to be followed with equanimity, he was reportedly stricken at the grave of Hui Shi and said he no longer had anyone to work with -- is the grief of losing a friend exempt from the teaching, or did he fail his own teaching there; and at least one on his remark, when his disciples worried about leaving his corpse exposed, that being eaten by crows above ground or ants below ground was robbery of one to favor the other -- I want to know whether that ease about his own corpse extends to the corpses of people he loves, or whether there is an asymmetry he never addressed. For each question, anticipate the answer he is most likely to give in character, and supply a follow-up question designed to push past that first answer. I would also like the sequence to close with a question about what he would say to my friend -- someone in fresh, raw grief -- that is neither a scolding nor a concession that his whole outlook collapses in the presence of actual bereavement. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold text, and no tables. The questions and their follow-ups can simply run on in paragraphs, in the order I should ask them.
