I serve on the five-member allocation board of a river basin authority in the American West, and our enabling statute is unusually thin: it instructs us to grant diversion rights in unclaimed streamflow according to, in the legislature's words, natural principles of just acquisition. Our general counsel has told us that, absent more guidance, a Lockean framework is the most defensible reading of that clause, and in preparing for this I've been working through the chapter on property in Locke's Second Treatise. We hear roughly fifteen applications per monthly meeting; decisions are appealable to state court, so the reasoning has to be reproducible in writing, and I have about half an hour of staff time per application before the board votes. I would like you to turn Locke's theory of acquisition -- the labour-mixing account of how unowned things become property, the proviso that enough and as good be left for others, and the limitation against taking more than one can use -- into a decision procedure my staff can actually run on each application.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions with, for each, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, where a failed answer terminates the application in a denial, and where the question is one the procedure cannot settle and must instead be flagged for board judgment. Please do not sand down the genuinely hard knots in Locke's theory -- translate them into operational tests instead. In particular: how should we treat labour performed by employees or contractors rather than by the applicant personally (Locke himself seems to count the turfs one's servant has cut, but is there a principled line between hiring labour and simply buying a claim)? Does surveying, fencing, or filing paperwork count as mixing one's labour, or must the resource itself be altered or improved, and if improvement is required, how do we handle applicants whose alterations arguably make things worse? On the proviso, the procedure must choose between, or at least sharply distinguish, a strict reading (roughly equal shares left for everyone else) and the weaker reading associated with Nozick (no one left worse off than they would have been had the resource remained in common) -- state which reading the procedure adopts at each step, what baseline it measures against, and what it costs. And the use limitation needs teeth: what do we do with claims held for speculative future development rather than present use? Wherever Locke's text is genuinely indeterminate and the procedure simply has to pick a side, say so explicitly rather than pretending the theory settles it.

Then run the procedure on three applications representative of our docket. First, a large agribusiness seeking rights to roughly forty percent of the remaining unclaimed flow, arguing that its high-efficiency irrigation will leave downstream users no worse off in effective terms than the status quo. Second, a homesteader who has been informally diverting a modest amount for twelve years without a permit to sustain a small orchard -- sympathetic on labour grounds, but with no paper trail and a basin that is nearly fully appropriated. Third, a development company seeking a large claim now for a reservoir it does not intend to build for at least a decade. Please carry each case through every step of the procedure to a verdict, and include at least one case where the procedure delivers an outcome the board will find politically unpalatable -- I want to see where the logic leads even when we won't like it, not a procedure reverse-engineered to reach comfortable answers. Finally, close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: which recurring fact patterns will expose its limits, and where we should expect a court to find our Lockean reasoning underdetermined.
