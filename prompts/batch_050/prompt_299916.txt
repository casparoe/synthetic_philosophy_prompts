Take the mid-90s fight over the placebo-controlled trials of short-course AZT to prevent mother-to-child HIV transmission, and run it through the concept of clinical equipoise. Quick recap so we're on the same page: the ACTG 076 trial had just shown that a full zidovudine regimen -- given during pregnancy, during labor, and then to the newborn -- cut transmission by about two thirds. After that, trials in Thailand, Côte d'Ivoire and elsewhere tested a much shorter, cheaper AZT course against placebo in pregnant women. Lurie and Wolfe's 1997 NEJM editorial called the placebo arms unethical and famously reached for the Tuskegee comparison; the NIH and CDC side defended the design on the grounds that the 076 regimen was unaffordable and logistically impossible where these trials were run, so the women getting placebo weren't being denied anything actually available to them, and an active-control design against 076 wouldn't have answered the question that mattered locally, i.e. whether a cheap regimen worked at all.

Here's what I want:

1. Equipoise on Freedman's original formulation is a fact about the expert community, not the individual investigator. Who's the relevant community here -- the global community of HIV researchers, or clinicians and researchers in the host countries? Work out whether equipoise held on each reading, and notice whether the defenders and the critics were implicitly assuming different communities.

2. The critics' real move, I think, wasn't "equipoise was absent" so much as "you picked the wrong comparison": once 076 worked, the honest trial was short-course vs. 076, not short-course vs. nothing. Is that a disagreement about what equipoise requires, or is it a disagreement about something equipose theory just doesn't cover, like which question a trial is allowed to ask? Say which.

3. Run the episode through both the placebo-orthodoxy and the active-control-orthodoxy positions. What does each one say the investigators should have done? Which one were the trials' defenders actually relying on -- or were they smuggling in a third consideration (responsiveness to local health needs) that belongs to neither?

4. The two-direction part: the short course turned out to work, roughly halving transmission, and defenders treat that outcome as vindication -- the trials changed practice in exactly the places a 076-controlled trial would've been useless. Does the episode confirm equipoise as the right standard, show that it's the wrong tool for international research and that standard-of-care / responsiveness considerations were doing all the real work, or show that equipoise talk mainly functions as a way to re-describe a standard-of-care judgment so it looks like a methodological one? Take a position.

5. Wherever the framework is just silent -- e.g. on whether the women in the placebo arms were wronged even if equipoise genuinely held -- say it's silent rather than squeezing an answer out of it.
