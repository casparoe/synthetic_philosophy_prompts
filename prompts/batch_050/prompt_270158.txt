I put this argument together after sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar, and it now worries me because it seems to prove far too much. I'm a graduate student in economics, so if it works, it works against my own discipline.

(1) Utility is a purely representational device: if a utility function U represents an agent's preferences, so does any strictly increasing transformation of U, so nothing in the agent corresponds to the size of utility numbers or the differences between them.
(2) Preferences, in turn, are just what choices reveal: to say an agent prefers x to y is to say she would choose x when both are on offer. This is precisely what the revealed preference approach was supposed to secure -- preference talk with no unobservable mental states behind it.
(3) So "she chose x because she preferred x to y" unpacks as "she chose x because she is disposed to choose x when offered it." The explanans is a restatement of the explanandum; the explanation is circular.
(4) Moreover, since any suitably consistent pattern of choices admits some utility representation, no pattern of behavior could ever falsify rational choice theory, and an unfalsifiable theory explains nothing.
(5) Therefore utility talk is explanatorily empty, and economists should either stop explaining behavior or reduce their explanations to the psychological and physiological mechanisms that actually produce it.

The conclusion is absurd. By parity of reasoning, "the apple accelerated because a gravitational force acted on it" would be equally circular, since our evidence for the force just is how things accelerate -- and microeconomics plainly has taught us non-obvious things about behavior. Yet every step looks like a truism from the first weeks of a micro theory course. I would rather surrender any single premise than accept (5); (2) is the one I'm most attached to, since it's what I took the whole revealed preference program to mean.

Identify exactly where it goes wrong -- which premise or inference you reject -- and explain why that step looked innocent given what economists themselves say about utility. Then price the alternatives: what must an economist concede about the content of preference attributions in order to keep utility talk genuinely explanatory, and what would it cost instead to reject one of the other premises?
