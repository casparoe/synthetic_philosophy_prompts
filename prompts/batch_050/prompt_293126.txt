I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on population ethics and animal welfare, and I sit on the advisory board of a small certification body for farmed insects — mealworm and cricket producers who want a credible welfare label. The board keeps hitting the same wall. Any serious welfare consideration here runs into the scale argument: a single facility may house billions of larvae, so if there is even a modest probability that they are sentient, the expected moral weight dwarfs that of, say, a cattle herd. But nobody on the board thinks the probability is high, and half the board thinks that multiplying a tiny probability by an enormous number is exactly where expected-value reasoning goes off the rails.

I want a decision procedure the board can actually run on a welfare proposal — requiring particular slaughter methods, capping stocking densities, mandating handling standards — in a one-hour meeting, without hiring a philosopher. It should specify, in order:

1. How to fix a credence in sentience for the species at issue: what kinds of evidence move it, and whether the procedure works with a point estimate or a range.
2. How to combine that credence with the headcount to reach a decision — and, critically, where the procedure refuses to simply multiply. How does it handle the objection that this is a Pascalian setup in which the arithmetic always swamps the conclusion? Does it use a probability threshold, a cap, discounting, a precautionary rule, or something else, and what justifies the choice?
3. Which steps must be settled by the board as value judgments rather than delegated to the procedure — for instance, how much certainty a certification body needs before imposing real costs on producers.
4. The predictable failure modes: where a procedure like this misfires (double-counting the same probability across many small decisions, the fact that these species were chosen for farming partly because they were presumed insentient, and so on).

Then run the procedure twice: once on a proposal to mandate particular slaughter methods for mealworm larvae, and once on a case where it delivers a verdict the board will hate — where it endorses or blocks the cheap, sentience-agnostic option for reasons the producers will resist.

Be explicit about what would change the procedure's verdicts: which empirical finding about insect neurobiology or behavior, or which argument about expected-value reasoning under deep uncertainty, would flip each recommendation, and where the design is most vulnerable to breaking down. Keep the whole response under 900 words; if you run out of room, cut exposition rather than either worked case.
