Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about the place of historical analogies in foreign-policy reasoning, and we ended at an impasse I’d like help dissolving. My side of the argument was that the famous “lessons of history” do far less epistemic work than their invokers assume. The lesson of Munich — that accommodation emboldens aggressors — gets applied to nearly every confrontation, while the lesson of Vietnam — that limited interventions become open-ended quagmires — gets applied to nearly every deployment, and the two lessons pull in opposite directions, with no principle offered for which one governs a given case. Meanwhile the failed analogies (every crisis declared “another Munich” that wasn’t) are quietly forgotten, so the track record of lesson-drawing is impossible to assess from the cases anyone bothers to remember. My friend eventually fell back on the familiar line that history doesn’t repeat itself, but it rhymes — treating this as a considered reply that concedes my point about literal recurrence while preserving the legitimacy of analogical reasoning. I replied that the sentence is exactly vague enough to survive any objection, and he replied that its vagueness is just what a truth about something as messy as history ought to look like.

What I’d like is a disambiguation, not a tiebreaker in the personal dispute. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that “history doesn’t repeat itself, but it rhymes” could be expressing. Some candidates I can half-see: a trivial claim that events never recur in every detail; a substantive metaphysical claim that the drivers of human affairs — fear, ambition, institutional incentives, the security dilemma — are stable enough that structurally similar situations recur; an epistemological claim that analogies from past cases carry genuine evidential weight, perhaps more than any rival source of evidence available to policymakers; a psychological claim about us rather than about history, namely that we are inveterate pattern-matchers who impose rhyme schemes on noise and then mistake the pattern for a datum; and a methodological claim that analogical reasoning is indispensable but systematically unreliable, so that it should be used with explicit correction for selection effects. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or actually false — and, crucially, what the operative notion of a “rhyme” would have to be for the reading to be testable at all: how would one measure the rhyme rate, and what would the world look like if the claim were wrong? Finally, tell me which of these readings my friend’s use of the line actually requires. It matters to me whether the reading that licenses his conclusion is one of the plausible ones, or whether the slogan functions the way I suspect — as a shape that shifts between a modest truth when attacked and a strong evidential claim when deployed.
