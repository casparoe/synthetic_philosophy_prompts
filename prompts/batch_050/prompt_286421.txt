I run public engagement for a mid-sized city transit agency. Every month or two we get a proposal -- new bus lanes, a fare change, cutting a low-ridership route -- and I'm the one who designs how the public gets involved. My boss wants a repeatable process instead of me improvising each time, and I have maybe a week per proposal and no budget for big surveys.

I've been reading about John Dewey's idea that democracy should work like inquiry. Roughly: a "public" isn't a fixed group, it forms around the indirect consequences of what people do; policies should be treated as experiments, with hypotheses, feedback, and revision built in; experts know the means and the facts, but only the people living with the consequences can judge what actually matters to them; and deliberation isn't about finding the one right answer, it's about running smarter experiments over time.

Turn that into a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposal. The questions to ask in order: how do I figure out who the relevant public even is when a route change in one neighborhood pushes traffic into another? What counts as setting up a policy as an experiment rather than just doing it? When do experts get to overrule residents and when do they not? Say what a good enough answer to each step looks like, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand things off to elected officials because it's a value fight that inquiry can't settle. Flag where it breaks -- an organized minority packing every meeting, or a public that just never shows up.

Then run it on two of my actual cases: adding a bus-only lane on a commercial street whose shop owners hate it, and raising fares to close a budget gap. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
