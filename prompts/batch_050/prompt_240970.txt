As part of my dissertation on the ethics of war, I'm stress-testing the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants against a case I've constructed and haven't seen discussed. Consider Mina, a soldier fighting on the unjust side of a war. Unlike her comrades, she has seen through her government's propaganda and knows full well that the war is unjust. She remains in service because deserters are executed and because her unit — which includes her conscripted younger brother — would be left dangerously exposed without her. One night, just combatants launch a legitimate attack on her position; she fires back and kills two of them.

Write a dialogue between a traditionalist in Walzer's mold, who holds that Mina acts permissibly because combatants on both sides occupy the same moral position, and a revisionist in McMahan's mold, who holds that she does wrong because the just combatants she kills are not liable to be killed. Requirements: both interlocutors must be equally sharp, and neither side may be strawmanned. The revisionist must not merely assert that unjust combatants are liable, but must grapple with Mina's excuses (duress, protective intent) and with whether an excuse can do the moral work the traditionalist needs. The traditionalist, in turn, cannot rest on soldiers' typical epistemic limitations, since the case is built to remove that excuse, and must explain how knowingly killing non-liable people could nonetheless be permissible — or else concede the case and clarify what remains of the doctrine. The dialogue must turn on each side's actual replies, address at least the contested analogy (or disanalogy) between war and individual self-defense and the question whether Mina's protective intentions alter the status of her shots, and end at the genuine crux of the disagreement rather than a contrived reconciliation. Aim for roughly 800–1000 words.
