I'm trying to pin down what I actually think about knowing how versus knowing that, so here are my gut verdicts on some cases and I want to know what principle unifies them, if anything does. My piano teacher plays a trill flawlessly but when a student asks how, she just says "like this" and does it again -- she clearly knows how. A biomechanics professor can describe exactly how to ski, every weight shift, but falls over instantly on the bunny slope -- he knows that, not how. A gymnast with both arms in casts still knows how to do a backflip, I'd say, even though she couldn't do one to save her life right now. A beginner at darts closes his eyes and hits a bullseye on his first throw, can't come close on the next twenty -- he did it but he doesn't know how. My friend can wiggle his ears and has zero idea which muscles he's using -- knows how. And here's the pair I keep tripping over: the ski professor has ALL the propositional knowledge but lacks the know-how, while the gymnast keeps the know-how despite having lost the ability entirely, so apparently propositions aren't sufficient and ability isn't necessary -- which feels like it rules out both of the simple accounts at once. Is there one coherent principle behind all my verdicts, and if not, which one should I give up? Also tell me what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a swimmer who learned by imitation and sincerely believes she kicks from the knees, but on video she actually kicks from the hips -- does she know how to kick?
