I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of history, and each year we spend three weeks on historical materialism -- specifically on the claim that the economic 'base' explains the legal, political, and ideological 'superstructure'. Each year the discussion stalls in exactly the same place, and I have begun to suspect the stall is structural rather than accidental. On one side sit the students (and, in the literature, figures in the tradition of G. A. Cohen's defence of the primacy of the productive forces) who insist that functional explanation does real work here: the superstructure persists because of its stabilising effects on the relations of production, and this is a perfectly respectable explanatory pattern, as respectable as functional explanation in biology. On the other side sit students influenced by the analytical-Marxist critique associated with Jon Elster, who reply that a functional explanation without specified microfoundations -- mechanisms at the level of individual agents showing how the stabilising effects get selected for -- is not an explanation but a promissory note that was never redeemed. A third group then declares the whole framing dead, on the grounds that economic activity is itself already saturated with norms, language, and law, so that the base/superstructure distinction cannot even be drawn cleanly enough for the dispute between the first two groups to get going.

Here is where it gets frustrating: all three positions have been on the table, in substantially their current form, for around four decades. Nobody's mind changes. The Cohen-style defenders concede that microfoundations would be nice but deny they are mandatory; the critics concede that functional talk is sometimes heuristically fruitful but deny it explains; and the third group keeps insisting the first two are fighting over a distinction that dissolves on inspection. My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but at least two stacked on top of each other -- a genuine meta-theoretical disagreement about what explanatory sufficiency requires (which may be rationally resolvable only in the way that analogous disputes in biology were, or were not, resolved), sitting on top of a possibly merely verbal disagreement about what 'the economic' is allowed to include. But I am not confident in this diagnosis, and I would rather be argued out of it than have it confirmed.

So: please diagnose the disagreement, not adjudicate it. Using what each side actually says rather than a textbook summary of Marx, tell me whether this is a substantive dispute about explanation, a verbal one about the extension of 'base', a clash of bedrock intuitions about what social science owes us, or the sides answering different questions under one heading. If you think my two-layer hypothesis is wrong or incomplete, say so and offer a better structure. Then, conditional on your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a worked example of a successfully specified mechanism, a comparison with how functional explanation fared in evolutionary biology, or an argument that nothing could settle it because the question itself is ill-posed. I want the diagnosis to be sharp enough that I could use it to restructure the seminar next year -- for instance, by re-ordering the weeks so the meta-question about explanation comes first. Please write your answer in British English.
