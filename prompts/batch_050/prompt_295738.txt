I'm in a second-year epistemology course and we've just done the Meno problem — the road to Larissa bit, where Socrates points out that a mere true belief gets you there just as well as knowledge does, so why is knowledge worth more. The answers we've been offered are the usual ones: knowledge is tethered where true belief wanders off (the Daedalus statues), knowledge earns you credit, knowledge survives counterevidence better.

For my essay I want to run the problem backwards. Construct a thought experiment in which someone ends up *worse off* with knowledge than they would have been with the corresponding mere true belief. Constraints: realistic everyday setting, no demons or weird psychology; same person, same true belief, same stakes in both versions, with only the epistemic status varying; and no cheating by bundling baggage into the knowledge version — the knower can't also learn something traumatic in the process, or become arrogant for unrelated reasons. The disadvantage has to flow from whatever it is that seperates knowledge from mere true belief, and it has to fall out of the epistemology rather than being stipulated.

Then show the case works: walk through why the knower does worse, say which of the standard accounts of knowledge's value it threatens and which ones shrug it off, and name the single feature doing the work — if you remove it, does the case just collapse into one where the belief stops being true? Finish with the strongest counterattack a defender of the value of knowledge could make against it, and whether you think it lands.
