I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term on the unity of the proposition, and for our final meeting I'd like to open with a poem rather than a reading. Write a villanelle -- the full nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with the standard ABA rhyme and two repeating refrains -- about Bradley's regress and what it does to our picture of how propositions hold together. The two refrains should carry the two poles of the problem: one giving voice to the demand that any relation meant to bind two terms must itself be bound to those terms by further relations, so the binding never bottoms out; the other voicing the stubborn fact that propositions do seem to be unities -- that a sentence about two people and what holds between them says something complete rather than presenting a list of three items sitting side by side. Across the tercets the alternation of the refrains should feel like the regress reasserting itself each time unity is confidently asserted, so that the villanelle's obsessive form is doing philosophical work rather than merely decorating the topic.

The closing quatrain, where the two refrains finally stand side by side, should enact the resolution the poem takes -- and I do want it to take one. The natural candidates: Bradley's own verdict that relations are appearance and reality a seamless whole that thinking can only falsify; Frege's answer that concepts are unsaturated, incomplete by nature, and so lock into objects without needing any further glue; or the later option that the unity is not something said by the proposition but shown in it, the possibility of combination being written into the constituents themselves. Whichever ending you choose, the poem should be accurate enough that someone who has worked through this material recognizes the problem rather than a name-drop of it, and graceful enough that someone who hasn't still reads a good poem about things that refuse to stay connected. After the last line of the villanelle, add a single line beginning "Moral:" stating in one sentence which resolution the poem commits to.
