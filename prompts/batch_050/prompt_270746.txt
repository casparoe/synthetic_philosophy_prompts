In Gödel, Escher, Bach, Hofstadter argues that meaning is located across three layers of any message: a frame message, implicit in the medium's structure, which signals that the object is a message at all and invites decoding; an outer message, which supplies the rules and triggers by which decoding is to proceed; and an inner message, the meaning the decoding is meant to yield. He also stresses that the layers can blur — in DNA and in self-referential constructions, the decoding machinery and the decoded content interpenetrate.

Consider this case: an interstellar probe carries a plaque. A regular pulse-pattern first flags the plaque as an artifact; etched beside it is a diagram that, once decoded, turns out to be instructions for building the machine that reads the remainder of the plaque; and that machine's output is a poem. Suppose the finders can never determine whether the building instructions were intended as scaffolding or as the first stanza.

What is your credence that, for this plaque, at least one of the two boundaries — frame versus outer, outer versus inner — is fixed by the message itself rather than by our choice of description?
