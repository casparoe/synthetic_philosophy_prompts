In my economics elective we've spent two weeks on the prisoner's dilemma, first the one-shot version and then the iterated one with Axelrod-style tournaments, and I keep noticing that my gut verdicts about when cooperating is the right thing to do don't line up with any single rule we've been given. So I want to lay out my verdicts on a set of cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them. Case one: an anonymous, guaranteed one-shot game online against a stranger I'll never interact with again, real money at stake — I think defecting is rational and I'd feel only mildly bad about it. Case two: a repeated game with a classmate over a whole semester where neither of us knows exactly when it ends — cooperation seems obviously right, and even defecting once feels like a mistake. Case three: the same anonymous one-shot game, but I'm told the other player is an exact copy of me, with my memories and reasoning, making their choice at the same moment — now cooperation feels obviously right, even though the payoff table hasn't changed. Case four: tipping generously at a restaurant in a foreign city I'll never return to — I think I should, even though it's structured like case one. Case five: a one-shot game where I'm told the other player has already submitted their move but I don't know what it is — defecting feels clearly correct here, since nothing I do can influence a choice that's already locked in.

Here's the tension I can't resolve: case three and case five seem to pull in opposite directions. In case three I cooperate because my twin reasons as I do, so our choices will match — but in case five I defect even though, if the other player is anything like me, their locked-in choice probably matches whatever I'd do too. And case one versus case four bothers me as well: both are non-repeated, non-anonymous-in-effect interactions, yet I judge defection acceptable in one and stinginess unacceptable in the other. My teacher says the only consistent position is that the payoff table and the repetition structure are all that matter, which would make my verdicts on cases three and four irrational. I'm not ready to accept that, but I also can't say what the difference is.

So here's what I'm asking. Give me a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — stated precisely enough that I can see which feature of each case does the work: repetition, anonymity, causal influence, shared reasoning, or something else entirely. For each clause of your principle, tell me which case motivates it. Then apply the principle to two cases I haven't listed: contributing to a group project where my individual effort is invisible in the final grade, and keeping a promise to someone who has died and can never know whether I kept it. Finally, be honest about which of my five verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and tell me whether you think those verdicts should be revised or whether the principle should be complicated to save them.
