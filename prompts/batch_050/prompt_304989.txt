I teach a mixed undergrad/grad seminar on concept learning and inductive inference — 14 students, one 75-minute session. I want an in-class activity built around Bongard-style problems (a handful of "in" examples and "out" examples, infer the rule) that makes students feel in their gut why induction from few examples is underdetermined: any finite set of examples is consistent with infinitely many rules, so "figuring out the concept" is never just reading the answer off the data.

Here's the twist I want built in, and please don't water it down: at least one round should be rigged so that there is no single right rule. Maybe the example set was generated by coin flips, or two different hidden rules classify every shown example identically but diverge on the very next card. Either way, students who are confident they've "got it" should discover that their confidence wasn't tracking anything. I want them to feel Goodman's grue problem in their stomachs before I ever say the word grue.

Give me: concrete materials, meaning actual example sets with the drawings described precisely enough that I can sketch them onto index cards myself (don't just say "prepare some Bongard problems" — design them, and make sure the rigged round's examples really are consistent with both rules); rules of play, including how students register guesses and how scoring works; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; a debrief script that maps specific moments from the game onto the philosophical points rather than lecturing; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it; and one honest paragraph about which feature of real-world concept learning the activity can't reproduce, which I'll just have to tell them.

Make definite choices throughout. If there are two reasonable ways to run a round, pick one and say why — I'd definately rather get one opinionated design I can push back on than a menu of options to choose between myself.
