I'm in a philosophy of law seminar, and we have spent three weeks going around in circles on the problem of naked statistical evidence, and I want to understand why the disagreement never moves. The standard cases: in the blue bus scenario, a plaintiff is run off the road at night by an unidentified bus, and the only proof is that the defendant company operates 80% of the buses on that route — enough, numerically, to satisfy the "more likely than not" standard, yet almost everyone agrees the plaintiff should lose. In the gatecrasher scenario, four hundred of a thousand people at a fairground event paid admission, and intuition balks at holding a randomly selected attendee liable on the strength of the 60% figure alone. Yet add a single unreliable eyewitness whose testimony does no better than those numbers, and liability suddenly feels defensible.

The explanations on offer have been stable for decades: that proof must be "individualized" rather than a bare base rate; that statistical evidence is inherently second-best, or insulated from cross-examination, or such that you'd advance it whether or not it was true; or, contrarily, that the reluctance is simply an irrational residue and statistical proof is fine. Everyone knows all of this; nobody converts. My working hypothesis is that the dispute isn't really about evidence but about what a legal verdict is for — whether it must constitute an epistemic warrant (we know this person did it) or only a decision-theoretic bet (ruling against him minimizes expected error). On the first conception naked statistics fail; on the second they pass.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is a genuine disagreement about whether base rates can ground knowledge being conducted under the same heading? Please give a diagnosis with reasons, grounded in what each position actually appeals to, and say what would count as progress — a sharper distinction, a formal result about proof, work on how jurors actually weigh such evidence — or whether the impasse itself shows that a single probability threshold is being asked to do two incompatible jobs, and if so, which job each side is really answering.
