Two friends of mine — Anna, a historian who has just finished a nonfiction book, and David, a reliability engineer — fell into an argument at dinner last week, and I'd like it settled.

Anna's book makes about four hundred checkable factual claims: dates, quotations, figures, attributions. She verified each one against primary sources and would defend any single claim on its own evidence. Yet her acknowledgments end with the customary sentence to the effect that errors surely remain and are hers alone. (1) David opened by saying this combination is straightforwardly irrational: she cannot rationally believe, of each of the four hundred claims, that it is true, while also believing that at least one of them is false. One attitude has to go — either she retracts the apology, or she admits there is some claim in the book she does not in fact believe.

(2) Anna replied that she believes every claim and believes the apology too, and that there is no contradiction because she never asserts the sentence "every claim in this book is true." Rationality, she said, attaches to individual beliefs held for adequate reasons, and each of her four hundred beliefs has adequate reasons. Besides, if merely having many well-supported beliefs made it irrational to hold them all, then the more careful and knowledgeable a person became, the less she would be permitted to believe — which cannot be right.

(3) David conceded that she never utters the big conjunctive sentence but said this makes her position worse, not better: the set consisting of "claim one is true" through "claim four hundred is true" plus "at least one claim is false" is inconsistent, and she believes every member of it. He offered an analogy. In a fair lottery with a million tickets she would be justified in believing of each ticket that it will lose, yet she plainly may not believe that they will all lose, since she knows one must win. Per-ticket justification does not aggregate into justification for the whole, and per-claim justification does not either.

(4) Anna said the analogy fails in both directions. In the lottery, the justification for each ticket is the same thin statistical fact, and one knows with certainty that some ticket wins. About her book, by contrast, she holds specific, independent evidence for each claim, and she does not know that an error exists — her expectation of error is itself just another fallible inductive belief, drawn from her own track record, so it sits at the same level as the claims it supposedly contradicts rather than above them. And if David's principle were correct, she added, no one could ever rationally publish anything longer than a page.

(5) David's final move: even granting all of that, her apology is not idle. If pressed, she would bet that the book contains at least one error rather than that it contains none, and she would also bet, claim by claim, on every single claim. Someone who takes both sets of bets, he said, is incoherent in action whatever she says about levels of evidence, and beliefs that cannot guide action consistently are not doing the work beliefs are supposed to do.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing — including whether the disagreement has a single resolution or whether each of them is right about a different question.
