For years I have taught the endowment effect as a straightforward laboratory artifact, but my own considered reactions to cases resist that tidy lesson, and I would like to know whether there is a principled way to sort them. My verdicts:

1. My late father's fountain pen: I would not pay $150 for its like at auction, yet no offer below several thousand dollars would induce me to sell. Verdict: entirely legitimate; nothing here needs correcting.
2. Concert tickets bought at $60 face value, now reselling at $300: I will neither sell mine at $300 nor buy at $300. Verdict: also legitimate — though I note it has exactly the structure of case 1.
3. The familiar mug experiments: students randomly given a mug demand roughly twice as much to part with it as unendowed students will pay to acquire one. Verdict: a bias, properly designed around in policy.
4. My neighbor refuses to sell his house for less than he paid in 2019, though the market has fallen, and he concedes he would not buy the identical house next door at his own asking price. Verdict: loss aversion distorting his judgment; he should take the market price.
5. In setting compensation for siting a waste facility, I hold that residents' willingness-to-accept is the correct measure, not their willingness-to-pay, and the large disparity between the two is no objection to that choice. Verdict: legitimate preference, not bias.

Cases 1 and 4 seem to exhibit the same asymmetry with opposite verdicts, and case 2 shares the structure of both. Cases 3 and 5 also pull against each other: if the mug disparity is a bias, why isn't the residents'? What principle distinguishes a genuine, reference-constituting attachment — one that makes parting with a thing a real loss rather than a foregone gain — from a mere error of evaluation? Please state the principle precisely, test it against all five cases, indicate what it implies for cases I have not considered (for instance, whether default enrollment in retirement plans exploits this phenomenon or respects it), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate.
