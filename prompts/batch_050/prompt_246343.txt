Twenty years ago I founded an investment fund with an unusual mandate. We buy and hold exactly the assets that ethically motivated investors refuse to touch: tobacco companies, defense contractors, for-profit prison operators, gambling firms. The founding thesis was not that these industries are good. It was that widespread divestment by conscientious investors depresses the prices of these assets below what a pure profit-seeker would pay, leaving excess returns on the table for anyone willing to hold them — and that those excess returns, donated to rigorously vetted global health charities, do more good than refusing the assets ever did. I believed then, and still believe, that the clean hands of other investors are precisely what generates our profits: every dollar we earn is, in a real sense, paid to us by someone else's scruples. Over two decades the fund has given away roughly two hundred million dollars.

Next month I retire, and the firm is marking its twentieth anniversary with a dinner. I am giving the closing speech, about eight minutes. The room is split. Half are long-time donors, mostly card-carrying consequentialists, who know the founding argument cold and will notice immediately if I get it wrong or quietly abandon it. The other half are spouses, business-school classmates, financial journalists, and ESG-industry colleagues who have spent twenty years regarding the fund as a cynical laundering operation — money rinsed through bed nets until it smells clean.

Please write the speech. It needs to do several things, and these constraints are not decoration but the structure of the problem. It must not claim or imply that the industries we profit from are benign; I will not spend my last public act as director doing public relations for cigarettes. It must not pretend the donors' hands are clean either — the whole point of the strategy is that purity was on offer and we declined to buy it, at a price. It must not mock or dismiss the investors who divest, because their refusal is the load-bearing wall of our returns; sneering at them would be both ungrateful and self-undermining. It must answer the complicity charge directly, without hiding behind the donation figures, and without leaning too hard on the thought that if we had not bought the shares someone else would have — that dodge, pushed far enough, dissolves the agency of everyone in the room, including the divestors, and I do not want to win the argument by proving that no one's choices matter. And it must confront the strangest feature of the strategy honestly: it only works because most people would never do it. I therefore cannot close, as anniversary speeches conventionally close, by urging the room to go and do likewise. If everyone did likewise, there would be nothing to do.

The speech should make the non-specialists feel the genuine difficulty rather than watch it get waved away, let the consequentialists hear their own position stated at full strength and at full price, and land as a defense I am actually entitled to make rather than the one I might wish to make.
