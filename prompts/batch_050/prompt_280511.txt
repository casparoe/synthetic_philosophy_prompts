I teach an ethics-of-AI class (mixed CS and philosophy undergrads), 16 students, one 75-minute session, no laptops allowed. Design an in-class card game that makes them feel the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness -- the fact that once two groups have different base rates and your predictor is imperfect, you can't have calibration and equal false positive / false negative rates across groups all at the same time. Key constraint: they must write down their own fairness criteria before seeing any data, and then get scored against their own criteria, so the impossibility lands as a self-inflicted wound rather than a theorem from a slide. Include: the exact card dataset spec (how many cards, how 'truly qualified' status and noisy test scores are distributed in each group), the rules and a points-based scoring scheme (no real money, and ideally a way to buy back a violated criterion at the cost of accuracy), and a second round where the base rates are secretly equal so the contradiction dissapears. Then a debrief that maps each round onto the formal result -- no lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the theorem's name until afterwards. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g. students conflating demographic parity with calibration) and which feature of the real-world problem the game can't reproduce.
