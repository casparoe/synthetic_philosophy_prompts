I am a third-year undergraduate writing the final essay for a seminar on normative ethics, and I need help diagnosing a dispute rather than settling it. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in normative ethics and metaethics. Think carefully and slowly; I would rather have a well-argued diagnosis I can build an essay around than a quick verdict on who is right.

The dispute is whether the supererogatory exists — whether there is a coherent, non-empty category of acts that are morally good, even morally admirable, without being morally required.

In my seminar, both sides accept the same cases and the same surface intuitions about them: the donor who gives a stranger a kidney, the soldier who falls on the grenade, the colleague who quietly covers your shifts for a year, and the mundane case of giving somewhat more to charity than duty plausibly demands. The divergence is entirely in what to say about these cases, and after several weeks neither side has conceded anything.

Side A holds that morality has at least two tiers: requirements, and beyond them a region of the good-but-optional. They argue that a theory without this region makes morality implausibly demanding, and that our practices of praise, gratitude, and admiration presuppose the distinction. Side B holds that if an act is the morally best thing one can do, then one morally ought to do it, full stop; the appearance of a third category comes from confusing what is required with what it is blameworthy to omit. We do not blame the person who keeps her kidney, they say, but that shows only that blame and obligation come apart, not that the obligation was absent.

Here is the device I constructed to break the impasse, and its failure is what prompts my question. Imagine a dial that continuously adjusts the personal cost of the morally best available act: the same life saved, the same benefit conferred, while the cost to the agent rises smoothly from trivial, to significant, to ruinous. I expected Side A to locate a threshold where obligation lapses and the act becomes supererogatory, and Side B to deny that any such threshold exists. Instead, each side described the entire curve in its own vocabulary at every point — Side A marking a line somewhere, Side B redescribing every case as either required-but-unblameworthy-to-omit or not-best-after-all — and the dial produced no new agreement whatsoever.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about obligation at all, but about what the word "ought" is doing in moral language: one side reads the moral "ought" as "most strongly supported by moral reasons," the other as "required of you, such that falling short is a defect in you," and since both readings are legitimate, the debate is at bottom verbal. My fallback hypothesis, in case the first is wrong, is that the real fault line is a bedrock intuition about how demanding morality is permitted to be — an intuition neither side can argue for without begging the question.

What I want from you:

1. A diagnosis, with reasons. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of two sides answering different questions under one heading? Does the dial case's failure to move anyone itself constitute evidence about which diagnosis is correct? Engage with what each side actually says, including the blame-obligation move, rather than reconstructing generic positions.
2. An assessment of my two hypotheses — accept, reject, or refine each one.
3. Given your diagnosis, a statement of what would count as progress: a distinction that would dissolve the dispute, a decision procedure, an empirical finding about our practices of praise and blame, or an argument that no progress is available and the debate should be abandoned as ill-posed.

Please begin by briefly brainstorming, in a few lines, the candidate diagnoses you will consider, then argue for the one you find most plausible. Aim for roughly 900–1200 words of connected prose, suitable for me to develop into my essay.
