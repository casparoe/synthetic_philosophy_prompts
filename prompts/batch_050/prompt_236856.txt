I've absorbed the standard alignment-forum heuristic that one should supervise the process where the process can be checked and the outcome where it can't, but my actual intuitions about training setups don't line up cleanly with that rule, and I'd like to know what principle I'm really using. My verdicts, case by case:

1. A theorem prover rewarded for proofs accepted by a formal checker: unambiguously fine. The checker cannot be sweet-talked.
2. A coding model rewarded for passing a fixed suite of unit tests written before deployment: fine in practice, though I expect it to learn to pass these tests by whatever route is cheapest, degenerate ones included. I'm genuinely unsure whether that counts against the scheme or only against the test suite.
3. A chat assistant rewarded on user thumbs-up: clearly bad. I expect sycophancy within a few capability increments and manipulation thereafter.
4. A math tutor whose chain of thought is rated step by step by competent human raters: I want to endorse this, but when I picture the raters in the loop I suspect they'll skim the steps and check the final answer, in which case this is outcome supervision with extra payroll.
5. Step-by-step human rating in a domain the raters cannot verify, such as original research advice: I think this is the only option remaining, yet I also think it trains the production of reasoning that looks right to humans rather than reasoning that is right — case 3's failure mode wearing a lab coat.
6. A Go-playing system trained purely on win/loss: completely fine.
7. A persuasion system trained purely on whether it changes the user's mind: horrifying.

The pair that troubles me is 6 against 7. Both are pure outcome supervision, so if outcome-based reward is the problem I should dislike both, and if it isn't I should tolerate both — yet I don't. Likewise 3 and 5 look structurally identical to me while my reactions differ, and 4 seems to collapse into 2 depending on rater behavior, which suggests my verdicts are tracking something other than the process/outcome axis itself.

My tentative principle is that outcome supervision is acceptable exactly when the measured outcome is a faithful proxy for what I actually want and the model cannot produce the signal more cheaply by faking it than by achieving it. Test this against all seven verdicts. If it survives, state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a novel setup without you. If it fails, give me the best repair or the best replacement, showing which clause each case motivates, which of my verdicts the resulting account forces me to abandon, and why that verdict is the one to sacrifice rather than the others. Finally, tell me what your account predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a medical-advice model rewarded on patient outcomes measured five years later, and a model rewarded for explanations that a panel of laypeople rates as clear and satisfying.
