I am a novelist at work on a multi-generational saga set in a port city on the Mediterranean between roughly 1550 and 1750. I read Braudel years ago, and his distinction between the three speeds of history — the near-motionless time of geography and climate, the slow rhythms of economies, demography, and social structures, and the rapid time of events, battles, and individual lives — has stayed with me, though I am sure my memory of it is rough. The problem I keep running into is this: I invent things constantly, as novelists must, and I need a disciplined way of deciding which inventions are legitimate and which ones falsify the period in a way readers with real historical knowledge will rightly resent. My current rule of thumb is something like: events are cheap, structures are expensive — you may invent any incident you like, but you may not casually violate the slow structures underneath. I suspect this is close to right but too crude, and I would like it turned into an actual procedure I can run each time I draft a plot element, grounded in what Braudel's framework, properly understood, does and does not license.

Please write that procedure. Specifically:

1. Begin by correcting and refining my rule of thumb. What does the tripartite scheme actually say, and where does the rule "events are cheap, structures are expensive" capture it and where does it go wrong? I am particularly unsure whether the intermediate layer — conjunctures, price movements, population trends — should be treated as inventable or as load-bearing.

2. Give me a sequence of questions I can ask, in order, about any proposed plot element: a fictional battle, a marriage, a fortune made or lost, a migration, a character's belief or ambition, a change in law or custom. For each question, say what counts as a passing answer, what counts as a failure, and which layer of the framework the question is testing. The procedure should be usable at my desk in under half an hour, without a library.

3. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to me as a judgment call — for instance, where the question is not "did this happen?" but "would anyone have thought it possible?" — and say honestly where the framework itself gives no guidance, because Braudel's own practice as a historian of structures does not settle how a novelist should treat individual agency.

4. Apply the procedure to three plot elements I am currently considering:
   (a) A fictional naval engagement, invented whole, inserted into a real war of the period, which I need for the plot but which changes nothing about the war's outcome.
   (b) A peasant protagonist who, alone among his neighbors, understands that the steady rise in prices over his lifetime is a structural fact rather than a run of bad harvests and personal misfortune, and acts on that understanding to enrich himself.
   (c) Compressing into a single generation, for narrative economy, a shift in inheritance practice that in reality took the better part of two centuries.
   I would like a verdict on each, and I expect — indeed I want — the procedure to rule against at least one element I am attached to. If it clears all three comfortably, I will assume the procedure is too permissive and should be tightened.

5. Close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of historical falsity it will not catch, and the kinds of fictional license it was never meant to adjudicate.

Do not supply citations or a reading list; I can find my own way back to the sources. What I want is the instrument itself.
