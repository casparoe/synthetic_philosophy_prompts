I'm a PhD student working on practical rationality and I've been circling the self-torturer literature for about a year now, and I want to vent a bit and then ask for a diagnosis rather than another paper recommendation. Quick recap of the setup so we're on the same page: Quinn's puzzle has a person hooked up to a device with a dial, settings 0 through 1000. Each notch up gives them a nice payout (say $10,000) and increases the pain by an amount too small to notice -- setting 501 feels just like setting 500. At each step it seems rational to advance: free money, no felt cost. But at setting 1000 they're in agony and would pay everything back to return to 0. Every individual step rational, the whole sequence a disaster. Some people (Temkin, Rachels, following Quinn) take this to show that rational preferences can be cyclic or intransitive -- rationality itself can march you in a circle. Others dig in on the "imperceptible difference" premise and treat it as a sorites problem, where the solution is really about vagueness and has nothing special to do with rational choice. And a third camp says the puzzle trades on evaluating each choice in isolation, and once you evaluate plans or trajectories instead of pointwise choices, the trap dissolves.

Here's what's bugging me. The literature on this is thirty-plus years old, the positions seem completely stable, and as far as I can tell everyone grants the same setup and nobody ever accuses anyone else of a factual or logical error -- they just keep... disagreeing. My current hypothesis, which I'm only moderately confident in, is that this isn't really one disagreement. It's two buried ones: first, a disagreement about the unit of evaluation (single choice vs. whole sequence of choices vs. whole life), which each side just assumes rather than argues for; and second, a disagreement about whether an imperceptible difference in felt experience can ground a genuine preference at all -- which is secretly a philosophy-of-mind question about hedonic introspection dressed up as decision theory. If I'm right, the intransitivity debate is a proxy war and no amount of new thought experiments will settle it.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is the intransitivity conclusion a substantive claim about the structure of rational preference, or is it what you get when you misdescribe a sorites series? Is the vagueness response actually answering the puzzle or changing the subject? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress here -- a sharper formal result, a distinction nobody's drawn yet, empirical work on discriminability of pain, or should we just accept that the three camps are answering different questions under one catchy name and stop expecting convergence?
