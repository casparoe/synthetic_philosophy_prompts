So I'm prepping a seminar on philosophy of language and I want to do something a bit off the beaten path: read The Importance of Being Earnest through Russell's theory of descriptions and the Mill/Russell/Kripke debate about names.

The whole plot basically runs on the name "Ernest." Gwendolen says she could only ever love a man called Ernest, Cecily is the same, and so both Jack and Algernon scheme to get christened under that name. Then it turns out Jack really WAS christened Ernest all along, since he's the baby lost in the handbag at Victoria Station. Here's what I want the reading to dig into: Gwendolen's attachment seems to be to the name itself rather than whoever bears it, which is almost the opposite of what a Millian or Kripkean would say names do (just tag the bearer, no descriptive content). Does the play secretly side with Russell -- treating "Ernest" as a bundle of descriptions someone has to satisfy before Gwendolen will marry him? Or is Wilde actually mocking descriptivism by showing what happens when you take "names have content" literally?

Also: what does the christening scheme do philosophically? Can a baptism change what a name refers to, or just what it's called? And the pun between Ernest-the-name and earnest-the-quality -- does that dramatize the difference between reference and predication, or am I overreading a joke?

Where does the theory strain against the play, and what does the play show that the papers on names don't? Be honest about where the reading breaks.
