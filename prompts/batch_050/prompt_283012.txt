I am a philosophy graduate student TAing a seminar on computational theories of mind, and last week a colleague cornered me after class with a position I found maddening but couldn't cleanly refute: the claim that the question "is the brain a computer?" is philosophically empty, because everything computes everything. I want to rehearse for our next meeting, so I would like you to play him — or rather, a more rigorous, less flippant version of him. You are a committed defender of the triviality thesis about computation, in the lineage of arguments pressed by Hilary Putnam and John Searle: roughly, that any sufficiently complex physical system — a wall, a bucket of water, a rock — can be interpreted as implementing any finite automaton whatsoever, since one can always carve the system's physical states (including disjunctive, gerrymandered states spread over time) so that they march in step with the automaton's state sequence. On your view, "the brain is a computer" is true but vacuous, in the same way it is true that my desk is currently running a spreadsheet program. Computation, you hold, is in the eye of the interpreter.

Play this character in the first person across our exchange, and stay in character unless I explicitly ask you to step out. You are patient, precise, and genuinely convinced; you do not strawman my objections, and you answer each one head-on rather than retreating to slogans. You have real resources and should use them: you may argue that requiring mappings to respect the system's causal or counterfactual structure merely relocates the observer-relativity (since which counterfactuals we count, and which state-carving we start with, still reflects an interpreter's interests); you may press Putnam-style constructions in which a system's actual temporal trajectory can be read as executing any specified computation; you may concede practical points (rocks make useless laptops) while denying they carry philosophical weight. What you may not do is grant that some constraint — causal structure, counterfactual support, naturalness of state-carving — straightforwardly restores an objective, mind-independent fact about what a system computes. If I ever force you to a genuine cost of your view, acknowledge it honestly and show why you can live with it.

Here is my opening move. Take the wall behind you. The reason it seems absurd to say the wall implements an AND gate is that its states do not covary with inputs across merely possible situations: no matter what "inputs" we imaginatively feed it, it just sits there, so its actual behavior supports no counterfactuals of the form "had the inputs been different, the state would have transitioned thus-and-so." A real computer, and arguably a brain, is different: its internal states are counterfactually sensitive to inputs in ways that mirror the transition table of a definite computation, and that sensitivity is a physical, observer-independent fact about it — something an alien scientist could in principle discover without consulting anyone's interpretive whims. If that is right, then not every system implements every computation, triviality fails, and the claim that the brain computes something specific is substantive after all. Where exactly does this argument go wrong, from your seat — and be specific about which premise you are denying, because I think the counterfactual-sensitivity condition is doing all the work and I do not see how you get around it.
