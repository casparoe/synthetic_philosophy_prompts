I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on the limits of rational choice theory, and each week one student runs an activity session for the rest of the group. My turn is coming up. There will be about fourteen of us, we have seventy-five minutes in a regular seminar room, and our current topic is transformative experience in L. A. Paul's sense: decisions where undergoing the outcome changes what the decider values or what it is like to be them, so that the standard model of choosing by assigning subjective values to outcomes in advance seems to break down.

I'd like you to design the activity for me. The hard constraint I want you to take seriously is that the relevant change must actually happen inside the session, in miniature: I don't want a hypothetical vampire scenario or a described case, because then participants just deliberate about a fantasy and the epistemic problem never bites. The design should engineer some small but genuine shift in what participants want, can appreciate, or can discriminate, brought about by the activity itself, and it should require each participant to make at least one binding, irreversible commitment before the shift and then live with the consequences of that commitment afterwards. Stakes should be real but trivial and non-monetary (things like forfeits, privileges within the session, or control over some shared resource are all fine). Please give me the full rules, any materials I need to prepare, and a timeline for the seventy-five minutes.

I also want a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the components of the argument: the difference between epistemically and personally transformative experiences, and the point that testimony from people who have already undergone the change cannot substitute for having undergone it. Two failure modes I'm predicting, which I'd like the design to force into the open rather than suppress: some participants will try to evade the structure by polling the people who went earlier and treating their reports as sufficient information, and others will retreat to a meta-preference along the lines of "whatever I become, I'll be content with it," which is exactly the move that dissolves the problem without answering it. Tell me how to handle both when they come up. Finally, be frank about which feature of the real cases the activity cannot reproduce, since no seventy-five-minute exercise will match the depth of something like choosing to have a child, and what I as facilitator have to supply verbally to bridge that gap.

I'd also like you to say how confident you are that the activity will actually produce a felt preference shift rather than a merely theatrical one, and to single out the single point in your design you are least sure of. Please write the whole response as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
