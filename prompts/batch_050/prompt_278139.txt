Since retiring from a thirty-year career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have been making up for lost time in philosophy and economics, and I now help organize a small interview series for the alumni association of my old university. My next guest is an economist who defends a position I find both intriguing and slippery: that classical expected-value reasoning is systematically misleading whenever payoffs unfold through time, because what happens to an individual is governed by the time average of a process rather than the ensemble average across parallel possibilities. His stock example is a gamble in which a fair coin raises your wealth by half on heads and cuts it by forty percent on tails: the expected value of each round is positive, yet almost every individual trajectory decays toward zero, and so, he says, a rational person should decline the gamble even though its expectation is favorable.

I would like you to act as an experienced philosophical interviewer who knows this terrain well. Before drafting anything, think carefully and, for your own preparation only, sketch the handful of points where this position is most exposed to pressure; then convert the best of them into eight to ten interview questions. The questions should not merely invite him to restate the view. In particular, I want the set to include: at least one question pressing him on whether the framework adds anything to what ordinary risk aversion and logarithmic utility already explained centuries ago; at least one on cases where there is no long run at all, such as one-shot decisions about climate policy or species extinction, where no individual trajectory gets to repeat and time averages seem to lose their grip; and at least one on insurance, where he argues that both sides of the contract can gain even though the insurer, diversified across thousands of policyholders, arguably faces something much closer to an ensemble than to a single trajectory, so that the asymmetry his theory exploits seems to reappear on the other side of the deal. For each question, please supply a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give: for instance, if he answers the utility objection by saying his approach derives risk preferences from dynamics rather than assuming them, the follow-up should ask what the framework says about an agent who can restart after ruin, where the dynamics story appears to lose its bite.

A final practical note: he is polished on air and fond of batting away objections by remarking that his critics are still thinking in ensemble terms. Wherever a question risks drawing that response, build in a follow-up that makes it costly for him to repeat it, and end the sequence with one question about what empirical observation or formal result would lead him to abandon the view.
