For a reading group on the philosophy of the social sciences, I put together the following argument. The conclusion strikes me as absurd, so the argument must prove too much — but I cannot locate the guilty step, and each premise looks defensible in isolation.

Some context. Malthus held that unchecked population grows geometrically while subsistence grows at best arithmetically, so that living standards must eventually be forced back toward bare subsistence. Two centuries of growth have not borne this out, and the standard defense of Malthus-style theories is that the prediction was right but early: industrial agriculture, the demographic transition, and technological substitution postponed the reckoning rather than cancelling it.

(1) A theory makes a genuine long-run empirical prediction only if some possible course of events would count against it; a forecast compatible with every outcome predicts nothing.
(2) For any long-run prediction about a complex society, whatever actually happens — sustained growth, stagnation, transformation, even collapse at the wrong time — some countervailing force (innovation, substitution, behavioral change, policy response) can be cited afterward to redescribe the outcome as deferral rather than failure.
(3) If every outcome can be redescribed as deferral, then no outcome counts against the theory.
(4) So Malthusian population theory makes no genuine long-run predictions; "right but early" is not a defense but an admission that the theory is compatible with anything.
(5) The reasoning in (1)–(4) is perfectly general. It applies equally to resource-depletion models that have quietly revised their dates (a celebrated 1970s systems study projected civilizational collapse within a century), to long-horizon climate projections, to forecasts of debt crises or secular stagnation — to any theory about complex systems over long timescales.
(6) Hence none of these theories makes genuine long-run predictions either; they differ from a millenarian sect that recalculates the date after each non-event only in social standing.
(7) But (6) is false: these theories plainly differ in empirical standing, and some of them legitimately guide policy.

Something in (1)–(3), or in the inferences connecting them, has to give. Which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent? Please also price the alternatives: what does it cost to reject each of the other candidates instead? I would like to keep (1) if I can, since something like it seems needed to convict the sect; and I would like to retain the verdict that at least some "right but early" defenses are doing something evasive — I just cannot tell whether the evasiveness lives in the defense itself or only in my argument against it.
