So my friend and I got into it last night over beers about historical analogies for transformative AI and we both walked away annoyed. Her line was basically: AI is like the printing press. A technology for making and spreading text, it scared the authorities of the day, it broke the church's monopoly on knowledge, caused decades of chaos and religious wars, sure, but it diffused to everyone and on net was obviously good, so all this doom talk is just priests panicking about losing control. My line was that nuclear weapons is the better analogy: a general-purpose capability where the race dynamics between rivals push everyone to cut corners on safety, where the raw capability itself is the danger, and where the right response was treaties and control regimes, not shrugging and letting it diffuse. We both suspected the other person was cherry-picking features to fit a conclusion already decided.

Audit both analogies for me. Give the explicit mappings (what's the "ink", what's the "manuscript", what's the "warhead", who plays the role of the church or of the nuclear states), then the disanalogies that actually matter for the policy conclusions each of us was drawing -- like, does it matter that the printing press only multiplied human-authored text while AI generates new content? Does it matter that nukes have no peaceful consumer use and AI clearly does? Which features is each analogy quietly smuggling in (diffusion-is-good vs. state-control-is-possible) that the AI case might just not have? And end with the part I don't want to hear: where my nuclear analogy breaks worse than hers does, and whether there's a third analogy (industrial revolution? something else) that beats both.
