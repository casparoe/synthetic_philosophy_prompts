At work, two colleagues got into an argument I keep thinking about, and I'd like it adjudicated point by point. We were deciding whether to accept a free stream of extra telemetry before a launch decision. Ana said this shouldn't even be a debate: there's a theorem in decision theory (Good's, on the value of information) showing that a rational agent facing a fixed decision is never worse off, in expectation, from free information, so declining free data is simply irrational. Ben replied that the theorem assumes an ideal Bayesian, which we provably are not: last quarter, engineers who could watch live experiment dashboards kept stopping A/B tests the moment results looked significant, inflating false positives, and the teams that committed to a fixed horizon in advance did better. Ana said that's misuse of information, not harm from it -- the correct fix is to update properly, and deciding to stay ignorant because you can't trust yourself is a second-best that actually confirms her point. Ben then switched examples: when a diagnostic test about a population becomes available before insurance contracts are signed, premiums adjust to the revealed risk, the risk-sharing that made everyone better off disappears, and everyone can end up worse off even if each party reasons ideally -- so ideal rationality doesn't save the claim. Ana's last move was that this is a market-equilibrium effect, not a single decision-maker, so it falls outside the theorem's scope and leaves her original claim intact.

Who is right at each step of this exchange? In particular: is Ben's dashboard case a counterexample to the theorem or only to Ana's use of it; does his insurance case escape the theorem or merely its assumptions; and what, if anything, are both of them missing about what the theorem actually requires?
