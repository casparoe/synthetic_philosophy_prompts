A friend who recently read a popular introduction to epistemology gave me this summary of Robert Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, and I'd like to know whether it's fair before I rely on it:

'Nozick says you know that p when, in addition to believing p truly, two further conditions hold: if p were false, you wouldn't believe it (you're sensitive to the truth), and if p were true, you would believe it. The sensitivity condition is what makes knowledge safe — if your belief tracks the truth across nearby possibilities, you can't easily be wrong, which is why Nozick's theory is sometimes called a safety theory. The real payoff is the sceptic. Take the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis: since your belief that you're not a brain in a vat tracks the truth, you do know you're not one, so Nozick refutes Cartesian scepticism. Admittedly, his theory has the minor technical quirk of rejecting the principle that knowledge is closed under known implication, but that's a small price for defeating the sceptic on his own terms.'

Something about this strikes me as off, particularly the claim that Nozick defeats the sceptic — I had the impression his position on sceptical hypotheses was more concessive than that. But the conditions as stated match what I remember, so I can't locate the problem. Please separate what is accurate here, what is a common simplification, and what gets Nozick's actual position wrong — including whether the conclusions my friend draws would survive a corrected statement of the view.
