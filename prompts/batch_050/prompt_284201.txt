I run a small reading group for amateur fiction writers, and at our last meeting we stumbled into a disagreement I couldn't settle, because I don't know whether the distinctions we were groping toward already have names in whatever field studies this. We were talking about Sherlock Holmes. Everyone agreed that some sentences about him are straightforwardly right (he is a consulting detective, he plays the violin) and some are straightforwardly wrong (he is a bank teller in Leeds), even though the man never existed. But then someone asked what his blood type is, and the room went quiet. It felt wrong to call that false the way "bank teller in Leeds" is false, and equally wrong to call it true; it felt like the story simply doesn't take a stand, and asking felt like a category mistake of some kind.

What interests me most, though, is a second pattern that came up when we pressed on why the quiet question felt different from the loud ones. Conan Doyle never, so far as any of us could recall, tells us that Holmes has a spleen, or that his London is subject to ordinary gravity, or that water in it is wet — yet all of that seems to hold in the stories, imported wholesale from the real world without anyone having to say it. At the same time the importation is clearly selective: the real Baker Street of Doyle's day contained no Holmes, so reality gets overridden exactly where the story needs it to be, while everything unrevised seems to carry over by default. And a third wrinkle: when a story is told by an unreliable narrator, even what the text explicitly states can fail to be true-of-the-story, so the words on the page are apparently neither the floor nor the ceiling of what the fiction contains. So there seem to be at least three different mechanisms at work — what's written, what's silently borrowed from reality until cancelled, and what stays genuinely unsettled — and they produce different kinds of rightness and wrongness about people who don't exist.

Is there an established vocabulary for this? I want to know, first, whether the default-borrowing-from-reality principle has a recognized name, and whether anyone has precisely stated the rule for when a default gets cancelled and when it doesn't. Second, whether the unsettled middle category — the blood-type cases — is a recognized phenomenon distinct from plain falsehood, or whether theorists just treat it as a gap with no special status. Third, I'm suspicious that what I've been calling the three mechanisms may not all be parts of one phenomenon, so if the literature carves this territory differently than I have — lumping some of my cases together and splitting others apart — I'd rather have that correction than a tidy confirmation of my scheme. Please don't just hand me a term like verisimilitude or suspension of disbelief, which seem to be about how fiction feels to a reader rather than about which sentences count as right and why; if the thing I've described genuinely has no name, say so plainly and propose one, with a definition tight enough that I can test it against hard cases: cross-story comparisons (is Holmes more observant than Maigret?), stories set in deliberately inconsistent worlds, and fictions about real people.
