I'm writing a long-form magazine piece on what happens when courts are forced to reason like moral philosophers, and I want the centerpiece to be the 2000 English case Re A (the conjoined twins known publicly as Jodie and Mary). The facts, briefly: the twins were born joined at the lower body in Manchester in August 2000; Mary had no functioning heart or lungs of her own and survived only because a shared artery drew on Jodie's circulation; doctors estimated that without separation both would die within months, while separation would save Jodie with high probability and kill Mary with certainty; the parents, devout Catholics, refused consent, preferring that both die; the Court of Appeal authorized the surgery, which went ahead in November 2000 -- Mary died, Jodie survived. The three appeal judges justified the outcome on different grounds (self-defence, necessity, and the surgeons' intention). Please analyze the case as a test of the doctrine of double effect: map the doctrine's conditions onto the surgery one by one, identifying what counts as the act, the intended good, and the foreseen harm, and then press on the two points where the fit is most contested -- whether Mary's death functions as a means to saving Jodie rather than a side effect, and whether the death is simply too bound up with the act of separation for the intention/foresight distinction to get a grip (the closeness problem). Conclude by running the argument in the other direction: if the doctrine cannot cleanly vindicate an outcome nearly everyone thinks was right, does the case expose a defect in double effect, or does it show that the doctrine needs supplementation by something like necessity or self-defence? Cite no secondary literature; reason from the case and the doctrine alone, in no more than 600 words.
