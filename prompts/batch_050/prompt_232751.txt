I host a small philosophy podcast and next week I'm interviewing an early-career philosopher whose new paper defends a view she calls fanaticism about expected value: roughly, that no probability is too small to be swamped by a big enough payoff, so if one option offers even a one-in-10^30 chance of an astronomically better outcome, rationality requires picking it over a certainty of a merely very good one. She bites the bullet on Pascal's-mugging-style cases in principle, though she insists real life rarely serves them up cleanly. She's extremely sharp and has obviously gamed out the standard objections, so my worry is I'll lob her softballs all episode.

She has two rehearsed moves I want to neutralize. First, whenever Pascal's mugging comes up, she says the mugger's promise is incredible, so the probability of the payoff is effectively zero and the case proves nothing about the view. Second, when I push on whether anyone can actually hold credences precise enough to distinguish 10^-30 from 10^-31, she says imprecise credences are no problem because the ranking survives any symmetric imprecision — the huge-value option still comes out on top. Write me a sequence of about 6-8 questions that pin her down rather than letting her retreat to "this is just decision theory." Specifically I'd like questions that (a) force her to explain where credences that tiny are even supposed to come from, and why they should be treated as measurements rather than guesses, with a follow-up ready for when she deploys the symmetric-imprecision move; (b) make her engage the case where fanatical reasoning seems available on both sides of a decision and the two cancel out — what does her theory actually recommend then, and is that answer stable; (c) press the leverage issue, i.e. whether my action genuinely shifts the probability of the astronomical outcome at all rather than just being correlated with talk about it, plus a follow-up for when she says expected value already prices that in; and (d) close by asking her what kind of case or result would actually make her give the view up, since "nothing could" would be pretty telling on air.

Order the questions so each one makes her escape hatches from the previous one less usable, and keep them askable out loud, not essay-length. Also, tell me how confident you are that each question really corners her instead of handing her an easy out, and single out the question in the sequence you're least sure will land the way I want it too.
