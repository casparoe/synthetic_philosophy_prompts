I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on political philosophy and AI ethics, and I'm applying for my university's summer undergraduate research grant (eight weeks, roughly 3,000–4,000 words of output, plus a short presentation). I need a one-page proposal, and I'd like help turning a half-formed idea into one.

The idea in rough form: discussions of aligned AI keep asking whose values an AI system should serve, and the answers usually fall into two camps. Either the system should defer to its individual user's values (with some hard limits), or it should be designed around some aggregate of everyone's values. What strikes me is that the first camp is implicitly borrowing the liberal state's stance of neutrality between citizens' conceptions of the good, just relocated to the relationship between one assistant and one user. But the classic justifications for state neutrality seem to rest on features that AI assistants lack: the state is coercive, inescapable, and faces citizens who can reasonably disagree; an assistant is optional, non-coercive, and competes with rivals in a market. Meanwhile assistants have features the state lacks: they can be persuasive in continuous, personalized, hard-to-monitor ways. So my question is whether the standard justifications for liberal neutrality, one by one, actually transfer to AI assistants, and what design guidance survives for whichever ones don't. For example, if neutrality is justified mainly by the threat of coercion, maybe assistants have no neutrality obligation at all and should be allowed to take substantive stances; if it's justified by respect for users' capacity to form their own convictions, maybe assistants need something stronger than neutrality — a duty not to shape values covertly, which pure deference to stated preferences wouldn't satisfy.

Write the proposal with: (1) a precise statement of the research question, framed so it has a determinate answer; (2) why the existing framing of value alignment doesn't already settle it — I'm especially interested in the point that both camps above may share an unexamined assumption about what neutrality is; (3) the method, meaning what I would actually do week by week for eight weeks, given that this is conceptual work with no experiments; (4) what the possible conclusions are and what considerations would decide between them; (5) what would count as the project failing rather than merely reaching an unwelcome answer; and (6) an honest note on scope — what's the biggest risk that this is too much for eight weeks, and what would I cut first. No bibliography or literature citations; my supervisor will help me add those, and I'd rather you refer to positions and arguments by description than guess at sources.
