I work at a mid-sized outdoor gear company and part of my job is signing off on the demand-forecasting models our two analysts build before we commit to inventory orders. I'm not a statistician -- I get maybe an hour per model, once a quarter, and if I wave through a bad one we end up with a warehouse full of snowshoes nobody wants. The thing that keeps bugging me is that almost every model looks great on the historical data it was built on, and then some of them fall apart the second conditions shift even slightly. A friend who's into philosophy told me this is basically Nelson Goodman's "new riddle of induction" -- the grue thought experiment -- which as I understand it shows that for any pile of past observations there are infinitely many patterns that fit them perfectly, some of which diverge the moment you extrapolate past the data, and that "it fits everything we've seen so far" on its own can't tell you which pattern you're actually looking at. (His example was something like: every emerald observed before some cutoff date being green equally supports "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue," where grue means green-if-examined-before-the-cutoff and blue-after.) That seems directly relevant to my situation but I have no idea how to actually use it.

So: write me a procedure I can actually follow -- an ordered checklist or set of tests I can run a proposed model (or really any rule of the form "the data shows X, so expect X to keep going") through, to flag which extrapolations are grue-like and which are on solid ground. The procedure should operationalize whatever philosophers and statisticians actually say distinguishes projectible patterns from non-projectible ones, e.g. whether the pattern's definition smuggles in a reference to a specific time or to the act of observation itself, whether the categories it uses are the kind that have figured in other successful generalizations, whether the pattern survives re-describing the same data in different units or groupings, that sort of thing -- but translated so a non-specialist can apply each test and know what a pass and a fail look like in plain language.

Then show the procedure working on three examples: one model that passes cleanly, one that fails obviously, and the nasty one -- a model that fits ten years of our sales data beatifully but that the procedure says to reject anyway, where you explain why rejecting it is right even though I'll get serious pushback from the analysts on that one. Finally, be honest about where the procedure bottoms out: which calls it can't settle and where I just have to exercise judgment, plus what questions I should be asking the analysts in those cases. Keep in mind the constraints -- an hour per model, no formal stats training on my end, and whatever I decide has to be defensible in a meeting with people who know more math than me.
