So at work basically everything runs on contracts: SLAs, API contracts, semver. If a library maintainer breaks backward compat without a major version bump, people get mad, and rightly so. Anyway last week a coworker (also an engineer, no philosophy training either) made this argument over lunch that's been bugging me. He said promises are just verbal contracts, and contracts only "bind" in any real sense because of enforcement and reliance -- people depend on you delivering, and there's a cost if you don't. So his claim was: a promise that nobody is relying on, and that nobody would ever find out you broke, doesn't actually bind you at all. His example was promising a dying relative to scatter their ashes in some specific lake, when it's expensive for you, nobody else knows the promise happened, and the relative obviously can't be harmed anymore. He said keeping that promise is like honoring an SLA for a service with zero users: pointless, feel free to silently deprecate.

I'm suspicious but I can't fully articulate why. Can you audit the analogy for me? Give me the actual mapping: what plays the role of the service provider, the downstream users, reliance, notice periods, breach, damages. And my coworker's "major version bump" move, where he says if you just announce in advance that you're not going to keep a promise, that's a deprecation with a migration window and not a breach at all -- does that part of the analogy hold or is it sneaking in the conclusion? Contracts usually need consideration and mutual assent and all that, whereas I feel like a promise creates the obligation in the very act of promising, before anyone's relied on anything. But maybe that's just sentimentality and he's right.

So: where does the promise-is-a-contract analogy break in ways that matter for his conclusion specifically (not just nitpicks), what does the contract framing quietly import that promising lacks, and does his "unenforceable promises are optional" takeaway survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If there's a better everyday analogy for what a promise does, I'd take that too -- he also floated "a promise is like taking on debt" at one point and I wasn't sure if that's the same analogy in a trenchcoat or a genuinely different one.
