So I run a small rationality reading group (mostly grad students from different departments, a couple of them econ, one philosphy of science person who argues with everyone) and we just spent two sessions on Aumann's agreement theorem and the "we can't disagree forever" follow-ups. The problem: everyone walked in with a gut take on what the theorem implies for real-life disagreement -- "persistent disagreement means someone's being irrational," "it only works under idealizations that never hold," "disagreement just traces back to priors and priors can't be wrong" -- and they kept talking past each other because nobody had laid out where exactly their views diverge. I want to fix that with a handout.

Write me a decision tree that starts from "I accept that Aumann's theorem is mathematically correct given its assumptions" and sorts a person into a position on what the theorem means for actual human disagreements. Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of names: each branch node should be a question someone can answer without knowing which position it leads to -- things like whether common priors are a rational requirement or just a modeling convenience, whether priors are the kind of thing that can be rational or irrational at all, whether the theorem's common-knowledge conditions are ever even approximately satisfied in real disputes, whether the convergence results (or complexity results about how much communication agreement would take) matter for practice, and whether honesty/rationality can ever be common knowledge between two real people. Then locate the actual positions in that space as leaves: I want at least the strong "honest truth-seekers cannot genuinely disagree" view, the "theorem is valid but practically vacuous" view, the prior-permissivist view that disagreement is blameless because priors aren't rationally assessable, and the revisionist view that the theorem is a reductio of some idealized assumption rather than of disagreeing people -- plus whatever else the space supports. Note any combination of answers that has no occupant and say briefly why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored), and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't cleanly separate, e.g. I suspect the prior-permissivist and the "rationality doesn't constrain updating either" permissivist might collapse into each other.

One more thing: at the end, tell me which branch question you're least confident actually carves this debate at the joints, and what argument or technical result would make you redraw the tree -- since I'll be defending this handout in front of people who will absolutely try to poke holes in it.
