I'm a journalist who covers the social sciences, and I'm drafting a long feature on the deep roots of national wealth — the running argument between institutions, geography, and culture. What I've realized, going through my notes, is that I don't actually have a view; I have a pile of gut verdicts on famous cases, and they may not cohere. I'd like you to help me find out. Here are the verdicts as I honestly hold them. The two Koreas: a decisive case for institutions — one people, one peninsula, one history until 1945, and then a natural experiment with radically different results; geography and culture can't explain that gap. Nogales, Arizona versus Nogales, Sonora — same desert, same climate, intertwined families — strikes me the same way. Argentina, a rich country in 1900 that slid down the rankings for a century while its climate and location stayed put: also institutions. Botswana's success next to its neighbors' failures: institutions again. But then: Singapore and Hong Kong both flourished under quite different political arrangements, and what they seem to share is a commercial culture and a Chinese diaspora that has prospered across wildly different institutional environments in Malaysia, Indonesia, and Thailand — that looks like culture doing the work. Haiti and the Dominican Republic share one island and yet have diverged enormously; my gut says that case damages geography as a master variable. And yet I can't shake the thought that the poverty of landlocked tropical Africa, the disease burden, the distance from markets — that geography is obviously doing something there, whatever the regression disputes.

So here's what I want from you. First, try to state a principle — or, if one won't do, a small set of principles — that captures when each kind of explanation is the right one to reach for: perhaps something about the scale of the comparison, or the timescale, or whether the candidate causes are held fixed by the case or themselves need explaining. Test your principle against every case I listed, tell me explicitly which of my verdicts it vindicates and which it cannot accommodate, and be honest about the cost: a complicated principle that saves all my verdicts may be less credible than a simple one that overturns one or two of them — if so, tell me which verdicts should go and why. Second, apply the principle to two cases I haven't resolved: China's transformation after 1978 (institutions changed, culture and geography didn't — or did they?) and the observation that the regions most prosperous before European colonization often ended up poorest after it, and vice versa.

Finally, given how contested this literature is — the instruments are debatable, the history is one-shot, and every side accuses the other of confusing correlation with cause — I want the piece to be epistemically careful. Please answer in American English, and end with a candid statement of how confident you are in the principle you've offered, which of my cases it handles least comfortably, and the single point in your own answer you are least sure of.
