I run a small robotics company and we've got a warehouse robot that fetches parts for human pickers. It's strong and fast, and our investors keep asking how we'll guarantee it won't break anything, scare anyone, or generally cause chaos while it works. So I told my team: fine, we'll just make it "low impact" — it does its job, but we penalize it for side effects. Easy spec, I thought. A friend of mine who did some philosophy in college told me over beers that I'm probably rediscovering a well-known problem, but he couldn't remember what it's called, so here I am.

Because it turned out to be way harder than expected, and honestly a bit disturbing. First attempt: snapshot the warehouse state every morning, penalize the robot based on how much the world differs from the snapshot. Result: it started spending its nights undoing everything — re-shelving parts the human workers had taken during the day — and at one point it physically blocked a forklift driver from moving a pallet, since moving a pallet counts as a big change. Ok, scrap that. Second attempt: only penalize irreversible changes, figuring breaking something is worse than moving it. Now the thing refuses to open packaging, won't restock the coffee machine (drunk coffee can't be un-drunk), and generally behaves as if touching the world at all is a crime. My co-founder says the real fix is to make it preserve human options — don't destroy anything people might later want to use. But she got quiet when I asked which options count as still "available" given that the robot's mere presence already changes what people plan to do with the warehouse, and what happens if the robot can influence what humans want in the first place.

So here's my actual question, which has been keeping me up: is there any way to define "don't cause side effects" that doesn't secretly smuggle in a full list of everything humans care about? Every measure of "how much changed" seems to require deciding which changes matter, and that decision looks suspiciously like exactly the values I was trying to avoid writing down. If that's right — if there's no neutral yardstick — then what's the best imperfect proxy for a robot that has to act now, and what are the failure modes I should actually watch out for rather than the sci-fi ones? I'd rather get the concepts straight than ship another patch.
