I'm running a small reading group on decision theory, and next week we're covering games whose expected value fails to exist. The group keeps falling into the same rut: half the room says such games simply have no rational price, full stop, and the other half says that can't be the end of the story. I'd like a dialogue that stages this disagreement at its sharpest, so I've set up the following scenario. An eccentric mathematician runs a stall at a village fête. She tosses a fair coin repeatedly until it first lands heads. If the first heads occurs on toss n, the game ends and she pays you (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n pounds — so you receive £2 with probability 1/2, pay £2 with probability 1/4, receive £8/3 with probability 1/8, pay £4 with probability 1/16, and so on, with the prize shrinking in exchange-rate terms but the outcomes alternating in sign. She offers to let you play for a flat entry fee of 60p, and the tosses happen one at a time, in real sequence, in front of you.

Write a dialogue of roughly 700–900 words between two visitors to the stall. One, whom you might call Ada, argues that the game has no well-defined expectation — the series of probability-weighted payoffs is only conditionally convergent, so by Riemann's rearrangement theorem its sum can be made to equal anything depending on the order in which terms are added, and since an expectation cannot depend on an arbitrary labelling of outcomes, there is no fact of the matter about the game's value and paying 60p is unjustifiable. The other, whom you might call Bento, argues that the physical, sequential way the game is actually played privileges the natural ordering of the terms — the one in which the series converges to ln 2 ≈ 0.69 — so the fair price really is about 69p and 60p is a bargain. Both characters should be genuinely competent: Ada must press Bento on why temporal order should matter for a mathematical expectation and what stops her rearrangement argument from applying, while Bento must press Ada on what her view implies for finite truncations, repeated play, and whether 'no expectation' really means 'no rational attitude' rather than just 'no single number'. Feel free to let them bring in a summation-method proposal (Cesàro or Abel-style averaging of partial sums) or a finitely-additive notion of expectation if it serves the argument.

The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth — each character must respond to the other's actual points, concede where a point lands, and exploit concessions, rather than delivering alternating monologues. Please don't resolve the debate with a cheap compromise or an external interruption; instead, end with each character stating, in a sentence or two, precisely where they now take the crux of the disagreement to live. No scholarly apparatus or citations are needed, just the exchange itself, and please write it in British English.
