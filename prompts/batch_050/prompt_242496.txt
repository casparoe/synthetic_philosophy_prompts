Please argue that the default American posture of suspicion toward government is a more reasonable civic attitude than the default Scandinavian posture of trust. For context: a friend and I argued about this for several hours last night and parted unconvinced by each other. She held that the high institutional trust found in Denmark, Norway, and Sweden is the sensible response to institutions that have broadly earned it, and that American distrust is a self-inflicted wound; I held the opposite. I want you to take my side and construct the strongest sustained argument for it, not a survey of both sides.

In doing so, please meet the following requirements, which reflect where our argument actually got stuck:

1. Do not rest the case on particular scandals, administrations, or episodes from either region, since those can be traded example-for-example indefinitely. Argue at the level of what a default attitude is for: how it shapes the information citizens get, the incentives officials face, and the kinds of errors a polity is prone to when the attitude is wrong.

2. Confront directly the strongest point in my friend's favor: that Scandinavian trust is correlated with competent, honest government, so trust looks vindicated by results. Your argument should address the possibility that trust is an effect of good government rather than a cause of it, and explain why the suspicion view survives even if that is partly true.

3. Address the self-fulfilling-prophecy worry: that widespread suspicion degrades the very institutions it monitors, for instance by discouraging capable people from public service. Either show this cost is smaller than it appears, or show that the corresponding cost of misplaced trust is larger.

4. Take seriously that the two attitudes may be answers to different questions — trust as a judgment about current officials, suspicion as a design principle for institutions that must outlast and constrain whoever holds office — and argue that the second question is the one a citizen's default attitude should answer.

Conclude by stating the single strongest objection to the position you have just defended, and giving the best reply available to it without pretending the objection has no force.
