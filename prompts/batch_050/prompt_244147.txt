Analyze the "microfoundations revolution" in macroeconomics as an episode in the debate between methodological individualism and methodological holism. The facts I have in mind are these: through the mid-twentieth century, macroeconomics operated with large-scale aggregate models whose equations related economy-wide quantities -- total consumption, investment, unemployment, inflation -- without deriving those relations from the optimizing behavior of individual agents. In the 1970s, Robert Lucas and others attacked this practice, arguing that aggregate relations estimated from historical data would shift when policy changed, because individuals would revise their expectations and behavior in response to the new policy regime. The remedy, on this view, was to rebuild macroeconomics on explicit microfoundations: models in which individual households and firms maximize utility and profit under rational expectations, and aggregate outcomes emerge as the equilibrium of their interaction. This program won; for decades, a macroeconomic model lacking optimization-based microfoundations was regarded as intellectually disreputable. Then, after the 2008 financial crisis, critics argued that the dominant microfounded models had been structurally blind to the crisis itself: they typically featured a single "representative agent" whose choices stand in for millions, little or no financial sector, and no way to represent panics, runs, cascades, or the breakdown of trust between institutions -- phenomena that look like properties of the system rather than of any individual.

Your analysis should run in both directions. First, map the episode onto the debate: which version of methodological individualism does the microfoundations program actually instantiate, and is the representative-agent device a genuine reduction to individuals or a covert aggregate postulate wearing individualist dress? Consider, too, whether the equilibrium condition itself -- the requirement that everyone's plans be mutually consistent -- is a holistic constraint imposed from above rather than derived from individual behavior, and whether "rational expectations" smuggles in a social fact (a shared model of the economy) that no single agent holds. Second, assess what a Durkheim-style holist would say macroeconomics was doing right all along, and whether the post-2008 critiques -- the demand to model financial contagion, network effects, and herd behavior -- amount to a vindication of holism or merely to a call for better individual-level mechanisms, which an individualist like Elster could happily accept. Third, draw the lesson in the other direction: does the episode confirm methodological individualism, refute it, or reveal that the individualism/holism dichotomy fails to capture what actually made some of these models succeed and others fail? Take a position on that last question and defend it, rather than surveying all three.
