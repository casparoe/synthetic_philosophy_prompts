Next spring, my university will seal a time capsule in the foundations of its new library, to be opened on the building's two-hundredth anniversary. As the senior member of the philosophy department, I have been asked to deliver the address at the sealing ceremony. There is one complication, and it is not a small one: I am a committed presentist. I have spent much of my career arguing, in print and in public, that only present things exist -- that the dinosaurs are not lurking somewhere in the past, and, more awkwardly for this occasion, that the people who will open this capsule in two centuries do not exist either, not in any sense, and that there is no one there to whom our letters, artifacts, and greetings are addressed. My colleagues will be in the front row, and they will notice instantly if I slip into language my own view forbids.

Please write the address, of roughly six hundred to eight hundred words, that I could sincerely give. The audience is mixed: alongside the philosophers there will be donors, students, the mayor, and the library staff, most of whom have come for a warm and dignified ceremony and none of whom want a seminar on the metaphysics of time. The speech has to work for both groups at once.

The presentism should do real structural work, not serve as a decorative anecdote. In particular: the conventional moves of this genre are closed to me. I cannot address the capsule's future openers as "you," cannot describe them as waiting, watching, or listening, and cannot reassure the crowd that we are sending a message to anyone. The speech should notice this problem openly rather than smuggle the forbidden language back in -- I would rather the audience be let in on the difficulty than be soothed by sentences I believe are literally false. But the address also must not curdle into a deflationary stunt that ruins the ceremony or mocks the donors' generosity. I need it to find, and articulate, something a presentist can honestly mean by this ritual: what the sealing is, whom it is for, and why it might matter, if not as a delivery to persons who will one day exist. A little well-placed humor about a philosopher congratulating an empty room would be welcome, provided it lands as warmth rather than self-satisfaction.

Do not name-drop historical philosophers or cite the literature; the speech should sound like a person thinking carefully out loud, not like a survey of the eternalism debate. End on a note that lets the mayor lower the capsule with a straight face.
