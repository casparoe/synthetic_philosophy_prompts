I'm hoping there's an established term for a habit I keep running into, and if there isn't I'd like a good coinage with real edges. I'm in an enviromental ethics seminar and I keep noticing that people measure the success of anything involving wild animals purely by how many of them come to exist. A rewilding project reports that the wetlands now produce 40,000 froglets a year as if that number alone settles that things are going well, and a nature doc will celebrate a colony of a million penguins in the same episode where half the chicks starve on camera. Meanwhile nobody scores human societies this way -- no government brags that its policies caused 200,000 extra people to exist, and if one did it would sound dystopian. It's not quite the logic of the larder, since that's about justifying farming by saying the animals wouldn't exist otherwise, and it's not really the repugnant conclusion either, since that at least asks how good the lives are before counting heads. So: does this "more wild lives = better" reflex have a name of its own, how does it differ from those two neighbors, and where exactly are its boundaries -- does it still seem right if most of the froglets die within a week? I have a feeling the whole thing presupposes that wild lives are net positive, and that this assumption is doing all the work while never getting said out loud.
