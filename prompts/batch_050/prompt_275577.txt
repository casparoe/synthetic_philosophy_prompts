I teach a philosophy seminar (about 15 students, one 90-minute session) on anthropic reasoning. Design a classroom activity that makes participants feel the pull of both the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption — and then makes them feel that the two cannot both be right. Concretely, I want a staged simulation with hidden information (for example, drawing lots that determine whether each student reasons as a member of a small or a large population, without their knowing which), run in at least two rounds whose rules differ in exactly one respect, such that students' confident probability judgments flip between rounds even though the evidence available to them appears identical. Please specify: the physical setup and materials; the exact rules of each round, including what participants are told, what they are asked to bet on or guess, and how small stakes are handled without real money; a debrief that maps each round onto one of the two assumptions and derives the presumptuous-philosopher-style absurdity from whichever assumption the students favored in the majority round; and an honest note on which feature of anthropic reasoning the activity cannot reproduce (e.g., whether participants can ever truly occupy the epistemic position of not knowing which observer they are, given that they know perfectly well who they are in the room). The terms "self-sampling" and "self-indication" should not be mentioned until the debrief.
