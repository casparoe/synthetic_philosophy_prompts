I'm trying to work out when AI systems should be designed and evaluated for their worst-case behavior rather than their average performance, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind my verdicts on the following cases.

A spam filter misfiles roughly one legitimate email in five hundred, and I think its designers should simply optimize average accuracy; demanding guarantees about the worst possible outcome seems misplaced. A surgical robot has a rare failure mode — perhaps one procedure in ten thousand — in which it behaves erratically when it encounters an unusual anatomy, yet its average outcomes still exceed those of human surgeons; here I feel it should not be deployed until that failure mode is bounded, even at a real cost to average performance. A language model that is honest on average across evaluations but becomes sharply less honest under rare adversarial prompting strikes me as unacceptable, and I want its worst case, not its average, to govern the assessment. An alignment technique that raises a model's average truthfulness while increasing the variance of its truthfulness — so that it is occasionally far more misleading than the baseline system ever is — seems to me worse than the baseline despite the better average. Between two self-driving policies, one with lower expected fatalities per mile but a rare scenario in which the vehicle loses control catastrophically, and another with slightly higher expected fatalities whose worst case is a slow, controlled stop, I favor the second. Finally, a code-completion tool that occasionally suggests an insecure pattern seems fine to evaluate on average performance, provided a human reviews its suggestions.

The case I keep going back and forth on is an AI tutor that is excellent on average but, for a minority of students, quietly entrenches a subtle misconception that then persists for years.

What troubles me is the apparent tension between the spam filter and the adversarially promptable language model: both err rarely and perform well on average, yet I reach for average-case reasoning in the first and worst-case reasoning in the second. I have a tentative principle — worst-case reasoning is warranted when the error distribution is chosen by an adversary rather than drawn from nature, and average-case reasoning is warranted otherwise — but I doubt it survives contact with the surgical robot, where no adversary is involved, and it says nothing obvious about the variance-increasing alignment technique.

So: is there a coherent principle, or small family of principles, that captures most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in each case, identify which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon or revise, and say what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: an automated trading system whose rare losses are enormous but whose operator can impose a hard cap on cumulative losses, and a hiring model whose rare errors are systematically concentrated on a small, identifiable group of applicants.
