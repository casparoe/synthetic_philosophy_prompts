I am a historical novelist, and my own craft has just been weaponized against me. My next book follows a widow who subscribes to a service that rebuilds her late husband from his emails, chat logs, and voicemail: she types, and something answers in his idiom, with his remembered opinions and a few new ones. Over dinner, a friend who teaches philosophy told me that twenty years of putting words into the mouths of the real dead commit me to a verdict about this service that I refuse to accept. Here is his argument, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) In my novels I attribute invented speech and invented inner life to real dead people: conversations they never had, motives they never recorded, in one case a confession the historical record contradicts.
(2) I do this without consent. The dead cannot give it, and I have never asked their descendants either.
(3) The practice is not merely tolerated but honored — reviewed kindly, assigned to students, given prizes. So constructing, without consent, a speaking simulacrum of a real dead person is in itself morally permissible.
(4) The widow's service constructs, without consent, a speaking simulacrum of a real dead person. So it too is permissible, unless there is some morally relevant difference between it and the historical novel.
(5) The candidate differences all collapse. The novel is fixed and the bot interactive — but we nowhere else think a wrong is laundered by being committed in a fixed medium rather than a responsive one. The novel announces itself as fiction while the bot speaks as the man — but the widow knows perfectly well that her husband is dead; nobody is deceived. The novel works from the public record while the bot mines private messages — but what my readers value is precisely the interior monologue, which was never on any record at all. The novel is art and the bot a product — but I take an advance and royalties.
(6) There is therefore no morally relevant difference. Griefbots of this kind are permissible; the novelist who condemns them is a hypocrite; and the shudder people feel at such services is novelty-aversion, the same panic that met photography and the telephone.

I cannot accept the conclusion, and I cannot find the exit. If forced to choose, I would rather defend (3) and my livelihood than endorse (6) — but I want to know what that choice costs, and at which step the argument actually slips, if it does.

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

**Bottom line** (60–80 words): name the step you reject — a numbered premise, or the inference between two of them — without hedging. If you judge the argument valid, say so here and name the premise whose surrender is cheapest.

**The diagnosis** (~250 words): state precisely what goes wrong at that step — the equivocation, the overgeneralization, the misdescription of one of the two practices — tightly enough that my friend could recognize his own argument in your retelling and feel its failure.

**Why the step looked fine** (~150 words): reconstruct the pull of the rejected step. I want to understand what seduced me, not merely where I erred.

**The price list** (~200 words): what rejecting your chosen step commits me to — including what it implies about how I may treat the real dead in my fiction — and what the most plausible alternative rejection would cost instead.

**For the novel** (~100 words): what your verdict implies for how I may fairly write the widow — whether the service in my book must be portrayed as doing something wrong, or whether there is room for the more unsettling verdict that it does something permissible that ruins her anyway.
