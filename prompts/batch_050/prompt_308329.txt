For the past year, a reading group I'm part of has been circling the Great Divergence, and two of us have hardened into camps that I think are talking past each other. Call them the Contingency camp and the Deep Roots camp. The Contingency camp holds, roughly, that as late as around 1750 the most advanced parts of China (the Lower Yangzi) and the most advanced parts of Europe (England, the Low Countries) were comparable market economies facing similar ecological ceilings, and that England industrialized first largely because of fortunate accidents: coal located near its manufacturing centers, and access to New World land that effectively relieved constraints on food, fiber, and timber that Chinese cores had to meet from their own peripheries. The Deep Roots camp holds that the divergence reaches much further back: differences in state formation, property rights enforcement, corporate and scientific institutions, fertility regimes, or labor markets meant that northwestern Europe was already on a different trajectory by 1700 or earlier, so that coal and colonies were at most accelerants, not causes. Both sides accept that the standard of living of ordinary people was probably not dramatically different across these regions in 1750; they fight about everything downstream of that.

Please draft the joint statement these two people could co-author after a serious adversarial collaboration. It should have three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign: not vacuous concessions like "geography mattered somewhat," but substantive points about what the wage-price data show, about which comparisons are legitimate (e.g., whether comparing all of China to England is a category error and one should compare England to the Yangzi Delta), about what the New World actually supplied to Europe, and about what the mere fact of European industrialization does and does not establish. Second, a disagreements table in which each row is a single crisp proposition that one camp affirms and the other denies, with each camp's position phrased in words that camp would itself accept -- this is the hard part, and I want you to resist formulations that smuggle in a verdict. Third, a cruxes section: identify the three or four sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one camp to substantially revise its view, and for each crux, name the evidence, argument, or counterfactual analysis both camps agree bears on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance. Candidate cruxes I suspect you should consider (but adjudicate for yourself): whether "why Britain and not China?" is even a well-posed contrastive question, or whether the explanandum shifts between camps (sustained growth vs. escape from ecological constraint vs. technological innovation), so that part of the dispute is about what is being explained rather than about what explains it; whether the counterfactual "give the Yangzi Delta cheap coal and a New World and it industrializes" is even coherent, given that the colonies' existence and exploitation were themselves products of European institutions; and whether factor-price arguments (high English wages inducing labor-saving invention) survive the objection that cheap Chinese labor coexisted with plenty of labor-saving devices.

One more instruction: if you find, in drafting, that a particular disagreement is verbal -- that the camps use a term like "cause," "necessary condition," or "institution" differently and would agree on the facts once the term is disambiguated -- mark it explicitly as such in the table and say what each side actually commits to once the ambiguity is resolved. I'd rather have a shorter table of real disagreements than a long one padded with disputes about words. Please write it as an actual document the two camps could sign, not as an essay describing what such a document would say.
