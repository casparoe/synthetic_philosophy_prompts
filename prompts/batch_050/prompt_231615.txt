I spent my career in molecular biology and have, since retiring, been working my way through the philosophical literature on human enhancement, where I keep encountering the therapy/enhancement distinction: the proposal that interventions restoring normal functioning differ morally from those raising capacities beyond the normal range. I recently rewatched Gattaca and would like a careful reading of the film through this lens. The premise seems to trade on the distinction -- Vincent is conceived without genetic selection and thereby relegated to an underclass, while his brother Anton is engineered -- and yet the plot's sympathies are complicated: the film condemns discrimination by genotype, but its triumphant arc is that of the naturally conceived man outperforming the enhanced through will and borrowed identity. So I am asking several things at once. Does the film presuppose the therapy/enhancement distinction as a stable line, or does it show that line collapsing into social convention, since what counts as defective there is fixed by insurance markets and employers rather than by any biological norm? Where does the film commit the genetic determinism my own field has spent decades complicating -- treating a sequence as a fate -- and is that determinism the film's target or an unexamined assumption it needs for its drama? And does the ending, in which authenticity and natural conception are vindicated, covertly smuggle back a valorization of the natural that the film's anti-eugenic message ought to resist? Please be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of a position paper, and do not bend the philosophical distinction to fit every scene. I would also value a closing word on what the film contributes to the debate that the journal literature, in my experience, tends not to.
