I'm drafting a chapter on Warren Quinn's puzzle of the self-torturer and keep running into the same frustration: every treatment I know uses roughly Quinn's own setup (a device with a thousand imperceptibly graduated settings, money paid per notch, agony at the far end), and I suspect the familiarity of that one example lets readers nod along without ever isolating what actually generates the paradox. For those unfamiliar: Quinn imagines an agent offered a payment each time they advance a dial one setting, where adjacent settings differ imperceptibly in pain but the thousandth setting is torture the agent would pay everything to escape; each single advance looks strictly worth taking, yet following that judgment a thousand times lands the agent in an outcome strictly worse than never starting.

Construct an original thought experiment with the same logical skeleton, built to the following specifications. First, the scenario must be mundane rather than medical or science-fictional: no implants, dials, drugs, or mad scientists; the incremental deterioration should run through something ordinary (career concessions, environmental degradation, sleep, debt, a relationship) that a real person could plausibly drift through over months or years. Second, the agent must know the entire structure in advance -- the full schedule of steps, the endpoint, and the fact that each step is individually attractive -- so the puzzle cannot be dissolved by pleading ignorance or surprise. Third, each individual step must be genuinely rational by the agent's own lights, not merely apparently so because of some sorites-style vagueness you decline to resolve; you should stipulate crisp payoffs and explain why imperceptibility or negligibility still does its work. Fourth, declining any step must remain costless and available throughout, so that coercion plays no role.

After presenting the scenario (in at most two paragraphs), demonstrate that it meets each specification: walk through at least three representative steps -- early, middle, and late -- showing why each choice is rational in isolation and why the endpoint is strictly dominated by the status quo ante. Then identify the single structural feature of your case that does the philosophical work, and say what happens if it is removed (does the case collapse into ordinary akrasia, ordinary shortsightedness, or nothing paradoxical at all?). Finally, adjudicate between two diagnoses: that the case reveals rational but intransitive preferences (the agent prefers each step to its predecessor yet prefers the beginning to the end), versus the rival view that the preferences are perfectly transitive and the fault lies instead in evaluating choices one at a time rather than as a whole sequence -- and say which diagnosis your particular construction supports, and how a defender of the losing diagnosis would try to redescribe your case.
