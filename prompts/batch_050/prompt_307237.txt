Having retired after some forty years as an experimental physicist, I have lately been reading the classical pragmatists with growing interest, and I keep encountering a claim I should like to see examined carefully rather than repeated. Among my former colleagues, the logical positivists' verifiability criterion was long treated as the last word on meaning: a statement counts as cognitively meaningful only if some experience could in principle confirm or disconfirm it. I now find Peirce's pragmatic maxim — roughly, that our conception of an object is nothing over and above our conception of the conceivable practical effects the object might have — routinely described as an anticipation of that very criterion. I am not persuaded that the two are the same instrument, and I would like a careful comparison.

Please address at least the following three points.

First, scope. The positivists proposed a test which whole sentences pass or fail, whereas Peirce appears to offer a rule for clarifying individual concepts. Is this difference in scope philosophically significant, or merely cosmetic?

Second, modal strength. The positivists generally demanded experiences obtainable in principle; Peirce speaks of conceivable effects upon practice, including effects on our habits and expectations of future experience. Which principle is the broader, and what in each doctrine accounts for the breadth?

Third, the intended target. The positivists wielded their criterion as an instrument of demolition against metaphysics. Did Peirce intend his maxim to do the same work, or something else — say, a tool of inquiry rather than a border guard?

Then apply both principles to a case from my old field, chosen because I believe it pulls them apart: the claim that an electron possesses a definite position at every instant, whether or not any measurement could reveal it, as certain hidden-variable theories maintain. A straightforward verificationist would dismiss such a claim as meaningless. Trace what the pragmatic maxim says about it, bearing in mind a subtlety: since Bell's work, some theories of this kind have turned out to carry experimentally distinguishable consequences, so the claim's "practical effects" proved to be a moving target rather than a fixed null. Does Peirce's maxim, with its orientation toward the indefinite future of inquiry, accommodate this moving target better than a verification criterion applied to the experimental resources of the present moment?

Conclude with your considered verdict on whether the pragmatic maxim is essentially an early form of verificationism or a genuinely distinct doctrine, and give the verdict at the very end in the form \answer{...}, containing one of the two labels "early verificationism" or "distinct doctrine" followed by a single justifying phrase.
