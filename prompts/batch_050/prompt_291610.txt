I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level philosophy of language course (3rd/4th year undergrads, all of them have logic) and I need one essay question on indicative vs counterfactual conditionals. They get 75 minutes on it, worth 25 marks.

I want the question built around a case I made up, so they can't just regurgitate the Oswald/Kennedy stuff they've memorised. Here's the case, feel free to polish the wording:

The harbourmaster's report. A cargo ship runs aground at night outside a small port. It's established from the log and two witnesses that the lighthouse lamp was completely dark when the ship passed. The lamp has exactly two possible failure points: the main filament and the backup generator. The harbourmaster writes in her report: "If the filament didn't burn out, then the backup generator failed." Everyone accepts this — something had to explain the darkness. But she refuses to write: "If the filament hadn't burned out, the backup generator would have failed." And she seems right to refuse: the generator was inspected the next morning and found in perfect order, and had the filament held, the lamp would simply have shone and the ship would have been fine.

The question should force them through roughly this dialectic: (i) explain why the indicative is assertible while the counterfactual is false even though they share antecedent and consequent — what each is sensitive to (her total evidence vs. what happens in the nearest filament-intact worlds); (ii) how this motivates the idea that indicatives obey the equation prob(if A then C) = prob(C given A), and that counterfactuals conspicuously don't; (iii) what Lewis's triviality result does to that idea — I want them to state clearly what it shows (no proposition-valued conditional can have its probability systematically equal the corresponding conditional probability without collapsing) even if they can't reproduce the proof; and (iv) the fork that result creates: either indicatives don't express propositions at all (Adams/Edgington style), which saves the equation but has the notorious problem making sense of embedded conditionals — they should construct their own example, a negation or a conditional with a conditional antecedent, and say what exactly the expressivist owes us — or indicatives are ordinary propositions and the equation is a mere assertibility rule, which handles embedding for free but owes an account of why the rule feels semantic rather than pragmatic. They end on a verdict; the verdict matters less than whether they see the two pressures pulling against each other.

Hard requirement: a student who writes "indicatives concern epistemic possibility, counterfactuals concern closeness of worlds" and stops there should fail this question. The question's wording should make that impossible to get away with.

Deliverables: (1) the exam question as it would appear on the paper, including the case text; (2) a model answer of about 650-700 words written at the level of your best student this year; (3) a marking scheme out of 25, split into four or five components, where each component names the specific move that earns the marks and has a one-line "how students typically blow this" note; and (4) a short paragraph on what seperates a first-class answer from a merely decent one — my guess is most students plateau at (i) and (ii) and treat triviality as some vague technical annoyance rather than the reason the whole fork in (iv) exists, so tell me if you think that's the right place to draw the line.
