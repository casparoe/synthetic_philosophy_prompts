I am a forensic accountant by training, and six months ago my mother's early-stage dementia made me her primary caregiver. My siblings and I have fallen into an ugly dispute: one of them has literally proposed that we keep a shared spreadsheet of caregiving hours and costs, on the theory that our mother "paid in" for decades and we are now settling the account, each according to our means. I suspect the spreadsheet is a conceptual mistake and not merely a cold one, and I recently encountered a philosophical debate that seems to bear on this, but I can only think clearly in my own professional vocabulary.

Here is what I would like you to do. There is a family of views in the ethics of filial obligation on which adult children owe their parents care as a kind of repayment — for sacrifices made, resources expended, debts of gratitude incurred. Against this, Jane English's friendship model holds that what parents do for children creates favors rather than debts, that favors differ from debts in that they generate an ongoing relationship of friendship rather than a balance to be discharged, and that grown children's obligations toward their parents therefore flow from the current relationship — its needs, its history, its mutual affection — and cease where friendship itself has genuinely ceased, rather than persisting as an unpaid ledger.

Please restate both positions, as faithfully and precisely as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: accounts receivable and payable, amortization, contingent liabilities, related-party transactions, goodwill, impairment, write-offs, going-concern assumptions. I am fluent in all of these, so do not simplify. Translate the debt model first, then the friendship model. For each claim, say explicitly whether it maps cleanly, maps only with distortion (and name the distortion), or has no accounting expression at all.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care most about. I suspect that the friendship model's central claim — that a favor, unlike a debt, is something whose settlement would end the very thing it was meant to sustain — has no clean bookkeeping counterpart, since a ledger treats a zero balance as completion rather than loss. If so, tell me whether that is a limitation of accounting vocabulary or a sign that English's claim only gestures at something it never quite states. Finally, apply your translation to my situation: does my sibling's spreadsheet commit the conceptual error English diagnoses, or does the accounting framework, taken seriously on its own terms, actually vindicate it?

Please answer in American English.
