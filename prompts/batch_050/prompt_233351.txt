You are a philosopher specializing in machine ethics and the theory of moral responsibility, with several years of experience consulting for organizations that deploy autonomous and semi-autonomous systems. Work through this task carefully: it will help to get the conceptual distinctions straight in your own mind before committing to any procedural design, and I will ask you to do some explicit brainstorming before the main deliverable.

I direct technology governance at a regional hospital network. Roughly twelve times a year, my office reviews a proposal to deploy a system with some degree of operational autonomy: diagnostic imaging models, a sepsis early-warning system, robotic delivery carts in our corridors, automated insulin dosing on a pilot ward, scheduling optimizers, and so on. Each proposal receives about ninety minutes of structured scrutiny from a committee of clinicians, nurses, and IT staff — none of whom have philosophical training — before our chief medical officer signs off.

Our recurring failure is not that our systems misbehave; it is what happens afterward. When something goes wrong, we discover that responsibility was distributed in a way that satisfied no one: the vendor points to the clinical staff who nominally supervised the system; the clinicians point out that they had no realistic way to second-guess it; procurement says it merely executed a contract; and the patient or their family finds that there is no one to ask for an explanation, no one who can be meaningfully blamed, and no clear route to compensation. I want a procedure that catches these situations before deployment rather than after an incident.

For the purposes of this procedure, work with the following four-way distinction among responsibility gaps (you may refine it if you think it is deficient, but do not collapse it):

1. Answerability gap: after an outcome, there is no agent who can be called upon to explain why the system acted as it did, in terms the affected party can actually engage with.
2. Culpability gap: no agent plausibly deserves blame or sanction, because the knowledge and control conditions for blame were scattered across actors who each possessed one but lacked the other.
3. Redress gap: there is no practicable route by which an injured party can be compensated.
4. Control gap (forward-looking): at the moment of decision, there is no agent who both can intervene effectively and is under a duty to do so.

Note that the available remedies differ by gap, and a procedure that ignores this will recommend the wrong fixes: insurance closes a redress gap but nothing else; logging and post-hoc explanation tools partially close an answerability gap; only redesign of the oversight arrangement can close a control gap, and nominal human-in-the-loop oversight frequently fails to do so when the human lacks the time, information, or disposition to intervene — the rubber-stamping problem.

Your deliverable is a pre-deployment decision procedure the committee can actually run inside its ninety minutes. It should specify: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points where the procedure halts with a recommendation not to deploy, or to deploy only at reduced autonomy; the points where it must hand the question to a person because it turns on values rather than facts; which of the four distinctions must survive translation into checklist language and which can safely be merged for a lay committee; and the ways you expect the procedure to misfire.

Then run the procedure on these three proposals:

(a) A diagnostic imaging model that radiologists may overrule but, in practice at comparable hospitals, is overridden in under two percent of cases — and when it is, the override is usually in the direction of agreeing with the model.
(b) A fleet of delivery robots sharing corridors with patients, which will occasionally cause minor collisions that no individual staff member witnesses or could have prevented.
(c) A sepsis early-warning system that only advises: every alert goes to a physician who makes the final call.

Our clinicians strongly favor proposal (c) and expect it to pass. If your procedure returns an answer they will dislike — either a rejection or conditions that substantially restrict the deployment — say so plainly and explain where their intuition goes wrong; if it passes, identify what important risk your procedure is thereby failing to capture.

Structure your response as follows. First, a brief brainstorm of the characteristic failure modes of naive versions of this exercise — for instance, checklists that mistake the existence of a named supervisor for the existence of control. Second, the procedure itself. Third, the three worked applications. Finally, an honest closing account of what the procedure cannot settle and what the committee should do with proposals that fall in that residue.
