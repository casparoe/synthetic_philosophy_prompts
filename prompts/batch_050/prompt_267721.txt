I'm a research lead at a mid-sized AI lab, and I keep having the same argument with my team. For every training run where we give the model feedback, someone asks: are we grading the final answer (outcome supervision) or grading the steps it took to get there (process supervision)? Right now we decide by vibes and whoever argues loudest in the meeting, and I want an actual procedure we can run instead.

So: please act as someone with real expertise in both ML training dynamics and the philosophy side of this (measurement, Goodhart-type problems, testimony/deference -- the stuff about when a proxy stops tracking the thing you care about). Think carefully, and before writing the procedure itself, spend a paragraph or two brainstorming the distinct ways each supervision style can fail -- I suspect the failure modes are assymetric between the two, and the procedure should basically be a way of figuring out which failure mode is more dangerous for the task at hand.

Then write the procedure itself. It should be something an engineer on my team can run in under an hour for a new task we're about to set up training for. Concretely:

- An ordered list of diagnostic questions about the task (can we even check outcomes reliably? can the reasoning be made legible without the model dumbing it down or performing for the grader? how long is the horizon? how much does it matter why the model did something vs. just that it did the right thing?), with guidance on what different answers imply.
- Clear stopping rules: conditions under which the procedure says "outcome supervision only," "process supervision worth the cost," or "neither, don't train on this signal yet."
- An explicit note of the points where the procedure hands things back to human judgment because it's a values call or the evidence just isn't there -- don't dress those up as mechanical steps.
- Then apply the procedure to three cases from our actual queue: (1) a math-solving model where answers are auto-checkable but we also want transferable reasoning, (2) a model that writes summaries of long legal documents where nobody reads the source carefully enough to check, and (3) an agentic coding assistant that runs multi-step tasks over several hours. For at least one of these, follow the procedure where it leads even if the answer is annoying for us -- e.g. if it says the supervision scheme we're currently using is worse than doing less, say so.

One more thing on epistemics: a lot of writing on this topic states things with more confidence than the evidence warrants. Please clearly separate (a) what's basically settled among people who work on this, (b) what's plausible but genuinely contested -- I'm thinking of stuff like whether chain-of-thought actually reflects the model's real reasoning, and whether process supervision scales to tasks humans can't check step by step -- and (c) where you're just reasoning from analogy. Don't present a contested claim as if it were consensus, even in the parts of the procedure where it would be rhetorically convenient.
