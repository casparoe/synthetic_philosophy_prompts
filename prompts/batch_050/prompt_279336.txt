You are a philosopher of perception who has taught introductory courses for many years and who thinks seriously about pedagogy — about the order in which ideas must be introduced, the examples that carry them, and the predictable ways students go wrong. Please work through this carefully, and do the thinking before the drafting: I would rather see your reasoning about sequence and misconception than receive a polished timetable.

I teach a first-year introduction to philosophy at a university. Next term I am responsible for the two-week unit on perception: one 75-minute lecture, followed a week later by a second 75-minute lecture, for about sixty students, most of whom have never studied philosophy. The week immediately before mine is devoted to Descartes' First Meditation, so my students will arrive with the dream argument and the evil deceiver fresh in their minds. I have a whiteboard and can do simple live demonstrations; I prefer not to use slides. The unit runs every year, so I want a reusable plan, not a script.

My current intention is to open the first lecture with the following passage from Bertrand Russell's The Problems of Philosophy (1912), Chapter I:

"For most practical purposes these differences are unimportant, but to the painter they are all-important: the painter has to unlearn the habit of thinking that things seem to have the colour which common sense says they 'really' have, and to learn the habit of seeing things as they appear. Here we have already the beginning of one of the distinctions that cause most trouble in philosophy—the distinction between 'appearance' and 'reality', between what things seem to be and what they are."

The unit's destination is the argument from illusion, with disjunctivism as the response I most want students to be able to state fairly. My worry about the Russell passage as an opening frame is this: it presents the appearance/reality distinction as something a painter must master before philosophy even begins — innocent, pre-theoretical — when the dispute the unit is building toward is precisely over what that distinction is a distinction between (two objects, an inner item and an outer one; or two fundamentally different kinds of perceptual state; or two ways of describing one experience). Russell's chapter goes on to argue for sense-data, and I had not planned to tell students that at the outset. Please address, as a substantive part of the plan rather than a footnote: whether this passage is the right way in; whether its apparent innocence stacks the deck against disjunctivism before the argument is even stated; whether and when to disclose where Russell himself ends up; and what alternative opening you would use if you judge this one compromised.

Then the plan itself. I want, in order:

1. The sequence of ideas across the two lectures, with the dependency structure made explicit — which pieces must precede which and why. The components I take myself to be working with are: the datum of perceptual variation and illusion; the inference from subjectively indistinguishable good and bad cases to a common mental item; sense-data as intermediaries and the veil they seem to interpose between mind and world; disjunctivism's refusal of the common-factor step; and the pressure back on disjunctivism from the causal argument about hallucination and from the continuity between illusion and hallucination. Flag every point where the order is a genuine choice rather than a necessity, and say what each choice costs.

2. The carrying example. One example should do most of the work. Candidates I have considered: the penny seen obliquely that looks elliptical, the straight stick half-submerged in water, the Müller-Lyer lines, and double vision produced by gently pressing one eyeball. State what the carrying example must have — live-demonstrable in a lecture hall; a case of genuinely seeing a real object as other than it is, not merely failing to see it; and resistant to the "you know better, so it isn't really an illusion" dismissal — and what it must not smuggle in. Pick one, with reasons, and tell me whether the submerged stick is ruined by the fact that students know the physics and will say the stick only "looks" bent because of refraction, full stop.

3. The misconception map. The errors students will bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. The ones I already expect: that the argument from illusion concludes the external world does not exist or cannot be known (a confusion with the previous week's Descartes); that disjunctivists deny hallucinations occur, or deny that they feel like perceptions; that "illusion" means seeing something that is not there at all, so that Russell's table and my carrying example get miscategorized; that neuroscience settles the question because the brain state is the same in the good and bad cases; and that subjective indistinguishability just obviously settles the metaphysics — the crux premise, which students tend to treat as needing no argument. Tell me which of these I have misplaced in the sequence and which predictable misconceptions I have missed entirely.

4. The check for understanding: one task or question, usable in the room, whose answers would show the idea has actually landed. My own candidate is asking students to reconstruct the argument for a different modality — taste, or afterimages — and to state in one sentence a non-philosopher could parse exactly which premise the disjunctivist rejects. Assess this or replace it.

5. What to leave out at this level, with a one-line "there is more here" note for each omission. My candidates for omission are adverbialism, intentionalist versions of representationalism, Austin's critique of the argument's setup, the time-lag argument, and the internalism/externalism machinery that contemporary disjunctivists rely on. Correct this list if it misjudges what first-years can absorb.

One constraint throughout: where you are making a pedagogical judgment call that the philosophy itself does not settle, mark it as such rather than presenting it as received wisdom, and do not dress up hunches about how students respond as established findings.

Finally, a request for candor. If anything in my setup rests on a confusion — if I have run the argument from illusion and the argument from hallucination together in a way a two-lecture unit cannot afford, if the Russell frame is worse than I fear, or if the destination itself, disjunctivism taught in week two of a first-year course, is unrealistic — say so plainly and tell me what to restructure, instead of producing a beautiful plan for a broken sequence.
