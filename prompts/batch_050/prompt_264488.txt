You are a philosopher of language specializing in pragmatics, with particular expertise in the saying/implicating distinction. Think carefully through the following cases before answering; I want a principled account, not case-by-case commentary.

I'm trying to work out what I actually think about what speakers communicate beyond the literal content of their words, and I'd like to know what principle my verdicts add up to. Here they are:

1. A letter of recommendation for a philosophy job says only that the candidate's attendance is regular and his handwriting is beautiful. The writer has communicated that the candidate is a poor philosopher, and I think he can be blamed for that communication -- if he later insists "I never said anything against him," he's being dishonest. Yet I also think that if the candidate sued him for defamation, the writer should win, because he said nothing false. I want to hold both of these.

2. When a colleague tells me "Some of the students passed," she communicates that not all did. But "Some of the students passed -- in fact, all of them" strikes me as odd rather than contradictory.

3. When a man says "I'm meeting a woman this evening," he communicates that it's not his wife or his sister. But "I broke a finger this morning" communicating that it was his own finger feels to me like a different kind of thing entirely -- less like something worked out and more like part of what the sentence means here. I suspect one of these two intuitions has to go.

4. "Can you pass the salt?" at dinner is simply a request; I don't think the request is something the hearer infers or that the speaker could take back by adding "but I wasn't asking you to pass it." That addition seems merely bizarre, not like an honest clarification.

5. "If you eat your vegetables, you can have dessert" communicates that no vegetables means no dessert, and I think the parent has not actually promised that -- she's implied it.

Here is what I want from you. First, state the principle or small set of principles that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, precisely enough that I can apply it myself, and identify which case motivates each clause. Second, be honest about which verdicts no coherent principle can jointly save -- I suspect cases 1 and 3 each contain a tension, so tell me which verdict in each pair you would overturn and why. Third, apply your account to two cases I haven't listed: (a) "John has three children," said in answer to "How many children does John have?" versus said in answer to "Can John apply for the scholarship for parents with three or more children?"; (b) "The flight departs at 6 or 7." Tell me what your account predicts and whether the prediction matches what a reasonable speaker would take away.
