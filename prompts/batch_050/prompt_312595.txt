A colleague of mine recently inflated the client-billing figures on a project we share, and I found out, said nothing, and let the numbers stand in a report that went to our board. Since then a friend has been telling me, with some heat, that silence is complicity. I have been turning that sentence over for weeks and I still cannot tell what it is actually claiming, which is what I want from you.

Act as a moral philosopher with expertise in the ethics of complicity — participation in wrongdoing one did not cause. Think carefully and, before composing your answer, brainstorm the full space of things the slogan could mean. Then present your analysis as a menu of readings, not a single interpretation.

In particular, distinguish at least the following candidate theses, and add any I have missed: that by staying silent I causally contributed to the wrongdoing (made it likelier or easier to continue); that I did not cause anything but nonetheless participated in it, in whatever way philosophers think one can be complicit without making a difference to the outcome; that silence constitutes an omission, and the claim is really that omissions can be as culpable as actions given a duty to act; that silence is a form of endorsement or signalling — an expressive wrong independent of any effect on the wrongdoing itself; that the slogan is not about my blameworthiness at all but about what my silence reveals about my character; and that it is purely rhetorical pressure, a way of converting inaction into a punishable stance without a precise thesis behind it.

For each reading, tell me: whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; what precise notion of complicity or responsibility it presupposes (causal, participatory, expressive, character-based); and whether it actually supports my friend's verdict about me, given that my silence plausibly changed nothing — the figures would have stood whether or not I spoke, since only he could have corrected them without triggering a review. Where a reading would let me off the hook, say so honestly rather than rescuing the slogan. Finally, say which reading you think people who use the phrase in ordinary political and workplace contexts most likely intend, and whether that reading is one of the defensible ones.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your overall verdict on which reading is strongest and whether it condemns me — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
