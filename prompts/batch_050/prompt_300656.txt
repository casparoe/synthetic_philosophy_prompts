In the seminar where we have been working through the first book of Aristotle's Politics, we keep returning to the natural slavery doctrine, and I have come to suspect that the scholarly dispute about it is stalled in an interesting way rather than merely unsettled. The passage our disagreement keeps orbiting is early in Book I (1252a–b), in Ellis's translation:

"It is also from natural causes that some beings command and others obey, that each may obtain their mutual safety; for a being who is endowed with a mind capable of reflection and forethought is by nature the superior and governor, whereas he whose excellence is merely corporeal is foremost to be a slave; whence it follows that the different state of master and slave is equally advantageous to both."

Roughly three camps form around this. One reads the passage as a serious application of Aristotle's natural teleology: the criterion (deliberative reason versus bodily capacity) is doing real classificatory work, the mutual-advantage claim is sincere, and the doctrine stands or falls with the biology behind it. A second camp treats the argument as ideology wearing a philosophical costume: the criterion is reverse-engineered to license an institution Aristotle had no intention of questioning, and the proof is that he never tells us how to identify the naturally slavish in practice, and concedes a few chapters later that nature's intention to mark masters and slaves bodily often fails, and that many actual slaves are slaves only by war and convention. A third camp holds that the two readings need not compete: the theory can be both sincerely derived and practically inert, a criterion in search of instances that Aristotle himself half-admits may not exist.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not really a disagreement about what the Greek text says — everyone cites the same sentences — but about what a text's internal tensions are evidence of: for one side, the concessions in chapters 5 through 7 show the theory's limits and hence its sincerity; for the other, they show the theory's failure and hence its apologetic function. If that is right, the two sides are applying different standards for when incoherence counts against sincerity, and the dispute will not move until that meta-question is addressed.

So my question is diagnostic rather than adjudicative. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a hidden factual question (about Aristotle's biological writings, or about the actual demographics of fourth-century slavery) that would settle it if answered? Is it a disagreement about interpretive charity and what charity is for? Is it a verbal disagreement, with the camps using "sincere," "ideological," and "coherent" in different senses? Or is it a bedrock difference about how political philosophy relates to the institutions around it, of a kind that no further reading of Politics I could dissolve? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a different way of reading the concessions, evidence from outside the text, a reframing of the question — or should we expect no convergence, and if so, why not?
