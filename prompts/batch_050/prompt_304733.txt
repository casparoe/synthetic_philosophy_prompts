At a philosophy reading group I'm part of, two members got into an argument after a session on Kierkegaard's critique of the press and his idea of "the public," and afterward both asked me to settle it. I couldn't, so I'm bringing it here.

Marta argued that Kierkegaard's analysis straightforwardly condemns the modern internet. His complaint about the press, she said, was that it lets people hold and spread opinions without ever standing behind anything, and anonymous forums and comment sections are that problem perfected: speech multiplies endlessly while no speaker is ever answerable for any of it. On her reading, an anonymous account is the purest possible instance of what he was attacking.

Daniel replied that this gets Kierkegaard almost backwards. Kierkegaard published much of his most important work under invented author-names, carefully constructed so that he personally would stay out of the reader's way. So the mere fact of unsigned or pseudonymous speech clearly wasn't the target. The target, Daniel said, was abstraction: "the public" as a phantom that everyone invokes and no one is. By that standard, a pseudonymous writer who sustains a consistent, recognizable voice for years is exactly the kind of speaker Kierkegaard could respect, while a broadcast segment purporting to report "what the public thinks" is the true heir of what he despised.

Marta answered that the pseudonym point proves too much. Kierkegaard's pseudonyms, she said, were openly acknowledged literary devices — he admitted to constructing them, and he signed plenty of other work under his own name. That is nothing like forum anonymity, whose whole function is to guarantee that the speaker never has to become anyone in particular.

Daniel conceded the literary-device point but insisted it actually supports him: what matters for Kierkegaard isn't the name on the byline but whether some concrete, existing individual owns the words and can be held to them. A pseudonym can do that; a name attached to "public opinion" cannot. Anonymity, he said, is a distraction from the real criterion.

Please adjudicate this dispute move by move. For each of the four moves above, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Then address the deeper question they both gesture at: what, precisely, does Kierkegaard's critique take to be defective about public discourse — the anonymity, the abstraction, the absence of personal commitment, or something else — and does that criterion cut differently against modern anonymous speech than against old-fashioned appeals to "the public"? Finally, say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both are missing. Please write your answer in American English.
