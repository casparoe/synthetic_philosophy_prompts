I recently signed up for a subscription service run by a small company whose entire business model rests on behavioral forecasting. Here's how it works: each month, before shipping anything, their algorithm builds a detailed model of me from my past behavior and predicts whether I would, if asked, voluntarily pay double the subscription fee for that month as a gesture of support. If the model predicts I would pay double, they ship me a premium box whose contents are worth far more than double the fee. If the model predicts I wouldn't, they ship nothing at all and charge nothing. The strange part is that they never actually charge me double — the prediction is all that matters. The service has been running for two years, and by all accounts their model has essentially never been wrong about anyone.

Last week a friend who works at the company let something slip over drinks. She said the model's most recent run predicted that I am the kind of person who would *not* pay double — largely because I keep calculating that paying is irrational, since nothing actually depends on my payment. But she also said the model is a near-perfect mirror of my reasoning: whatever I genuinely conclude I should do, it will have concluded for me. So if I now decide that I *am* someone who would pay double, then (because the model tracks me so well) the prediction it already made must have been that I would pay — and the premium box, which is what I want, is what gets shipped. The prediction was finalized yesterday, but the causal order seems backwards: my decision today cannot change a prediction made yesterday, and yet acting as the kind of person who pays appears to be the only way the good outcome happens.

I've gone around in circles on this. One part of me says the prediction is a settled fact, so deliberating about what kind of person I am is pointless posturing. Another part says that since the model just *is* my reasoning mirrored back at me, refusing to pay would be a way of defeating myself. I haven't told the company what I intend. Setting aside whether their forecasting claims are plausible — take the setup as given — what should I actually decide, and what is the right way to think through a case where the outcome depends not on what my action causes, but on what my action reveals about a decision that has, in some sense, already been made on my behalf?
