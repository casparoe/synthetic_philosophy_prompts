I’m giving a 15-minute philosophy department talk next week and could use some serious prep. Please act as both an Augustine scholar and a hard-nosed ethics professor, and start with a brief brainstorming map before turning it into a polished objection-and-response sheet.

Background: this recently stopped being abstract for me because I lied to my sister about where my brother was after he asked me not to tell her. She found out, and now the whole family is arguing about whether the lie was justified because I was protecting his privacy. I definitely don’t want the talk to become family therapy, but that case got me thinking.

My thesis is that Augustine’s absolute opposition to lying is better understood through what assertion does than through a simple rule that falsehoods are always forbidden. Roughly: when I assert something, I present my statement as evidence of what I actually think. Lying breaks that connection even if nobody is successfully deceived. So on my reading, the central wrong is not merely causing a false belief in someone else; it is intentionally giving false testimony about one’s own mind. That means a failed lie is still a lie. But a literally true statement meant to mislead someone belongs in a different moral category: it may be manipulative or otherwise wrong, but it is not the same direct misuse of assertion. I am not claiming Augustine would approve of misleading people.

I’ll apply the view to three cases:

1. falsely denying that I know where my brother is, in order to protect his privacy  
2. giving a technically true but carefully misleading answer so my sister draws the wrong conclusion  
3. lying about a surprise party, where everyone accepts the convention and no lasting distrust results

The audience will be awkwardly mixed. There are two Augustine specialists who care most about whether my reading is historically accurate and whether I’m smoothing over changes or tensions in his thought. One Kantian ethicist will suspect I’m just repackaging Kant in Augustinian language. One social epistemologist thinks the wrong of lying is fundamentally damage to shared information networks, not some internal relation between speech and belief. A couple of graduate students are pretty consequentialist and will immediately bring up dangerous or abusive people trying to get information. Also one person who knows contemporary philosophy of language and will push on whether assertion really means presenting something as evidence of one’s belief.

Please give me:

- the objections in roughly the order they are most likely to come up during Q&A  
- each objection phrased naturally in the voice of the person most likely to raise it  
- an explanation of whether the objection is mainly textual, conceptual, ethical, or just based on a misunderstanding  
- what I should concede, what I should firmly hold onto, and a concise reply I could actually give in under a minute  
- the weakest part of my thesis, especially the objection I’m secretly hoping nobody asks  
- any place where I should revise my wording before the talk so I don’t overclaim  
- one sharper formulation of my thesis that could survive the strongest objections

Don’t give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. I’m much more interested in pressure-testing the position than in pretending I’ve read everything. Also, be candid if the distinction between lying and misleading-by-truth is doing more work than it can bear.
