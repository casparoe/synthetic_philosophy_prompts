I retired three years ago after forty-odd years as a physiologist (nerve and muscle work, mostly) and have since fallen rather badly into philosophy — it started as a busman's holiday with Aristotle and has spiralled from there. An old friend of mine lectures in religious studies and has been snowed under this term, so I've been helping him mark a pile of first-year essays. Don't tell his department.

The essay question was this:

**Read the following passage from Maimonides, The Guide of the Perplexed, Part III, chapter 32 (trans. M. Friedländer, 1903):**

"Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed. ... it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner ... By this Divine plan it was effected that the traces of idolatry were blotted out, and the truly great principle of our faith, the Existence and Unity of God, was firmly established; this result was thus obtained without deterring or confusing the minds of the people by the abolition of the service to which they were accustomed and which alone was familiar to them."

**(a) Reconstruct, in your own words, the argument of this passage. What problem was the command to sacrifice meant to solve, and how? (b) A critic says: 'On this account, the sacrificial laws are arbitrary rules whose only point is obedience, and Maimonides must regard them as temporary scaffolding that lapses once idolatry is forgotten.' Drawing on the passage and on your wider knowledge of Maimonides, assess this claim. (300–400 words.)**

Here is one student's answer, names removed:

"Maimonides' argument turns on the power of habit. Worship in the ancient world simply meant sacrifice, incense and bowing down in temples; this was the only religious vocabulary the Israelites possessed. Just as one cannot jump from one extreme to another in nature, a lawgiver cannot demand that a people abandon overnight the only form of worship it knows. Had Moses commanded a purely inward, wordless service — as if a prophet today told us to serve God 'in thought, and not by any action' — the people would simply have rejected the Law. God's solution was therefore redirection rather than abolition: the familiar apparatus of altar, temple, priesthood and sacrifice was retained but transferred to His own name, with sacrifice to any other being strictly forbidden and the cult confined to a single sanctuary. In this way the forms of idolatry were made to serve the fight against idolatry, until 'the traces of idolatry were blotted out' and belief in God's unity was secured.

This shows, I think, that the sacrificial laws belong to the hukkim, the statutes for which, on Maimonides' view, no reason can be given and whose whole point is training in obedience; the passage itself concedes as much when it admits the commandments were not commanded 'for their own sake'. They are instruments of divine pedagogy rather than acts with any intrinsic rational point.

The critic is therefore also right that sacrifice is temporary scaffolding. Its whole rationale presupposes a world steeped in idolatry; once that world has vanished, the means loses its point. Maimonides accordingly envisages the messianic age as one in which the Temple cult is superseded and the service of God continues in the purified form of prayer and knowledge alone — the very extreme towards which the concession was leading all along. The sacrificial laws resemble the milk provided for the young mammal earlier in the chapter: perfectly fitted to a developmental stage, and outgrown once that stage is passed."
