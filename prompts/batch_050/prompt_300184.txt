Eighteen months ago I lost most of my sense of smell to a virus, and it came back imperfectly over the better part of a year. Coffee now tastes flat to me, and here is the part that has started to genuinely unsettle me: I cannot tell whether coffee tastes the way it always did and I have merely lost the capacity to appreciate it, or whether the experience itself is different. A friend who teaches philosophy of mind told me I am living inside Dennett's coffee-taster case -- the one about Chase and Sanborn, the two fictional tasters who both report that their Maxwell House no longer tastes as it used to, one blaming the coffee's flavor, the other blaming his own changed standards -- and she then walked me through an argument she finds convincing and I find alarming, because it seems to prove far too much. I have written it out as carefully as I can:

(1) If qualia exist, they are properties of experience on which the subject is the final authority; a quale whose own bearer could be permanently and in principle undecided about it is not doing any of the work qualia were posited to do. (2) In my situation, either the qualitative character of my coffee experience changed while my standards stayed fixed, or my standards changed while the character stayed fixed, and nothing available to me settles which -- memory runs through the same fallible machinery as present judgment, and no behavioral test distinguishes the two hypotheses either. (3) What my own first-person perspective cannot settle even in principle, nobody can settle at all: third-person evidence, however sophisticated, can at most show which of my internal machinery changed, never whether the character of the experience did, since that character is constituted by how things seem to me. (4) A supposed fact that cannot in principle be settled by anyone, including the very person whose fact it allegedly is, is no fact at all; it is an idle theoretical posit and ought to be quined. (5) Therefore there is no fact about whether my coffee experience today has the same qualitative character as it did two years ago -- and since every comparison of experiences, across time within one person or across persons at a time, runs through equally fallible channels, there are no facts about qualitative character, full stop.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd, and the absurdity seems generalizable in a way that should embarrass the argument itself: by the standard set in (3) and (4), there is no fact about whether my memory of my grandmother's face resembles the face itself, since I cannot now hold them side by side; no fact about whether the painting in the museum today is the same one that hung there in 1900, since diachronic identity is always judged through fallible records; arguably no fact about whether a pain I am only half attending to actually hurts. We are not about to quine grandmothers, paintings, and pains. But when I try to locate the misstep, each premise looks defensible on its own -- I am especially reluctant to give up (2), since it is simply a description of my own case, and (1) seems like the minimal job description for a quale. Please give me a committed answer, not a menu: name the single step where you think the argument goes wrong, explain why that step looked fine to me, state honestly what rejecting it costs, and tell me why the alternatives -- including just accepting the eliminativist conclusion -- cost more.
