Read Kafka's The Trial through the Hart–Fuller debate. On its face the novel looks like a gift for Fuller: K. is arrested without charge, the Court sits in attic offices, its law is never published and K. cant even find it, proceedings drag on without any prospect of an ending, and in the doorkeeper parable the Law clearly exists but a man spends his whole life waiting to be let in. Grade Kafka's Court against Fullers eight principles of legality from the King Rex story -- which desiderata does it actually fail, and does total failure mean, as Fuller thought, that there's no law here at all rather than just bad law? But then give Hart his turn and do it honestly: the Court is weirdly stable, ritualized, internally consistent -- it has advocates, procedures, its own customs -- so maybe Kafka shows that an order can satisfy enough of Fuller's internal morality and still be a nightmare, which is basically Hart's reply that the inner morality of law is a morality of efficacy rather than goodness, and that declaring evil law to be no law dodges the real question of whether it should be obeyed. Which reading does the book as a whole support? I especially want to know where the novel resists the Hart–Fuller framing entirely -- my hunch is that the scariest thing in it is K.'s instant acceptance of the Court's authority over him, and I don't see that either Hart or Fuller has much to say about that. I'm writing an essay on this, so give me the strongest case for each side before you pick one.
