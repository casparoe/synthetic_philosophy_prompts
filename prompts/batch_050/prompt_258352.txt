A colleague of mine assembled the following argument after a seminar on gender and occupational choice, and although every one of us wants to reject the conclusion, none of us could locate the step we wanted to reject. I would like a diagnosis.

First, the empirical background as we understand it: countries that rank highest on standard measures of gender equality — equal legal standing, high female labor-force participation, strong educational outcomes for girls — tend to have a smaller share of women among graduates in fields like engineering and computer science than countries that rank far lower, where economic necessity and family pressure push many women toward lucrative technical employment whether or not it matches their inclinations. This pattern is sometimes called the gender-equality paradox.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) Career choices are shaped by two kinds of factor: external constraints (economic necessity, discriminatory gatekeeping, legal restriction) and the chooser's own preferences.
(2) As a society becomes more gender-egalitarian, it removes external constraints on women's occupational choices.
(3) The fewer the external constraints on a choice, the more that choice expresses the chooser's own preferences rather than someone else's imposition.
(4) Therefore, in the most gender-egalitarian societies, women's occupational choices express women's own preferences more fully than anywhere else.
(5) If an outcome pattern is the unconstrained expression of the preferences of the people involved, there is no injustice in it for policy to correct.
(6) Therefore, the relatively low share of women in engineering in egalitarian countries is not an injustice to be corrected.
(7) Moreover, since the gendered pattern in those countries is the freest expression of preference on record, a country in which the pattern is more pronounced is, other things equal, closer to gender justice than one in which it is less so.
(8) Hence the Nordic combination of high formal equality and high occupational gender segregation is not an embarrassment for egalitarian policy but its best result, and the policy goal of gender balance in technical fields is at best pointless and at worst a program for overriding free choices.

We find (8) incredible. The argument seems to prove too much: it would imply that every advance in egalitarian institutions that happens to coincide with more sharply gendered choices is thereby an advance in justice, and that any attempt to shift gendered outcomes in an already egalitarian society is an interference with freedom rather than a remedy for anything.

Which step should we reject, and what does rejecting it cost? A good answer should name the weakest premise or inference, explain why it looked innocent in context, and price the main alternatives: what one gives up by denying that egalitarian institutions actually remove the relevant constraints, versus denying that unconstrained choice straightforwardly reveals genuine preference, versus denying that justice is exhausted by non-interference with choice. If forced, we would rather keep (1) through (4) than (5), but we want the price of every option stated plainly.
