I'm a graduate student TAing a first-year undergraduate seminar on rational choice, and I've been assigned a single seventy-five-minute session on the dollar auction and the escalation of commitment. The students will have seen basic expected-value reasoning and the prisoner's dilemma but no game theory beyond that, and I can assume nothing about their economics background. I've become convinced that the standard way this topic gets taught -- present the auction, watch the bids climb past a dollar, and conclude that people are irrational about sunk costs -- is actually the least interesting version of it, because the deeper point is that each individual bid is locally sensible: once two bidders are in, bidding $1.05 for a dollar beats losing the 95 cents you already bid, and that's true at every stage, so the trap is structural rather than a failure of arithmetic. I was arguing about this with some friends over dinner, and the person who insisted she'd "just never bid" couldn't explain how that policy generalizes to the real cases -- litigation, bidding wars over houses, doubling down on a failing product line -- where you're typically already inside the auction before you recognize it as one. I want the session to land that distinction.

Plan the session rather than delivering it. Map the misconceptions I should expect -- I'm anticipating at least "the bidders are just being stupid," "the lesson is simply never to bid," and a conflation with the winner's curse, where overpayment comes from uncertainty about the item's value rather than from the payment rule itself -- and say where in the arc each one should be headed off and with what move. Tell me how to transition from the auction to escalation cases outside money, which one carrying non-monetary example I should build that part around, and what would count as the students actually getting it: give me a final question or exercise that would distinguish a student who has internalized the structure from one who has merely learned to say "sunk cost fallacy."
