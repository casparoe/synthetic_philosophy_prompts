Got a job for you in collective intentionality. Construct me a thought experiment where Bratman's account of shared intention and Gilbert's joint commitment account give different verdicts on whether two people are genuinely doing something together -- specifically, Bratman's conditions come out satisfied but Gilbert would say there's no joint action happening. Constraints: the scenario has to be mundane and one paragraph max (two neighbors clearing a fallen tree, not spies or orchestras); both parties are competent adults with no deception about what the activity is; and you may not use coercion to split the accounts, since that's basically Gilbert's own stock move and I want the divergence generated by something else -- ideally something about how the arrangement gets set up rather than about what either party privately intends.

After the scenario: (1) run both analyses out loud -- show that each Bratman condition (interlocking intentions, meshing subplans, common knowledge of all that, mutual responsiveness in pursuit) is actually met by the facts of the case, then identify exactly which requirement for a joint commitment Gilbert would say fails and why; (2) spell out what follows on each view about whether one party is entitled to rebuke the other for bailing halfway through, and show your case genuinely delivers that contrast rather than just asserting it; (3) name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, and tell me what happens if you remove it -- does the case collapse into full-blown shared agency on both accounts, or into two people acting side by side on neither?; (4) give the strongest reply available to a Bratman defender who wants to say the case shows the pair *is* acting together and that Gilbert's rebuke test is simply the wrong test for jointness.

The point of the exercise is to figure out whether the normativity of joint action (who owes what to whom mid-project) can float free of shared intention, so a case that just stipulates the parties feel obligated is cheating -- the obligations, or their absence, have to fall out of the structure. Bonus points if the scenario is one undergrads would actually find gripping, since I'm workshopping this with a seminar next week and they can smell a contrived case from a mile away.
