I keep encountering a claim in discussions of the liar paradox — on blogs, in comment threads, occasionally in print — that the essential ingredient of every paradox in that family is self-reference: eliminate the loop, and no such paradox can arise. I believe the following two constructions, taken together, refute that claim twice over, and I want to know whether the phenomenon they illustrate is an established topic with a name of its own.

First construction. Take an infinite list of sentences, one for each natural number, where sentence n says: every sentence after sentence n on this list is false. No sentence refers to itself, and no sentence refers to the list as a whole; each sentence speaks only of sentences strictly later in the ordering, of which there are always infinitely many. Yet working through the cases yields a contradiction. If sentence n is true, then sentence n+1 is false, which means some sentence later than n+1 is true — contradicting what sentence n says. And if every sentence on the list is false, then for any sentence n, what sentence n asserts — that everything after it is false — is exactly right, so sentence n is true after all.

Second construction. Take a single sentence that says: if this very sentence is true, then the moon is made of green cheese. This one admittedly retains a loop, but it appears to need nothing like negation — no sentence calls anything false or untrue — and yet it seems to prove an arbitrary absurdity. Suppose the sentence is true. Then what it says obtains, so the moon is made of green cheese. Since we derived that consequent from the assumption of the sentence's truth, the conditional the sentence expresses holds; but that conditional just is the sentence, so the sentence is true simpliciter; and applying the conditional one final time, the cheese follows without any assumption at all.

My questions:

1. Are these known constructions, and under what names? I have a vague sense that the first circulates in online logic discussions, but I do not know whether either appears in the formal literature or is treated seriously there.
2. More importantly: is there an established umbrella concept for the general phenomenon — paradoxes of the liar family that survive the removal of whichever ingredient was thought essential (the loop, or the truth predicate in combination with negation)? I am not looking for the word "paradox" simpliciter. I want to know whether anyone has carved out this specific class, stated what structure all its members share, and shown that the same structure generates each of them — or whether the literature treats each construction as a one-off curiosity with no common core.
3. If such a unified account exists, how does it classify the first construction? I have seen the objection that generating every sentence on the list from one uniform recipe smuggles circularity back in through the recipe itself, since each sentence is really the same predicate applied to the whole remainder of the list. I cannot tell whether this objection is generally respected or generally dismissed, and it matters to me, because if it succeeds then the first construction was never loop-free and my counterexample to the opening claim collapses.

The label matters less to me than the carving. If there is no established umbrella term, I would rather have a precise statement of the shared structure, together with the boundary cases: why benign infinite regresses do not instantiate it, why harmless self-reference like "this sentence is in English" does not instantiate it, and what the minimal extra ingredient is that tips a structure over into paradox.
