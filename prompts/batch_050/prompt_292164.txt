A colleague of mine, an entomologist, is retiring next month after thirty-five years spent building the empirical case that insects — his life's subjects are honeybees — plausibly feel something like pain, and that this ought to matter to how we farm and experiment. I've been asked to give the toast at his retirement dinner. The room will be half entomologists and philosophers of mind who know the sentience literature well, and half his family and neighbors who mostly know him as the man who talks to bees.

I'd like the toast (about five minutes when spoken) to be built around a single idea he and I have argued about for decades: that sentience is not a switch that's on or off but a dimmer, and that our obligations track the dimmer's position rather than waiting for certainty about where the switch flips. The catch is that he thinks the dimmer metaphor undersells his empirical work — he believes the evidence for bees is strong, not partial — while I've always pressed him on the continuum problem: if sentience fades by degrees, we need a principled story about why it stops somewhere short of the thermostat. The toast should stage this disagreement affectionately, as the shape of a friendship, without resolving it in my favor on his night.

Two constraints. First, no panpsychism jokes: he finds them tedious and has heard them all. Second, the speech needs one concrete image or anecdote that lets the family half of the room grasp what "degrees of sentience" even means before the toasts go around — something drawn from ordinary experience rather than from the literature, since this must work for people who have never heard the phrase "moral status" and would find it chilling if they had.
