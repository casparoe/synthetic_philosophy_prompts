I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of religion, and I need one essay question on Molinism and middle knowledge. The problem with most Molinism questions is that students can answer them by reciting the standard three-moments picture — natural knowledge, middle knowledge, free knowledge — plus a sentence about reconciling divine providence with libertarian freedom. I want a question on which that recitation earns a bare pass at best.

Here is the crux I want the question built around. A critic of Molinism presses a dilemma about counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (conditionals of the form: if person P were placed in fully specified circumstances C, P would freely do A). Either such a counterfactual is grounded in the free choice the creature actually makes, or it is not. If it is, the counterfactual seems to become true too late: at the logically prior moment when God is deciding which world to create, no choice exists yet, so there is nothing for middle knowledge to deliver. If it is not, then nothing about the creature makes the counterfactual true; God merely discovers a fixed landscape of brute conditional facts he did not author and cannot alter, which sits badly with divine sovereignty. The question should ask students to reconstruct both horns precisely, to identify which Molinist desideratum each horn threatens (explanatory priority for providence on the first; something like aseity or authorship of truths on the second), to evaluate the strongest available Molinist reply to at least one horn — for instance, the reply that grounding in the actual choice involves explanatory rather than temporal order, or the reply that individual essences suffice to ground such truths without any concrete choice occurring — and to reach a verdict on whether middle knowledge still explains how God reconciles meticulous providence with libertarian freedom, or merely relabels a residue of luck.

Please deliver three things:

1. The exam question itself, phrased as students will see it, with the dilemma stated in the question text. It should be doable in 45 minutes, worth 25 marks, and should not require students to have memorized any particular philosopher's terminology beyond the core vocabulary of the debate.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words — the quality of answer you'd give full marks.
3. A marking scheme as a table with four columns: Component (the move being assessed), Marks, What full marks require (the specific content a top answer must contain), and Cap-triggering error (the characteristic mistake that should hold an answer below full marks on that component — e.g., conflating logical with temporal priority, treating the sovereignty horn as a version of the problem of evil, or evaluating a reply no Molinist would actually give). After the table, add one short paragraph stating the single feature that most reliably separates an A answer from a B answer.
