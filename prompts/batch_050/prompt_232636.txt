I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. Last week on our engineering Slack, during a thread about why teams chronically underestimate project timelines, I suggested that overconfidence about deadlines might be an evolved adaptation: maybe groups whose members were unrealistically optimistic about what they could accomplish ended up attempting and completing more, so selection favored the bias. A coworker replied, "That's a just-so story," and the thread effectively died. Since then I've noticed people deploy that accusation against all sorts of evolutionary explanations — of taste preferences, of gossip, of religious belief — as though it ends the conversation. I want to know what work it can actually do. Lay out the distinct precise claims the phrase "just-so story" might be making, ordered from the trivial (a hypothesis offered without evidence is merely a hypothesis) to the radical (adaptive historical explanations are in principle unfalsifiable, or most traits aren't adaptations at all but byproducts of development, architectural constraints, or drift). For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible methodological principle, or simply false. Then commit: under the most defensible reading, does my deadline-optimism suggestion genuinely deserve the accusation, and what would a non-lazy evolutionary explanation of that trait have to establish — what evidence separates a genuine adaptation from a byproduct in a case like mine? I'd rather you take definite positions and defend them, including on where my coworker was right, than hand me a neutral menu of options to choose between.
