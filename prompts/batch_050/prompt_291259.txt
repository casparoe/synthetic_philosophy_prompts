I serve on the secretariat of a national climate council, and our chief adviser has an unusually consistent record of verdicts on proposed instruments, but I cannot work out the principle behind it. Her record so far:

- A carbon tax on all fossil fuels, rising on a pre-announced schedule, with all revenue returned to citizens as an equal per-capita rebate: endorsed.
- An earlier version of that same tax proposal, without the rebate: rejected, on the grounds that it was distributively unacceptable even if environmentally sound.
- A cap-and-trade system for heavy industry with no floor on permit prices: rejected, on the grounds that a collapsing permit price would let emitters buy cheap compliance rather than invest in abatement.
- A statutory ban on the sale of new internal-combustion vehicles from 2035: endorsed.
- Purchasing international carbon offsets to meet part of the national target: rejected, on the grounds that the national economy must actually be transformed, not merely compensated for elsewhere.
- A large subsidy programme for home insulation and heat pumps, funded from general taxation: endorsed.
- A mandatory cap on annual flight numbers at the country's major airports, intended to cut aviation demand directly: rejected.

Next month we vote on a personal carbon allowance scheme: every adult resident receives an equal, free, annually declining allowance of emissions permits covering household energy and personal transport fuel; allowances are fully tradeable on an open market, so low emitters can sell their surplus and high emitters must buy more.

I would like a prediction of her verdict, with the reasoning laid out properly: first, enumerate the distinct principles that are each consistent with all seven prior verdicts — I suspect at least three fit the record, for example one about constraining supply rather than rationing household consumption, one about distributional compensation, and one about guaranteed versus merely incentivised domestic abatement, but please refine or replace these; second, state what each candidate principle predicts about the personal allowance scheme, and identify which pairs of principles diverge on it; third, give me your best single prediction and the considerations that favour it — including which prior verdict carries the most evidential weight as the closest analog; and fourth, if the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, describe the one further hypothetical proposal whose verdict would most efficiently tell the competing principles apart, and say why that proposal is maximally discriminating rather than merely difficult.
