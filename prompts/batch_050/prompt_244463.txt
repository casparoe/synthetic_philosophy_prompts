For a capstone project in my high school's debate league, my partner and I have to stop arguing and produce a single adversarial-collaboration statement that both of us can sign. I favor substantially stricter gun regulation; she defends a strong individual right to own and carry firearms and is skeptical of most proposed restrictions. Please draft the document we should produce.

Structure it in four sections:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of statements both sides can sign without reservations. Candidates I have in mind: that the research on whether shall-issue concealed-carry laws raise or lower violent crime is genuinely inconclusive, with credible studies pointing in opposite directions; that survey-based estimates of defensive gun use exceed crime-victimization-survey estimates by orders of magnitude, so the true frequency is uncertain; that a majority of U.S. gun deaths are suicides rather than homicides; that the Second Amendment's text and the historical record underdetermine many modern regulatory questions, even given the Supreme Court's recent rulings establishing an individual right; and that both individual liberty and the prevention of wrongful death are serious values, whatever their relative weight.

2. Disagreements table. The exact points where we still differ, each row phrased so that each of us would accept the wording of our own position. These should be genuine disagreements rather than restated slogans: whether the right to own arms is more analogous to freedom of speech (restrictions require very strong justification) or to the licensed operation of dangerous equipment (regulation is ordinary governance); whether defensive gun use is a benefit that smart regulation could mostly preserve or an unavoidable casualty of restricting access; whether a policy should be assessed by its aggregate effect on deaths or by whether it infringes a right independently of outcomes; and whether widespread gun ownership carries deterrent or social-trust benefits that death statistics do not capture.

3. Cruxes with predictions recorded in advance. For each crux, identify the empirical study, natural experiment, or conceptual argument both of us agree would bear on it, and write down beforehand what each side predicts and what result would actually move each of us. Consider: what a well-designed study of a state liberalizing or tightening its carry laws would have to show to count as informative; what it would imply if suicide rates did or did not fall through method substitution after reduced gun access; and what kind of evidence could narrow the gap between the competing estimates of defensive gun use.

4. Verbal-dispute audit. Flag any part of our disagreement you judge to be about words rather than substance — for instance, whether "gun control" picks out the same policies for each of us, or whether "right" means legal permission versus moral claim — and say which part and why.

Constraints: no bibliography and no papers cited by author or title; describe evidence by its design and findings instead. Keep every formulation neutral enough that both of us would actually sign it.
