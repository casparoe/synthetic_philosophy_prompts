I'm teaching a film-and-philosophy seminar and we're doing Kurosawa's Rashomon. Read it through the debate over historical objectivity: Ranke's idea that the historian's job is to show how things actually were, against Carr's line that facts only become historical facts through the historian's selection and framing, and Hayden White's claim that any historical account gets its shape from being emplotted like fiction. The lazy reading of the film is pure scepticism — four incompatible testimonies, no verdict, truth is lost. Push against that. Each witness's story seems to follow a different genre: the bandit gives a swashbuckling romance of the honorable outlaw, the dead samurai (through the medium) gives an honor tragedy, the wife gives a victim melodrama, and the woodcutter something more deflationary — which is almost a lab demonstration of White's emplotment thesis. But the film also gives us the final account and the scene with the abandoned baby at the gate, and it clearly wants the priest's restored faith to land. Does the film thereby smuggle in a minimal Rankean claim — that some things did happen a certain way and that it matters which account is closest? Where does it resist the full Carr/White reading, and what does it show about testimony and self-serving narration that the prose arguments don't? Don't bother quoting lines, I know the film. After the reading, give me a table with columns: witness; genre/emplotment of their story; what the story does for the teller; whether the film treats any part of it as fact.
