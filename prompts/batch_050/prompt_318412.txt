I spent forty years as an experimental solid-state physicist before retiring, and only in the last few years have I begun reading philosophy in earnest, starting with the literature on personal identity. What puzzles me is an asymmetry I keep encountering. Many writers seem to accept that a person would survive the gradual replacement of her neurons by functionally identical prosthetic components -- one neuron at a time, over months, with the patient awake, conversational, and reporting no change throughout -- while denying that she would survive being scanned and reinstantiated as a digital copy, even a perfect one. I have discussed this with a colleague from my old department, and we found ourselves on opposite sides: he insists the gradual procedure is obviously survival because there is no moment at which anyone dies, whereas I argue that if the perfectly copied end-state of the gradual procedure would not be the original person, then the gradual procedure is simply a death so slow that nobody, including the victim, notices it -- and conversely, if the gradual procedure preserves the person, the copy must too, since the two are functionally indistinguishable. Neither of us has managed to move the other.

I would like you to draft the joint statement my colleague and I should be able to produce if we conducted our dispute as a proper adversarial collaboration. Take the two camps to be, on one side, the continuity view: personal survival requires an unbroken causal and organizational thread, which gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement preserves and instantaneous scan-and-copy does not; on the other side, the instability view: the continuity position is untenable because it must either accept both procedures as survival or neither, there being no principled functional line between slow replacement and fast copying. The document should contain, first, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign -- for instance, both should concede that the patient's sincere moment-to-moment reports during the procedure do not by themselves settle whether she survives it, and both should say what relevance, if any, the ordinary turnover of matter in living bodies has to the dispute. Second, give the exact remaining disagreements, each formulated in wording its own side would accept without feeling caricatured. Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one camp to concede. I suspect these include whether personal identity is a further fact beyond physical and psychological continuity or something thinner and partly conventional, whether first-person anticipation of future experience is evidence of anything, and whether identity tracks the same stuff or only the same organization -- but correct my list if I have it wrong. For each crux, specify the thought experiment, argument, or feature of the gradual-replacement scenario that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that consideration will show.

Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a table of disagreements with one row per disagreement and columns for the neutral formulation, each camp's own statement of its position, the crux it turns on, and each camp's recorded prediction. Close with a final line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, the single crux both camps agree is most likely to decide the dispute.
