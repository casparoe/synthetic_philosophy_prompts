Teaching topic in my grad seminar this week was Kavka's toxin puzzle — the eccentric billionaire who wires you a million dollars if at midnight tonight you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tommorrow afternoon that will make you violently ill for a day, where the payout depends entirely on whether the midnight intention is in place, and not at all on whether you actually drink. One of my sharper students stayed after seminar with an argument he says shows the puzzle is easy, except that the argument seems to him to prove way too much, and neither of us could pin down the bad step. Roughly as he gave it, numbered by me:

(1) Rationality attaches to actions at the time of acting: an action is rational iff, at the moment you perform it, it best serves your ends given what is still up to you at that moment.

(2) At noon on toxin day, drinking gains you nothing — the million was already won or lost at midnight — and costs you a day of misery. So by (1), drinking at noon is irrational, and a rational agent won't drink.

(3) At midnight you can foresee all of (2). You know your future self, being rational, won't drink.

(4) You cannot genuinely intend to do something you are fully confident you will not do; at best you can hope, or pretend to intend.

(5) So at midnight you cannot form the intention, and the prize is unwinnable by an agent who stays rational throughout.

(6) But nothing in (1)–(4) is specific to the toxin. Any time a plan's payoff is locked in before the plan's costly step comes due — keeping a promise no one would ever catch you breaking, carrying out a threat after deterrence has already failed, running the last mile once the bet about finishing is settled — the same reasoning detaches the intention from the follow-through.

(7) So rational agents can never intend to do anything whose benefits have stopped depending on actually doing it — which would make promising, resolving, and ordinary self-command impossible across a huge range of ordinary life.

(7) is absurd: people keep costly resolutions and follow through on detached-payoff commitments constantly, and a theory of rationality that predicts they can't has gone wrong somewhere, not them. But each premise sounded fine as he said it. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Does your fix require giving up time-local rationality in (1), or the intention–belief link in (4), or does it mean biting down and saying drinking really can be rational? And whatever you reject, does your answer actually get the million back, or just explain why the overgeneralization in (6) fails?

One more thing — I'd like an honest confidence rating on your diagnosis, and please single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure of and say why.
