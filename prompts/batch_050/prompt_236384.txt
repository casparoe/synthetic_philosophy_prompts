I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and somehow I've ended up writing a long piece on the European Union's legitimacy problem — don't ask, it started as a story about AI governance and spiraled. Anyway, every Brussels person I've interviewed for it reaches for the same line eventually: the EU is like a bicycle, it has to keep moving forward or it falls over. Usually credited to Jacques Delors, though by now everyone says it. And it's doing real work in these conversations: the "democratic deficit" is unfortunate but tolerable, the thinking goes, because the alternative to another treaty, another deepening, isn't a stable status quo — it's collapse. Keep pedaling or crash.

The thing is, I went back to the Schuman Declaration (9 May 1950, the founding text, more or less) and the actual mechanism Robert Schuman describes doesn't sound much like a bicycle. Here's the bit that stuck with me, after he lays out the plan to pool French and German coal and steel: "In this way, there will be realised simply and speedily that fusion of interest which is indispensable to the establishment of a common economic system; it may be the leaven from which may grow a wider and deeper community between countries long opposed to one another by sanguinary divisions." Leaven! Yeast, not a bike. And notice "simply and speedily" — seventy-five years later, you tell me.

So here's what I want from you, and please write it in American English: audit the bicycle analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the rider, the pedaling, the forward motion, the falling over, and who, exactly, is supposed to be steering when there are 27 member states with different ideas about the destination. Then tell me where the analogy breaks: what does it quietly smuggle in (unidirectionality? motion being good in itself? collapse as the only alternative to further integration? the assumption that a political order, like a bike, can't just be parked?) and which of those imports are load-bearing for the "ever closer union, like it or not" conclusion. Then set it against Schuman's own metaphor: leaven works slowly, from the inside, only if the dough is right, and you can't force it by pedaling harder. Does the Schuman passage, read closely, actually support the bicycle logic — concrete achievements creating de facto solidarity that then has to be fed with new achievements — or is it a genuinely different picture of how integration legitimates itself, one where you can stop and consolidate without tipping over? And if the bicycle is the wrong vehicle (sorry), what's a better analogy for what the declaration is actually proposing? Finally, give me a verdict I can put in the piece: can the bicycle line carry the weight it's asked to carry — that pausing integration is existentially dangerous — or was it always just a nice way of making a contestable political choice sound like physics?
