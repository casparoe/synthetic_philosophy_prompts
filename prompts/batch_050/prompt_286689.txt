I am taking a seminar on collective decision-making and the ethics of catastrophic-risk prevention. My university’s research-integrity society has thirty trained members, each of whom is independently authorized to submit a confidential dossier about dangerous research to an external funding body. A single submission automatically opens a formal investigation and cannot be withdrawn. Even a false alarm can seriously damage a researcher’s career and discourage legitimate work, while failing to report a genuine danger could leave a harmful project funded.

The society’s rule is that each member should submit a dossier whenever, in that member’s judgment, doing so has positive expected value. In our current case, all thirty members have received the same core evidence, although each has somewhat different expertise and background knowledge. After discussing the case, twenty-two members conclude that an investigation would probably do more harm than good. Eight, including me, conclude the opposite. Everyone agrees that the disagreement is sincere and concerns how to weigh uncertain evidence rather than any private conflict of interest. Since any one of the eight can trigger the investigation, it now seems very likely that an investigation will occur even though a substantial majority thinks it should not.

I can see a troubling pattern in the rule itself: if members make independent errors and each is allowed to act alone, the cases most likely to be acted upon will be those that the most optimistic member overestimates, even when most members judge them negatively. But I am unsure what follows for me. If I still believe that submission is warranted after considering my peers’ views, would refraining amount to abandoning my own judgment merely because it is unpopular? Or does the fact that twenty-two equally informed people disagree give me a reason to distrust my estimate, independently of the particular arguments they have offered?

Please advise me on two levels. First, as one of the eight, should I submit, refrain, or insist on a further collective process—and how should the answer change if our judgments were formed independently rather than after discussion? Second, what institutional rule would be justified here? A majority vote and a supermajority requirement would prevent one unusually optimistic person from controlling the outcome, but they might also suppress a lone member who is uniquely right. I would like an answer that addresses the structural problem about who is selected to act, rather than merely recommending better communication or more careful deliberation.
