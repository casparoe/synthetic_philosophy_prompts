Someone in my philosophy group chat (he's a physics PhD, read one SEP article I think) posted this summary of the Hume vs. anti-Hume fight about laws of nature and I want to know how badly to trust it before I cite it in an argument:

"Humeans think the universe is just a giant mosaic of particle positions and field values, one damn thing after another, and the 'laws' are just whichever compression of that data strikes the best balance of simplicity and strength -- so laws are basically a human bookkeeping device, and aliens with different standards of simplicity would have different laws in the same universe. Anti-Humeans think the laws are real extra things, kind of like Platonic objects floating outside spacetime that push matter around. The upshot is that Humeans can't say anything causes anything, since the mosaic has no necessary connections, and anti-Humeans can explain why the laws hold, while the Humean just has to shrug."

My instincts: the "one damn thing after another" part tracks with what I've absorbed from rationalist-adjacent stuff about Hume, but the bit about aliens getting different laws sounds like a reductio dressed up as the view itself, and the "Platonic objects that push things around" line smells like a strawman of the governing-laws people. But I'm not confident, and I've never seen dispositional essentialism mentioned in any of this, which makes me suspicous the whole taxonomy is too clean.

Can you go claim by claim: what's a fair statement of the Humean best-system view, what's an overstated or loaded version of a real worry about it, what's an outright misrepresentation, and whether that closing "upshot" survives once the summary is fixed? Also, is there a live anti-Humean option that isn't laws-as-external-governors that he's just missing?
