Two members of my philosophy reading group, Mara and Dev, got into an argument after we discussed pain asymbolia — a condition in which patients with certain brain lesions report that they still feel pain but no longer mind it. Here is their exchange, as best I remember it:

Mara: The asymbolia cases show that the badness of pain can't lie in the sensation itself. The sensation is still there; what's gone is the aversion. So pain is bad because we dislike it — remove the dislike and you've removed the badness.

Dev: That doesn't follow. What these patients report may not be pain in the full sense at all. The aversive feel is part of what pain *is*, so a sensation stripped of it is a different sensation — not the same old pain minus its badness.

Mara: But patients describe the sensation in exactly the same terms as before — same location, intensity, and quality (burning, throbbing, stabbing). Morphine patients say the same thing: the pain is there, it just doesn't bother them. If it walks and quacks like pain, it's pain.

Dev: Even granting the sensation is identical, "they don't mind it" only shows they don't care about it, not that it isn't bad for them. Asymbolia patients show flattened concern across the board — they'll leave a hand on a hot stove. Their testimony about what matters to them isn't authoritative about what's actually good or bad for them.

Mara: That move proves too much. If you discount people's own reports of what they mind whenever the reports threaten your theory, you can immunize any account of badness against any counterexample. First-person testimony about one's own experience has to carry real weight, or the whole debate is unfalsifiable.

Dev: I do count testimony — I just don't treat it as incorrigible. And your view has its own cost: it implies that if I took a drug that made me stop minding my pains, they would cease to be bad for me, and I'd have no reason whatsoever to avoid them. That seems plainly wrong.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Acting as an expert in philosophy of mind and value theory, please adjudicate their exchange carefully. Go move by move: for each of the six moves above, say who is right about the specific point being made and why, noting any conflations or steps that don't follow. Then say what both parties are missing — for instance, relevant distinctions they never draw (badness versus badness-for, phenomenal unpleasantness versus motivational aversion, constitutive versus causal claims) — and finish with an overall verdict on whose position is closer to the truth.
