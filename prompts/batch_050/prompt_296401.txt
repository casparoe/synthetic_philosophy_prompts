Last week a friend and I ended a dinner with an argument neither of us could settle, and I would like it adjudicated. The dispute was about the ENCODE affair of 2012: the consortium reported that roughly 80 percent of the human genome exhibits some biochemical activity — transcription, protein binding, chromatin modification — and its public framing presented this as the demise of the idea of junk DNA. Critics objected that biochemical activity is not biological function in the relevant sense: a stretch of DNA can be weakly transcribed without doing anything for the organism, and considerations such as mutational load, the huge variation in genome size among similar organisms, and the poor sequence conservation of most noncoding DNA suggest that much of the genome persists without any organism-level function.

My friend claimed that Stephen Jay Gould, who died in 2002 and so never saw this debate, would have sided decisively with the critics. Her reasoning: attributing function to a genomic element merely because it does something detectable is the molecular analogue of what Gould and Lewontin attacked in their 1979 spandrels critique — the reflex of assuming that wherever there is structure, there is a purpose selection put there — and the distinction Gould and Vrba drew with the term exaptation gives exactly the vocabulary for sequences that are co-opted or merely present rather than built for their current role.

I resisted this. The spandrels argument, as I understand it, targets adaptationist explanation of organismal traits: the inference from a feature's current utility to a selective history that produced it for that use. ENCODE's claim, whatever its faults, is about present-day molecular activity, not about adaptationist just-so stories, and the critics' own arguments are population-genetic rather than anti-adaptationist. I also doubt Gould would have wanted to be conscripted into a crusade for one side of a molecular dispute his framework was not designed to address.

So: which of us is closer to the truth? Please take a definite position rather than surveying both sides. Commit to a verdict on what Gould would most plausibly have said about ENCODE, grounded in the actual structure of the spandrels argument and the exaptation concept; state precisely where the analogy between ENCODE's inference and adaptationist reasoning holds and where it breaks down; and if the historical record underdetermines the answer, say so, but still declare which way the weight of evidence tips and what single consideration you take to be decisive.
