In the 1980s Douglas Hofstadter ran two cooperation experiments that sit awkwardly with classical game theory. In the Platonia dilemma, twenty acquaintances must independently decide whether to submit an entry; if exactly one enters, that person wins a large prize, but if none or more than one enter, nobody wins anything. In the Luring Lottery, announced to a mass readership, anyone may submit as many entries as they like, and the prize is a fixed sum divided by the total number of entries received, so each extra entry dilutes it; many entrants responded by submitting astronomically large numbers of entries. John von Neumann, who died in 1957, never saw these experiments or the notion of superrationality Hofstadter invoked to explain the restraint he had hoped for. Predict, with reasons, what von Neumann would have made of them: would he dismiss the hoped-for restraint as straightforwardly irrational, explain it away by respecifying the game (richer utilities, shadow of repeated play, tacit coordination), or concede that his framework needs revision in the face of such cases? Take a definite position on which of these he would say and why, rather than surveying the possibilities. Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
