I recently served for six months on a randomly selected citizens' panel advising my city on housing policy, and the experience has scrambled my political instincts. I went in assuming that a body of unelected amateurs would be captured, bored, or simply out of its depth; instead I watched thirty ordinary residents become remarkably well-informed and reach a compromise that the elected council had been unable to touch for years. A colleague of mine on the panel's oversight board had the opposite reaction: she watched the same process and concluded that, however thoughtful our deliberations, we were unaccountable to anyone, unrepresentative in all the ways random samples quietly fail (who can afford to take the time?), and no substitute for politicians who must face voters. She and I have agreed to write a short joint statement for the local democracy-reform group we both belong to, in the style of an adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to draft it. Our quarrel has an old pedigree: in Book IV of the Politics (1294b, Rackham translation), Aristotle writes that "it is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic," and each of us reads this sentence as vindicating our side. I take it to show that elections have always had an aristocratic bias that sortition corrects; she replies that Aristotle is describing the design space of a mixed constitution, not ranking the elements, and that accountability through election was not even on his menu in its modern form.

Please draft the statement itself, with the following structure. First, a numbered list of claims we can both sign — these should be substantive rather than vacuous, and should cover, among other things, what each mechanism (election, lot) actually selects for, what the citizens' panel evidence does and does not establish, and which of Aristotle's observations both readings must accommodate. Second, a set of residual disagreements, each phrased in a single sentence that both of us could accept as a fair statement of her own position and mine, taking care to separate genuine disagreements from ones that are really about words (I suspect our argument about whether the panel was "representative" is partly verbal, while our argument about accountability is not). Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one of us. I suspect the real cruxes include whether elections function primarily as a selection mechanism for competence or as a discipline mechanism for behavior, whether descriptive similarity to the public is a source of legitimacy in its own right or only a proxy for it, and whether the panel's good performance depends on its being advisory rather than sovereign. For each crux, give a concrete case, observation, or piece of evidence we would both accept as bearing on it — historical or contemporary, but stated generally enough that we could check it ourselves — and record in advance what each of us predicts about it and what result would count against our own view.

Two constraints on the drafting. Do not adjudicate between us; the statement should leave the disagreement intact but exact, and any wording one of us would refuse to sign is a drafting failure, not a verdict. And be honest about the limits of the exercise: where no plausible evidence would move one of the camps (mine included), say so and identify that position as held on grounds the collaboration cannot test, rather than manufacturing a fake crux. Finally, append a brief self-critical note: state which part of the statement you are least confident you have rendered neutrally, and what argument or consideration would lead you to revise the overall architecture of the disagreements — for instance, if the real dividing line turns out to be somewhere other than where you have drawn it.
