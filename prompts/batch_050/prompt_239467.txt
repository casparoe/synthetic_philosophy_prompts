I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on Renaissance political thought, and I'm drafting a paper on chapter 25 of The Prince, the one on fortuna and virtù. My translation has Machiavelli say that fortune is the arbiter of one half of our actions, while we direct the other half or a little less. Here's where I'm stuck, and I'd like help working out the argument before I commit to it. If fortune genuinely controls half of what happens to us, then Machiavelli seems to be offering something like a compatibilist position: half determined, half free, roughly a coin flip. So my plan was to argue that virtù gives a prince about even odds against fortuna, and that Cesare Borgia is the counterexample that proves the odds are actually worse than even — Machiavelli presents him as the model of virtù, and yet he lost everything the moment his luck turned when his father died. Doesn't Cesare's collapse show that even a perfectly virtuous prince loses whenever fortune turns against him, so that the real ratio is nowhere near half?

There's a second problem that worries me more, because it looks like a flat contradiction in the chapter itself. Machiavelli compares fortune to a flooding river and says prudent men build dikes and barriers during fair weather, which sounds like an argument for caution, foresight, and long preparation. But then at the end of the very same chapter he concludes that it is better to be impetuous than cautious, since fortune favors the bold, and his example is Pope Julius II succeeding through sheer impetuosity. If impetuosity is what's rewarded, then building dikes in calm weather seems like exactly the kind of cautious behavior Machiavelli just told us fails. So either the chapter contradicts itself within a few pages, or the dike metaphor was never really advice about preparation at all. I'm inclined to argue in the paper that the contradiction is real and that the chapter quietly abandons the half-versus-half framework for an all-or-nothing view where boldness is the only strategy worth having.

Is that the right way to read the chapter? If I've got the structure of the argument right, I'd like to defend the inconsistency reading against the obvious objection that the two passages can be reconciled, so I'd also appreciate hearing the strongest version of that objection and whether it can be answered.
