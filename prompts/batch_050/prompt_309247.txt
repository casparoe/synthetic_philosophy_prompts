So I've been reading about Robert Brandom's inferentialism and I want to use it on a book club pick we're doing: Jane Austen's Emma. Quick sketch of the Brandom stuff in case it's fuzzy: his idea is that what you mean by saying something isn't about what's going on in your head or some word-world hookup, it's about what your utterance commits you to and entitles you to -- meaning as inferential role. Saying "that's a fox" commits you to "that's an animal", rules out "that's a houseplant", etc. And conversations are basically scorekeeping games where speakers track each other's commitments and entitlements, hold each other to things, and you can be on the hook for inferences you never consciously drew.

Read Emma as a novel about exactly this. Highbury feels like a giant scorekeeping machine: everyone's social standing is a ledger of what they've said, implied, promised, let slip. Some things I'm chewing on:

- Emma constantly says things whose *inferential* content outruns what she thinks she said -- flirting with Frank Churchill commits her to stuff she never intended, and everyone else draws the conclusions. Is she Brandom's poster child for someone who doesn't grasp her own commitments?
- Mr. Knightley seems like the novel's ideal scorekeeper -- he actually tracks what Emma is entitled to say vs. what she's just performing. The Box Hill scene where he calls her out over Miss Bates: is that basically an inferentialist account of moral failure, i.e. the wrong isn't rudeness as a feeling but a misuse of status in the game of giving and asking for reasons?
- And the flipside: Harriet Smith and Miss Bates. Brandom says you need to be a player in the game to mean anything at all. Does Austen show what it costs to be a low-status scorekeeper whose commitments nobody bothers to track?

But I also want the honest version: where does the reading strain? Austen clearly thinks there's stuff going on in Emma's head (self-deception, unacknowledged desire) that matters independent of any public commitments, and Brandom's whole thing is that the inner stuff is secondary at best. Does the novel's ending vindicate the inferentialist reading or undermine it?

Give me a reading that does justice to both -- don't just use Emma as a sock puppet for Brandom, and don't flatten Brandom into "words have consequences lol". Point me to specific scenes, and say what the novel shows about meaning-as-commitment that Brandom's dry scorekeeping examples can't.
