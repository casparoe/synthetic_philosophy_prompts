I'm putting together a seminar on Moloch-style dynamics and I keep running into a question I want a clean case for. Every stock example of a multipolar trap involves distinct parties competing: arms races, overfishing, academic hiring where everyone does more signaling and nobody's better off. But is competition between seperate agents actually essential to the structure, or is it just the usual vehicle? I want to know whether you can get the full ratchet -- each step locally rational or even locally improving, the endpoint worse for the very agents taking the steps, and no one able to stop it unilaterally -- without any real competition at all.

So: construct a thought experiment that isolates this variable. A scenario exhibiting genuine race-to-the-bottom structure in which there is either a single decision-maker extended over time, or a group of agents whose preferences are fully aligned with each other. Constraints, and I'm serious about these:

(i) Realistic. Something that could plausibly unfold in a household, a firm, or an individual life. No sci-fi, no demons, no perfect predictors.
(ii) No addiction, craving, or weakness-of-will cases. That would be cheating, because intrapersonal conflict of that kind arguably just smuggles multiple competing agents back in under one skull.
(iii) No appeal to bounded rationality or ignorance. Everyone in the case sees the whole trajectory perfectly clearly in advance and still the ratchet turns.
(iv) The scenario itself should be one paragraph.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. First, name the ratchet mechanism: what makes each step locally rational, and what makes reversal costly. Second, state explicitly which structural feature of your case is substituting for competition -- what does the work that rivalrous interaction normally does? Third, check your case against the structural defintion of a multipolar trap (locally dominant moves, globally worse endpoint, no unilateral escape) and be honest about whether it fully qualifies or merely mimics one, and if it falls short somewhere, say exactly where. Finally: how would a skeptic who insists Moloch just is externalities between distinct agents respond to your case -- could they redescribe it as a mundane problem of commitment or time-inconsistent preferences rather than a trap, and does that redescription actually miss anything? Tell me what, if anything, the trap framing adds that the commitment framing doesn't capture.
