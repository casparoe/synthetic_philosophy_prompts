I retired from a career in cell biology a couple of years ago and have been bumbling my way through philosophy of biology since, and I keep hitting this wall: everyone agrees the word "function" does real work in the life sciences (the function of the heart is to pump blood, not to make thumping noises, even though it does both), but nobody agrees on what a theory of function has to actually accomplish. Before I read one more paper arguing selected-effects vs causal-role, I want the job description first.

So: write the desiderata for a theory of biological function. What must any adequate account of what it means to say "the function of trait T is F" deliver? I want, for each requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it -- not just a bare list. Some things I'd expect to see addressed, but please don't limit yourself to these and feel free to tell me some of them are bogus:

- It has to get malfunction right: a diseased heart that can't pump blood still has pumping blood as its function, and the theory should say why without hand-waving. This one seems non-negotiable to me but maybe I'm wrong.
- It has to draw the line between function and mere side-effect (pumping vs thumping), and say where exactly the line gets drawn and by what.
- It should tell me what to say about traits that fixed by drift or by hitchhiking rather than selection -- does a trait that spread for no adaptive reason have no function at all, and is that a verdict or a bullet to bite?
- It should handle novel traits -- a mutation that does something useful its very first generation, before any selection on it has happened. My physiologist colleagues would say it has a function the day it saves the organism. My evolutionary colleagues would deny it. Who has to give?
- It needs to say something about functions in physiology done in the lab, where nobody asks about evolutionary history at all -- when I measured ion channels for forty years I never once asked what they were selected for, and yet we all talked about their function constantly.
- Exaptation: feathers allegedly co-opted for flight from something else. Does a theory of function need to say the function *changed*, and can it say when?

For each desideratum, mark whether you think it's genuinely non-negotiable, a negotiable preference of one camp (and which camp), or an outright prejudice. And here's the part I actually care about: I suspect some subset of these requirements can't be jointly satisfied -- that the physiological usage and the evolutionary usage are pulling toward two different theories and the "one account of function" project is doomed from the start. If you think that's right, say which subset is unsatisfiable and why. If you think it's wrong, show me the theory-shape that satisfies everything.

Then, briefly, score the two main contenders -- the selected-effects (etiological) account and the causal-role account -- against the list, but keep this part short. I can read the scorecard myself; what I can't get from the literature is the spec, because everyone seems to build their theory first and then retro-fit the desiderata to whatever their theory already does, which is exactly backwards from how we did things in science. Or so it seems to me -- tell me if I'm being unfair.
