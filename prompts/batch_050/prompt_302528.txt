A colleague of mine co-organizes our department's animal cognition journal club, and after two years I still cannot pin down the principle behind her rulings on when a behavioral finding licenses attributing a mental state to an animal. Her track record so far: the "guilty look" dogs show after getting into the trash she dismisses as a learned response to owner cues rather than guilt; a chimpanzee using a mirror to inspect parts of its own body it cannot otherwise see she accepts as self-recognition; scrub jays re-hiding food caches specifically when a competitor watched the original hiding she accepts as tracking what another individual has seen; honeybees solving multi-step navigation and puzzle tasks she insists can be fully handled by associative mechanisms; elephants lingering over the bones of dead herd members she refuses to call grief, saying a motivational description suffices; and rats freeing a trapped cage-mate she refuses to call empathy, preferring distress contagion. Next week we are discussing the cleaner wrasse: fish that, after a period of mirror exposure, apparently try to scrape off a colored mark on their throat that is visible only in the reflection. I want to predict her verdict before the meeting rather than just hear it.

Three readings of her record seem live to me: a strict Morgan's-canon reading, on which she accepts a cognitive attribution only when controlled experiments have eliminated the simpler mechanistic accounts; a representational-but-not-affective reading, on which she grants perceptual and epistemic states but never emotional ones, since the latter always admit motivational redescriptions; and a convergent-evidence reading, on which she credits only species showing flexible cognition across many independent domains and distrusts any single striking result. All three agree on her six past verdicts, but I believe they split on the wrasse, and possibly on how much the specifics of the controls matter.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) state the candidate rules you take to be consistent with her record, adding any I've missed; (2) say what verdict each rule predicts for the cleaner wrasse and why; (3) give your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasons you weight most; (4) identify which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog to the new case and what the analogy turns on; (5) describe the single further case or experimental result that would best discriminate between the two most plausible rules — and if you think her record genuinely underdetermines the wrasse verdict, say so there and state what principle she would be adopting with each possible ruling.
