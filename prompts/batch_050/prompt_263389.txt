I lead a church basement reading group (two retired engineers, a nurse, one undergrad philosophy major) and next month we're doing Augustine on time in Confessions, Book XI. I've decided to build the whole hour around this passage from chapter 28 (Pusey translation), where Augustine describes reciting a psalm he knows by heart:

"I am about to repeat a psalm that I know. Before I begin, my attention is extended to the whole; but when I have begun, as much of it as becomes past by my saying it is extended in my memory; and the life of this action of mine is divided between my memory, on account of what I have repeated, and my expectation, on account of what I am about to repeat; yet my consideration is present with me, through which that which was future may be carried over so that it may become past."

Don't explain the passage to me — plan how I should teach it. What has to come before it in the hour (I suspect they need to already feel the squeeze of "the past is gone, the future isn't yet, the present has no length" or this just reads like ordinary autobiography), why the psalm is the right thing to build the session around rather than the famous "what is time?" bit, the wrong conclusions this group will predictably jump to (im betting at least one person says "so Augustine thinks time is all in your head, like a dream"), exactly where in the sequence to head each one off, one closing question that tells me whether they got that the measuring happens in the mind without time becoming unreal, and what I should openly refuse to attempt at this level. Exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro or wrap-up.
