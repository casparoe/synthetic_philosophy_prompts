A professor I respect summarized Bernard Williams's account of reasons for me as follows. Williams argued that a consideration can be a reason for a person to act only if it connects with something already in that person's "subjective motivational set" — their desires, projects, and dispositions. So if nothing I currently care about would be served by honesty, then I simply have no reason to be honest, and anyone who insists otherwise is confused about what reasons are. Furthermore, since reasons are grounded in an agent's own motivations in this way, Williams held that an agent cannot be mistaken about what they have reason to do: their existing desires settle the matter. Finally, because the view implies that a contented cruel person — someone with no desire whatsoever to treat others decently — has no reason to change, Williams's position reduces all talk of reasons to an inventory of what people happen to want, which is reason enough to reject it. I'm inclined to trust this professor, but the conclusion feels stronger than the premises. Which parts of this summary faithfully capture Williams's view, which parts subtly distort it, and would a defender of the view accept that the cruel-person case is a refutation rather than a consequence to be explained or embraced?
