I am a novelist planning an alternate history, and I want my premise to survive contact with an old historiographical worry: that individual shocks are reabsorbed by deep structural forces (demography, geography, technology, economic interest) so that history re-converges within a few generations, while other shocks genuinely redirect it. Below are six candidate points of divergence, all well attested. Rank them from most to least consequential at the scale of, say, two centuries -- that is, by how defensible it is that the world of 1900 or 2000 would look deeply different had the event gone otherwise, rather than recognisably the same with different names on the letterheads.

1. The storms spare Kublai Khan's invasion fleets and Japan is conquered in the 1280s.
2. Franz Ferdinand's driver does not take the wrong turn in Sarajevo, 1914; the assassination fails.
3. Smallpox and the other Old World diseases never reach the Americas, so contact-era epidemics do not occur.
4. Booth's pistol misfires and Lincoln serves out his second term.
5. The Mongol armies in Europe do not withdraw after Ögedei's death in 1241 but press on past Vienna.
6. The Spanish Armada lands its troops and England falls in 1588.

For each, give a sentence or two explaining its placement, distinguishing the immediate ripple (easy to imagine, easy to overstate) from the long-run divergence (which requires saying which structural forces would *not* have reproduced the historical outcome anyway). Then, wearing my hat rather than the historian's: note which two of the six make the strongest foundations for a novel -- meaning the divergence is both genuinely load-bearing and narratively traceable to the original event -- and which one is, on your analysis, the most seductive gimmick: dramatic, beloved of the genre, yet likely reabsorbed by structural forces. End with your full ranking, most consequential first, in the form \answer{3 > 5 > ...}.
