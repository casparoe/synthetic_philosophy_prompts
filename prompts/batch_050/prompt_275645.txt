ok so I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend Priya about Mencius vs Xunzi and I'm still kind of annoyed about it. Her entire case rested on one passage she made me read out loud, from the Gaozi chapter of the Mencius (Book VI, Part I, in James Legge's translation), where Mencius is replying to Gaozi's analogy comparing human nature to the willow tree:

"Can you, leaving untouched the nature of the willow, make with it cups and bowls? You must do violence and injury to the willow, before you can make cups and bowls with it. If you must do violence and injury to the willow in order to make cups and bowls with it, on your principles you must in the same way do violence and injury to humanity in order to fashion from it benevolence and righteousness! Your words, alas! would certainly lead all men on to reckon benevolence and righteousness to be calamities."

Priya's take: this demolishes Xunzi in advance, since his whole program of ritual, teachers, and conscious activity is exactly the steaming-and-clamping of crooked wood that Mencius is calling violence to the material. My take: Mencius is dunking on Gaozi's analogy, not on Xunzi, and the passage does nothing against a view that thinks the material needed fixing to begin with.

So settle it obliquely. Write me a satirical op-ed, around 600 words, in the voice of Xunzi, responding to this passage as if Mencius had just published it as a devastating personal takedown of him. Deadpan, first person. The whole joke is that Xunzi refuses to be insulted -- of course you do violence to the wood, have you seen the wood -- and the humor has to come from his actual position: that human nature is bad, that goodness is the product of conscious activity, that crooked timber needs the press-frame, that the potter does not consult the clay, that teachers and ritual are how you end up with straight boards. He must never once slip into conceding Mencius's premise -- no secret sprouts, no hint that the wood secretly wanted to be a bowl -- because the minute he does the satire is just cheating.

And the piece should make my interpretive point without ever stating it outright: that Mencius's argument is aimed at Gaozi, who thought nature was neutral, and that whether it lands on Xunzi depends entirely on whether ritual formation counts as "violence and injury" to nature or as the only thing that makes the material usable at all. Let that distinction do the comedic work -- Xunzi cheerfully owning the word violence, for instance, or treating "your words would lead men to reckon righteousness a calamity" as a marketing problem rather than a refutation.

Then, after the op-ed, step out of character and give me 3-4 honest sentences: is the satire's implicit reading fair to Mencius? Is Priya right that the passage hits Xunzi too, or is it really only an argument against Gaozi? I want to know whether I actually won the arguement or just enjoyed having it.
