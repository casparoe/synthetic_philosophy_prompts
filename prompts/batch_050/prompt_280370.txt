My tech lead has a strong track record of calling out over- and under-deliberation in our design reviews, and I want to predict how she'll rule on an upcoming decision. Her past verdicts, each of which she defended at the time:

1. Logging library for a new service: she approved taking the first option that passed our integration tests, without surveying alternatives.
2. Payments processor: she insisted on a two-month evaluation with a weighted scoring matrix before committing.
3. A proposal to benchmark faster serialization libraries for a service that isn't latency-sensitive: she rejected it as wasted effort.
4. A week a teammate spent standardizing the team's editor configuration: she called it over-deliberation and capped the task at one afternoon.
5. Hiring for a six-month contract position: she approved making an offer to the second candidate, who cleared our bar, and canceling the remaining interviews.
6. Message queue for a system we already knew we'd replace within a year: she approved going with the team's existing default after a single day of review.

The upcoming decision: we're open-sourcing an internal library and have to pick a license. The commercial stakes are modest — it's a side project, not core IP — but the choice is effectively irreversible, since whatever we release under it stays released under it.

Predict her verdict on how much deliberation the license choice deserves, and justify the prediction. Then say which decision rule her track record is implicitly running — effort proportional to stakes, effort proportional to irreversibility, pure satisficing against a fixed quality bar, or some hybrid — and describe one further hypothetical decision, along with the verdict she'd have to give on it, that would distinguish between the rules her current record can't tell apart.
