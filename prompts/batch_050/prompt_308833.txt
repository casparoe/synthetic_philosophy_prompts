I am putting together a handout for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of social science on the individualism–holism dispute, and I want a decision tree that walks a student from "I want to explain some social phenomenon — say, a bank run, persistent inflation, or a university's decision to change its admissions policy" to a named position on methodological individualism versus holism. Please act as a professional philosopher of social science who has taught this material for years, and think carefully about the logical space before you write: begin with a short private sketch (which you may include at the end, clearly marked) of which questions actually sort the positions and which only appear to.

Requirements for the tree itself:

1. Build the tree from diagnostic questions, not from labels. Each branch node should be a claim or case the student can take a stand on without already knowing where it leads. The questions I most want used — though you may add or reorder — are: whether facts about societies supervene on facts about individuals and their relations; whether an explanation that never bottoms out in individual-level mechanisms is thereby incomplete; whether groups (corporations, states, churches) can literally hold beliefs and intentions rather than merely having records that function like them; whether social roles and relations can be attributed to individuals without smuggling in the very social facts at issue; and whether the pull toward individualism is really an explanatory commitment or a political-moral one about whose interests explanations should center.

2. The leaves should include at least: strict or atomistic individualism; structural or relational individualism (individuals yes, but essentially related); a supervenience-only individualism that concedes holism at the level of explanation; explanatory holism without ontological holism; and full ontological holism about group agents. If your tree naturally produces further leaves, add them.

3. Map the logical space explicitly: note any combination of answers your questions permit that no one seems to occupy, and in a sentence or two diagnose why — incoherent, unstable under pressure, or simply unexplored.

4. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what further question would be needed to split them.

5. Use concrete cases at the nodes where possible — the bank run, the inflation spiral, and the university's admissions decision above, plus at least one case of your own choosing — rather than purely abstract formulations.

6. Aim for eight to twelve branch nodes.

Finally, append a short epistemic note distinguishing what is genuinely settled in this literature from what remains live and contested: for instance, be candid about whether any of these positions has been refuted outright versus merely fallen out of fashion, and do not present a currently dominant view as a consensus if the underlying dispute is unresolved. The handout will be more useful to my students if it is honest about which branches of the tree are battlegrounds.
