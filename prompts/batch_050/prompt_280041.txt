My friend who took a decision theory seminar last year tried to explain to me why coherence arguments supposedly force you to be an expected utility maximizer, and I want to check whether his version holds up before I repeat it to anyone. His summary went like this:

'The basic result is the von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem. It says that if your preferences over gambles satisfy a few obvious rationality axioms -- mainly completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence -- then there's a utility function such that you maximize expected utility, full stop. So rationality just is expected utility maximization. The way they prove you need the axioms is the Dutch book: if your preferences violate transitivity or independence, a clever bookie can offer you a series of bets each of which you accept, but which together guarantee you lose money no matter what happens. So incoherent preferences make you a money pump, and that's the cash-value argument for the axioms. Also worth noting: because expected utility is linear in probabilities, the theory says risk aversion is irrational -- a rational agent should be indifferent between $50 for sure and a 50/50 shot at $100. And the independence axiom is uncontroversial, it's basically just the idea that irrelevant alternatives shouldn't change your ranking.'

A few things make me suspicious. First, I thought Dutch books were about your degrees of belief in propositions, not about preferences over gambles, so I'm not sure he has the right argument attached to the right theorem. Second, the claim about risk aversion seems way too strong -- surely the standard view can accomodate preferring the sure thing? Third, I'm pretty sure I've heard of the Allais paradox specifically as a challenge to independence, which doesnt square with calling it uncontroversial.

Could you go through his summary and tell me: which parts are accurate statements of what coherence arguments actually establish, which parts conflate distinct arguments (and what the correct pairing of arguments to conclusions is), and which parts are overstatements? Also, on the 'rationality just is EU maximization' claim -- does the representation theorem actually establish that direction, or does it show something weaker, and what would a defender of the axioms say the money pump / Dutch book style considerations really prove? I want to know what I'm actually entitled to conclude if I grant the axioms are rationality requirements.
