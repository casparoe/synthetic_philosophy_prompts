So I'm a retired biochemist, spent forty years in a lab, and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole a bit (started with Parfit, now I'm all over the place). One idea that really grabbed me is the unilateralist's curse: roughly, if a bunch of people can each independently launch some initiative, the thing that actually gets launched is whichever one the most enthusiastic person wanted, and the most enthusiastic person is systematically the one who's most overestimated how good it is — even if everyone's judgement is unbiased on average. A winner's curse for do-gooders, basically.

Here's my situation. I sit on the committee of a small grant-making trust (we hand out maybe thirty small grants a year, plus we occasionally publish little reports, sponsor events, that sort of thing). And the curse is everywhere. Any one of us can more or less unilaterally nominate a project for fast-track funding, or send a report to the local paper, or invite a speaker, or commit us to a stall at the county fair. Meanwhile saying no to something takes ages and needs a proper meeting. So the keen person always wins, and I now lie awake wondering how many of our brilliant initiatives were just someone's overoptimistic estimate.

What I'd like you to write me is an actual procedure a committee member could follow before acting unilaterally — me first, but ideally something I could propose to the whole commitee. I want the translation of the philosophy into practice to be done properly, not just "think twice before doing things". Specifically:

- The questions you ask yourself, in order, before you pull the trigger on something. Include the bit that I find genuinely tricky: how do you work out whether your high estimate of the idea is real information or just the fact that you're the most optimistic of twelve people? What do you actually *do* with that thought quantitatively — do you shave your estimate down, and by how much, and does it depend on how many other people could have acted?
- When the procedure tells you to circulate your idea and collect other people's estimates first, versus when it's fine to just go ahead (surely sometimes — if I'm buying biscuits for the meeting I'm not doing a Delphi round).
- The asymmetry thing: does the curse apply to vetoes and delays the same way it applies to launches? If one person can quietly kill a proposal, is that cursed too, or is it different because inaction is the default? I genuinely can't work this one out and I want the procedure to take a position.
- Where the procedure has to stop and admit it's a judgement call. I was a scientist, I can smell a fake number, so please mark clearly which steps are real reasoning and which are dressed-up intuition.

Then run the procedure on three cases, with the working shown: (1) me fast-tracking funding for a flashy citizen-science project I personally find exciting; (2) a colleague wanting to publish a short report naming and shaming a local landlord's company, which any of us could post on our website tomorrow; (3) me deciding *not* to renew our subscription to a data service nobody else seems to use — note this is a unilateralist's non-action, if that's a thing, and I want to see whether the procedure handles it. I'd like at least one of the three to come out with an answer I won't enjoy, i.e. the procedure telling me to shelve or slow down something I'd otherwise just do.

Finally, a short honest section on what the procedure can't do — where the curse idea just doesn't apply (tiny committees? repeated decisions where you learn over time?), and where following it could itself make things worse, e.g. endless consultation theatre where nothing ever happens, which is its own failure mode I've seen in enough university departments for one lifetime.
