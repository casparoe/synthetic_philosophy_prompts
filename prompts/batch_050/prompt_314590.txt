I host a small current-affairs podcast, and on a recent episode about whether our country should hold a second referendum on a policy question the first referendum settled narrowly a few years ago, my co-host cut the discussion short with the line "the will of the people has been expressed -- reopening it means defying the people themselves." Listeners loved it; I found it slippery. Since then I've noticed the phrase "the will of the people" doing this same move everywhere: after elections with forty-percent turnout, after referendums won 51–49, after polls on questions most respondents had never thought about before being asked, and once -- by the losing side -- about an election they lost, on the grounds that the real people had been ignored. It seems to function less as a claim than as a gavel.

Act as a political philosopher who specializes in democratic theory and who is unsentimental about slogans. I want you to think carefully about the different precise theses that "the will of the people" could be expressing when invoked in these debates, and lay them out as a menu I could read on air: for each reading, state the thesis precisely enough that one could say what evidence or argument would bear on it. I expect the menu to include at least the following kinds of candidates, but do not limit yourself to my list and feel free to split any of these further if they conceal two ideas: (a) a purely descriptive claim that a majority of some specified group wants some specified thing at some specified time; (b) a procedural claim that whatever emerges from a legitimate procedure thereby acquires authority, independent of what anyone wants; (c) an epistemic claim that aggregated judgments of the many track the right answer better than the judgments of the few; (d) a claim about a collective agent -- a "people" -- that has a will over and above the distribution of individual wills; and (e) a thin rhetorical usage where the phrase just means "my side won and the matter is closed."

For each reading, give a verdict: is it a platitude that no one disputes but which cannot bear the argumentative weight placed on it, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something probably false? Then, and this is the part I most need, assess which readings -- if any -- would actually support the specific use my co-host made of it, namely that a narrow referendum result settles a question against later reconsideration. Pay particular attention to the problems that arise for that use: the fact that "the people" is a different set of individuals a few years later, that majorities are a function of which options are on the ballot, that intensity and ignorance are invisible in a vote count, and that the same speaker typically denies the phrase applies when their side loses. If no coherent reading both survives scrutiny and delivers the gavel-bang conclusion, say so plainly, and say what the slogan's actual function in argument is, in that case. End with a two-or-three-sentence reply I could give on air that concedes whatever is true in the slogan while refusing the move it was being used to make.
