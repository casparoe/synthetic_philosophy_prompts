I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level undergraduate course on formal epistemology, and I want one essay question on objective Bayesianism and the maximum entropy principle. The problem I keep running into with my students is that they can all recite the standard narrative -- the principle of indifference falls to Bertrand-style paradoxes, maximum entropy supposedly generalizes and fixes it, hence rational priors exist -- without ever noticing that MaxEnt only yields a unique distribution once you've already chosen a sample space, a base measure, and a set of constraints, and that these choices can be made differently with equal-looking justification. A colleague and I were arguing about whether the invariance-based defense (deriving the "right" prior from the symmetries of the problem, in the style of Jaynes's treatment of the Bertrand chord problem) genuinely dissolves this worry or only works in toy cases that happen to have enough symmetry. I want the exam question to force students to take a side on exactly that dispute. So: please design a question that gives students a concrete setup along the lines of a factory producing cubes with side lengths known only to lie in some interval, where privileging side length versus privileging volume yields different priors, and asks them to (i) state precisely what inputs MaxEnt needs before it outputs anything, (ii) use the setup to show how equally defensible inputs produce conflicting priors, (iii) evaluate whether appeal to the problem's transformation symmetries resolves the conflict in general or only in specially structured cases, and (iv) deliver a verdict on whether there are rational priors, stating explicitly in what sense of "rational" their answer holds. The question should be answerable in 75 minutes by a strong student and unanswerable by reciting the potted history.

Along with the question, write a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme out of 25 points. The marking scheme should be built around five or six specific moves, with the marks each is worth, and for each move a one-line description of the difference between how an A answer executes it and how a B answer fumbles or omits it -- for instance, a B answer will typically state MaxEnt's formula but treat the constraints as simply "given by the data," while an A answer notes that the choice of which moments to constrain is itself an unprincipled input; a B answer will mention Bertrand's paradox generically without working the cube example through to two actual distributions. At least one of the moves should reward students for articulating the strongest version of the position they argue *against*, since I want to penalize one-sided verdicts in either direction. Finally, add a short closing note, addressed to me rather than to students, saying where the model answer commits to a contestable philosophical stance (for example, on whether symmetry considerations can be objectively privileged or themselves smuggle in indifference) and what consideration or argument would warrant revising the model answer's verdict and, correspondingly, the marking scheme. Begin your response with the exam question itself, with no preamble or restatement of my request.
