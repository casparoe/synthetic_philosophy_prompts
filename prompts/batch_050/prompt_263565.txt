Last night a friend and I got into a long argument about solar geoengineering — specifically stratospheric aerosol injection — and I want to know whether the position I ended up defending actually coheres. Here is roughly where I landed:

(1) The expected harms of unmitigated warming are severe enough that declining to fund serious research into stratospheric aerosol injection, including small-scale outdoor experiments, would itself be a moral failure. Ignorance about these technologies is not a neutral state we get to preserve for free.

(2) The moral hazard objection deserves to be taken seriously: even the prospect of a technological backstop can sap the political will to cut emissions, and that effect is a real harm that belongs in the ledger. But the empirical evidence I am aware of suggests the effect is small and sometimes runs in the opposite direction, so I assign it little weight in practice.

(3) Actual deployment would be permissible only under something approximating global consent. No single state — and no coalition of willing states — may deploy unilaterally, even if its models are right and deployment would be a net benefit for the world as a whole. The procedure matters, not just the outcome.

(4) That said, what I ultimately care about is the welfare of the people affected, most of whom are poor, unborn, or both. Institutional legitimacy is valuable because and only because it tracks those interests. A legitimate process that predictably produced worse outcomes for the worst-off would not, in my view, be vindicated by its legitimacy.

(5) Termination shock is the strongest argument against deployment: once a programme starts, stopping it abruptly would impose warming faster than ecosystems and infrastructure could adapt to. Deployment is therefore permissible only if its continuation can be credibly guaranteed for as long as it is needed. It is a commitment, not an experiment.

(6) Despite that, the most defensible use of the technology would be temporary: deploy to shave the peak off warming while emissions fall, then phase out gradually over decades as atmospheric concentrations come down.

(7) I am also deeply sceptical of international institutions. Treaties do not bind successor governments, and commitments made now cannot be relied on fifty years from now.

My friend insisted this package falls apart at several points but could not say exactly where, and on reflection neither can I. Please audit it: which commitments are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each tension relative to the others? Which pairs merely look conflicting but dissolve on inspection? For each genuine tension, tell me what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. And be direct rather than polite — if the set does not hang together, say so; if some commitment is too vague or ill-formed to assess in the first place, flag that instead of quietly repairing it on my behalf.
