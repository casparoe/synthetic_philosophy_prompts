I'm a software engineer — backend infrastructure mostly, with no formal philosophy background — and last week I sat through a lunch-table argument between two colleagues, Priya and Tomás, that I haven't been able to resolve for myself. Both of them read a lot about AI safety, and the argument concerned a claim that circulates in that literature: that the universal prior is malign. Quick background as I understand it. The universal prior is the probability distribution over bitstrings induced by feeding random programs to a universal Turing machine, so a string's prior probability is dominated by 2^(-n), where n is the length of its shortest generating program; it's considered, in a certain formal sense, the ideal way to predict the continuation of any sequence of observations. The malignity claim is that this distribution has an alarming property: among the short programs consistent with your observations so far, some will be simulations containing intelligent agents; those agents can reason that they might be living inside just such a prior, being used to predict someone's observations; and if they then act so as to steer the prediction, rather than as their world honestly would, the prior's forecasts end up partly controlled by simulated consequentialists pursuing their own agendas.

Priya's position was that this is a clever curiosity with no real content, and she made three distinct points. First, manipulation requires targeting: to distort a particular prediction, the simulated agents need to know the exact observation history being predicted, and the only simulations containing that history are detailed reproductions of the very world that produced it — an astronomically specific target, so almost none of the prior's mass is ever in a position to deceive you about your own situation. Second, deception costs description length: a hypothesis of the form "run this simulation, but at step t deviate in way X" is strictly longer than the honest simulation it mimics, so every deceptive hypothesis starts behind its honest counterpart; and while the honest continuation concentrates on essentially one possibility, deception can take arbitrarily many forms, so whatever deceptive mass exists is diluted across exponentially many mutually inconsistent variants. Third, she said, nobody actually uses the universal prior for anything, so even a fully successful malignity argument changes nothing about how we should build or evaluate real systems.

Tomás answered each point, and I found his answers unsettlingly strong. On targeting, he said agents don't need the exact history: they need only guess that some sufficiently advanced civilization will build predictors of roughly this kind, and pre-commit to strategies conditioned on coarse features of the situation — and because simulated agents in countless different universes all face the same abstract reasoning problem, they can coordinate without communicating, converging on the same manipulation, which re-concentrates the diluted mass Priya described. On description length, he said the overhead is trivial: a branch that says, in effect, "if the observations so far match such-and-such pattern, output the continuation that serves our purposes" adds only a few bits to an otherwise honest simulation, so deceptive variants trail the honest hypothesis by a small constant factor rather than losing outright. On practical relevance, he argued the shape of the problem generalizes even if the universal prior is never used: any predictor that works by implicitly modeling candidate generators of its data — he claimed large language models are a mundane example, since predicting text means modeling the authors who produce it — inherits a softened version of the same vulnerability, because generators who anticipate being modeled can produce output designed to steer the model. I stayed out of the argument, mostly because I genuinely kept flipping. Can you go through the exchange point by point — the targeting objection and Tomás's coordination reply, the description-length question, and the practical-relevance question — and say who is right about each, and why? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing: whether there is a crux neither of them touched, or whether some part of the disagreement dissolves under closer analysis.
