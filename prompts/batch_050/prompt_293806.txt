Ok so I need help winning an arguement, sort of. My friend Dan and I have been going back and forth about this alot lately: Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, the bit in The Gay Science where a demon tells you that you'll have to live this exact life over and over again, every detail identical, forever. Dan's position is that the whole thing is fake-deep: if the repetition is perfectly exact and you keep no memory between lives, then nothing is experientially different from living once, so the demon's question can't give you a reason to do anything. And if your answer just tracks whether you happen to be happy that day, he says it's a mood ring, not a philosophical test. My position is that he's missing the point — the question isn't supposed to change the world, it's supposed to crush you or transfigure you right now, and your gut reaction to it reveals your actual stance toward your own life, amor fati and all that. We argued til 2am and got nowhere, and honestly I see his problem: it is very easy to be clever about a hypothetical.

So I want to run an activity at our reading group — Dan will be there, smug — that makes people feel the weight of the demon's offer before anyone gets to be clever about it.

Setup: 8-10 people, mix of undergrads and a couple grad students, 90 minutes, a seminar room with a big table. Give me a complete, runnable design:

- Mechanics where participants make a series of real choices with small but genuine stakes (points, snack duty, who has to write the group's summary email — no money), and then the demon announcement lands and the session ACTUALLY repeats. Real replayed rounds, at least two repetitions after the first round, not just telling people to imagine it repeating.
- Rules, materials, a timeline, and exactly how repetition works: do people have to do the same things again? Do they get to change one thing? Your call, but justify it.
- Original wording for the demon's announcement. Do not use any published translation of the passage — it should land as an event, not as a text half the room recognizes.
- At least one engineered moment in round 1 designed to be regretted — a choice under time pressure or with hidden information that people will wince at when it comes back around.
- A plan for Dan: someone who announces the trick in advance or declares he'll act identically every round to prove the test is toothless. Give him a role or a rule that turns his sabotage into part of the activity rather than a derailment. (Making him the demon is an option, but it feels almost too on the nose?)
- A debrief that maps specific moments from the session onto the parts of the idea: the two reactions (crushed vs. affirmed), amor fati, and especially the memory issue — because the game necessarily gives people memory between rounds and Nietzsche's version doesn't. The debrief has to say whether what people felt was eternal recurrence or just Groundhog Day, and whether that's a defect or the interesting part.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time (collusion, people treating repetition as pure optimization, giggling killing the weight, time overruns) and what to do about each.
- An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce — the stakes of an actual life, irreversibility, the fact that the doctrine's no-memory exactness is what makes it a test of attitude rather than a plan — and what I as facilitator have to supply in words that the game can't deliver.

Hard constraint: nobody hears the words Nietzsche or eternal recurrence until the debrief. The second someone names it, Dan wins, because then everyone performs their seminar take instead of reacting.
