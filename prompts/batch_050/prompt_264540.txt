In discussions of the problem of divine hiddenness, I've repeatedly encountered a particular analogy used to defuse it: God's silence during our lives is like a teacher's silence during an examination. The teacher taught the material all semester, but while the test is in progress she does not answer questions or intervene, because the point of the exam is that the student demonstrate what they have internalized; helping would defeat the purpose. On this picture, earthly life is the test, free and uncoerced response to God is what is being assessed, and a God who made His existence unmistakable would be like a teacher standing over your shoulder whispering answers — the test would cease to measure anything. The analogy is typically deployed to conclude that divine silence is not evidence against God but is exactly what we should expect if God exists and values something like freely given faith or love.

Audit this analogy. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the semester's instruction, the exam paper, the proctor, the student's prior certainty that a teacher exists at all, the grading, and the consequences of failing. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Consider, at minimum: the student is never in doubt that the teacher and the school exist, whereas the hiddenness debate concerns doubt about the examiner's very existence; the exam lasts an hour rather than a lifetime, and the silent period is bracketed by instruction and feedback on both ends; students consented to be enrolled; and it is contested whether a teacher visibly present would actually coerce answers in the way the analogy assumes, given that students routinely defy or ignore teachers they know to be real. Finally, say whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that silence is no evidence against a loving God — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying more weight than its actual structure permits; and if there is a better analogy a defender of hiddenness could reach for, say what it would be and what work it could and could not do.
