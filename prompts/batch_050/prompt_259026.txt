I work in the permits office of a state wildlife agency, and I keep landing in philosophical quicksand. We issue scientific collection permits phrased like "you may take up to 10 individuals of species X," and about once a week I get a case where nobody can say what an individual is. Recent ones: a researcher wanting fragments from an aspen clone that (if I've understood the news coverage right, it's that Pando stand in Utah) is genetically one plant spread over a hundred acres; a marine team wanting to clip staghorn coral, which regrows from fragments so one "coral" can become fifty; and a grad student who wants to collect six Portuguese man o' war, which apparently are colonies of specialized zooids and not single animals at all. Last month a colleague drafted a three-question test -- is it genetically uniform, can it reproduce on its own, can its parts survive separation -- and the test immediately fell apart: the man o' war fails "parts survive separation," the aspen fails "genetically uniform" is fine but "reproduces on its own" is weird since it mostly clones itself, and an Argentine ant supercolony passes or fails depending which question you weight. I've also got a loophole problem: someone argued that if a coral head is really thousands of polyps, then a permit for ten individuals recieves them basically nothing, so the limit should be renegotiated, and I couldn't tell if that was sophistry or correct.

What I want is a decision procedure my staff can actually run in twenty minutes per case, with no philosophers on payroll: a checklist or flowchart-in-words grounded in whatever philosophy of biology genuinely says about biological individuality (I gather there's work on Darwinian individuals versus organisms, bottlenecks, policing of cheater cells, that sort of thing -- but I need the parts that change a permit decision, not the seminar version). Tell me explicitly which distinctions have to survive the translation into bureaucrat-speak and which can be safely dropped. Include worked applications to at least three cases -- please use the aspen, the man o' war, and one you choose that you think breaks the procedure -- and for at least one of them I want the procedure to give an answer I will not like operationally, so I can see what that feels like before it happens with a real applicant. Mark the points where judgment can't be eliminated and a human has to just decide, and say what the procedure can't do (e.g., should it also handle edge cases like a pregnant animal, or symbiont consortia like lichens where two species are one functional unit?).

One more thing: please be committal. I don't want a menu of positions in the literature with pros and cons -- I need one procedure with definite choices about which criteria count and why, defended against the obvious objections, even if reasonable philosophers would pick differently. If a choice is basically a coin flip between defensible options, flip the coin, tell me which way it landed, and say what I recieve and what I lose with that call.
