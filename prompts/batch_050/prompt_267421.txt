I'm building an interactive "what is pleasure, anyway?" flowchart for my philosophy podcast's website. The idea is that listeners answer gut-level questions about cases and get sorted into a theory of what all pleasures have in common — the heterogeneity problem: a hot bath, solving a crossword, an orgasm, hearing that your rival got passed over for promotion. What, if anything, do these share that makes them all pleasures?

Write the flowchart as a decision tree with these constraints:

- Start from the naive assumption that pleasure is one unified thing, and split from there. Every branch node has to be a concrete, case-based question someone with zero philosophy background can answer from the gut. Not "is pleasure phenomenal or attitudinal?" but something like: think of the bath versus the good news about your rival — does it seem like there's one feeling present in both, or two totally different feelings that just both count as good?
- Leaves I definitely want on the tree: (1) the view that all pleasures literally share a single hedonic tone, differing only in intensity and duration, with the variety coming from their causes; (2) the attitudinal view, where pleasure is a stance we take toward states of affairs rather than any feeling at all; (3) a desire-first view, where something's being a pleasure is explained by our wanting it or being disposed to want it; (4) a functionalist view, where pleasure is whatever occupies the reward-and-motivation role in our psychology; (5) the disunity view — the question has no answer, "pleasure" lumps together genuinely different states, and hedonism has to either fragment or pick one of them and drop the rest. If there's a sixth serious option I've missed (something evaluative or perceptual, maybe?), work it in.
- Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: figure out which combinations of answers are even possible, locate the real positions in that space, and mark any coherent-but-unoccupied region with a sentence on whether it's empty because it's incoherent or just because nobody's bothered to defend it.
- At least one node must split on unnoticed pleasure (you only realize afterward, pulling into the driveway, that you'd been enjoying the drive the whole time — was pleasure there while you weren't attending to it?) and one must split on pleasures directed at things that didn't happen (delight at your team winning when they actually lost — was the pleasure real, and if so what was it a pleasure in?).
- For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the bullet you have to bite to live there.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually tell apart using cases alone, and say what further question would seperate them.
- Cap it around 8 decision nodes. My suspicion is that the attitudinal and functionalist leaves end up adjacent and hard to split with a case a lay listener can answer — if so, that's exactly the kind of thing I want flagged, not fudged with jargon.
