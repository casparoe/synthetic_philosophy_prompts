Write an essay on origin essentialism — the thesis that a person's origins (in particular, the sperm and egg they developed from) are essential to them, such that no one originating from a different sperm and egg could have been you. Weigh the arguments on both sides and defend a verdict. Write in British English.

Your essay must be organised around the following three cases, and should treat them as the primary test cases rather than as illustrations tacked onto a general discussion.

Case one — twinning. Suppose you are one of a pair of monozygotic twins: a single zygote formed, then divided into two embryos. Consider the nearby possibility in which that very zygote never divides, and a single child develops from it. Is that child you, your twin, neither, or somehow both? If the child is neither of you, then it seems each of you has an origin (the zygote plus its subsequent fission) that goes beyond the gametes, which strains the standard statement of the thesis. If the child is you, consider whether you could equally have been the singleton born from the division event happening a day later instead — and where the regress of 'nearby origins' should stop.

Case two — gamete asymmetry. Consider two counterfactual possibilities: in the first, the very egg you came from is fertilised by a different sperm from your father; in the second, the very sperm you came from fertilises a different egg from your mother. The standard thesis counts both resulting individuals as non-you. But argue for whether the two cases really stand or fall together. Is there a principled reason to think that one gamete contributes more to who you are than the other — via mitochondrial continuity, via the fact that the egg's cytoplasm rather than the sperm's initiates development, or via some other consideration — and would an 'asymmetric' origin essentialism be defensible or merely arbitrary?

Case three — the same gametes, different month. In this possibility, your parents conceive a child from what are, as it happens, the same two gametes, but the conception occurs a month later than it actually did. Most origin essentialists count this child as you. Now extend the series: same parents, different month, different sperm; different egg; increasingly remote. The thesis must either tolerate the small shifts and reject the large ones, drawing a line somewhere, or insist that any difference in gametes destroys identity. Explain which commitment you take the thesis to require, and whether the line it draws can be principled rather than stipulative.

Structure the essay as follows. First, set out the strongest motivation for origin essentialism (you may draw on the familiar thought that identity across possibilities needs some anchor, and that origins are the best candidate). Second, work through the three cases in turn, stating explicitly what verdict each of the main positions — full necessity of both gametes, an asymmetric one-gamete view, and outright rejection of origin essentialism — is forced to give in each case, and which verdicts are the most costly. Third, reach a conclusion: defend one of those positions, or a modification of one, as the least costly overall. Do not end on an even-handed 'each view has strengths and weaknesses' note; a conditional conclusion of the form 'if twinning cases are taken seriously, then asymmetric origin essentialism wins, otherwise the standard view stands' is acceptable only if you also argue for whether twinning cases should be taken seriously.

The essay should be around 1,200–1,800 words. Do not include citations or a bibliography; argue from the cases and from general considerations about identity, essence, and possibility.
