In a seminar on G. E. Moore I admitted that my judgments about beauty and friendship seem to be pulled toward “organic unity” language, especially the table-of-contents sentence in Principia Ethica: “The investigation of intrinsic values is complicated by the fact that the value of a whole may be different from the sum of the value of its parts,” followed by the suggestion that “The term organic whole might well be used to denote that a whole has this property.” My verdicts are these: an uninhabited valley of great beauty has some value even if no one ever sees it, but much less than the same valley attentively enjoyed; one person’s refined solitary pleasure in a painting is genuinely good, yet two friends coming to love the same painting together often make a better whole than the pleasure plus the friendship added separately; a friendship that merely uses the other person as a cultivated audience can contain more aesthetic episodes than a plainer friendship while still being worse; an ugly shared object, like a hideous lamp both friends mock for years, can raise the value of the friendship rather than lower it; and, the pair I cannot easily reconcile, I think a beautiful thing unseen is worth very little, but I also think a friend’s private grief that I never witness can matter greatly to what our friendship is.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures these verdicts without simply reducing value to pleasant consciousness, and how should it interpret Moore’s claim: as saying that beauty, affection, awareness, and appropriateness of attitude compose a non-additive whole, or only that relations among parts can change the contribution each part would have had alone? Please test any proposed principle against two further cases I have not decided—an exquisite performance heard by one listener who will soon forget it, and an ordinary friendship preserved only because both friends falsely remember a beautiful trip they never took—and say honestly which of my reported verdicts the simplest adequate principle must overturn.

Format the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: state the leading principle; identify which case motivates each clause; give its verdicts on the two undecided cases; name the verdict or verdicts it cannot save; and say whether the Moore passage supports that principle or merely sounds as if it does.
