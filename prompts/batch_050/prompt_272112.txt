For several years I have followed the philosophical debate over whether there is any intrinsic moral difference between doing harm and allowing harm — between killing and letting die — and it strikes me as completely stalled, which I find puzzling given how concrete the arguments on both sides are.

On one side stand the equivalence arguments: constructed pairs of cases that differ only in whether the agent acts or stands by. The classic example has one man drowning his young cousin in the bath for an inheritance, while a second man, in a parallel case, merely watches his cousin drown, ready to intervene if needed but never needing to. Both act from greed, both get the death they wanted. The conclusion drawn is that because the two agents are morally on a par, the doing/allowing distinction carries no moral weight in itself; any appearance to the contrary comes from correlating factors like motive or effort.

On the other side, defenders of the distinction concede the pairs without conceding the point. The bathtub cases, they say, show only that motive and outcome can swamp whatever difference the distinction makes — equal badness of agents is compatible with a real but small contribution from the doing/allowing factor itself. They then point to cases where the distinction seems indispensable: a doctor may honour a terminally ill patient's refusal of treatment but may not administer a lethal drug at the same patient's request for the same relief; I may permissibly fail to donate my spare income to famine relief but may not poison someone's food even to achieve an identical balance of outcomes.

Both moves have been on the table for roughly fifty years, everyone knows them, and nobody has moved. My working hypothesis is that the phrase 'relevantly alike' is doing all the unargued work: the equivalence argument only bites if the paired cases differ in nothing but the doing/allowing feature, yet whether cases differing in that feature can be relevantly alike is precisely what is in dispute, so each side's argument covertly presupposes its own conclusion. A friend of mine offers a rival diagnosis: the two camps are answering different questions under one heading — one asking what makes particular acts wrong, the other asking what rules our institutions (medicine, law, aid) should encode — and each is largely right about its own question.

What I want from you is not a verdict on the distinction itself but a diagnosis of the impasse. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two questions being traded under a single name? Is there a hidden conceptual question — about what makes cases 'relevantly alike', or about what a moral difference has to be to count — doing the real work?
