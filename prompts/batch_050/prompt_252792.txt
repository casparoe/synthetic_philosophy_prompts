In 1976, Appel and Haken announced a proof of the four-colour theorem: having argued that any smallest counterexample to the theorem would have to contain one of 1,936 particular configurations, they used a computer to check that every configuration in that set is reducible. Goldbach's conjecture, meanwhile, has been verified by computer for every even number up to roughly 4 × 10^18. In both cases, then, a machine has examined a large collection of instances far beyond what any human being could survey by hand, and in both cases our acceptance of the result rests on trust in the hardware, the software, and the diligence of the programmers involved. Since the epistemic situation appears to be parallel, why is the four-colour theorem counted as proved while Goldbach's conjecture is not? Is the difference between the two cases anything more than a sociological one — a matter of which computations the mathematical community happens to feel comfortable relying on — or is there a principled distinction to be drawn here, and if so, where exactly does it lie?
