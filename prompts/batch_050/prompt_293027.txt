I've been reading about evidential cooperation in large worlds -- the idea, associated with the decision-theoretic literature around functional decision theory, that if the universe (or multiverse) is big enough, there are plausibly many agents whose reasoning is correlated with mine, so my acting generously is evidence that they are acting generously too, which makes generosity instrumentally worthwhile even when no causal link exists. I was discussing this with friends over dinner and we couldn't stop inventing the charity pitches it would license, so now I want the joke done properly. Write a fundraising letter, roughly 650-750 words, from the fictional Multiversal Benevolence Trust, addressed to prospective donors, whose entire case for giving rests on evidential cooperation in large worlds.

The letter should be deadpan throughout and use the real machinery, exaggerated but not invented: your donation as evidence about what your decision-theoretic counterparts across the cosmos are doing with their resources; the asymmetry of being able to influence agents you can never meet, verify, or receive thanks from; the delicate question of whether counterparts with worse values than yours are also 'cooperating' with you, and what that does to your expected impact; the measure problem treated as an actuarial detail (how many correlated donors are there, exactly, and does your gift scale with the multiverse?). Include at least one testimonial from a satisfied counterpart (name withheld on grounds of causal inaccessibility), a gift-matching scheme in which every pound you donate is statistically accompanied by counterpart donations you will never observe, and a complaints procedure for donors who believe their counterparts have defected. A brief FAQ at the end is welcome if it fits the form.

The humour must come from taking the position seriously and following it one step further than its proponents do, so that someone who knows the FDT/EDT literature laughs in recognition rather than wincing at a strawman, while a reader who has never heard of decision theory still finds it funny as a portrait of bureaucratic overreach. The target is the practice and its rhetorical temptations, not any real named person. British spelling and tone throughout, in the voice of a moderately pompous development director. End with a final line beginning 'Summary:' containing one sentence stating what the letter's satire ultimately lands on.
