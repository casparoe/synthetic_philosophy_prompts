A friend and I recently fell into a long argument about whether you can coherently assign a probability to your own not-yet-made decision — say, whether I'll order the fish or the pasta while I'm still reading the menu. Her view was that genuine deliberation requires treating your options as open, so any forecast of your own choice smuggles in a spectator's stance that undermines the deliberating; my view was that I know my own habits better than any bookie knows a horse, and my predictions about myself are usually right. Rather than settle it, I'd like the tension turned into comedy.

Write a mock opinion column, roughly 600 words, in the voice of a professional bookmaker — the sort who will cheerfully quote odds on elections, comet strikes, and the survival of his own marriage — explaining, with mounting exasperation, why he refuses on principle to take bets on what a customer will choose to do while that customer is still making up their mind. The target of the satire is not the bookmaker but the assumption that a deliberating agent can step outside his own choice and price it like a racehorse. The humor should grow out of real features of the problem, not invented absurdities: any odds he quotes become a fresh piece of evidence the customer can reason with, so the prediction keeps kicking over its own foundations; treating one's own act as already settled seems to convert deliberation into mere self-observation; and the bookie has noticed that the customers most eager to bet on themselves are precisely the ones whose odds he should trust least.

Keep it deadpan. He should regard himself as a hard-nosed empiricist being wronged by an uncooperative market, never as a philosopher, and the deeper point should surface only through his complaints — that there is something self-undermining about forecasting a choice you still have to make, and that the town's last true believer in free will turns out to run the betting shop. Give him an invented town and one or two recurring customers with concrete, slightly pathetic bets. Do not let him articulate the resolution too cleanly: the piece should end with his frustration intact, and the reader should be left holding the actual question.
