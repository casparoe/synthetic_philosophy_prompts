I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a long piece about how different societies draw the line between corruption and ordinary relationship-building, and I keep running into a puzzle about what the Western anti-corruption framework actually assumes. Here's the concrete stuff that's bugging me. The US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act makes it a crime to bribe foreign officials, but it has an explicit carve-out for "facilitating payments" -- small amounts paid to speed up routine things like getting a phone line connected or cargo through customs. So the same statute says money-for-official-action is corrupt in one breath and fine in the next, apparently depending on whether the official had discretion. Meanwhile, in the US itself, a lobbyist can buy a senator a fancy dinner, fund their campaign, and hire their staffer's spouse, and that's all legal and disclosed, but handing the same senator an envelope of cash for a vote is a felony. And in lots of places -- I'm thinking of the gift-giving expectations in Japan, or guanxi in China, or the way a mayor in a small Mediterranean town is *expected* to find jobs for his constituents' nephews -- behavior that looks like textbook clientelism by OECD standards is locally understood as basic decency, loyalty, or hospitality, and refusing to play along is what marks you as untrustworthy.

So here's what I want help with. What does the mainstream Western legal practice around bribery actually presuppose about what corruption *is*? It seems to assume some sharp separation between a person and their public role -- that an official acting from personal gratitude or personal ties is corrupted, while the same official acting from "the rules" is fine -- and I want that reconstructed carefully. Which rival pictures does it quietly rule out? For instance, a view where relationships of reciprocal gift-giving are how trust is built in the first place, and impersonal rule-following is the suspicious, anti-social thing. Is the facilitation-payment exemption coherent with the rest, or does it reveal that the real underlying principle is something more like "corruption is whatever subverts decisions that matter" rather than anything about gifts or roles? And if the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- if several different theories of corruption would license roughly the same laws -- tell me where, because I suspect a lot of the confident talk about "universal integrity standards" papers over that.

Last thing, since it will shape how I write the piece: if we dropped the presupposed Western view and replaced it with the relational/reciprocity picture, concretely what would have to change? Would the FCPA have to go, or just shrink? Would disclosed campaign donations start looking like the scandal and the envelope of cash like the misdemeanor? I don't need a bibliography or a reading list, just the sharpest reconstruction you can give me of the implicit theory, where it's coherent, and where it's held together with tape.
