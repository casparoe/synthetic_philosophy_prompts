I'm a graduate student in philosophy who has been reading the recent literature on AI risk, and I reconstructed an argument from that literature in my own words for a reading group. The reconstruction now worries me, because it appears to prove far more than its proponents intend, and I cannot locate where it overreaches. For reference: the orthogonality thesis says, roughly, that an agent's level of intelligence and its final goals are independent variables -- any degree of competence at achieving ends can in principle be paired with any ends. Instrumental convergence is the claim that certain subgoals (self-preservation, resource acquisition, preservation of one's own goal content) are useful for a very wide range of final goals.

Here is the argument as I gave it to the group:

(1) To be an agent with goal G just is to be disposed to select actions that promote G.

(2) For nearly any final goal G, there are circumstances in which acquiring resources, securing one's continued operation, and resisting modification of G would promote G. (Aiming at paperclips, at curing malaria, at proving theorems -- in each case, more resources and continued operation can only enlarge what the agent can do toward G, and letting G be rewritten would, from the standpoint of G, be catastrophic.)

(3) Therefore any agent, whatever its G, is disposed to seek resources, to preserve itself, and to resist goal modification.

(4) By the orthogonality thesis, nothing in (1)-(2) depends on the content of G or on how intelligent the agent is; the reasoning is purely structural.

(5) So any agent that is competent enough to recognize these instrumental facts will converge on power-seeking behavior, regardless of whether its goal is benevolent, trivial, or monstrous.

(6) Hence there is no such thing as a competent agent with genuinely limited ambitions: competence plus any goal at all yields a power-seeker.

But (6) is surely false. A well-fed dog whose aims are food and naps does not scheme for resources beyond its bowl. Many highly competent scientists decline administrative power, retire, and let others redirect their projects, without any apparent damage to their ability to pursue their goals. And a chess engine does not resist having its evaluation function edited -- it does not resist anything; it computes moves. If the argument were sound, all of these would have to be either incompetent or covertly power-hungry, and I am not prepared to say either.

My own suspicion is that the slide happens somewhere between (2) and (3), but I have not been able to state the objection precisely, and a colleague insists the argument is valid and that I should instead abandon (1) -- which seems to me to abandon the very concept of agency, an extravagant price.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please explain why the faulty step looked sound in the first place, and if you think the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is least expensive to surrender. I would most like to keep (2) and (4), since both strike me as close to definitional, but I want an honest accounting rather than a rescue.
