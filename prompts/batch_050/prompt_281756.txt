In my physics class a few weeks ago, we were going over how Einstein's relativity superseded Newtonian mechanics, and a classmate asked the awkward question everyone was thinking: if Newton's theory turned out to be false, and so did the theories before it, why should we be any more confident in what we're being taught now? Our teacher brushed this off with a line she uses fairly often: every theory in the history of science has been closer to the truth than the one before it. The class seemed to accept this and moved on, but I haven't been able to stop picking at the phrase, because the more I think about it the less I understand what it's even claiming. When I tried to press her after class, she said Newtonian gravity is "approximately right" and relativity is "more approximately right," which honestly felt like repeating the slogan with extra steps.

My suspicion is that every interpretation I can come up with is either trivial, confused, or false. If the slogan just means our predictions match observation more closely over time, that's plainly true but seems too weak to answer my classmate, since a theory can predict well and still be deeply wrong about what exists (we learned that Ptolemaic astronomy predicted eclipses fine). If it means newer theories contain more true statements and fewer false ones, I'm not sure that's even coherent, since presumably any false theory still entails infinitely many true things and infinitely many false ones. If it means our theories are somehow converging on a final true description of the world, I don't see what evidence could establish that in advance. And if it means each theory is "literally closer" to the truth in some measurable sense, like points on a number line, I can't imagine what the units would be.

Could you lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims this slogan could be making, ordered roughly from the most trivial to the most radical? For each reading, I'd like you to state it carefully, assess whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or confused or false, and then say whether that reading would actually support using the slogan to dismiss my classmate's question, since that dismissal is the work the slogan was doing in class. I'd like to bring the list to our next discussion, so please make it something I can follow without a philosophy background.
