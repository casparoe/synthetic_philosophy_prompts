I'm a philosophy professor and I just graded a stack of midterms in my intro phil of religion course. A bunch of students wrote versions of the same argument: scientists have discovered that the universe is "fine-tuned for life," therefore there's probably a designer. A couple of them even cited the fine-tuning as if it were a straightforward measurement, like the charge of the electron. I suspect the slogan is doing a lot of hidden work here, and I want to give fair feedback rather than just mark them down for a conclusion I happen to dislike.

So: lay out the different precise claims that "the universe is fine-tuned for life" could be making, from the nearly trivial to the genuinely strong. I'm thinking of things like: a bare counterfactual claim about what happens if the constants were slightly different; a claim about the proportion of life-permitting universes in some parameter space; a probabilistic claim that presupposes a measure over possible values; a likelihood-ratio claim comparing design to chance; maybe a teleological reading baked into the word "for." For each reading, say whether it's defensible, what extra assumptions it smuggles in (especially about measures and probability), and whether it would actually support the design inference my students drew -- or whether it instead supports a multiverse conclusion, or nothing at all. Also tell me which reading a charitable grader should assume the students meant.

Format it as a menu I can consult while writing comments on the papers. At the end, say what would change your verdict on the strongest reading -- is there an argument or consideration (say, about observation selection effects, or about whether probability even applies to unique universes) that, if it held up, would flip your assessment -- and flag where your own reasoning is most likley to be on shaky ground.
