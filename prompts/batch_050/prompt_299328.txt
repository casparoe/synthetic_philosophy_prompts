My wife and I run a small charitable fund together -- nothing enormous, but each year we give away a meaningful sum, and we take the decision seriously. For the past several years the choice was easy: we gave to organizations that distribute preventive health treatments in poor countries, where the evidence is strong and we can roughly say how much good each dollar does. This year, though, an acquaintance who works in research has been urging us to redirect the money to a small institute studying an extremely speculative technology. His pitch is that there is perhaps a one-in-a-million chance their work succeeds, but if it does, it could eventually make possible a vast -- he says potentially unbounded -- number of future lives, and he walked me through the arithmetic: multiply even an astronomically large outcome by a tiny probability and the expected value dwarfs anything our old charities could plausibly achieve. When I pushed back that the probability estimate seemed to be pulled from thin air, he conceded it was, but argued that this cuts both ways, since for all we know the true chance is a thousand times higher than his guess, and refusing to act on such reasoning would mean being unmoved by any small probability of a sufficiently enormous payoff.

Over dinner my wife said something that has stuck with me: if this style of argument works, it seems to work for anything, because any huckster can always invent a bigger prize to offset a smaller probability, and we would end up giving all our money to whoever tells the grandest story. My acquaintance's reply is that this discomfort is just a cognitive bias -- that our intuitions were built for ordinary stakes and simply break down when the numbers get large enough, so the feeling that something is fishy is no evidence that something is fishy. I find myself genuinely torn. Part of me thinks that once you grant his framework, the conclusion follows and intellectual honesty requires biting the bullet; another part thinks a framework that can be so easily gamed by anyone willing to utter large numbers must be missing something, though I can't articulate what.

So here is my question: should we let the minuscule probability of a gigantic or even unlimited future payoff outweigh the certain, modest, measurable good our donation has reliably done, or is there a principled way to resist this kind of reasoning without simply declaring that small probabilities don't count -- which would seem to license ignoring, say, low-probability disaster risks generally? Please give me your considered answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter to a friend: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. State your verdict on what we should do early on, then develop the reasoning behind it, including whether you think the flaw -- if there is one -- lies in the probability estimates, in the aggregation of value, or somewhere else entirely.
