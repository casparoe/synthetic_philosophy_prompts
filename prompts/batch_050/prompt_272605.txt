Ok so I built an argument out of the Grand Inquisitor chapter for my reading group and everyone hated it but nobody could tell me exactly which step breaks. Setup, paraphrased not quoted: the Inquisitor tells the returned Christ that man is weak and rebellious, that the freedom Christ offered is a crushing burden, and that most people would gladly hand it over to whoever gives them bread, miracle, mystery, and authority, so they can be happy like children instead of agonized like adults.

The argument:

(1) Most people are happier without the burden of freedom — the Inquisitor's whole speech in the cell is basically this.

(2) If a choice predictably makes people miserable, taking that choice away is compassionate rather than cruel.

(3) So the Church's substitution of miracle, mystery, and authority for freedom is justified.

(4) But nothing in the reasoning from (1) to (3) depends on the choice being about salvation — it only depends on the choice being a source of suffering.

(5) Career choices, romantic choices, what to read, what to believe — all of these cause anxiety and regret for most ordinary people, arguably more reliably than theological questions do for most of us day to day.

(6) So a benevolent authority is justified in making all of these choices for us, right down to small everyday ones.

(7) That's obviously absurd — a state picking my breakfast cereal for my own good isn't the Inquisitor's compassionate order, it's just petty tyranny.

(8) So something in (1) through (4) has to give.

My group says (2) is the weak step, but honestly the Inquisitor seems to actually believe (2) — he frames the whole project as love for the weak millions who can't carry what the strong few can. My own suspicion is the crack is somewhere in (4), like maybe the speech is only about freedom of conscience on ultimate questions (the three temptations stuff) and not freedom generally. But then I cant explain why the Inquisitor talks as if man is too weak for freedom full stop, and not just too weak for freedom about God.

Which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost for a reading of the chapter as a whole — including Ivan's framing of the poem and Alyosha's reaction at the end? If you think the argument is sound and the Inquisitor really is committed to the cereal-picking state, tell me that instead; I'd rather get a sharp disagreement than a diplomatic both-sides answer. And if I've misread the chapter anywhere, dont be polite about it, just say so.
