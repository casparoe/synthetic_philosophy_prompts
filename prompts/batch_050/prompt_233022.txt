I'm a TA for an intermediate logic course (mostly philosophy majors, a few mathematicians) and I'm working through the stack of problem sets from the unit on intuitionistic logic. The students have seen the Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov reading of the connectives, natural deduction without double-negation elimination, and the basics of Kripke semantics. One submission has me going back and forth, and I'd like a second grader's read before I commit to a mark.

The assignment asked students to consider four formulas of intuitionistic propositional logic and, for each, state whether it is a theorem, giving either a derivation or informal BHK-style justification if so, and if not, an explanation of why not (for instance by sketching a Kripke countermodel, or by showing that the formula would collapse the logic into something stronger). The four formulas were: (1) ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P); (2) ¬(P ∧ Q) → (¬P ∨ ¬Q); (3) (¬P ∨ ¬Q) → ¬(P ∧ Q); (4) ¬¬¬P → ¬P.

The student's submission, verbatim: (1) Not a theorem. Since P ∨ ¬P is not intuitionistically provable, and intuitionistic double negation is strictly weaker than assertion, prefixing ¬¬ cannot make an unprovable disjunction provable. A one-node Kripke model in which P is not forced at the single world witnesses this: P fails there, and ¬P is never forced either, so ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P) fails. (2) Not a theorem. This direction of De Morgan's law is classically valid but would, if added, yield the law of excluded middle, since substituting ¬P for Q turns the intuitionistically provable ¬(P ∧ ¬P) into ¬P ∨ ¬¬P, i.e., excluded middle; so it cannot be derived. (3) Theorem. Assume ¬P ∨ ¬Q and, for contradiction, P ∧ Q. From the left disjunct, P and ¬P clash; from the right, Q and ¬Q clash; either way we get ⊥, so ¬(P ∧ Q) follows by negation introduction. (4) Theorem. Assume ¬¬¬P and P. From P we can derive ¬¬P (any assumption of ¬P contradicts P), which contradicts ¬¬¬P; discharging P gives ¬P, and discharging ¬¬¬P would give the further step to ¬¬¬P → ¬P.

Please grade this out of 20 points (5 per item) and give me the specific per-item comments I could paste into the feedback box: for each item, whether the verdict is right, whether the justification actually establishes it, and exactly where any reasoning step fails or proves less than the student thinks it does. Where the student reaches the right verdict for a subtly wrong reason, or the wrong verdict with reasoning that is almost right, I want that distinguished explicitly rather than averaged into the score. End with the total and a one-paragraph summary comment suitable for the student.
