Two friends of mine who both took a philosophy of mind course got into an argument at a party last weekend, and later both texted me separately asking me to say who won. I honestly cant tell, so I'm outsourcing it to you. Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

Maya was defending the view that mental content is fixed by biological function -- a state's content is whatever the mechanism producing it was selected to detect or do. Her pitch was that this is the only naturalistic story that explains how a representation can be *wrong*. A frog's fly-detector still means FLY when a passing bee-bee triggers it, because the mechanism's function is to catch flies and the bee-bee isn't one. Pure causal or informational theories, she said, can't get you error, just correlation.

Theo came back with what I think is a standard worry: in the frog's actual environment, every small dark moving thing happened to be a fly. So the mechanism was equally "selected for detecting small dark moving things." The selection history doesn't discriminate between those two descriptions, so the view doesn't actually pin content down any better than the crude causal theories she was dunking on.

Maya's response: there's a difference between what correlates with selection and what explains it. The mechanism proliferated because flies are nutritious -- that's what made detection pay off. "Small dark moving thing" was extensionally equivalent in that environment but it's not what did the explanatory work, so the content is FLY and not the gerrymandered alternative.

Then Theo invented a case: imagine a cave fish living near hydrothermal vents where its prey only ever occur together with a particular mineral haze, and the co-occurrence isn't accidental -- it's fixed by the local chemistry, basically lawlike. The fish's detector evolved in a world where prey and haze could not possibly have come apart. Now, he said, both explanations of the selection seem equally true -- it's overdetermined. So either content is indeterminate on her theory, or she has to say the detector means PREY-OR-HAZE, which seems wrong.

Maya tried a move: look at what the downstream systems do with the signal. The fish's whole setup is aimed at *eating* -- the consuming mechanisms treat the signal as a guide to prey, and that's where the fine-grained content comes from. Theo said that doesn't help because the consumer systems also never had to distinguish prey-cases from haze-cases, so nothing anywhere in the fish's history breaks the tie. And then he piled on two more things: first, a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a frog that congeals out of a swamp by sheer accident has no selection history at all, so on her view its inner states mean literally nothing, which he called insane. Second, he asked about ML systems trained by evolutionary search: does the training algorithm count as "selection," giving the system content of its own, or does it only inherit content from the engineers? If the algorithm counts, content seems absurdly cheap. If it doesn't, the theory goes silent about the most interesting representational systems being built right now.

Maya said the swamp-frog bullet is biteable -- content is a historical kind, like being a genuine dollar bill rather than a perfect counterfeit -- and the party got loud so it ended there.

Can you go through this point by point and tell me who's right at each step? Specifically: does Maya's explanation-vs-correlation reply actually survive Theo's overdetermined cave fish case, or does the tie really not break? Does the "look at the consumer systems" move do any work? Is the swamp-frog a refutation or a fair cost? And is there anything important both of them are missing about what a theory of content even needs to deliver here?
