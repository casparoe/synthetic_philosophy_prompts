I'm drafting a short statement of my position on mental causation for a reading group, and before I circulate it I want to know whether the package actually hangs together. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Every concrete particular event is identical with, or wholly constituted by, physical events; there are no nonphysical substances or events.
(2) Mental properties are nonetheless not identical with physical properties, since mental states are multiply realizable across radically different physical substrates.
(3) The physical domain is causally closed: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause.
(4) Mental properties are causally efficacious as such: my headache's hurting, and not merely its neural realizer, is part of why I took the aspirin.
(5) It would be intolerable if ordinary human behavior were systematically overdetermined by two independent sufficient causes; any acceptable account of mental causation must avoid that.
(6) Causation is best understood in counterfactual, interventionist terms: a property counts as a cause of an outcome when suitable interventions on it would make a difference to that outcome, other things held fixed.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me on the exclusion problem in general. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and how severe is each? Which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection? For each real tension, identify the cheapest available fix and what it costs. Finally, if I had to revise exactly one commitment, which single revision would restore the most consistency for the least philosophical cost?
