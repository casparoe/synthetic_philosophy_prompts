I serve on the five-member conduct and appeals board of a small consortium that runs study-abroad programs, and two connected matters will come before us next month, so I need a written opinion I can circulate in advance. The first is an individual appeal. Our student code requires participants to comply with local law and to show respect for local norms of conduct, and it makes violations grounds for a formal conduct notation that follows the student home. One of our students, placed in a mid-sized German city, was fined by police for crossing a completely empty street against a red pedestrian signal shortly after midnight; the host institution reported it, and our program director entered a notation. The student appeals. Her argument, in substance, is that at that hour, with no traffic and no one watching, the red-signal rule functions the way queueing or tipping customs do — as a convention rather than a serious moral requirement — that conventions vary from place to place in ways a visitor cannot be expected to have internalized, and that it is unfair to punish her for failing to treat as morally loaded something her hosts themselves experience as a mere fine. She also argues, pointedly, that if the board is going to punish legal-but-harmless infractions, consistency demands it punish queue-jumping, tipping failures, and loud phone calls on trains, which no one proposes to do.

The second matter is a policy proposal from our central office that would settle this class of cases by rule: any violation of local law, however trivial and whatever the local enforcement practice, triggers an automatic conduct notation, while violations of mere convention trigger only an advisory conversation. Roughly half the board supports this on grounds of clarity and administrability — students can be told a bright-line rule, and we avoid judging which customs count. The other half thinks the law/convention line is morally arbitrary in both directions: some conventions (orderly queuing, honoring informal turn-taking) are genuine cooperative schemes whose violation harms identifiable people, while some laws (the midnight signal on an empty road) bind, if at all, only through something like a general obligation not to free-ride on others' compliance. There is also disagreement about the visitor question specifically: one camp holds that guests have heightened reason to defer to local rules precisely because they cannot reliably judge which ones matter; the other holds that visitors' predictably imperfect knowledge is a reason for leniency, not strictness.

Please draft the board's written opinion. It should reach and defend a concrete bottom line on both matters: how to rule on this student's appeal (uphold, overturn, or uphold with a modified sanction), and whether to adopt, reject, or amend the central office's proposal, with the amended text sketched if you recommend amending. The opinion should engage seriously with the strongest point on whichever side loses — including the consistency argument that law and convention cannot be cleanly separated, and the question of what, if anything, follows morally from being a guest rather than a member — rather than simply asserting that rules are rules. Reasoning the student and the dissenting board members could actually respond to is the goal; this is a document we will act on, not an essay.
