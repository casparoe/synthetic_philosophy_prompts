Next week is the third session of my second-year metalogic seminar (philosophy majors, ~14 students, 75 minutes). We've just spent two weeks on Cantor — diagonalization, the uncountability of the reals — and the class has thoroughly absorbed the lesson that some infinities are bigger than others. Now I have to break that, because the following week I'm introducing the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem and Skolem's paradox, and I know from past years what happens if I just state it: half the room decides it's a verbal trick, the other half decides set theory is incoherent, and neither group recovers.

So design me an in-class activity for the session BEFORE they ever hear the theorem's name, one that makes them feel the central weirdness on their own: that whether a collection "has an enumeration" seems to depend on which functions exist in your world — that a community equipped with only countably many usable objects could run a perfectly legitimate proof that some set is uncountable, while someone standing outside can see the enumeration they can't reach. Some half-formed ideas of mine, use or scrap as you like: split the room into "insiders" restricted to some fixed stock of names or symbols for building candidate enumerations, plus one or two "outsiders" holding a master list; a game where the insiders have to certify a collection as uncountable and the outsider keeps frustrating them; maybe a round where the insiders get to run the diagonal argument themselves and it works, internally.

What I need: concrete rules and materials (no computers; index cards, envelopes and a whiteboard are fine), a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, a win condition that doesn't involve money, and a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the actual theorem and onto the relativity of set-theoretic notions. Be honest about where the analogy breaks — a card game obviously can't reproduce real model theory — so tell me exactly which features the activity genuinely captures and which I have to supply in lecture afterwards (I'm guessing the outsider's enumeration simply not being a set of the insiders' world is the crux, and maybe something about why the insiders' theory stays consistent the whole time). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I strongly suspect at least one group will accuse the outsider of cheating, and I want to know whether to design that moment in or head it off.
