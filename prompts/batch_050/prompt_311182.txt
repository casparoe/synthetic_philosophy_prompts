A colleague who teaches moral psychology has been classifying whether offenders know that what they did was wrong, assuming the conduct is in fact wrong. Her rulings so far: a psychopathic shoplifter who can recite the rules and predict punishment but treats “wrong” as merely “punished” does not know; a psychopathic fraudster who distinguishes moral from conventional violations and gives victim-centered justifications, but says only that “people shouldn’t defraud others,” does know; a psychopathic burglar who says, “I have conclusive reason not to do this,” while feeling no guilt and remaining entirely unmoved does know; a remorseful ideologue who understands his victims’ suffering but sincerely believes his cause removes every reason against harming them does not know; and a nonpsychopathic imitator who reproduces all the standard classifications without grasping why pain or betrayal matters does not know. Her next case is a psychopathic embezzler who passes the standard tests, explains why the victims’ losses count against fraud, and, when occupying an impartial judge’s perspective, says that he ought not to do it. When deliberating in the first person, however, he denies that those reasons apply to him—not because some goal overrides them, but because he regards himself as exempt from morality. Predict her verdict, identify at least two principles consistent with all her earlier rulings, explain how they diverge here, and name one further case that would best distinguish them.
