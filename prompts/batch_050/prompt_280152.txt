I teach a seminar on formal epistemology (upper-level undergrads, about 14 students, one 75-minute session) and I want an in-class activity that makes the Judy Benjamin problem actually bite -- the weirdness of updating on a conditional, where "just conditionalize on what you learned" doesn't settle anything.

Here's the setup I'd give them, in case you want to build on it: Judy is dropped somewhere in a field split into four quadrants. Her priors: P(Red) = 1/2, P(Blue) = 1/2; given Red, P(HQ) = 3/4; given Blue, P(HQ) = 1/2. So Red-HQ = 3/8, Red-Infantry = 1/8, Blue-HQ = 1/4, Blue-Infantry = 1/4. Then she gets one radio message and learns it for certain: "If you're in Red, you're in HQ." That's it. Question: what's her new P(Blue)? The fun is that conditionalizing on the material conditional (just rule out Red-Infantry and renormalize) pushes Blue up to 4/7, but moving the excluded mass onto Red-HQ instead keeps Blue at 1/2 -- and both feel defensible, and students split on it instantly.

What I want from you:

- Concrete rules and materials for a game where teams have to commit to an update rule and stake something on it (points, not money -- some simple scoring scheme that punishes being confidently wrong). Dice or a deck of cards is fine for generating actual positions.
- My favorite idea, which I'd love you to make work mechanically: run the SAME message under two different hidden protocols. In World A, the message is a guarantee (Red-Infantry was never going to happen; it's removed from the deck). In World B, the message is a policy (if she lands in Red-Infantry she gets moved to Red-HQ). Same words, same truth, but the right posterior over Blue differs between worlds -- 4/7 in one, 1/2 in the other. Ideally one sequence of card draws gets scored in both worlds in parallel so teams discover that no single rule wins both. If that parallel-scoring thing is too fiddly to run, tell me and give me the cleanest version that survives.
- A timeline for the 75 minutes, including when teams post their P(Blue) publicly (the disagreement on the board is half the value).
- A debrief that maps the two protocols onto the two update rules by name (straight conditionalization vs. imaging) and lands the real punchline: the message alone underdetermines the update, and you can't pick a rule without knowing how the message was generated.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., students conflating "not Red-Infantry" with the conditional itself, someone insisting the message is uninformative because it was guaranteed true, scoring noise over too few rounds, teams hedging by splitting points).
- An honest note on what the game can't reproduce: in the actual philosophical case there's no hidden protocol, Judy just learns the conditional, and the question of whether there's a protocol-free rational update is the whole open problem. The game dramatizes that but can't settle it -- I have to supply that part in the debrief. Flag anything else the activity distorts.

Don't lecture the students up front; they should hit the disagreement before anyone says "imaging" or "van Fraassen." Assume they've seen basic conditional probability but nothing else formal.
