A colleague has proposed the following spectrum argument against the transitivity of all-things-considered betterness. She calls it the Vanishing Pain Argument:

1. There is a finite sequence of possible lives L0, L1, ..., Ln. In L0, a person experiences forty years of uninterrupted, severe agony and then dies. Each subsequent life is exactly like its predecessor except that one additional millisecond of agony is replaced by neutral experience. In Ln, no agony remains: the person has forty years of wholly neutral experience and then dies.

2. If two lives are indiscriminable for the person living them—if she would not notice the relevant difference while it occurred and could not afterward determine which of the two lives she had lived—then neither life is better for her than the other.

3. Each adjacent pair in the sequence is indiscriminable in this sense. A single millisecond of agony, considered by itself, is too brief and too insignificant for the person to notice or remember.

4. Therefore, no adjacent life is better or worse for the person than its neighbor.

5. If neither of two outcomes is better than the other, they are equally good. Equal goodness is transitive, so each life in the sequence is exactly as good as every other.

6. Consequently, L0 and Ln are equally good: a life containing forty years of severe agony is exactly as good as an otherwise similar life containing none.

7. Since L0 is plainly worse than Ln, either the transitivity of betterness fails somewhere in the sequence, or the judgment about the endpoints must be abandoned.

My colleague thinks (7) poses a serious dilemma. I suspect that the argument trades on more than one notion of being indiscernible, insignificant, and equally good, but I am unsure where the first irreparable step occurs.

Which premise or inference should be rejected first? Explain why that step initially looks compelling, and compare the costs of at least three diagnoses: denying that indiscriminability implies equal welfare, treating adjacent lives as merely roughly equal or on a par rather than exactly equal, and allowing an indeterminate boundary at which sufficiently many tiny improvements become a substantial improvement. Finally, say what remains of the challenge to transitivity once the faulty step is removed. I would prefer to preserve both the transitivity of betterness and the judgment that L0 is worse than Ln, but I do not want either claim simply assumed.
