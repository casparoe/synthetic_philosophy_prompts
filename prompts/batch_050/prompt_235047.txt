As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I keep seeing the same argument about whether the world is becoming less violent, and it seems strangely resistant to evidence. One side produces familiar trends: declining homicide rates, fewer deaths in interstate war, the legal disappearance of slavery and torture, and falling rates of casual cruelty. The other responds that this accounting is parochial: nuclear deterrence substitutes a permanent possibility of unprecedented slaughter for ordinary battle; authoritarian surveillance replaces public beating with quieter coercion; industrial animal suffering has expanded enormously; and a period without catastrophe may merely be a lucky draw rather than evidence that the underlying danger has declined. The first side then accuses the second of stretching the concept of violence until every unwanted outcome counts, while the second accuses the first of mistaking low observed victimisation for safety. Nobody seems to move.

To make the structure clearer, consider two otherwise similar societies of 100 million people. In Society A, ordinary assault and murder have been eliminated, but everyone lives under pervasive non-lethal algorithmic supervision, and an automated security system has a 0.1 per cent annual chance of malfunctioning and killing ten million people. In Society B, there are roughly one hundred intentional killings and ten thousand serious assaults each year, but no comparable catastrophic mechanism and much less institutional control. Is A less violent because fewer people are presently attacked, or more violent because its population bears greater expected harm and lives under constant domination? Now vary the example: keep the expected number of deaths equal but concentrate A’s deaths in rare mass events; make A’s surveillance entirely benevolent in intent; or compare societies whose populations differ by a factor of one hundred. Which intuitions survive these variations?

My hypothesis is that the dispute is stalled because violence is being used as an audit predicate with several concealed switches: realised harm versus credible risk, direct bodily injury versus institutional coercion, intentional wrongdoing versus system-mediated harm, per-capita rates versus absolute totals, and average years versus exposure to extreme tails. But perhaps I am over-systematising, and the real disagreement is a straightforward empirical one about which harms are likely to dominate in the long run. What kind of disagreement is this, in the last analysis? Are the sides using different concepts, applying different standards of evidence, disagreeing about the appropriate moral unit, or answering different questions under one label? Is there a fact of the matter about whether violence has declined, or only a set of legitimate measures that need not rank societies alike? Given your diagnosis, say what would constitute progress: a sharper taxonomy, separate indices rather than a single scalar, an empirical test, a formal result showing that no index can satisfy all our intuitive requirements, or simply a decision to stop asking the question in its present form.
