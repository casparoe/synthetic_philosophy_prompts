So last weekend I got into this stupidly long argument with my friend Marcus that started over brunch -- the cafe had two identical seats free and I just sat in one without thinking, and he goes, half-joking at first, that that's actually irrational, like if I genuinely had no reason to prefer either seat then I had no reason to sit at all, and a truly rational person would be stuck like that donkey between the two haystacks. He pushed it way further than I expected: he thinks every justified choice bottoms out in some preference, even a tiny one, and if I can't name the preference then I must have had an unconscious one, and "I just picked" is always a confabulation. I kept saying no, sometimes options really are tied and picking anyway is exactly what rationality looks like, and the demand for a further reason is what's broken. Anyway it got under my skin enough that I turned it into a talk.

I'm presenting at our department's works-in-progress series in three weeks, defending roughly the thesis that choosing between genuinely equal options without any tie-breaking reason is not only permissible but can be the uniquely rational move, and that the burden is on the other side to explain why rationality requires a reason where no reason can exist. The room will be about fifteen people: mostly economists and rational choice people from the business school who come to these things, plus a couple of formal epistemologists and one Kantian who comes to everything. Nobody does medieval philosophy, so I don't need help on the history of the ass, I need help on the firing line. Prepare me for question period: what objections will this specific audience actually raise, phrased the way they'd phrase them (the econ people will come at it through revealed preference and utility functions, the formal epistemologists through something else, the Kantian through something else again), ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do if I fumble them. For each one give me a short reply I could deliver in under two minutes, a note on where that reply is weakest, and flag anything I should just concede up front rather than fight. Also tell me the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks.

One more thing, and I realize this is slightly masochistic: one of the objections I got from Marcus at brunch was "if picking arbitrarily is rational, why does it feel so weird and effortful to do it deliberately" and I genuinely didn't have a good answer, so if that maps onto something this audience would ask, include it. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like a long email from a friend who's thought about this -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I absorb this stuff better as a letter I can read twice on the train than as a spreadsheet.
