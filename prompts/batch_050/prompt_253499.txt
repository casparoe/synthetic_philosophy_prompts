At a recent panel on AI and the arts, one speaker defended AI-generated images with an analogy I have since heard repeated often: prompting an image model is like operating a camera. When photography first appeared, painters objected that the machine did all the work and the photographer merely pressed a button; history vindicated the photographers, because choosing the subject, the framing, the timing, and the settings turned out to be a genuinely creative practice. By the same reasoning, the speaker argued, a person who writes a prompt, iterates on it, and selects among the outputs is exercising creative authorship, and dismissing AI images on the grounds that the model did the work simply repeats the mistake the painters made about photography. The analogy was doing real argumentative work in the room: it was used to close the question of whether prompters deserve to be called artists.

I would like to know how far this analogy actually holds. Please provide the following.

First, an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the camera, the photographer, the scene in front of the lens, the decisive moment, the choice of lens and exposure, and the darkroom or editing stage, on the AI side of the comparison. Be precise, since I suspect some of these roles have no good counterpart and others have more than one.

Second, an audit of the disanalogies, separated into those that matter for the authorship question and those that are merely superficial. Please address at least the following. (a) A photograph is causally coupled to a real scene that resists the photographer and can surprise her, whereas a generated image answers to a prompt rather than to the world; does the kind of creativity involved in confronting a resistant subject matter differ in kind or only in degree from steering a model? (b) A camera is a general-purpose instrument, but a generative model's weights are built from vast numbers of existing human works, so part of the aesthetic content of any output arguably originates with the artists in the training data rather than with either the prompter or the machine; the photography analogy seems to make this contribution invisible. Does that matter for who deserves authorship credit? (c) The historical premise itself: the painters' complaint was arguably about mechanical reproduction lacking artistic intention, not about labor-sharing with a device, so it is worth asking whether the analogy even reads the photography episode correctly.

Third, a judgment on whether the speaker's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy fails in the relevant respects, say what better comparison captures the prompter's role — curator, art director issuing a commission, client with taste, improvisational collaborator — and what that alternative comparison would imply about authorship instead.

End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence how far the photography analogy carries the authorship conclusion.
