I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding blogs for years, so superrationality is familiar territory -- but I keep running into a one-liner version of it that I suspect is doing several different jobs at once. The form I hear most often is: "cooperate, because someone exactly like you will reason exactly the same way and reach the same conclusion." Most recently a friend used it to end a debate about voting: I was arguing that one vote in a large electorate has negligible causal effect, and he replied that I'm not causally choosing for myself alone -- everyone sufficiently similar to me runs the same deliberation, so my deciding to stay home is, so to speak, everyone's deciding to stay home. When I pressed him on the "so to speak," he just repeated the slogan more slowly. I've also seen the same sentence deployed for one-shot prisoner's dilemmas against identical copies, for climate restraint, and once, confusingly, as if it were just the golden rule dressed up.

What I'd like is a disambiguation, not another explanation of Hofstadter's idea. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "someone like you will reason the same way" could be making, from the most modest to the most ambitious: for instance, the bare logical point that two genuinely identical deterministic processes yield identical outputs; the evidential claim that my decision is strong evidence about what my counterparts decide; the causal claim that my choosing cooperation somehow brings about my counterpart's cooperation; the decision-theoretic claim that on the right decision theory (evidential? functional? timeless?) defecting is simply irrational in symmetric games; and the quasi-Kantian reading where the slogan is really about universalizability rather than correlation at all. For each reading, I want verdicts, not just exposition: which are trivially true but toothless, which are substantive and defensible, and which are straightforwardly false or incoherent. In particular, tell me plainly whether the evidential reading actually survives the objection that my decision screens off nothing in a genuinely one-shot, no-communication case, and whether the logical-identity reading does any real work once the two reasoners are in slightly different environments (as voters are, unlike clones in a thought experiment).

Then commit: say which single reading, if any, actually justifies my friend's move in the voting debate -- where the agents are merely similar, not identical, and the causal channel is nil -- and whether the honest response to my negligible-impact argument is a decision-theoretic one, a game-theoretic one, or a concession that voting is a bad bet on consequentialist grounds and the slogan is a distraction. I'd rather you take a definite position and defend it than leave me a menu. Please give each reading a short label as you go, and end with a final line of the form \answer{label}, naming the one reading that survives scrutiny as a justification for the voting argument -- or \answer{none} if you conclude none of them do.
