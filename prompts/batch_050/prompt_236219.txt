I'm the treasurer of a housing cooperative of about forty households, and we've stumbled into a problem that a friend of mine, who reads a lot of political philosophy, tells me is called the boundary problem. We have to make a big decision soon, about whether to sell a piece of land the co-op owns, and the argument over who is even entitled to vote on it has turned out to be nastier than the argument about the land itself. Some members say everyone affected by a decision should get a say, but the sale would affect our whole street, and surely the whole street can't vote in our co-op meeting. Others say only people who have to live under the co-op's rules count, but that would exclude the families on our waiting list, who want to join precisely so that the rules will apply to them. The deeper I think about it, the stranger it gets: someone has to settle the electorate before anything else can be settled, but settling the electorate is itself a decision, and a decision seems to need an electorate already in place to make it. It feels like a circle with no legitimate way in, and yet real groups clearly do get started somehow.

Before our real meeting, I want to run a 90-minute workshop for about fifteen co-op members (adults of mixed ages, no academic background, several of them firmly convinced the eligibility question is a boring formality to rush through) so that they feel in their bones why the question of who votes is genuinely hard, and why whoever gets to define the group quietly controls what the group decides. I'd like you to act as an experienced workshop designer with a background in both group facilitation and political philosophy. Design an in-person activity or game that stages this problem rather than lecturing about it. Concretely, I want: an object-level decision with small but real stakes that the workshop itself must produce (something like how to spend a modest refreshment budget, not a hypothetical); a set of role cards with plausible claims to inclusion, such as a founding member, a new applicant from the waiting list, a former member who moved away last year, a neighbor affected by everything we do, and someone who does most of the maintenance work but never formally joined; rules arranged so that the outcome of the object-level decision flips depending on which electorate is chosen, and so that every attempt to vote on eligibility forces the group to confront who gets to vote in the eligibility vote; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the structure of the problem, including the competing principles for drawing the boundary and the apparent regress. Participants should not hear the phrase boundary problem or any philosophy vocabulary until the debrief, and there should be no lecture up front.

Before writing the final plan, please think carefully and brainstorm at least three candidate core mechanics, briefly weigh their strengths and weaknesses, and tell me why you're choosing the one you go with. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- for instance, I anticipate people demanding that I, as facilitator, just set the rules, which seems philosophically interesting in itself -- and how to handle each failure mode. Finally, be frank about which feature of the real problem the activity cannot reproduce (the real stakes of exclusion, the fact that in actual politics there is no neutral facilitator standing outside the group) and what I should say out loud to compensate for that gap.
