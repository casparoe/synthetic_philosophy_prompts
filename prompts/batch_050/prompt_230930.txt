I'm a PhD student in cognitive science (working on sensorimotor approaches to perception, but my department is mostly predictive processing people, which is relevant below). For my second-year review I've been asked to submit a one-page statement of my theoretical commitments, and before I hand it in I want to stress-test it for internal coherence, because my supervisor has a habit of finding the one pair of claims that doesn't fit and spending the whole meeting on it.

Here's the set, roughly as I'd write it:

1. Perception isn't the passive intake of information that then gets processed; it's an active, skilled engagement with the environment, and perceptual experience is partly constituted by sensorimotor know-how rather than merely caused by what's in the head.

2. Predictive processing is basically right as the unifying computational framework for the brain: perception, action and learning are all minimising prediction error under a generative model.

3. I'm anti-representationalist. Explanations that posit internal content-bearing states are a hangover from cognitivism, and the good explanations in the literature (the dynamical systems ones, e.g. for coordination and navigation) show we can do without them.

4. I'm on board with the extended mind: in the right coupling conditions, Otto's notebook genuinely is part of his memory, not just a tool his memory consults.

5. On consciousness I'm a neural internalist: the phenomenal character of an experience is fixed entirely by what's going on in the brain, whatever story you tell about cognition more broadly.

6. Case verdict: the blind cane user literally feels the texture of the ground at the tip of the cane; the cane is transparent equipment, like my fingers are to me.
