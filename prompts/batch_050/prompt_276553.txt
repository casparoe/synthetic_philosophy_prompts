Next month I am giving a forty-minute talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on the foundations of statistical mechanics, and I want a serious rehearsal for the question period. My paper defends a package I call the Boundary View: the thermodynamic arrow of time is fully explained by the hypothesis that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate, where that hypothesis is to be understood not as a brute contingent fact and not as a special dynamical law but as lawlike within a best-system account of laws, jointly with a uniform probability distribution over all the microstates compatible with that initial macrostate. My argument has three moves. First, that this package is the simplest systematization of an enormous body of fact, which is what qualifies it for lawhood on the best-system picture. Second, that the low-entropy past plus the uniform measure recovers, in one stroke, the asymmetry of records, the asymmetry of intervention and control, and the asymmetry of causal influence, so that everything we mean by the direction of time traces back to it. Third, that rival approaches -- a law that directly makes entropy increase, or an appeal to some fundamental asymmetry in the dynamics -- either smuggle the arrow in or buy less explanatory unification at higher cost.

The audience matters more than the thesis here. The room will contain: several philosophers of physics known to be sympathetic to the time-symmetry critique, the line that whatever explanatory work is done by a low-entropy condition imposed at one temporal boundary would be done equally by imposing it at the other, so that my view either proves too much or covertly assumes the very asymmetry it explains; two working statistical mechanicians who will press the classic reversibility and recurrence worries -- time-reversal-invariant microdynamics cannot yield strictly monotonic entropy increase, and a bounded system must eventually return arbitrarily close to its initial state -- and who are skeptical of philosophical talk of laws in general; one cosmologist who will want to know what the early low entropy actually consisted in, given that the matter content of the early universe was near thermal equilibrium and the disequilibrium seems to live in the gravitational degrees of freedom, and whether cosmic inflation explains the initial condition or merely presupposes it; and one metaphysician of science hostile to best-system accounts who will ask by what right a probabilistic posit about a single unobservable event at the edge of time counts as a law of nature rather than an article of faith.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the generic textbook ones. Give me, in order of expected damage to the paper -- weighing both how likely each is to be raised and how bad it is if my answer fails -- the objections, each phrased the way that particular member of the audience would put it, in their idiom rather than mine. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and then an honest note on where that reply is softest, so I know which fights to prolong and which to exit gracefully. Tell me what I should concede at the outset, before anyone asks, to take the sting out of the room. Tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session, and why that one. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the best answer I could plausibly give if it comes.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write me a long letter before the workshop: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. One objection per paragraph, in the order you have ranked them. Please write in American English.
