Many organizations give each employee final authority over a defined set of "personal" choices — hairstyle, religious dress, whether to display family photos at one's desk — and refuse to override those choices even when coworkers unanimously disapprove. Yet the same organizations typically endorse a general efficiency norm: if everyone would prefer some change and no one objects, it should be made. Sen's liberal paradox shows that these two commitments cannot be jointly satisfied in general: once people hold preferences about one another's personal choices, granting even two individuals decisive authority over their own spheres can conflict with always implementing unanimous (weakly Pareto) preferences. What, then, does the workplace practice presuppose about how this conflict is to be resolved? Does it implicitly reject Pareto, launder "meddlesome" preferences out of the ones that count, or treat rights as constraints on which arrangements are admissible at all rather than as one input among others? Are these reconstructions genuinely different commitments, and does the practice strictly require one of them, or is it compatible with several? Consider what the answer implies about further features of the practice — that disputes are often settled by informal social pressure rather than formal override, and that employees may voluntarily waive their authority. Finally, what would the policy have to look like if the organization instead treated unanimous preference as inviolable?
