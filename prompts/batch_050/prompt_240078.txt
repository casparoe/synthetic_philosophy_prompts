Ok so I've been chewing on the complicity / "participating in bad stuff you didn't cause" cluster for a while (the usual LessWrong-adjacent path: ineffective altruism worries, consumer ethics, "but my one purchase doesn't matter" arguments) and I've written down where I've landed. Before I post this anywhere I want to know if the package actually hangs together:

(1) Buying factory-farmed meat is wrong even though any individual purchase almost certainly changes nothing, because of the standard threshold/expected-value argument: sometime your purchase is the one that tips a bulk order, and the ex ante expected harm is nonzero and bad.

(2) "If I don't take the job, someone else will" is no excuse. Taking a job at, say, a weapons manufacturer or a predatory lender is wrong even when the slot gets filled either way and the same work gets done.

(3) Individual carbon footprints are a rounding error and people should stop stressing about them. Flying to a conference is fine; the marginal tonne does nothing and treating personal emissions as morally loaded is just moralizing noise.

(4) Merely benefiting from injustice isn't complicity. Wearing clothes with exploitation somewhere upstream in the supply chain, using platforms run by companies whose politics I hate, enjoying infrastructure built by people who were treated terribly — none of that makes me a participant in the wrongdoing. Complicity requires participation, not benefit.

(5) Voting for the lesser evil isn't complicity in the lesser evil's crimes. If both candidates will do some awful things, picking the less awful one doesn't implicate you in the awful.

So: which of these are actually in tension, which just look like it, and if something has to go, what's the cheapest revision and what does it cost me? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning — and if one single revision cleans up the most, flag it. One thing I want you to actually check rather than wave at: whether the argument I lean on in (1) quietly does work for or against some of the others, because I have a nagging feeling the expected-value machinery cuts in more than one direction here.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — no preamble, no restating my position back at me, just the verdict and then the reasoning. And say how confident you are in the audit overall, plus specifically which of your calls you're least sure about.
