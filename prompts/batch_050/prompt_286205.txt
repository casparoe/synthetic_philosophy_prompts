A position I keep coming back to after years of reading decision-theory discussions on the blogs is roughly this: in a one-shot, symmetric, no-communication game where I have strong evidence that the other players reason much as I do -- the Luring Lottery setup Hofstadter ran in his magazine column, where one person wins a prize whose size shrinks with the number of entries, or a Platonia-dilemma-style one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a near-copy of myself -- the rational move is to cooperate (or refrain from entering), because my choice and the other players' choices are correlated even though nothing causal flows between them. I'm aware this is exactly the kind of reasoning that makes defenders of orthodox causal decision theory reach for the word "cooperating with a clone of yourself is still defecting," and I've absorbed the standard reply that logical correlation isn't causal influence and that you can't control what you can't affect. I've stayed on the superrational side anyway. But I want to stress-test the position rather than just rehearse the arguments I already like.

So: what would count against it? Please play it straight and give me a map of my exposure. Which results, cases, or arguments -- formal or intuitive -- would genuinely damage the view, sorted from fatal to merely awkward? I'm interested in several candidate defeaters in particular: cases where the correlation premise is doing all the work and evaporates on inspection (say, when the other players are merely similar in disposition rather than similar in reasoning); the charge that the view is really evidential decision theory in a trench coat and inherits that theory's embarrassing verdicts in medical-Newcomb-style cases; the worry that it recommends cooperating in setups where a small asymmetry in payoffs or population makes the recommendation clearly absurd; and the question of whether any experimental result about how actual humans play these games (including the published write-ups of the original lottery's outcome) should move me at all, given that the claim is normative rather than descriptive. For each defeater, tell me whether a committed superrationalist would concede it as a real cost or dismiss it as question-begging, and be specific about which dismissals are legitimate and which are defensive insulation.

One more thing, and here I want a committed answer rather than a menu: after laying out the defeaters, take a definite position on whether the superrational-cooperation view survives them, and if it survives only in a qualified form, state the qualification precisely -- which conditions on the game and on my evidence about the other players are load-bearing, and which parts of the folk-rationalist version of the view have to be abandoned. If you think the honest conclusion is that the position has been qualified into something with no real exposure left, say that directly rather than softening it.
