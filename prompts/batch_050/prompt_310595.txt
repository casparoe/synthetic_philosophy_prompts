So I'm presenting at my department's brown bag series in about two weeks (psych dept, but we get philosophers drifting in). My talk argues that the standard move from "moral judgments vary a lot across cultures" to "so moral objectivity is in trouble" mostly fails, because once you dig into the cross-cultural data the variation looks less like disagreement about values and more like (a) disagreement about empirical facts, like whether the dead can punish you or whether corporations are persons, and (b) different but internally coherent orderings of shared values when they conflict. My claim is that genuine bedrock value disagreement is much rarer than the WEIRD-psychology literature gets taken to imply, and that the remaining cases (I use honor-related violence and purity/disgust judgments as my examples) can be handled by a limited error theory that only covers a few domains rather than morality as a whole. So I end up defending what I'd call objectivity for most thick moral concepts, with a few carve-outs.

The audience: mostly cultural and developmental psychologists who know the Henrich stuff and the cross-cultural trolley/dilemma replications way better than I do, plus a couple of social psych people who are pretty commited to moral foundations-style pluralism, plus (this is the part that worries me) two metaethicists from philosophy who always show up and one of whom I think is a constructivist. No anthropologists, annoyingly, since they'd push hardest on my reading of the ethnography.
