Write a villanelle about Samuel Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture," the claim developed in his Tanner Lectures that our confidence in the value of what we do depends less on our own survival than on the belief that human life will continue after we are gone. Scheffler's central case is an infertility scenario: everyone now alive would live out a normal lifespan, but no further children would ever be born. His observation is that this prospect would drain meaning from conservation, scientific research, the writing of books, and even modest personal projects far more thoroughly than the prospect of one's own death does — and that we rarely notice this dependence because we take the collective afterlife entirely for granted.

The poem should honor the form strictly (nineteen lines, two repeating refrains, the closing quatrain), and the refrains should do philosophical work rather than decorate. One refrain should name, or enact, the thought that one's own death is bearable in imagination because others carry things forward; the other should name what collapses when the collective afterlife is withdrawn. Because a villanelle's refrains recur and recombine, let their recontextualization across stanzas track the structure of the argument: the first refrain should come to feel less like comfort and more like dependency as the poem proceeds, and in the final quatrain the two refrains should be brought together so that each comments on the other. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who has read Scheffler would recognize the conjecture itself rather than a generic memento mori, and it should not resolve into either consolation or despair, since Scheffler's point is diagnostic — about where our values actually get their support — rather than therapeutic.
