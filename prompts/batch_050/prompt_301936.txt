For a seminar on intergenerational ethics, I have been trying to determine whether my rejection of pure time preference survives contact with my actual case judgments. My verdicts are these: if a treatment would spare the same person an identical and equally certain year of severe pain either this year or thirty years from now, timing alone makes no moral difference; if one program would save an existing child now while another would save a different but otherwise similar child who will be born in thirty years, I favor saving the existing child, even assuming both outcomes are certain; if future beneficiaries will be substantially wealthier than present ones, transferring resources to them has less moral value because of diminishing marginal utility; if a future benefit is less likely to materialize because of political instability, technological change, or human extinction, it should be discounted for that risk rather than for its temporal location; present spending on education or public health may deserve priority because its benefits compound across later decades; irreversible losses, such as species extinction or permanent ecological damage, merit additional weight because they foreclose future options; I personally prefer receiving $100 today to receiving $101 next month, but I regard that as impatience and option value rather than evidence that my future welfare matters less; and although a government may use a market interest rate when comparing financial expenditures, I resist applying the same rate to lives or welfare.

Is there a precise principle that captures most of these verdicts while clearly distinguishing pure time preference from uncertainty, compounding effects, diminishing marginal utility, option value, irreversibility, opportunity cost, and any special claims possessed by presently existing people? Please state the best candidate principle, or a small set of competitors, identify which case motivates each clause, and be explicit about which of my verdicts must be abandoned if the principle is to remain simple. In particular, assess whether my preference for the existing child can be justified through a person-affecting distinction without covertly reintroducing discounting by date. Then apply the resulting view to a case I have not settled: a fixed budget can either remove a durable pollutant that would otherwise cause 10,000 severe illnesses beginning in 2150 or treat 8,000 equally severe illnesses now, with all effects certain and no expected difference in the patients’ wealth. Would the answer change if future technology were likely, but not certain, to neutralize the pollutant?
