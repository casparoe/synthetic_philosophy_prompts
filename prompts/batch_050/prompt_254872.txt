I am writing the final examination for my upper-level undergraduate course on freedom and moral responsibility, and I want one essay question that defeats the usual recitation. My students reliably come in with a rehearsed taxonomy: hard determinism, libertarianism, compatibilism, Frankfurt cases, consequence argument, done. What I actually want to test is whether they have absorbed the lesson I associate with Dennett's phrase about varieties of free will worth wanting: that the interesting philosophical work lies not in defending a position on whether determinism threatens freedom, but in decomposing the folk notion of free will into distinct capacities and asking, of each, whether it is worth wanting, whether determinism threatens it, and whether anything could ground our practices of blame and deliberation without it.

Accordingly, please design the question around the following stipulative apparatus, which I want students to engage rather than any material from the readings. Distinguish at least four candidate goods that get lumped under the heading of free will: (i) the capacity for one's actions to be governed by one's recognition of reasons; (ii) the capacity to have done otherwise in the exact circumstances, holding history and laws fixed; (iii) being the ultimate originator of one's character in a way that makes one's values self-authored rather than given; and (iv) the capacity that would make genuinely deliberating, rather than merely predicting oneself, intelligible from the first-person point of view. The exam question should present a case involving two agents, each of whom possesses some of these goods and lacks others, and require students to say which agent has the freedom worth wanting for a specified purpose (for instance, being an appropriate target of blame, or being someone whose deliberation is not self-undermining), and to explain why the goods come apart in the first place.

Please provide: the exam question itself, worded so it cannot be answered by naming positions; a model answer of roughly 650 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 built around the specific moves a first-class answer must make, with a note for each indicating the characteristic way a weaker student fumbles it. In particular, the scheme should reward students who notice that (ii) and (iii) are plausibly impossible in principle rather than merely threatened by determinism, and who treat the first-person deliberative question (iv) as distinct from the metaphysical ones, rather than assuming that settling determinism settles everything at once.
