I'm a high school senior preparing an entry for a philosophy essay competition, and my thesis is that the famous utility-monster thought experiment is a decisive objection to total utilitarianism: if a creature could convert any given resource into vastly more welfare than the rest of us combined, total utilitarianism seems committed to handing it everything, and that strikes me as a reductio. A classmate who disagrees claims I have only refuted a caricature, and before I submit I want to stress-test my own thesis. So treat my position as the object of scrutiny: what would count against the claim that the utility monster refutes total utilitarianism?

Please work through the following points of exposure, which worry me most:

1. Measurability. As I understand it, utility assignments within a single person are fixed only up to a choice of zero point and unit. But then the claim that the monster gets more welfare per slice of cake than I do seems to presuppose a common scale across persons. If there is no principled way to calibrate one person's utility scale against another's, is the monster even coherently describable? And if the utilitarian gets to choose the calibration, could they always redescribe the case so that no monster exists? Would that show my objection secretly depends on a theory of interpersonal comparison that total utilitarianism itself need not grant?

2. Two probe cases I constructed. First, the swarm: trillions of creatures, each only marginally more efficient at converting resources into welfare than a human. Total utilitarianism still delivers them everything, yet I feel far less indignant — which suggests my reaction tracks something other than efficiency. Second, the gray monster: a single being whose welfare is astronomically high but perfectly uniform and featureless — no projects, no variety, nothing I would recognize as a good life. The pull toward giving it everything feels much weaker here. If my intuition responds to the richness of the monster's experience rather than to distributive unfairness, is the objection really about distribution at all, or is it covertly a dispute about what welfare consists in?

3. Scope. Does the argument bite only against a naive, act-by-act maximizing version of utilitarianism? If rule-based or multi-level variants escape it, have I refuted utilitarianism or just a doctrine few philosophers defend?

4. Entanglement. I've noticed the monster's structure resembles worries in population ethics about adding enormous numbers of barely worthwhile lives. If the two cases stand or fall together, does my thesis inherit the fate of positions in population ethics — and what follows for its strength?

In your answer, rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward; indicate which of them a committed defender of my thesis would have to concede as genuine damage, rather than dismiss as the utilitarian dodging the case; say what my objection establishes that rival anti-utilitarian arguments — rights-based side constraints, or priority for the worse off — don't already establish more cheaply, that is, whether the monster is pulling any independent weight; and close with an honest verdict on whether bullet-biting has made total utilitarianism unfalsifiable in this neighborhood — and if it has, whether that counts for or against my thesis, since an objection the target can absorb without cost may be an objection that was never doing any work.
