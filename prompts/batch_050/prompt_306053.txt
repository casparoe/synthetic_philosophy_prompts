So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general magazine, and I've agreed -- possibly foolishly -- to do a 25-minute slot at an ideas festival on Hofstadter's Careenium thought experiment. In case it's not something you've run into: imagine a huge sealed tray full of tiny frictionless marbles (he calls them simmballs) rattling around and colliding with each other. Now zoom out far enough that you can't track individual marbles anymore, only big clusters of them. At that zoom level the clusters -- the simms, the "symbols" -- seem to bang into each other, merge, split apart, trigger each other, follow their own loose logic. The point of the setup is to model how something mind-like could sit on top of physics: the symbol level isn't an extra layer of stuff, its the same marbles, and yet the symbol-level story somehow does real explanatory work. The festival gave my slot the question "do symbols push the substrate around?" so I kind of have to deliver on that.

Audience: smart, curious non-specialists. No philosophy of mind background, most have never consciously thought about levels of description. I get a whiteboard, and I could bring a bowl of actual marbles if that helps.

Don't explain the thought experiment to me -- plan how I should explain it. Specifically:

- The order of ideas. What has to be in place before the Careenium even lands as interesting rather than as a weird story about marbles? I'm guessing the audience first needs to accept something about levels of description in general, but tell me exactly what they need to already believe before the two-levels-one-system move makes sense, and what depends on what.
- The carrying example. Should the Careenium itself be the spine of the whole talk, or do I need a warm-up case of levels-of-description first (temperature? traffic jams? money?) so the tray doesn't arrive out of nowhere? Whatever you pick as the spine, tell me what the example has to have and -- just as important -- what it has to lack.
- The misconception map. The ones I expect: people picturing the simms as little agents literally shoving marbles around (spooky downward causation); the opposite crowd saying "it's all just marbles, so the symbols are nothing" and thinking they've dissolved the question; and people taking the tray as a literal model of a brain and getting distracted by whether brains contain anything marble-like. For each one, tell me where in the sequence to head it off -- before they form it or right after.
- One question I can put to the audience near the end that shows whether the two-levels-one-system idea actually landed, rather than them just having enjoyed the imagery.
- What to explicitly leave out at this level and say I'm leaving out. I'm tempted to gesture at consciousness and free will in the last two minutes -- tell me if thats a mistake.

And the plan has to end by giving them a way to hear the title question as something with a sensible structure, not a straight yes or no. No reading lists, it's a talk not a syllabus.
