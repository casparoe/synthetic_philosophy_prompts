Okay, here’s the situation: I’m a novelist running a 75-minute workhsop for other fiction writers, most of whom love worldbuilding but tend to treat archaeology like a treasure hunt where every object has one obvious symbolic meaning. I want to teach them how archaeologists might cautiously infer beliefs and institutions from material remains, and how writers can use that same reasoning when designing a fictional society.

Don’t give the workshop lecture itself. Plan how I should teach it.

The central idea I want to get across is a three-step inferential ladder: objects can point to repeated practices; repeated practices can point to institutions; institutions can weakly and indirectly point to beliefs. The important twist is that every step is underdetermined, so a confident interpretation has to come from several independent kinds of remains converging, while awkward leftovers are explained rather than ignored. I’m not wedded to those labels if you think there’s a better way to structure it.

Plan the sequence in detail:

- What has to be introduced before the ladder even makes sense, and why the pieces have to come in that order.

Tell me exactly what remains the writers see, what the site must include and exclude, and why it should avoid writing, burials with obvious grave goods, and a single spectacular ritual object. Keep it practical and a bit skeptical, but don’t make archaeology sound like nothing can ever be known. Also, remember that these are storytellers: the aim is not to make them fake experts, but to give their invented worlds inferential depth.
