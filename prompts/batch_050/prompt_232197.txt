At our last faculty senate meeting we were arguing about the university's new AI chatbot, the one that answers student questions about registration, degree requirements, add/drop deadlines and so on. Turns out it had told a bunch of first-years they could drop a course after the deadline with no penalty, which is false, and some of them acted on it and got burned. I said the thing was deceiving our students and that we had a responsibility problem. One of the CS guys cut me off with "it can't lie, it can only hallucinate," and that definately ended the conversation way too early.

Write me up a disambiguation of that move. What are the distinct, precise claims packed into "it can't lie, it only hallucinates"? Lay them out as a menu, roughly from trivial to radical, with a verdict on each: platitude, substantive-but-defensible, false, or just confused. I don't want one reading explained at length, I want the whole menu. Make sure it covers at least these dimensions:

- What lying actually requires: belief that the claim is false? An intention to deceive? Assertion as opposed to mere token-production? And whether a system without beliefs or intentions is thereby automatically in the clear, or whether that just relocates the problem.
- The gap between lying and deceiving: plenty of mindless things deceive (a stick insect fools birds without a single belief), and you can mislead with strictly true statements. So "it can't lie" might be true and completely beside the point.
- The word "hallucinate" itself: does it smuggle in a perceptual norm, i.e. a system that's trying to register how things are and misfiring? Is that metaphor earned for a next-token predictor, or is it just flattering?
- A wrinkle I keep chewing on: if the bot had, by sheer luck, produced a true sentence through the exact same internal process, nobody would call it a hallucination. That suggests "hallucination" describes the falsity of the output rather than anything about the process — and then it's not obvious the term does any exculpatory work at all.
- Where responsibility goes if the artifact genuinely can't lie: does it vanish, or migrate to whoever built and deployed the thing, and does "it can't lie" actually help the administration's case here or hurt it?

Finish by telling me which reading (or readings) my colleague would actually need for his dismissal to succeed, and whether any of those survive scrutiny. I want to bring this to the next meeting, so keep it list-like and quotable rather than essayistic.
