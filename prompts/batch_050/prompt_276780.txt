Got into a massive argument with my friend Tom last night (he's doing a neuroscience MSc, I'm a philosophy grad student, so these pub nights always end up like this) and I'm still chewing on it. At some point, in response to me saying I don't understand why it feels like something to be me, and specifically why that feeling seems to belong to someone rather than just floating around, he just said "the self is an illusion" and acted like that settled it. When I pushed him on what that actually means, he mentioned Metzinger and Dennett and something about there being no "owner" of experiences, only experiences, and then later, confusingly, brought up Hofstadter and strange loops, which seemed like a completely different claim to me but he treated them as the same thing.

What I want is a proper breakdown I can take back to him. What are the distinct claims "the self is an illusion" could actually be making? I can see it might mean anything from something almost trivial (there's no little man sitting in the head watching the show) to something quite radical (there literally is no subject of experience and the impression that there is one is itself a kind of cognitive error, illusionism-style), with stuff in between like the self being a model or representation the brain constructs, the self being a story we narrate rather than a thing, or the self being a "strange loop" where the symbol that does the referring is itself constituted by the referring, whatever precisely that commits you to. Some of these strike me as true but boring, some as interesting but probably false, and I'm not even sure they're all compatible with each other.

So: lay out the main readings as a list, say for each whether it's trivial, substantive-and-defensible, or just false, and flag which readings Tom's friends (Metzinger, Dennett, the Hofstadter lot) are most plausibly committed to. Most importantly: which of these readings, if any, would actually justify using the slogan to shut down my original question about why experiences seem to have an owner? My suspicion is that the strong reading that would justify the dismissal is way less defensible than the weak reading that's obviously true, and he was sliding between them -- but I want to check whether that's fair before I accuse him of it next week.
