Exam question: "Is the brain a computer? In your answer, distinguish the claim that cognition is computation from the claim that the brain literally implements a Turing machine. Explain how multiple realizability bears on the first claim, discuss the objection that computational descriptions can be given of virtually anything (and so are vacuous), and assess whether Searle's Chinese Room argument shows that a digital computer could not have a mind."

Student's answer:

"Whether the brain is a computer depends on what we mean. The strong claim, that the brain implements a Turing machine, is almost certainly false in a literal sense, because Turing machines have infinite tape and the brain is finite; but the weaker claim that cognition is computation is the core of the computational theory of mind, and it is supported by multiple realizability. Multiple realizability is the observation that the same mental state, like pain, can be realized in human neurons, in octopus nervous systems, or in silicon chips, which shows that what matters is the functional organization rather than the physical substrate. This means that a mind is essentially the software that runs on the brain's hardware, and it follows that a sufficiently accurate simulation of a person's brain on a computer would itself be conscious, since the substrate is irrelevant.

Against the computational theory, there is a serious triviality objection. Putnam argued, using his famous example of the wall behind him, that any sufficiently complex object can be interpreted as running any program, so the claim that the brain computes is vacuous unless we can say what computation is in a non-interpretation-relative way. Searle offered a related result, proving that every ordinary rock implements every finite-state automaton, which is often taken to show that computational states are not intrinsic to physical systems.

Searle's Chinese Room argument goes further. A person who understands no Chinese sits in a room manipulating symbols according to an English rulebook, producing fluent Chinese responses. Searle concludes that running the right program is not sufficient for understanding, because the person has the syntax without the semantics. His conclusion is that no digital computer could ever be conscious, since consciousness requires more than symbol manipulation. This is a powerful argument, but the systems reply shows it fails: although the person in the room does not understand Chinese, the whole system of person, room, and rulebook does. Overall, I think the triviality objection is the deeper problem for computationalism, because unless we can define computation non-semantically, the claim that the brain is a computer says nothing at all."
