EXAM QUESTION (30 marks)

A long-run theory is sometimes defended against failed predictions with the claim that it is "right but early": the underlying mechanism is correct, and the predicted outcome will still arrive once temporary countervailing factors — technological progress, institutional adaptation, one-off resource windfalls — stop masking it. For example, an economist writing today might say: "Malthus's checks on population have merely been postponed by industrial agriculture. Given soil degradation and aquifer depletion, his prediction will be borne out by 2150."

(a) State Malthus's core argument about population and subsistence, and identify which of its components constitute the theory's indispensable core and which are revisable background assumptions (about growth rates, technology, timing). (10 marks)

(b) Using Popper's falsificationism, explain why indefinitely postponing a predicted crisis threatens a theory's scientific standing, and assess whether supplying a new, specific date for the crisis resolves the problem. (10 marks)

(c) Using Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, explain under what conditions revising a failed prediction's timeline counts as a progressive problemshift rather than a degenerating one, and apply this to the economist's claim above. What verdict should we reach about continuing to work within this programme? (10 marks)

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) Malthus argued that population, when unchecked, increases geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8...), while the food supply can at best increase arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4...). Because population will therefore always tend to outrun subsistence, it must be held in check: by preventive checks such as moral restraint and delayed marriage, which lower the birth rate, or by positive checks such as famine, disease, and war, which raise the death rate. The indispensable core of the theory is the structural tendency of population to press against the means of subsistence — the imbalance mechanism itself. The revisable assumptions are the particular rates of growth, the premise that agricultural productivity improves only slowly, and any proposed dates by which the checks will operate. These sit in what Lakatos would call the protective belt.

(b) For Popper, a scientific theory must take risks: it must forbid certain observable states of affairs, and the more it forbids, the more empirical content it has. Malthus's theory did precisely this — it issued risky, datable predictions about food supply failing to keep pace with population — and events in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries contradicted those predictions, as agricultural output grew faster than population. The theory was therefore falsified, and since a falsified theory is unscientific on Popper's view, Malthusianism fails the demarcation criterion. The "right but early" defence makes matters worse: by pushing the predicted crisis indefinitely into the future, the defender renders the theory compatible with any near-term evidence whatsoever, an immunizing stratagem that drains its empirical content. Supplying a new date, such as 2150, does not repair this, because when 2150 arrives without catastrophe the same manoeuvre can simply be repeated, and a theory whose defenders can always do this forbids nothing.

(c) For Lakatos, a research programme consists of a hard core shielded by a belt of auxiliary hypotheses, and scientists direct the modus tollens at the belt rather than the core. A revision of the belt is progressive only if it is content-increasing — that is, if the revised theory predicts some novel fact that the earlier version did not, and at least some of these novel predictions are corroborated. Any modification that merely accommodates an anomaly after the fact is ad hoc and must be rejected. Applying this to the economist: if she derives genuinely new, specific consequences from the revised Malthusian framework — for instance, quantified thresholds of soil loss and aquifer depletion that predict regional food-price spikes by given dates — and these are later confirmed, then the timeline revision is a progressive problemshift. But if the date has simply been moved in response to past failures, the shift is degenerating. As the case is described, the economist's claim appears degenerating, and since a degenerating programme is one it is irrational to keep working on, the correct verdict is that Malthusianism should be abandoned forthwith in favour of a rival programme.
