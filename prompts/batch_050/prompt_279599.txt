I'm a journalist who covers AI and cognitive science, and I'm writing a piece on the "stochastic parrots" fight -- do LLMs actually understand anything, or is it all just really fancy autocomplete? Both sides keep talking past each other in the coverage I read, so I want to imagine they sat down and did an adversarial collaboration.

Draft the joint statement they'd come out with. Side A: the skeptic who thinks LLMs are just doing next-token prediction over statistical patterns and so can't understand in any meaningful sense. Side B: the defender who thinks training on prediction, at sufficient scale, forces the model to build something that deserves to be called understanding.

I want:
1. The claims both can sign. Stuff like: both agree the models pass tests that would count as evidence of understanding if a human passed them; both agree prediction and understanding are at least conceptually separable; both agree we don't really know what internal representations the models build. Push for the strongest shared ground you can find, not just filler.
2. The residual disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would actually accept -- e.g. is the dispute about whether understanding requires grounding in embodied experience, or about whether the training objective matters or just the resulting capabilities?
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling it would move at least one side. I'd guess one is what we'd find if we could fully inspect the internals, another is how much weight to put on the models' weird failure modes (the stuff no understander would do).
4. For each crux, some observation or experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

End with a short note on how much of the fight is really about the word "understand" itself and how much is a real empirical disagreement -- I need to know wheter my piece is about a science question or a definition question.
