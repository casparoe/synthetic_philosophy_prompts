Some time ago I started turning over a thought experiment, and I have not been able to put it down. Imagine that shortly before Einstein's death, a team of researchers recorded everything about how his mind worked: every answer he would give to any question, every hesitation, every joke, every shift in mood, all of it written down as an enormous system of rules filling a whole library of books. The books are cross-referenced so thoroughly that a patient person with no understanding of physics at all could hold a convincing conversation with Einstein. You write a question on a card, follow the lookup procedure through the volumes, and after enough page-flipping you arrive at the entry telling you what Einstein would say next, including his confusion at a vague question, his wit, and his irritation at a stupid one. A single exchange might take weeks of work, but the output, in principle, is indistinguishable from corresponding with the man himself.

I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have thought about this long enough to arrive at a position, and I want it stress-tested rather than confirmed. My position is that nothing in that room is conscious in any sense that matters. The books are just ink, the librarian is just following instructions he does not understand, and there is nobody home in the system as a whole: no inner life, nothing it is like to be that conversation, even though from the outside it perfectly mimics a person. I am fairly convinced that consciousness requires the right kind of active process happening at the right scale and speed, not a static record of dispositions that a clerk could grind through by hand.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed this position actually is. What would count against it? I mean that seriously: which considerations, thought experiments, or possible discoveries would be genuinely fatal to my view, and which would merely be awkward? If a defender of the opposite view argued that slowing a mind down to one step per week does not extinguish it, or that my insistence on the right kind of process is just disguised prejudice in favor of neurons, how strong are those challenges really? I would also like to know what someone who shares my position would themselves concede as damaging, as opposed to what only its critics would press. Finally, I want your honest judgment about whether my view risks anything at all, or whether I have insulated it with so many qualifications about the right kind of process that no conceivable case could touch it, and whether that would be a strength or a warning sign.

Please write your answer in American English, as continuous prose only, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
