Here's an argument I worked up after reading about Parfit's hitchhiker case, and it seems to prove too much. (1) A rational agent should adopt whichever stable dispositions will produce the best outcomes for her, given how others actually respond to her. (2) Because other people can often detect our dispositions — we are at least partly transparent — someone disposed to pay the driver after being rescued, to keep promises when keeping them costs her, and to refuse to be blackmailed, will be rescued, trusted, and left alone, while a calculating person will not. (3) Therefore rationality requires adopting dispositions that will sometimes lead one to act, at the moment of action, against what rationality recommends at that moment. (4) What applies to rescue, promises, and threats applies generally: bargaining, deterrence, joint projects, and friendship all reward people who are disposed not to deliberate case by case. (5) Since such interactions constitute most of social life, rationality requires being disposed to act irrationally across most of social life. (6) Therefore an ideally rational agent is one who has largely trained herself out of responding to reasons, and the cultivation of unreflective loyalty, spite, and stubbornness is a rational duty on par with learning logic. (7) But that is absurd. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) than accept (6), but I want to know exactly what rejecting the offending step costs me.
