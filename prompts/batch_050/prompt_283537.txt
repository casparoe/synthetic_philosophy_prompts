I'm a second-year philosophy student taking an epistemology seminar that has just spent three weeks on testimony. We started with the Hume-versus-Reid contrast (roughly: the hearer needs independent, non-testimonial grounds to trust testimony, versus testimony being a basic source of knowledge we're entitled to accept by default), and we've since muddied things with local versus global versions of reductionism, the problem of young children who couldn't possibly have established anyone's track record, and cases like a teacher who knowingly asserts things she doesn't believe. Here is my frustration: whenever our seminar splits into the two camps, they talk past each other for forty minutes and then, on their way out, seem to concede almost everything the other side said — only to re-enter next week as sworn enemies again. I suspect the joint area of agreement is large, and that the genuine residue is maybe two or three precise disagreements plus some verbal clutter, but I've never seen anyone write down what that would look like.

So please draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between a Humean reductionist and a Reidian anti-reductionist on testimony would publish after a year of working together. Use this structure: (I) Agreed Claims — a numbered list of claims both parties would actually sign; each claim must exclude some position a philosopher might plausibly hold, so no platitudes like 'testimony sometimes gives knowledge'; (II) Disagreements Table — rows with three columns: the issue, the reductionist's position phrased as the reductionist would phrase it, the anti-reductionist's position phrased as they would phrase it, plus a fourth column judging whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal, with a brief justification; (III) Cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to revise, with each side recording in advance which outcome would make them budge; (IV) Shared Evidence — cases or arguments both parties accept as bearing on the cruxes, with each side's prediction or verdict recorded in advance rather than after the fact. To keep this concrete, both parties must address two scenarios in section IV: first, a visitor to an island where the locals happen to be scrupulously truthful but the visitor has no way of discovering this and no track record to consult; second, a young child learning everyday facts from adults before she could possibly have assembled independent evidence of their reliability. For each scenario, each party must state whether the hearer acquires testimonial knowledge and whether she is epistemically blameless in believing, and must flag whether that verdict is a prediction of their theory or a bullet they are choosing to bite.

One more constraint, since this is what I found missing from our seminar sessions: every formulation in the statement must be something the opposing party would accept as a fair statement of that party's own view — no strawmen dressed in the other side's vocabulary — and wherever the collaborators conclude that part of the traditional debate was really about words (e.g., what 'default entitlement' or 'independent grounds' were ever supposed to mean), they must say so explicitly and quarantine it from the genuine disagreements. End the statement with a single line beginning 'Residual crux:' that names, in one sentence, the disagreement both parties would still walk out of the room fighting over, and whether it is substantive or verbal.
