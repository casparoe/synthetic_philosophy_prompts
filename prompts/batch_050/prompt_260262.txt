I am a PhD student in philosophy, TAing an upper-level undergraduate course on decision theory (about 24 students, one 75-minute session, no prior exposure to the Allais paradox — they will have covered expected utility and the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, including independence, the previous week). I want a classroom game that makes students *feel* the cost of Allais-type preferences before the paradox is ever named, rather than just eliciting the familiar two choice-pairs on a worksheet.

Here is the specific mechanism I want the activity built around, so please design to it. The common consequence effect becomes dynamically inconsistent when the Allais lotteries are re-expressed as compound lotteries: a choice between an 11% chance of a prize and a 10% chance of a larger prize can be staged so that, conditional on surviving an 89% first stage, the player faces a sure prize versus a 10/11 gamble — and many who ex ante chose the riskier option will, at that second stage, take the sure thing. A banker who knows this can extract value: sell the exchange from the safer to the riskier lottery for a small fee, then watch the player flip back at the second stage, having paid a fee to end up exactly where the safe option would have put them. I want students to fall into this pump themselves, with real (small) stakes — e.g., points convertible into a prize draw or chocolate.
