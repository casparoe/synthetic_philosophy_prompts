I work as a backend engineer at a mid-sized company that trains language models, and our leadership recently announced a single "alignment initiative" that would consolidate a handful of scattered projects -- reward model work, interpretability, red-teaming for deceptive behavior, and a small group thinking about what our models should be optimized for in the first place. At lunch last week, two senior colleagues got into a genuinely interesting argument about whether this consolidation makes conceptual sense, and neither convinced the other. Marcus's position was that alignment is one problem: every failure mode people worry about, from reward hacking to goal misgeneralization to a model strategically misleading its evaluators, is ultimately the same failure -- the system optimized something other than what its designers actually wanted -- and carving it into subproblems is taxonomy-mongering that fragments effort and lets everyone avoid the hard core. Elena pushed back that the reduction hides what matters: the fixes don't transfer across these cases. Better reward design does nothing to help you detect whether a model is reporting its reasoning honestly; interpretability tooling does nothing to settle what the model ought to want; and a team with no shared method and no shared success criterion isn't one initiative, it's a floor plan. If alignment were really one problem, she asked, why do proposed solutions cluster so cleanly by subproblem rather than addressing the supposed common core?

Marcus replied that this confuses a problem with its methods. Curing cancer is operationally dozens of problems -- different organs, different mechanisms, different treatments -- but nobody says there's no such thing as "the cancer problem," because a problem is individuated by its success condition, not by the diversity of approaches it takes. And alignment, he claimed, has a crisp success condition: the system reliably does what we want even in situations where it could get away with doing something else. Elena's counter was that this success condition only looks crisp because it equivocates -- "what we want" slides between what we said, what we intended, and what we would have wanted given full information -- and each disambiguation generates genuinely different failure modes that require different kinds of evidence to detect. Marcus said that was just an argument that the one problem is hard to state precisely, and that difficulty of statement isn't multiplicity of problems. Elena said that if stating the problem differently changes what counts as solving it, then calling it one problem is bookkeeping, not insight.

I'd like you to adjudicate this properly, move by move: for each of the main turns in the exchange, say who is right about that specific point and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner. I'm especially interested in whether there's a principled way to individuate problems at all -- whether "one problem versus several" is a substantive question about the structure of alignment or just a choice of descriptive granularity, and whether both of them are missing something about how the answer should affect practical decisions like whether to run these efforts as one team or many. Feel free to take a side overall, but only after going through the individual moves.
