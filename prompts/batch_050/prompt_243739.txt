I'm a high school student writing a paper on Bayesian confirmation theory, and I'm stuck on the problem of old evidence. My understanding of the puzzle is this: if a piece of evidence E is already known with certainty, then P(E) = 1, and Bayesian conditioning gives P(H|E) = P(H), so already-known evidence can seemingly never raise a hypothesis's probability. Yet this seems to happen all the time — imagine a new theory of tumor growth that turns out to predict an odd pattern which had been sitting unnoticed in decades of hospital records. One fix I've come across says to evaluate confirmation using the credences I *would* have had before learning E. But it seems to me this fix has a hole: often what does the confirming isn't E itself but the newly discovered fact that the theory entails or explains E, and I usually only grasp that connection *after* already knowing E. So if I rewind my credences to before I knew E, must I also rewind my understanding of the connection — and if I instead keep the connection while deleting E, haven't I rigged the calculation in the theory's favor? Please explain whether this objection is fair, and what a Bayesian should say is really doing the confirming when an old fact supports a new theory.
