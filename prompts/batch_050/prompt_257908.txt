I'm a novelist working on a near-future war book and I keep getting stuck on when a human being "in the loop" of a killing machine actually means anything. Here are my gut verdicts, and I'd love to know if there's a real principle under them or if I'm just superstitious about robots:

1. A loitering drone picks its own targets in a valley; some operator 8000 miles away gets an alert and 20 seconds to tap "abort" on a screen. This is NOT meaningful control. It's a rubber stamp with extra steps, the operator has no context and no time.

2. A general writes rules of engagement, and pilots follow them for months without anyone signing off on individual strikes. This feels like genuine control, even though the general is never near a trigger.

3. A sniper on standing orders -- anyone crossing the bridge at night armed -- fires without radioing in. Feels fine. But structurally this is exactly case 1 with a human body instead of a drone, and that bothers me alot.

4. A ship's air-defense gun that shoots down incoming missiles way faster than any human could possibly decide. Here the absence of human control feels not just acceptable but good, even though a malfunction kills people.

5. A dumb landmine buried twenty years ago. More predictable than the drone in case 1, no AI anywhere, and yet it feels worse than case 1, not better.

6. A fully autonomous drone kills whoever it kills, and afterwards humans review the footage and can ground the fleet. Post-hoc accountability feels like nearly nothing -- but is it zero, or is it a weak version of the same thing the general has in case 2?

So: what principle, if there is one, sorts these the way my gut does? I especially need it to explain why 3 passes and 1 fails when they look identical on paper, and why removing the human helps in 4 but damns the whole setup in 6. If no single principle saves all six verdicts, tell me which one I should surrender -- I'm happy to lose a verdict, I just want to know which one and why.
