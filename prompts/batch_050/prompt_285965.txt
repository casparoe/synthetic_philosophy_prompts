I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting leans heavily on the moral weight of non-human minds, so I have been taking my own intuitions about brain size more seriously than usual. What I would like is the principle behind my verdicts, if there is one. Here they are, stated as honestly as I can manage.

First, the cases where I feel confident. The death of a whale seems to me a far greater loss, morally, than the death of a mouse, and the sheer size and complexity of the whale's brain is part of why. A crow's death seems to me to matter more than a cow's, even though the cow's brain is considerably larger; what I know about corvid cleverness outweighs the raw numbers for me. An octopus matters a great deal to me as well, and I am aware that most of its neurons sit in its arms rather than its head, so I suspect any crude measure of brain size would undercount whatever it has. A human infant matters more to me than a pig, even though I gather their cognitive capacities are broadly comparable, and I concede this may be sentiment rather than judgement.

Then the harder ones. If some future geneticist doubled the size of a chicken's brain without changing its behaviour or apparent experience in any way, I do not think the chicken would matter any more than it did before, and yet the whale and the mouse seem to differ in moral weight because of brain size. And when I imagine a large-brained creature -- say something invented, a fictional beast with an enormous cortex but no behavioural sign of a rich inner life -- it matters little to me, which sits awkwardly next to my verdict on the octopus, whose inner life is nearly as opaque to me and whose relevant tissue is not even in a brain.

So: is there a single principle, stated precisely, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? If you find you need a small family of principles rather than one -- say, one for what makes a being matter at all and another for how much it matters -- that is a perfectly respectable answer, but then tell me which verdict motivates which clause. Say what the resulting account predicts for cases I have not raised: a parrot against a salmon, a dog bred to have an unusually small brain, a person in a persistent vegetative state against a person with locked-in syndrome. And be candid about which of my verdicts the best account cannot accommodate and must therefore ask me to abandon, because I would rather lose an intuition than keep an incoherent set.

Two requests about the manner of the answer. Please separate clearly what is genuinely settled among people who work on these questions -- the relevant facts about neuron counts, neuron density, and the distribution of nervous tissue -- from what is still contested, such as whether any such measure tracks moral status at all, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus. And lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Please answer in British English.
