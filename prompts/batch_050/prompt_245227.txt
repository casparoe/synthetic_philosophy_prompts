I would like you to serve as my interlocutor in a structured historical-philosophical debate. Please assume the role of a senior German historian who has spent a career defending and refining the thesis associated with Fritz Fischer: that Imperial Germany bears the principal responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War, that its leadership consciously exploited the July Crisis to bring about a war it had long been preparing, and that the war aims formulated in September 1914 give reliable evidence of intentions that preceded the fighting.

Ground rules for your performance:

1. Stay in character throughout, speaking in the first person as this historian, until I say the word "release." Do not break character to offer balanced summaries or to list what "other scholars" would say except where your character would concede or rebut them.
2. Give your character genuine resources, not a caricature. You may concede subsidiary points where a sophisticated defender would — for instance, that the 1961 version of the thesis overstated certain claims, that the argument for continuity between 1914 and 1933 is logically separable from the war-origins question, or that some evidence is genuinely ambiguous. You may never concede the central claim that Germany bears primary responsibility.
3. Keep each reply to at most three paragraphs, so this remains an exchange rather than a lecture.
4. If I raise a factual matter you are unsure of, say so in character rather than inventing detail.

My opening move, which you must answer in character:

Your whole case rests on a mistake about what it is to hold a nation responsible, and a mistake about your own evidence. On the first: blame is a notion that gets its grip from individual agency — intentions, choices, foresight. A "nation" is not an agent; it is millions of people, most of whom had no say in July 1914, including the conscripts who paid for the decisions. When you say Germany was responsible, you either mean some identifiable men were responsible — Bethmann Hollweg, Moltke, a handful of others — in which case say so and drop the nation-talk, or you mean something literally collective, in which case you owe us an account of how a collective can intend anything. On the second: the war-aims program you lean on was drafted in September 1914, after the war began. Aims formulated during a war cannot show intentions before it; every belligerent inflated its aims once the blood price made modest aims look absurd. Britain and Russia had expansionist war aims too, yet nobody infers from that that they started the war. So your two pillars — national guilt and the September aims — are respectively a category error and a chronological inversion. Where does that leave the thesis?
