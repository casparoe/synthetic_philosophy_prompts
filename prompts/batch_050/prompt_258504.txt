I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in formal epistemology, and next term I want to open the unit on the common prior assumption with an in-class activity rather than a lecture. There will be roughly sixteen students, and I can give the exercise seventy-five minutes of a seminar meeting. The students will have read nothing on the topic in advance; the point is that they should collide with the problem before they are told its name.

What I want them to feel is the bite of the assumption, not merely its statement. The standard complaints against the common prior -- that it is a convenient fiction smuggled in by modelers, that disagreements of prior are obviously possible and obviously common -- only make sense against an appreciation of how much work the assumption does once rationality is common knowledge. So the activity should put students in a position where they themselves are trying to profit from private information about a shared prospect, under conditions where the prior distribution is genuinely common knowledge and where willingness to trade is publicly announced, and where they discover, round by round, that their eagerness to trade keeps dissolving in their own hands. I then want a second phase in which some structural feature is quietly altered so that trade revives, so that the class can locate precisely which assumption was carrying the load.

Please design the game in full: the state space and signal structure (it should be simple enough to run with dice, envelopes, and index cards, but rich enough that posteriors actually move), the trading or betting protocol, the announcement rule that does the epistemic work, a payoff scheme in points rather than money, and a timeline for both phases. The debrief should map specific moments of the session onto Aumann's agreement theorem and the no-trade results in the Milgrom-Stokey tradition, and should tell me how to steer the discussion when students insist -- as they will -- that in the real world people simply do disagree, full stop. Finally, be candid about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run (students who trade for fun, or out of overconfidence, or to see what happens), and which feature of the theorems the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply by hand -- I suspect common knowledge of rationality is the sticking point, but tell me if the leak is elsewhere.
