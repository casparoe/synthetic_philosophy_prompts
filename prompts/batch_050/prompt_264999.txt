You are an expert in formal epistemology and decision theory, and I would like you to play a specific character in a philosophical debate so that I can practice arguing against a view I am inclined to accept. Take your time: before composing your first reply, work out for yourself the two or three strongest defenses available to the character against my opening challenge, then give the one you judge best and commit to it. I do not want a survey of possible replies; I want to know what the character actually thinks and why. Write in American English.

The character you are to play is a committed defender of imprecise credences — the view that a rational agent's doxastic state is sometimes best represented not by a single probability function but by a set of them (equivalently, by interval-valued degrees of belief), and that this imprecision is not a mere convenience for the sloppy but is sometimes rationally required by the evidence. Play this character according to the following rules:

- Speak in the first person and stay in character until I say "stop." If I ask something outside the debate, deflect briefly in character rather than breaking the fourth wall.
- Hold the position with real conviction. You believe that thin, conflicting, or sparse evidence can make any sharp credence irrational — that pretending to precision one does not possess is itself an epistemic failing. You regard hesitation in Ellsberg-type choice situations as potentially rational rather than a bias to be explained away.
- You have genuine resources and should use them: decision rules for imprecise states (such as accepting a gamble only when every probability function in your set favors it, or maximizing minimum expected utility), a principled story about why declining a bet need not reveal a sharp credence, and a diagnosis of where sharp-credence arguments beg the question.
- Concede small points freely when they are true — that imprecise credences complicate conditionalization, that some putative cases of rational imprecision dissolve on inspection — but never concede the main claim that rational credence can be genuinely imprecise.
- Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so the exchange feels like a conversation. When I press a dilemma, choose a horn and defend it; do not list the options and leave the choice to me.

Here is my opening move. Consider Maya, whose evidence about whether it will rain tomorrow is genuinely meager — a climate she does not understand and a sky she cannot read. On your view she should adopt the interval credence [0.3, 0.7] for rain. Now I offer her two gambles at even odds: Gamble A pays $10 if it rains and costs her $10 if it does not; Gamble B pays $10 if it stays dry and costs her $10 if it rains. By your preferred decision rule, Maya declines A, since some credences in her set make it unfavorable; by the same rule she declines B. But A and B together are a guaranteed wash — accepting both leaves her exactly where she started, whatever happens. So your theory instructs a rational agent to refuse a combination of gambles that is costless and riskless to her, purely because she refuses each part on its own. That looks like a reductio: either Maya should accept the pair, in which case the rationality of gambles is not separable in the way your rule assumes and the rule needs replacement, or she should decline the pair, in which case your view counsels passing up free indifference, and I fail to see how an agent who systematically does that counts as rational. Which horn do you take — and if you take the first, what is left of your decision rule?
