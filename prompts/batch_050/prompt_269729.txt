My reading group has spent the last few sessions on Hume's essay on miracles from the Enquiry, and we keep circling the question of whether his argument is merely cautious or actually self-defeating. I want to settle it with a test case, so here is the construction job. Build a thought experiment in which a scrupulously Humean reasoner ends up rationally required to believe that a genuine violation of a law of nature has occurred, on pain of violating Hume's own maxim about apportioning belief to the strength of the evidence — that is, a case where doubting the miracle is, by Hume's lights, the less reasonable position. Constraints: the scenario must be realistically describable, set in the present day, with no stipulated gods, angels, or demons, no time machines or other science-fiction devices, and the law of nature at issue should be the sort of thing backed by the uniform experience Hume has in mind — something like conservation of mass or the impossibility of an unaided human rising into the air and staying there — rather than an abstruse result from particle physics that almost nobody has experiential access to. Crucially, the case must not cheat by making the miracle merely an astronomically improbable event, like a coin landing heads a thousand times in a row; it must be an event of a kind that experience has uniformly contradicted, and the evidence for it must come through testimony of the sort Hume's maxim actually engages with, not through the reasoner's own direct observation of the event.

Once you have the scenario, which should itself fit in a single paragraph, show that it meets the specification: state Hume's maxim as you understand it, work through in prose how the balance comes out so that the falsehood of the testimony would be a greater departure from uniform experience than the reported event itself, and identify which structural feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting — the number and independence of the witnesses, their established track records, their lack of anything to gain, or the existence of independent physical records — and what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed. Then give the strongest attack a defender of Hume who wants to resist the conclusion could mount, and say whether that attack amounts to abandoning the maxim, restricting its scope, or simply applying it more carefully. I would like the whole response written as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to the reading group: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
