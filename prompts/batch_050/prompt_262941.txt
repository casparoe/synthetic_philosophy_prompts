In Book I of the Politics, Aristotle argues that some people are slaves by nature, and his central argumentative device is an analogy: the master stands to the natural slave as the soul stands to the body, or as the deliberative part of the soul stands to the appetitive part. On this picture, rule by the superior element is natural, mutually beneficial, and even protective — the body fares well under the soul's direction, and the natural slave, whom Aristotle describes as able to grasp another's reasoning while lacking the capacity to deliberate fully for himself, fares well under a master's. I teach an undergraduate course in ancient political philosophy, and I have noticed that this analogy does most of the persuasive work for the students who are otherwise hostile to Aristotle's argument; I would like a rigorous audit of it before next term.

Please do the following. First, lay out the intended mapping explicitly: which features of the soul-body relation are meant to correspond to which features of the master-slave relation, including Aristotle's claim that natural rule is exercised for the sake of both parties. Second, identify what the analogy quietly imports from its source that the target plausibly lacks — for instance, that a body has no independent standpoint or interests apart from the person whose body it is, that the parts of a person cannot separate, be emancipated, or have the relation reversed, and that there is no clear analogue of a body being misgoverned for the soul's exclusive gain. Third, stress-test the analogy against Aristotle's own finer-grained claims: he holds that the deliberative faculty is present in women but without authority over emotion, present in children but undeveloped, and absent in natural slaves, and elsewhere he observes that appetite can listen to reason yet also resist it. Do these gradations and the possibility of internal resistance strengthen or undermine the binary master-slave picture? Fourth, distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion the analogy is made to carry from those that are merely incidental. Finally, say what, if anything, survives the audit: could the argument at most license a limited paternalism rather than slavery, and is there a better analogy — available either to Aristotle or to a modern paternalist — that would carry the intended conclusion without inheriting these objections?
