So last weekend I had this argument with my friend Dana that went til like 1am and it's still bugging me. She did her PhD on early modern philosphy and she's a full-on "New Hume" person: she insists Hume genuinely believed there are real causal powers out there in objects, thick necessary connections holding nature together, and that we just can't ever know them. I said that's a wild reading — Hume is the guy who boiled causation down to constant conjunction plus a felt determination in our heads, and all his talk of "secret powers" is either ironic or him reporting what other people think. We went in circles for hours and neither of us moved an inch, and afterwards I realized the actual literature has been having basically this same fight since at least the 1980s (Strawson and co. vs. the traditional regularity reading) without moving either.

The passage we kept circling back to is from Hume's *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section VII, Part I:

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

Dana's whole case hangs on the word "discover": she says it's a purely epistemic claim — we never *detect* the power, which leaves it wide open that the power is there anyway. I say Hume's entire method in that section (find the impression or admit the idea is empty) means he's denying we even have a coherent idea of such a power, so the "it's there but unknowable" position is one he had no room to hold, let alone hold himself.
