In the introduction to The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy (1860), Jacob Burckhardt — the historian who more than anyone else established the Renaissance as a distinct period — pauses to warn his reader: "It is the most serious difficulty of the history of civilization that a great intellectual process must be broken up into single, and often into what seem arbitrary categories in order to be in any way intelligible."

Write an essay that takes this concession seriously and asks whether it undermines the project of the book it introduces. Are the "Middle Ages" and the "Renaissance" real joints in history — genuine discontinuities in how people lived, thought, and organized their world — or retrospective constructions that mainly served the self-image of those who coined them: humanists declaring their own age a rebirth against a darkness they largely invented, and nineteenth-century historians who needed a clean origin story for modernity? In developing your answer, weigh the strongest considerations on both sides: for the constructionist, the participants' obvious interest in proclaiming a rebirth, the deep continuities in institutions, religion, and learning (including earlier medieval flowerings such as the twelfth-century revival), and the dependence of any period boundary on what one chooses to measure; for the realist, the possibility that contemporaries across many independent domains noticed the same shift, and that some changes — in political self-understanding, in the uses of antiquity, in the scale and sources of patronage — may be too clustered to be a labeling artifact. Say what a period concept would have to be true of in order to count as real rather than merely convenient, apply that test to this case, and reach a defended verdict: whether Burckhardt's categories carve history at its joints, whether they are useful fictions, or whether the truth lies in some more precise position you should state.
