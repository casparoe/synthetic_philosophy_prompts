I'm a PhD student in criminal law theory and I'm stuck one step before the actual research question. I keep reading arguments for and against strict liability offences, and everyone seems to be arguing past each other because nobody states what a theory in this area would even need to do to count as adequate. So before I read any more defences or critiques, I want a specification, not another verdict.

Please write the desiderata for a theory of when (if ever) it is defensible to punish someone without proof of fault as to some element of the offence. By desiderata I mean: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must respect, the objections it must dissolve rather than dodge, each with the consideration behind it. Some things I'd want the list to grapple with, though feel free to restructure or add to it:

- It has to take a stand on the genuinely faultless defendant. My test case: a shopkeeper sells sealed tins from a reputable wholesaler, no way to inspect them without destroying the stock, and one turns out to be contaminated. She did everything reasonable. If your theory permits punishing her, it has to own that and say what's being achieved; if it forbids it, it has to say what the state should do instead about the fact that demanding proof of fault here makes the whole regulatory scheme unenforceable.
- It has to sort out whether a strict liability offence with a due diligence defence is actually strict liability, or just negligence liability with the burden flipped and a worse label. I suspect half the literature collapses because people treat these as different when they're the same, or vice versa.
- It must respect the elements/defences distinction, since most "punishment without fault" cases involve fault as to other elements, and also deal with mistake of fact vs mistake of law, which gets muddled constantly here.
- It has to engage with the censure point: if convicting someone expresses blame, and blame requires fault, then either strict liability conviction is a lie or the theory has to explain what a conviction means in these cases and whether a low-stakes ticket is a different moral object from a criminal record.
- It should distinguish the stigma-and-imprisonment cases from the regulatory-fine cases, because "strict liability" covers both and they may need different answers.

The part I most want: flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied (I have a hunch the enforcement desideratum and the never-blame-the-blameless desideratum are in real tension, but maybe I'm wrong), and mark which desiderata you'd treat as non-negotiable vs which are really just prejudices of the retributivist or consequentialist camps.

Please be committed about it -- don't just lay out the space and leave me to pick. Tell me which desiderata you'd actually sacrifice first and why. And British English please, I'm at a UK university and the American spellings in my notes are already annoying my supervisor.
