I teach a master's-level seminar in formal epistemology, and next week I am giving the session on the maximum entropy principle as a candidate answer to the problem of the prior. The students are comfortable with basic probability and information theory (they have seen Shannon entropy as a measure of channel capacity) but have not thought about the foundations of probabilistic inference. I do not want you to explain the principle to me; I want a plan for how I should teach it in ninety minutes.

Specifically, please lay out the following:

1. The order in which the pieces have to be introduced, with a reason for each dependency. My instinct is that the principle of indifference and its failure modes (Bertrand-style paradoxes, where different parametrizations of the same problem yield different 'uniform' assignments) have to come before anything else, so that the students feel the need for a principled rule rather than hearing MaxEnt as an arbitrary technical proposal. Tell me whether that instinct is right and what else must be in place before the entropy-maximization rule can even be stated -- for instance, the distinction between constraints that genuinely come from one's evidence and constraints one merely assumes, and the idea of choosing a distribution rather than an event.

2. The one carrying example for the session. I am considering a die known to land on an average above 3.5, where uniform assignment is demonstrably wrong but infinitely many skewed distributions fit the constraint, so the students can watch MaxEnt pick one and can be asked what justifies the pick. If you think a different example carries the idea better, say what it must have and must lack (e.g., it must have an underdetermined solution set; it must not smuggle in physical randomness that makes one distribution 'true').

3. The misconceptions to expect and the exact point in the sequence where I should defuse each. I anticipate at least: (a) that MaxEnt outputs the objectively true probabilities rather than the least committal assignment given one's constraints; (b) that it reduces to 'always assign uniform probabilities,' since they have only ever seen entropy maximized by the uniform distribution; (c) that the entropy being maximized is the thermodynamic quantity they may know from physics; (d) that the result is guaranteed to make good predictions, rather than being the unique distribution that adds no information beyond the constraints. For each, tell me where in the plan it will naturally arise and what move heads it off without derailing the session.

4. One check for understanding I can run in the final ten minutes: ideally a small new problem where a student who merely memorized the recipe will produce the uniform distribution while a student who understood the justification will instead notice that the constraint changes the answer, or where the right response is to refuse to maximize entropy at all because the 'constraint' was not actually given by any evidence.

5. What I should openly refuse to cover at this level -- I am thinking of the continuous case and its need for a reference measure, and of the justification of the entropy functional itself (Shannon's desiderata versus later derivations) -- and a one-sentence honest version of what I should say when students push on each of those.
