I am a novelist working on a book set in a fictional Bronze Age society, and in the course of my research I have been reading a good deal of archaeology, where I keep running into an inferential situation I have no word for. The situation is this: two genuinely different pasts leave behind exactly the same physical traces, so that the surviving evidence cannot, even in principle, choose between them. A few examples of what I mean. A settlement contains large communal grain stores; one excavator reads them as the apparatus of a redistributive temple economy, another as royal taxation in kind, another as a voluntary insurance arrangement among households, and as far as I can tell, nothing in the rooms, the jars, or the grain itself could settle it, because all three institutions would produce precisely this layout. Or again: a pit full of deliberately broken figurines might be a votive deposit, an episode of iconoclasm, or simply a trash midden whose contents were broken by accident, and the excavators argue from the "carefulness" of the breakage, which seems to me to admit all three readings. It struck me that for my fictional society I could decide that its grain stores were run by priests, and no future archaeologist of my invented world could ever catch me out.

My question is whether this specific situation has an established name in archaeology or in the philosophy of science, and I want to be careful about what I am asking, because it is not quite any of the things I already know words for. It is not merely that evidence has been lost -- plenty was lost in these cases, but the puzzle would remain even with perfect preservation, since the problem is that the two histories converge on the same material outcome. It also seems different from the philosopher's general point that theories are underdetermined by data, because that is usually stated as a global, almost logical worry, whereas what I am noticing is a concrete, recurring feature of particular inferences from objects to institutions and beliefs, sometimes avoidable with a lucky find (a deciphered archive, say) and sometimes not. So: is there a term of art for this convergence of distinct pasts on an identical record, or for the stronger claim that inference from material remains to institutions is systematically vulnerable to it? If there is, how does it relate to, and differ from, underdetermination and from arguments about preservation bias? And if there is no established term, please coin one, define it precisely, and give its boundary cases -- what would count as an instance, what would only look like one, and whether a novelist should take comfort or despair from it. Please write your answer in American English.
