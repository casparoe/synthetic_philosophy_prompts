So I got into the algorithmic fairness literature last year and there's something about it that keeps bugging me. Quick sketch of how I understand the history: around 2016 ProPublica ran the COMPAS story showing the recidivism scores had much higher false positive rates for Black defendants than white defendants -- people flagged high-risk who didn't go on to reoffend, basically. The company that built the tool replied that the scores were calibrated: of everyone given a score of 7 out of 10, about 70% reoffended, and that held within each racial group. Then the theory people came in and proved, as actual theorems, that when the underlying rates of reoffense differ between groups you can't have calibration and equal error rates at the same time, except in degenerate cases. Pick two, lose the third, roughly.

Fine. So you'd think the field would take this on the chin, have a big fight about which leg to saw off, and move on. But it didn't, or at least it doesn't feel like it did. People are still publishing "X is the correct fairness criterion" papers almost a decade later, practitioners still argue in review threads about which constraint to impose on their model, and in my lab meetings the same three positions get re-litigated every semester like nothing was ever settled. Everyone in these arguments knows the impossibility results. Nobody disputes the proofs. So what is the disagreement actually about?

My working theory, which I only half believe: the theorems didn't create the disagreement, they just forced it into the open, because "fairness" was never one thing. One camp means treat-similar-individuals-similarly, another camp means don't-systematically-disadvantage-a-group, a third is really asking what the scores are *for* (describing risk vs allocating punishment), and each camp's favorite metric just encodes its answer to a question the other camps aren't asking. If that's right, then no amount of additional math can settle it and everyone is talking past each other under one word.

But maybe that's too cynical and there IS a substantive question underneath -- like a real empirical question about which kinds of errors cause which downstream harms in actual sentencing, or a real normative question about what a decision-maker owes the people being scored, thats being fought out proxy-war style through the metrics.

So: diagnose the stall. What kind of disagreement is this -- verbal, an empirical question in disguise, competing background theories of justice doing all the work, people answering different questions under the heading of fairness? Work from what the sides actually say (calibration vs equalized odds vs individual fairness people), not from a caricature. And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly whether anything could move it -- a distinction, a new kind of result, better empirical work on harms -- or whether we should just expect it to keep going forever, and why.
