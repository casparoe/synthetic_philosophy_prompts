I'm a software engineer, and over the past year I've been trying to write down a personal framework for making large, hard-to-reverse life decisions -- the kind where "prototype it first" isn't an option. The immediate trigger is the question of whether to have children, but I want the framework to cover things like emigrating, switching fields mid-career, and major health decisions too. Before I start relying on it, I'd like an audit of whether my commitments are actually jointly coherent, since I assembled them from different sources at different times.

Here is what I currently hold:

1. Big decisions should be made roughly the way I'd make an engineering decision: enumerate the options, forecast the outcomes as best I can, weight them by how much I value them, prefer reversible options where available, and gather testimony from people who have already taken each path, since their reports are the closest thing to empirical data I can get about my own counterfactual future.

2. I'm persuaded by L. A. Paul's work on transformative experience: some choices -- parenthood being the central case for me -- are epistemically inaccessible in advance (you cannot know what it will be like) and personally transformative (they change your core preferences and values). For such choices, the standard forecast-and-weight procedure in (1) simply fails, because both the outcomes and the weighting function are unavailable before the choice.

3. Nonetheless, transformative choices can still be made rationally: you decide on the basis of whether you want to undergo the transformation itself -- whether you want to become the kind of person who has had that experience and sees things its way -- rather than on the basis of the experience's anticipated phenomenal character or downstream satisfactions.

4. The best retrospective test of whether a major decision was correct is whether my later self endorses it. Sustained, clear-eyed regret is strong evidence that the decision was a mistake, and wholehearted endorsement by my future self is strong evidence it was right.

5. When people undergo a major change and adapt to it -- emigrants, people who acquire disabilities, people who leave lucrative careers -- and report being roughly as happy as before, I take those reports at face value as good evidence about how such a change would go for me, and I think this is exactly the kind of testimony-gathering (1) recommends.

My questions: Are these five commitments jointly tenable? Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which pairs only appear to be? I'd like you to be discriminating rather than flagging everything -- I suspect at least one apparent conflict here dissolves once you're careful about what the commitments actually say. For each genuine tension, rate its severity relative to the others and identify the cheapest revision that resolves it, meaning the revision that forces me to give up the least. Finally, if you had to pick a single change to this set that removes the most inconsistency per unit of philosophical damage, which would it be, and what would I have to stop saying?
