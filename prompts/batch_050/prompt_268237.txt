In 1985, the Coca-Cola Company replaced its flagship drink with a reformulated version after blind taste tests appeared to show that consumers preferred the sweeter formula both to the original and to its main competitor. The launch provoked a sustained public backlash — protest groups, hoarding of the old stock, a flood of complaints — and within months the company restored the original recipe under a new label alongside the reformulation.

Analyse this episode through the lens of revealed preference theory. In particular: (i) On the strict behaviourist reading, on which preference just is a disposition to choose, what exactly did the taste tests reveal, and does the backlash show that consumers' preferences reversed, or rather that the tests measured choice over the wrong objects — sips of drinks rather than the option of living in a world where the original remains available? (ii) Can a preference for the availability of a product one rarely buys be given any revealed-preference gloss at all (for instance, via preferences over menus or second-order preferences), or does accounting for the backlash force the theory to admit attitudes, attachments, or identity-considerations it was designed to do without? (iii) What does the episode show about the relation between choice, utility, and welfare: did the company, by ultimately restoring the original, thereby make consumers better off, and could the theory itself settle that question?

Write your answer in British English. Please separate what is settled in the theory of revealed preference from what is genuinely contested among economists and philosophers — in particular, do not present any one view on whether choice constitutes preference or merely evidences an underlying welfare-relevant attitude as if it were the consensus.
