A friend of mine, an engineer like me (well, I was a chemist actually, I retired from pharma research a few years back), was over for dinner last week and we got onto cosmology, the way you do after the second bottle. He trotted out this argument I've now heard three or four times from different people, always with the same story attached. The story goes: suppose you're stood in front of a firing squad, twelve marksmen, they all fire, and somehow every single bullet misses. You'd never just shrug and say lucky me, coincidences happen. You'd conclude something fishy was going on -- the guns were loaded with blanks, or someone had bribed the soldiers, or a thousand squads had fired that day and you're the one guy still breathing to wonder about it. Then he says, the universe is like that. The constants of physics, the masses of the particles, the strength of the forces, all these dials have to sit in an absurdly narrow band or there's no chemistry, no stars, no us. So by firing squad logic, we should conclude something set the dials -- or that there are vast numbers of universes and we just happen to live in one of the liveable ones. He finds both options equally plausible, which I suspect is him being polite about option one.

Here's what I want. I'm giving a little talk at my philosophy discussion group (we're four retirees and a very patient grad student) and I want to take this firing squad analogy apart properly, because it does an awful lot of the heavy lifting in how people talk about this stuff. So: what exactly is being mapped onto what? Lay out the correspondences explicitly -- what plays the role of the marksmen, the bullets, the surviving prisoner, the prior expectation that marksmen don't miss. Then tell me where the mapping quietly breaks. Some disanalogies I half-sense already: in the firing squad case I have independent knowledge of what normally happens, of marksmen's accuracy rates, whereas with the constants we have a sample of exactly one universe and no idea what "normally" even means. And there's something odd about the survivor being the one doing the wondering, like the surprise is generated by the fact that I'm here to feel it. But I don't want to wing it -- I want to know which disanalogies actually damage the argument and which are just pedantry, and whether the analogy smuggles in anything (like the existence of agents with intentions, or a definite probability distribution over outcomes) that the cosmological case simply doesn't supply.

Most of all: does the friend's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? He used the story to license a specific inference -- that "some explanation is needed and it has to be either design or multiplicity" -- and I want to know whether a cleaned-up version of the analogy can still carry that conclusion, or whether the honest version of the comparison is weaker and supports something much more modest. If the firing squad is the wrong analogy, what's the right one? I've heard a lottery version (somebody has to win, and the winner always feels singled out) but I'm not sure whether that's the same argument in different clothes or a genuinely different and maybe better one. Audit it all, and don't be gentle -- I'd rather have the holes pointed out now than have the grad student do it on the night.
