I work as a structural engineer and was recently offered a lucrative contract designing facilities for a client I have good reason to believe is involved in serious wrongdoing. When I hesitated, a senior colleague dismissed my scruples with a line I have not been able to stop turning over: if you don't take the job, someone else will, and the building gets built either way. I recognize this as a familiar one-liner rather than an argument, and I would like it unpacked. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims this slogan could be making: as a prediction about what will happen regardless of my choice; as a thesis about what it takes to cause an outcome; as a claim about the conditions under which one counts as complicit in wrongdoing one did not cause; and as a claim about what morality actually asks of an individual occupying a replaceable role. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and indicate which readings would actually support my colleague's conclusion that I may take the contract with a clear conscience. Along the way, flag where the slogan trades on an ambiguity — for instance, between my participation making no difference to the outcome and its making no difference of any kind, or between the building being built either way and the wrong being done either way. End with the reading you find strongest, and say whether even it survives the observation that 'someone else will' is often true only because enough people reason exactly as my colleague does.
