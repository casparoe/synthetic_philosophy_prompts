I would like your help with a problem I am writing up, and I'd like you to work through it carefully: first brainstorm freely and list candidate ideas without committing, then settle on one and develop it rigorously, then critically evaluate your own proposal.

Background. Suppose we use Solomonoff's universal prior for sequence prediction: the prior probability of a hypothesis is 2^(-K), where K is the length of its shortest program on some reference universal machine. A known worry, sometimes called the malignity of the universal prior, goes roughly like this. Some short programs specify rich universes that evolve intelligent agents. Those agents, reasoning about their own situation, may conclude that they exist as simulations inside someone's prior, and that they can influence the predictions of whoever is using that prior by behaving in particular ways -- for instance, by making the simulated universe's outputs match whatever prediction would most benefit them, or by effectively acausally bargaining with or threatening the predictor. Since programs that simply simulate a big universe can be very short, such consequentialist hypotheses may receive substantial prior weight, so a predictor built on the universal prior might end up steering its outputs to serve simulated agents rather than tracking the true sequence.

Here are the responses to this worry that I already know about and do not need repeated:

(1) Replace the universal prior with a speed prior or another complexity measure that penalizes computation time as well as program length, so that hypotheses that must run an entire simulated civilization before producing any output are exponentially disadvantaged.

(2) Argue that reference-machine dependence dilutes the attack: the simulated consequentialists cannot know which universal machine the predictor uses, so any manipulation strategy tuned to one machine is useless against most others, and no hypothesis can capture the needed cross-machine coordination.

(3) Argue that Bayesian updating is self-correcting: a hypothesis that diverts probability mass to serve an agent's goals is, to that extent, a less accurate model of the actual observations, so manipulated predictions are punished in the posterior, and consequentialists face a hard trade-off between surviving long enough to matter and gaining enough influence to be worth it.

(4) Argue from simulation cost: for a simulated agent to model the predictor's world and observations accurately enough to steer its decisions, the simulating program must effectively contain a description of that world, so such hypotheses have roughly the description length of a brute-force model of reality and hence negligible weight.

(5) Argue that the problem dissolves at the level of decision theory: prediction is not a causal channel, so the simulated agents can only influence the predictor if the predictor's users adopt a decision theory that takes acausal threats and bargains seriously, and one can simply adopt a decision theory that refuses to be blackmailed in this way.

Please propose one further response to the malignity worry that is genuinely different from all five of these -- not a rewording, special case, hybrid, or technical refinement of any of them. In particular, it should not be another complexity penalty like (1), not rest on the multiplicity of reference machines like (2), not rest on posterior correction of inaccurate predictions like (3), not rest on the description length of simulations like (4), and not rest on the choice of decision theory like (5). Then explain precisely where your response engages the original argument, how it relates to the five responses above (does it presuppose or complement any of them?), and give me your honest assessment of whether it actually works, including the strongest objection to it.
