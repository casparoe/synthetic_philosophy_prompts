I am preparing a seminar on Dharmakīrti’s epistemology and want to replace the usual summary of perception, inference, and momentariness with an adversarial collaboration between two charitable interpretations. For the purposes of the exercise, assume the following minimal picture: perception is a nonconceptual, causally produced awareness of a particular; inference is conceptual and arrives at knowledge by excluding alternatives, as when one infers fire from smoke; and momentariness is the claim that whatever is causally efficacious exists only for a moment.

Draft the joint statement that two interpreters could both sign. The first, the causal-continuity interpreter, emphasizes that smoke is reliably produced by fire and argues that inference depends on stable causal connections across momentary particulars. The second, the exclusion-first interpreter, emphasizes that inference operates through concepts constructed by excluding alternatives and argues that such concepts can guide cognition even if the underlying particulars never persist. Their dispute concerns how momentariness can make inference possible rather than impossible.

Structure the statement as follows:

1. A numbered list of claims both interpreters accept. Include claims about the difference between perceiving a particular and inferring a conceptualized object, the role of causal connection in the fire-smoke case, and the respect in which momentariness creates a genuine problem about stability.
2. A table of the remaining disagreements. State each position in language its defender would accept, without making either side sound naively committed to permanent objects or to wholly unconstrained conceptual fabrication.
3. A list of at least three cruxes. For each crux, provide a concrete example or thought experiment that both sides would accept as relevant, and record what each side would predict or emphasize in response. At least one crux should concern whether talk of the same fire across moments is merely useful conceptual shorthand or is required for reliable causal explanation; at least one should concern what makes an inference reliable if neither smoke nor fire persists.
4. A concluding section identifying which part of the disagreement, if any, is primarily verbal, and which part reflects a substantive difference about the relation between causation, concepts, and knowledge.

Do not provide a bibliography, citations, or a verdict about which interpreter is ultimately correct. The aim is a constructive document both parties could endorse while making their residual disagreement exact. Please write the response in American English.
