I have been working through the ravens paradox on my own and have converged on a broadly Bayesian position that I would like stress-tested before I commit to it in a paper. Here are my commitments, stated as precisely as I can manage. My question is whether they are jointly tenable — and if not, exactly which one has to go, and what amputating it would cost the rest of the package.

1. Confirmation is Bayesian relevance, full stop: relative to background K, evidence E confirms hypothesis H iff P(H | E, K) > P(H | K), disconfirms iff it lowers that probability, and is irrelevant iff it leaves it unchanged. There is no further syntactic condition on confirmation. In particular, I do not accept Nicod's criterion as an independent constraint — it should stand or fall as a theorem of the probability calculus, not as an extra plank.

2. The equivalence condition holds without exception. Since a universal generalization and its contrapositive ("all ravens are black"; "all non-black things are non-ravens") are logically equivalent, any body of evidence confirms or disconfirms them to exactly the same degree. There is no such thing as evidence that bears on one formulation but not the other.

3. The standard dissolution of the paradox is correct. A white shoe really does confirm the raven hypothesis — but by a negligible amount, given how vastly more non-black things there are than ravens, and that negligibility, rather than the shoe's irrelevance, fully explains why ornithologists are rational to ignore footwear. The paradox trades on confusing minuscule confirmation with no confirmation.

4. Positive instances always support a generalization. An observed black raven always raises the probability that all ravens are black, at least slightly. Whatever the background knowledge turns out to be, finding a raven to be black cannot count against the generalization — that is just what it is for an observation to be a confirming instance rather than a counterinstance.

5. How the evidence was gathered matters to how much confirmation it provides. A white shoe encountered while deliberately sampling non-black objects, and the same shoe encountered while sampling objects at random, do not confirm to the same degree, because the sampling protocol enters the likelihoods. Any precise statement of the paradox must therefore specify the sampling story.

6. Confirmation relations are objective given the evidence and the background. Two rational agents who share K and E cannot disagree about whether E confirms H; disagreement is always traceable to a difference in what they know, never to a bare difference in doxastic temperament.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, ordered by severity? For each real tension, tell me what I would have to give up and what the cheapest repair costs the rest of the package; a small numerical model is more use to me than prose at that stage. Please also identify the apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection, since I suspect I am double-counting somewhere — I have never been fully certain that 3 and 5 sit comfortably together, or that 2 follows from 1 as cleanly as I have been assuming.
