I am drafting a position statement for an interdisciplinary workshop on individual responsibility for collective harms, and before circulating it I would like an audit of whether my commitments are jointly tenable. Please go through them pairwise, tell me which combinations are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank any real tensions by severity, and for each one state what would have to give. Finally, tell me which single revision would restore consistency at the lowest cost to the overall view. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want to know whether this particular package hangs together.

Here is what I hold:

1. Welfarist constraint. An act can only be wrong in virtue of its effects on the interests of sentient individuals. I reject the idea that outcomes can be impersonally bad, and I reject complicity- or participation-based accounts of wrongdoing that would condemn an act independently of its effects on anyone.

2. Inefficacy verdicts. In large-number cases, individual actions are almost always causally inefficacious. A single vote in a national election has a probability of being pivotal on the order of one in tens of millions. A single consumer's chicken purchase makes no difference to how many chickens are raised, since the industry smooths out individual fluctuations. One person's additional flight changes nothing detectable about the climate.

3. Obligations nonetheless. There is a genuine obligation to vote in large elections and to abstain from factory-farmed meat. These are duties rather than ideals of personal virtue, and they bind people who find voting inconvenient and meat enjoyable.

4. Expected-value grounding. The obligations in (3) are grounded in expected value: a tiny probability of an enormous effect can outweigh a modest certain cost. Voting is obligatory because the expected benefit of casting a potentially pivotal vote exceeds the cost of the trip. Abstention from meat is obligatory because there is some chance that one's purchase is the one that crosses a threshold in the supply chain's response, and the magnitude of the change when a threshold is crossed compensates for the smallness of the chance.

5. Deliberative disregard principle. Rational deliberation may simply set aside possibilities whose probability falls below some very low threshold; otherwise one would be paralyzed. One need not factor in the remote chance that an evening walk leads to a pedestrian's death, or that driving to the polling station causes a fatal accident. I regard any decision procedure that forbids such disregard as fanatical, and I hold that a correct moral theory must be usable by an agent who knows only rough, order-of-magnitude probabilities.

6. Replaceability is no excuse. An oil company executive cannot defend staying in his position by observing that, were he to resign, an equally willing successor would be found within the week and extraction would proceed unchanged. His contribution to climate harm is real and serious even though the counterfactual marginal effect of his tenure is approximately nil.

For each tension you identify, I would like to know precisely which commitments generate it and what the cheapest escape route is, not merely that the combination feels uncomfortable. And where you conclude that an apparent tension dissolves on inspection, please show why rather than simply asserting it.
