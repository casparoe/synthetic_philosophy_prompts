I've been following the lethal autonomous weapons debate for a while now -- first in a grad seminar on war ethics, now as part of a policy internship -- and the thing that strikes me is how completely it has converged and stalled at the same time. For over a decade, basically every party to the discussion, from arms-control NGOs to military powers that clearly want these systems, has endorsed the slogan that weapons must remain under "meaningful human control." And yet nothing has moved. As far as I can tell there are at least three different things people mean by it: an engineering standard (the system is predictable and bounded enough that a human commander can genuinely understand what it will do), an agency standard (an actual human being deliberates over each use of force, or at least each engagement), and an accountability standard (there is a specific identifiable human who can be held responsible for what the machine does, wich might be satisfied even if nobody was in the loop at the moment of firing). These can come apart radically -- a system could be perfectly predictable with a clear chain of responsibility while no human ever deliberates over a single target.

My working hypothesis is that the vagueness is doing political work: "meaningful human control" survives as a consensus precisely because everyone reads their own version into it, and any attempt to sharpen it would shatter the coalition that formed around it. So the stall isn't a failure of argument, it's a feature of the concept. But I'm not confident in that reading. Maybe there's a real substantive disagreement buried underneath -- about whether current AI systems can ever be predictable in the relevant sense, or about what the proportionality and precaution judgments in humanitarian law actually demand of a decision-maker, such that only a deliberating human could perform them. Or maybe it's a genuinely empirical dispute about reliability that just feels philosophical.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on autonomous weapons themselves. What kind of disagreement is this, really -- verbal, empirical, legal, or a clash of intuitions about what killing-by-machine requires to be permissible? Support the diagnosis with what the different camps actually say and prioritize in their own arguments. And given your diagnosis, tell me what progress would even look like: a sharper technical definition of control, some kind of test or certification standard, a new legal ruling, or an honest admission that the concept is a diplomatic placeholder that should be retired in favor of more specific questions -- like who is responsible when an autonomous system kills unlawfully, and what a human must actually do, moment to moment, for their control to deserve the name.
