My ethics group chat has latched onto the phrase 'one thought too many' and now it gets deployed everywhere. First it was the classic case: a man can save either his wife or a stranger from drowning and saves his wife, and someone said that if he first checks with himself whether that's morally permissable, he's had one thought too many. Fine. But last week someone used the exact same line to dismiss effective altruists running cost-effectiveness comparisons on their donations -- 'one thought too many!' -- and a third person claimed the slogan basically refutes utilitarianism as a theory, since a utilitarian agent is always having the extra thought.

Can you lay out the different precise claims this slogan could actually be making? I suspect they range from fairly weak (something about what your motivating thought should be in the moment of action, while the justification stays in the back of your mind) to very strong (something about a relationship being corrupted if it even *needs* an impartial justification, or about a moral theory being defective if it can't be the thing you think while acting). For each reading: is it trivial, substantive, or just false? Is it plausible? And does it actually support the two uses from my chat -- the dig at donation calculations, and the claim to refute utilitarianism wholesale? Also say which reading best fits the original rescuer case, because I have a feeling the group chat version has drifted a long way from whatever the point originally was.
