I am preparing a one-page statement of my philosophical commitments for a study group on Indian philosophy, and I want it stress-tested before I circulate it. The tradition is Advaita Vedānta, and here is roughly where I have landed:

1. Brahman — pure, non-dual consciousness — is the sole ultimate reality. The world of distinct objects, persons, and events is māyā: not nothing, since it is experienced, but not ultimately real either; it is anirvacanīya, not classifiable as existent or as nonexistent.

2. My innermost self (ātman) is numerically identical with Brahman and always has been. Liberation is therefore not a change of state but the removal of a misapprehension: nothing new comes into being when someone wakes up.

3. Bondage is beginningless ignorance (avidyā). There was never a moment at which I fell into ignorance, yet ignorance can be terminated by knowledge, as darkness is ended by light.

4. The tradition's means are genuinely effective: study of the Upaniṣads under a qualified teacher, reasoning, and meditation can actually bring about liberation in a prepared mind.

5. A liberated person (jīvanmukta) continues to act — eats, teaches, responds to events — until the momentum of past action (prārabdha karma) runs out, the way a potter's wheel keeps turning after the potter's hand is withdrawn; and such action is not propelled by egoistic desire.

6. Most puzzles in this area dissolve once one distinguishes the conventional standpoint (vyavahārika) from the ultimate standpoint (pāramārthika): conventionally there are seekers, teachers, texts, and liberation; ultimately there is neither bondage nor liberation nor anyone who was ever bound.

Audit this set. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, diagnose its source — whether it is an artifact of my particular formulations or a pressure point internal to Advaita itself (for instance, the question of where beginningless ignorance resides, given that Brahman is supposed to be self-luminous) — and identify the cheapest revision that would resolve it, together with what that revision costs. One constraint on method: since commitment 6 already deploys the two-standpoint distinction, treat any appeal to it as a claim to be argued for, not as a free pass; if you invoke it to dissolve a tension, show that it actually does the work rather than merely relocating the problem one level up. Do not assume I have any preference about which commitment to surrender.

After your prose analysis, give me a table with these columns: Pair (the commitments involved, by number); Status (genuine tension / apparent tension / dissolved-by-distinction); Severity (high, medium, or low); Cheapest revision (one sentence); Cost of the revision (one sentence). Then a final row naming the single revision that would do the most to clean up the whole set.
