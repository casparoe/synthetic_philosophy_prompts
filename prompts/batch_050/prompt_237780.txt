I have no formal training in history or philosophy, but I've spent the last year turning over a question that won't leave me alone. It started when a family story I'd repeated my whole life -- about how my grandfather came to this country -- turned out to be contradicted by a ship manifest I found online. What unsettled me wasn't the mistake; it was how reluctant I felt to stop telling the old version, and how my aunt flatly refused to, even after seeing the document. Since then I've noticed the same pattern at larger scales: towns whose founding legends don't survive contact with the archive, nations whose schoolbooks describe events their own records contradict. I keep circling the question of what ought to give way when a community's living memory and the documentary record conflict -- and whether "giving way" is even the right picture.

I run a monthly discussion group (10 to 14 adults, no academics among us, we meet for two hours in a room at the public library), and I'd like to spend a session on this. Rather than opening with a lecture or a reading, I want an activity or game we can actually run, in which the participants feel the force of both sides of this conflict before anyone names it. The structure I have in mind -- though you should improve on it -- is a staged simulation: the group plays the members of a small fictional community that possesses a cherished shared story about some event in its past, and over the course of the session documents surface in stages that first complicate and then plainly contradict the story, forcing the group to a concrete decision (for instance, what to put on a commemorative plaque, or whether to change an annual ceremony).

Please design the activity in full, including:

1. The fictional community and its shared story, written as usable handout material, plus the staged documents. I'd like the documents to arrive piecemeal and to require some interpretation themselves, so that the group discovers the record isn't a single unambiguous voice either -- "just follow the documents" should turn out to be less simple than it sounds.

2. Roles with something real at stake. At least some participants should hold roles whose standing, livelihood, or sense of self within the community depends on the old story, and at least one role should represent someone the old story harms or erases. Whoever plays the record-keeper or archivist should be constrained from simply dictating the answer.

3. Rules for the decision itself -- how the group must arrive at its verdict, under what time pressure, and with what decision rule. If there's a mechanism you can build in so that the memory-keepers and the record-keeper are forced to negotiate rather than one side overruling the other, I'd especially value that.

4. A minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, including when each document is released.

5. A debrief plan: which moments in the activity map onto which parts of the underlying problem; what will predictably go wrong when this is run with ordinary adults (people refusing to budge, people treating their assigned role as a joke, the group rushing to a comfortable compromise) and how I should handle each; and an honest statement of which features of the real phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce -- I suspect real generational attachment and real historical trauma can't be simulated in a library room, and I'd rather you tell me what I'll have to supply in words than pretend the game covers it.

Everything should be runnable by a facilitator with no academic background, using nothing more than printed handouts, and the conceptual vocabulary should only be introduced in the debrief, after the group has already lived the problem.
