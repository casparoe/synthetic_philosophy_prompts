I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on the epistemology of statistical inference, and I have run into an argument—my own, unfortunately—that I cannot refute. My working plan was to use the Principal Principle as the bridge between objective chance and rational credence: roughly, when an agent knows the objective chance of an event, rationality requires her credence in that event to match it. But I have convinced myself that this makes the principle empirically testable, and therefore refutable, in a way that collapses the whole project. The reasoning is as follows. By the law of large numbers, objective chances are revealed in long-run frequencies; so if the Principal Principle is correct, rational credences should converge onto observed frequencies as evidence accumulates. Yet competent scientists and forecasters routinely—and, I take it, rationally—hold credences that diverge sharply from observed frequencies: when they know a coin has been bent but have tossed it only a handful of times, when the obvious reference class has been gerrymandered, or when a well-motivated theory outruns its data. The Principal Principle appears to condemn all of these cases as irrational, and since they patently are not, the principle must be false. Where exactly does this argument go wrong? My supervisor says the mistake is philosophically deep but declines to elaborate. I would like a diagnosis precise enough to write up: does the error lie in my identification of chance with limiting frequency, in the inference from the law of large numbers to a norm about credence, in a misunderstanding of how the principle interacts with an agent's background evidence, or somewhere else entirely? And does any of this bear on the undermining problem associated with Lewis's formulation, which I have so far set aside as a technicality?
