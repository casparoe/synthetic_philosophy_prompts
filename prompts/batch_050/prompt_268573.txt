So I've been chewing on this for months, mostly because of an argument with my coworker Dave that keeps restarting at lunch. Dave reckons history basically runs on rails — like if Napoleon had never been born, some other French guy would've conquered Europe anyway, because the economic conditions and the armies and all that were pointing one direction regardless. I keep saying nah, huge chunks of history happen because of dumb accidents — a horse spooks at the wrong second, someone catches a cold, and the whole next century looks different. Neither of us can budge the other, and honestly I think it's because we're just trading anecdotes and neither side can ever lose.

So I want a thought experiment built to order that actually settles something, or at least shows where the disagreement really lives. Specs:

- Two worlds that are total duplicates of each other up to some date. Same people, same geography, same weather, same everything.
- They diverge because of ONE small event that is a genuine accident — not a difference in economic conditions, institutions, culture, anything structural. A spooked horse, a sneeze in the middle of a speech, a train that leaves four minutes late. That kind of thing.
- Jump forward a few hundered years and the two worlds are drastically different — different empires, different dominant religions, different maps.
- Here's the hard constraint: the accident has to be built so Dave's standard move fails. His move is always "okay fine, but if that accident hadn't happened, some other accident or some other ambitious guy would've produced roughly the same outcome — history keeps spare nails lying around." So design the case so there is no spare nail: nothing and nobody waiting in the wings who could substitute in.

Then do the accounting, in plain language because I'm going to use this on Dave directly:

1. Walk through what Dave's runs-on-rails view is forced to say about the two worlds, and why this case counts against it rather than just being a story he can shrug at.
2. Tell me which feature of the scenario is doing all the work. My gut says it's something about bottlenecks — moments where everything has to squeeze through one person or one afternoon — but I'd rather you identify it properly.
3. Show what happens if you remove that feature, e.g., tweak it so there were ten other people positioned to do the same thing. Does the argument collapse, or just weaken?
4. Anticipate Dave's last-ditch retreat, which I can already hear coming: "okay, the worlds differ in the details, but the big picture converges — same technology level, same kinds of states, just different flags on the castles." Tell me whether the scenario can be rebuilt to beat that version too, or whether that's actually a different, stronger claim that no duplicate-worlds setup can touch — in which case I want to know exactly what I'd have to concede to him.

No time travellers, no wizards, no alternate physics — just two ordinary histories and one accident. One or two paragraphs for the scenario itself, then the accounting.
