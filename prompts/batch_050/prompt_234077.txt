In my political theory reading group we recently spent an evening on sortition, and one member -- a defense attorney -- kept leaning on an analogy I want stress-tested before I reuse it in an essay. Her claim: we already entrust randomly selected ordinary citizens with the most consequential decisions the state makes, since juries decide questions of guilt and, in some jurisdictions, effectively life or death; a legislature chosen by lot would just be this same institution scaled up, so anyone who accepts jury trial has already conceded the principle and can only object to lottocracy on details. She used it to rebut the standard "voters are too uninformed" objection: if twelve people off the street can absorb weeks of forensic testimony and reach a sound verdict, a few hundred can learn enough about tax policy. I noticed the room nodded along more than the analogy seemed to earn, and I'd like a proper audit of what it can and cannot carry.

Give me the mapping explicitly -- a correspondence table is welcome. What plays the role of the judge, who instructs on the law and excludes inadmissible material? Of the adversarial lawyers, who package expert knowledge for laypeople, and does their counterpart in a lottery legislature become the civil service, lobbyists, or the media? Of voir dire, sequestration, the unanimity requirement, the short and bounded term of service, the fact that jurors answer a question framed for them rather than choosing which questions to ask? Then tell me where the mapping breaks in ways that matter for my friend's rebuttal specifically. A few candidate fractures I want adjudicated: juries find facts within a law someone else wrote, while legislators choose values and write the law itself; a verdict ends and the jury dissolves, whereas a seated chamber persists for years and can be captured slowly; and the jury arguably works because it is embedded in, and constrained by, institutions -- elected legislators, appointed judges, appellate review -- that the sortition proposal would partly be replacing, so the analogy may smuggle in dependence on the very thing it claims to make unnecessary.

Finally, say whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are priced. Three outcomes interest me: the analogy carries the full weight and the uninformed-voter objection really is dead; it fails wholesale; or -- my suspicion -- it supports something weaker and more specific than lottocracy, such as randomly selected bodies with a defined brief, a time limit, and oversight (a citizens' assembly on one issue, or a sortition chamber with veto power but no agenda control), precisely because those preserve the features of juries that do the work. If the third is right, name the one or two features of jury design that the analogy licenses exporting and the ones it forbids. Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each verdict, and end by singling out the single point in your audit where you feel least sure of your ground.
