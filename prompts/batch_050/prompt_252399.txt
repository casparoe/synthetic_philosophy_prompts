Write a satirical brochure, in American English, of roughly 600-700 words, for a fictional consultancy called Halloway & Sons, National Heritage Consultants, which sells "antiquity services" to recently founded or recently self-conscious nations. The firm is entirely sincere within the fiction: it markets, with straight-faced corporate confidence, exactly the processes that historians describe when they explain how young states come to possess supposedly immemorial cultures.

The brochure should be structured as a real sales document: a brief company history (in which the firm boasts of its own long pedigree despite being founded in 2011, and treats this as no obstacle whatsoever), three tiers of service packages, a short FAQ, a disclaimer section, and two invented client testimonials. The humor must come from the brochure performing, with perfect accuracy, the very mechanisms a specialist in nationalism studies would recognize: the mass production of a shared vernacular through print and schooling; the retrospective stitching of a usable past onto an improvised present through repetitive ceremony; the role of census categories, official maps, and museums in conjuring a bounded people whose membership then feels natural and eternal; the exportability of the whole package, so that any population can be fitted with the same modular nationhood; and the serene confidence that a tradition's youth is a marketing detail rather than a contradiction.

The piece must never use the terms "invented tradition," "imagined community," or "print-capitalism," and must never mention any real scholar or real book; the concepts should be enacted, not named, so that a reader who knows the scholarship recognizes each mechanism with a jolt and a reader who does not still finds the brochure funny as an absurdity. At least one joke should turn on the paradox that the product's selling point is its agelessness while the production schedule is measured in fiscal quarters. The FAQ should include the question of what happens if a client's manufactured past conflicts with the archaeological record, and the answer should be evasive in a way that is itself philosophically pointed. The disclaimers, in fine-print legalese, should quietly concede the deepest claims of the constructivist view while sounding like routine liability language.

Keep the irony deadpan and legible: no winking, no overt moralizing, and no real nations, real leaders, or living persons named as clients. The target is the practice of manufacturing national antiquity and the style of discourse that conceals it, not any actual country. End with the firm's slogan, which should be a sentence that a sincere patriot and a cynical historian could both say, meaning opposite things.
