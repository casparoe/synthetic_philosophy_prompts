So I just got out of a genuinely annoying two-hour argument with my friend Mara about whether history is basically propaganda. Her view was that archives are deliberately rigged — the winners decide what gets kept, everyone else gets erased on purpose. I kept pushing back that a lot of the silences are way more boring than that: it's expensive to keep records, most people through history couldn't write, nobody bothers to write down the stuff that seems totally obvious at the time (which is exactly the stuff a historian 500 years later would kill for), and half of it is just that paper rots and some people's letters happened to be in the dry building. We couldn't settle it, and I suspect the honest answer is "both, and telling them apart is the hard part." Anyway, I teach a first-year undergrad seminar on how we know things about the past, about 18 students, and I want them to feel this in their gut instead of just nodding along to a reading.

Design me an in-class activity for one 80-minute session where the students produce an archive themselves and then discover what's missing from it. My rough seed idea, which you can totally replace: split them into groups who have to document some staged event or simulated week-in-the-life-of-a-town under constraints (limited "paper," limited time, a clerk who has to decide what's worth recording), then swap the archives between groups, and each group has to reconstruct "what happened" purely from the other group's records — and then we compare against what actually happened and watch them discover the silences they themselves created five minutes ago without even noticing. Give me concrete rules, roles, constraints, and a timeline that actually fits in 80 minutes, including how to run the comparison phase so it's the students who name what's absent and not me lecturing.

Also include a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the actual conceptual stuff: the difference between deliberate suppression and neglect and mere non-survival, the way record-keepers' idea of "obvious" or "unimportant" does invisible work, and whether a silence in a source is evidence of anything at all. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I assume chaos around the swapping phase, and at least one group cheating by just remembering instead of using the archive), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce — like, in a classroom everyone knows it's a game, nobody's actual life is being misrecorded, and real archival silence falls on people who often know they're being left out, which my students won't experiance. Can the activity get anywhere near that part, or do I just have to say it out loud?
