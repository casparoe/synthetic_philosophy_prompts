I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm building an interactive feature on the debate over what safety work costs AI developers. The running question in that debate: is alignment research a "tax" -- time, compute, and capability sacrificed that a less cautious rival won't sacrifice -- and if so, does the pressure of competition make it rational to underpay it (the "competitiveness constraint")? My readers have heard the slogans. I want to sort them by what they'd actually accept, not by which camp they've joined.

Please write a reader-facing quiz of nine or ten questions that assigns each reader a position in this debate, plus a scoring key. Requirements:

1. Every question must be a concrete scenario with three answer options, never a direct attitude probe. Nothing like "do you believe safety slows progress?" Instead, choices of the form: an evaluation protocol adds three months to your release schedule and a rival lab is known to skip it; a safety technique costs twenty percent of training compute and buys reliability customers haven't asked for; a proposed rule is one your large lab can absorb but a two-person startup cannot; a foreign competitor operates under no such rule. The reader should be able to answer each question without knowing which position it feeds.

2. The scoring key should locate readers in a two-dimensional space, and the key itself should make the dimensions explicit. Axis one is empirical: is serious alignment work a net cost, roughly free, or actually capability-positive? Axis two is strategic: given that others may not pay the cost, should one pay it unilaterally, push for binding rules on everyone, condition restraint on enforceable international coordination, or decline to pay and let the race run?

3. The leaf positions must include at least: the "no tax exists" view (safety and capability advance together, so the dilemma is illusory); safety-as-differentiator (the tax is nominally real but customers and regulators pay a premium that erases it); pay-the-tax unilateralism; level-the-field regulation; coordination-or-race (restraint only under enforceable global agreement); and the view that the constraint is largely an incumbent's story told to invite rules that entrench incumbents. Give each leaf a short name, a one-sentence definition, and its most awkward commitment.

4. Flag any pair of leaves your quiz cannot reliably separate, and say what additional question would separate them if one exists.

5. Identify at least two regions of the answer space that no prominent position occupies, and diagnose each: is it empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored?

The tone of the questions should be neutral enough that no option reads as the obviously correct one -- readers should feel sorted, not graded.
