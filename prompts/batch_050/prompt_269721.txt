I am a high school senior preparing an independent philosophy project on the timing of conscious experience. Please act as an expert philosopher of mind who is also comfortable reasoning about experimental psychology. Think carefully and begin with a brief brainstorming section identifying the important distinctions; then provide a polished, structured answer.

The case I am focusing on is the color-phi phenomenon. A red dot appears on the left, disappears, and then a green dot appears on the right. Observers often report seeing one dot move from left to right and change from red to green partway across, even though the green dot had not yet appeared when the apparent motion supposedly began. Philosophers sometimes contrast two possible explanations: an Orwellian explanation, on which the subject initially has one experience that is then retrospectively replaced or misremembered, and a Stalinesque explanation, on which consciousness is delayed until the brain has constructed the final moving-and-changing sequence. Please do not assume that either explanation is correct, or even that they must describe genuinely different states of affairs.

What I want is a theory-neutral specification: what must an adequate theory of when experiences happen be able to do in order to explain this phenomenon and related cases? For each requirement, give:

1. The requirement itself.
2. The case or consideration that motivates it.
3. Whether it is plausibly non-negotiable or instead the preference of a particular theoretical camp.
4. What would count as a theory failing to meet it.

Please make sure the list addresses at least the following:

- It must distinguish the time of the physical stimulus, the time of relevant neural processing, the time at which an experience occurs, the time represented within the experience, and the time of any later memory or verbal report. If you think some of these distinctions can be collapsed, explain why.
- It must distinguish revising an already-conscious experience, delaying an experience until more information arrives, and constructing a later memory of an experience that never occurred.
- It must explain how later sensory input can affect the experienced content of an apparently earlier event without automatically making every experience retroactively fabricated.
- It must give some account of what makes a representation conscious that does not simply identify consciousness with either verbal report or long-term memory.
- It must remain compatible with the broad facts that neural processing takes time, perception can be predictive, later stimuli can affect the interpretation of earlier ones, subjects can misremember their experiences, and information can sometimes guide action without being consciously reportable.
- It must say what authority, if any, a subject’s later memory has about what was experienced earlier.
- It must state whether the Orwellian and Stalinesque explanations make different empirical predictions. If they do not, it must explain whether the distinction is still meaningful, is merely verbal, or concerns something not captured by observable evidence.
- It must avoid treating the brain as having one privileged finishing line at which the complete experience is presented, while still explaining why experience can seem unified and temporally ordered.

To test the requirements, please apply them to these four stipulated variations:

A. The red dot’s representation becomes available to reasoning and action systems for 100 milliseconds and is then replaced by the integrated red-to-green motion representation.

B. The red dot is processed locally but never becomes available to reasoning or action; the integrated motion representation appears only after the green dot is processed.

C. The red representation becomes available to reasoning and action systems for a very short time and then disappears completely, leaving no memory or possibility of report.

D. The red representation guides an immediate eye movement, but only the integrated red-to-green representation is available to the verbal-report system.

For each variation, say whether the theory should classify it as Orwellian, Stalinesque, neither, or genuinely underdetermined, and identify what further fact would settle the classification.

Most importantly, examine the requirements as a set. Are any two or more of them jointly unsatisfiable? If so, explain the conflict precisely rather than merely noting that different theorists disagree. Which single requirement would eliminate the largest number of familiar approaches? Which alleged requirement is actually just a prejudice of one camp?
