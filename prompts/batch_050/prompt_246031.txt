You are a business ethicist who specializes in sweatshop labor and the problem of mutually beneficial exploitation; please think carefully, privately canvassing several principles that could account for the record below before you commit to an answer. For two years I have sat on the ethics advisory panel of an apparel company, and I have kept a record of our chair's rulings on supplier arrangements. Her verdicts so far:

1. Approved: a supplier pays roughly twice the local going wage for comparable work, conditions are safe, and applicants far outnumber the positions.
2. Rejected: a supplier whose recruiters understated dormitory crowding and never disclosed the production quotas, even though the workers who signed on remained better off than they had been at home.
3. Rejected: a plantation that bought up and fenced the common land villagers had farmed for generations, then offered them day labor at wages above anything now available to them locally.
4. Approved: a small importer that keeps about seventy percent of the retail price of the weavings it buys, because the price it pays the weavers is the best any buyer offers in the region and its own margins barely cover its costs.
5. Rejected: a factory that required workers to surrender their passports as a deposit against quitting early, where the workers agreed to this and were, on paper, still better off than in their next-best option.

The panel now faces a new case. A supplier for our flagship sneaker pays the equivalent of $2.60 a day where the next-best local option pays $2.00; conditions are decent; hiring is honest; and neither we nor the supplier played any role in producing the region's poverty. At the same time, we clear roughly $100 on each pair sold at retail, and tripling the line workers' wages would raise our cost per pair by less than $2, which we could absorb without noticeable strain. The supplier's position is that the current wage is fair because it beats the available alternative and everyone consents.

Tell me how the chair is most likely to rule, and why. In doing so, identify two or more decision rules that each fit all five prior verdicts and show that they diverge on this case; name which prior verdict is the closest analog to the new one and whether the analogy survives scrutiny; and describe the hypothetical case that would most cleanly distinguish whichever rules you cannot yet tell apart. State how confident you are in your prediction, and single out the specific point in your analysis you are least sure of. Give the entire response as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
