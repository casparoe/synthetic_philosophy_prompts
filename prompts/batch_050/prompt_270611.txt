Two of my friends got into it at a pub last week and its still going in the group chat, so I want a ruling. The topic was that old line (Wigner, I think?) about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in physics — why math cooked up for purely internal reasons keeps turning out to be exactly what the universe runs on.

Maya's position: it's a genuine mystery and it's weird how often the pattern repeats. Her examples: complex numbers were basically a mathematical toy for centuries and then quantum mechanics makes them load-bearing, you can't state the physics without them; Dirac just follows where the math of his equation leads and it tells him there's an antiparticle nobody's ever seen, and then there it is in a cloud chamber; group theory gets developed with zero physics in mind and ends up organizing particle physics. She says: if math were just a tool we'd shaped to fit the world, you'd expect it to fit where we built it to fit — not to keep making correct predictions way past that. So the match between abstract math and physical reality needs explaining, and she suspects the only available explanations are kind of spooky (math is out there, or the world is made of it, or something).

Dan's position: no mystery, just selection bias plus hindsight. The warehouse of mathematics is enormous and most of it never touches physics; we remember the hits and forget the misses. Physicists also only ever build theories they can actually handle mathematically, so of course the successful ones are written in math that works — the effectiveness is engineered in at the front end, not discovered after the fact. And for every Dirac there's a pile of elegant formalisms that predicted nothing or predicted wrong, which nobody writes essays about.

Maya came back with: fine, selection explains why SOME math is useful, but it doesn't explain the surplus — the way the math is often more accurate than it had any right to be and coughs up entities nobody put in. Dan said that's cherry-picking the greatest hits, and also that the same physics can usually be written in totally different math (matrix mechanics vs wave mechanics), so what's matching the world isn't any particular mathematical structure, it's the whole space of formalisms humans find tractable — which we've been selecting on all along.

Then Maya tried a move I'm not sure works: she said even granting all that, there's a leftover puzzle about why the effective math is so often simple — the equations that work are short, and they didn't have to be. Dan said short equations are the only ones we'd ever find, because long complicated ones are unsolvable and unfundable, so that's selection again. She said that's not the same thing: selection explains why we FIND the simple theories among the ones that work, not why the ones that work are simple in the first place. And that's where it stalled, more or less.

I don't have a dog in this fight. Adjudicate it move by move: does the selection-bias response actually dissolve the puzzle, or does something survive it? Is Maya's surplus-accuracy point a real thing? Does the multiple-formalisms point help Dan or accidentally hurt him? And on the last exchange about simplicity — is that a genuinely new puzzle or the old one in a wig? Also tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please answer in American English.
