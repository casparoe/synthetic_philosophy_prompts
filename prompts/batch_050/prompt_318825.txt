Here are the objections I know to the claim that the maximum entropy principle yields rational priors -- that given partial constraints (say, a known expectation), a rational agent should adopt the unique maximum-entropy distribution satisfying them:

1. **Partition and coordinate dependence.** Maxent inherits the Bertrand-style paradoxes: the answer depends on the parametrization, since entropy is not invariant under reparameterization (the cube-factory problem being the standard illustration).
2. **No canonical ignorance measure.** "Maximize entropy" presupposes a background measure relative to which entropy is computed; choosing that measure is just the prior-selection problem pushed back a step, not solved.
3. **Failure on infinite or unbounded domains.** Uniform distributions over infinite spaces don't exist, and maxent often has no solution there (e.g., no maxent distribution on the reals given only a mean), so the principle silently fails exactly where priors are hardest to choose.
4. **Conflict with conditionalization.** When constraints arrive as evidence, maxent updating can diverge from ordinary Bayesian conditionalization, so a maxent agent must either abandon one of its two update rules or live with inconsistency.
5. **Equivocation objection.** Maxent conflates lacking evidence of asymmetry with possessing evidence of symmetry; mere ignorance arguably licenses no distribution at all rather than the uniform one.

Please state one further objection that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or conjunction of items 1–5. Present it precisely, in its strongest form, and then: (a) explain how it relates to the listed objections -- which it is closest to and how it differs; (b) assess whether it survives the obvious replies a defender of objective Bayesianism would make; (c) say whether you think it ultimately succeeds, and why.
