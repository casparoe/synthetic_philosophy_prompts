I'm putting together a "where do you actually stand on epistemic closure?" decision tree for a grad reading group that's about to hit the Nozick chapter. Act like an epistemologist who's built a few of these sorting instruments before, and think carefully -- sketch the logical space of possible answers first, then place the actual positions in it, rather than starting from the names.

The tree should start from the commitment "I know I have hands" and lead to named leaves: Nozick-style sensitivity/tracking denial of closure, Dretske's relevant-alternatives picture, Lewis-style contextualism, a straight Moorean, a safety theorist who keeps closure intact (Sosa/Williamson flavor), and the skeptic who just bites the bullet and says you don't know you have hands. Each branch node should be a question about a concrete case -- the zebra vs. the cleverly painted mule, fake barn country, the lottery, the brain in a vat -- phrased so someone can answer honestly without knowing which position it funnels them toward. At least one node needs to probe the reaction the whole debate turns on: whether people find the combination "I know I have hands, but I don't know I'm not a handless brain in a vat" intolerable or perfectly fine (DeRose called these abominable conjunctions; don't use the term in the quiz itself).

Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate -- I suspect Dretske and the contextualist might collapse into each other on case verdicts even though they disagree about what's going on -- and note any unoccupied combination of answers with a one-line diagnosis: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored?

End with a short self-critical note: where is the tree's logic most likely to mislead, and what argument or consideration would make you restructure it -- e.g., what would change if someone convinced you that closure failure for knowledge doesn't line up with closure failure for justification?
