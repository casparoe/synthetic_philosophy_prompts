I am a PhD student in philosophy (my thesis concerns psychological explanations of religious belief), and I work as a teaching assistant on a second-year undergraduate module on Freud and the critique of religion. I am currently marking the midterm essays and would like an independent assessment of one script before I finalise my own mark for it. Our rubric weights accuracy of exposition at 40 per cent, completeness relative to the question at 30 per cent, and engagement with the set passage at 30 per cent. The essay question was as follows:

The following passage is from the Author's Preface to Sigmund Freud's Totem and Taboo, in A. A. Brill's authorised English translation: "In this book the attempt is ventured to find the original meaning of totemism through its infantile traces, that is, through the indications in which it reappears in the development of our own children. The close connection between totem and taboo indicates the further paths to the hypothesis maintained here. And although this hypothesis leads to somewhat improbable conclusions, there is no reason for rejecting the possibility that it comes more or less near to the reality which is so hard to reconstruct." (a) Explain what Freud means by seeking the "original meaning" of totemism "through its infantile traces", and show how this method is carried out in the book's final chapter. (b) Reconstruct, step by step, the chain of events and psychological mechanisms by which Freud derives the origins of religion and morality from the primal horde. (c) In the light of the passage, how does Freud himself characterise the epistemic standing of his hypothesis?

The student's answer, in full, was this:

Freud's guiding idea is that what has vanished from the mental life of civilised adults may still be visible where development is incomplete: in so-called primitive peoples and in our own children. The infantile traces of totemism are the points at which totemistic thinking reappears in the child, above all in the child's ambivalent attitude towards the father; the final chapter therefore reconstructs the prehistory of the species by analogy with the prehistory of the individual.

The reconstruction runs as follows. Following Darwin's conjecture, the earliest form of human society was a horde dominated by a violent, jealous father who kept the females for himself and drove away his sons as they matured. The expelled brothers banded together, and it was Atkinson, in Primal Law, who supplied the decisive scene: one day the sons joined forces and killed the father. The sacramental interpretation of the totem feast — the clan consuming the totem animal as a father-surrogate and thereby renewing its identification with him — Freud likewise owes to Atkinson's account. But the brothers had not only hated their father; they had also loved and admired him. Once the deed was done, the tender impulses resurfaced as remorse, and the dead father became stronger than the living one had been. From the ensuing guilt sprang the two fundamental taboos: the totem must not be killed, and no woman of the same clan may be taken — the two crimes of Oedipus writ large. Religion, morality and social organisation are thus successive monuments to a single act of parricide, and the Oedipus complex of the modern child is its inherited echo.

Freud is candid that all this is speculative. In the preface itself he concedes the "improbable conclusions" and presents the whole story as a consciously constructed fiction: we are not asked to believe that the parricide ever literally happened, only that treating it as if it had brings welcome order to the ethnological facts.
