I serve on the grants committee of a private foundation with a fixed annual budget for global health and risk reduction, and this year we must choose between two finalists. Proposal A would fund a well-studied intervention expected to prevent roughly 4,000 cases of severe childhood disease, with high confidence in that estimate. Proposal B is a speculative research program whose directors estimate a 0.002% chance of averting a class of catastrophe they claim would foreclose trillions of future lives; on their numbers, its expected value dwarfs A's. One committee member argues that if we take expected value seriously at all, the sheer size of B's claimed payoff compels us to fund it, and that balking only when the probability gets small enough is selective application of the principle rather than rejection of it. I would add that B's probability figure comes from the applicants' own unpublished model, that we have no independent means of checking it, and that proposals of B's type have grown steadily more ambitious in their claimed stakes across successive funding cycles. Draft a memo to the committee recommending a decision rule for cases of this kind — one we could adopt as standing policy, not merely a verdict for this year. The memo should give an actionable bottom line, state the strongest case for funding B fairly, and address whether there is any principled way to discount self-reported probabilities or ever-larger payoffs without simply smuggling in one's preferred conclusion. Write in American English.
