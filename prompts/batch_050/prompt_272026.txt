I recently moved from a machine learning research role into a position where I oversee pricing for a small insurer's newer product lines — cyber liability and climate-exposed property — alongside its mature auto book. For the mature lines we have decades of loss data; for the new ones we have scattered industry reports, expert judgment, and my own unease. I have noticed that I consistently demand a larger expected-profit margin before writing policies in the new lines than any reasonable confidence interval around my loss estimates would justify, and I am trying to figure out whether this is disciplined epistemics or a bias I should train out of myself.

A colleague told me this is exactly what the Ellsberg paradox is about. I know the bare shape of it (two urns, one with a known composition, one with an unknown composition, and betting preferences that no single probability assignment can rationalize), but the literature around it — ambiguity aversion, maxmin expected utility, smooth ambiguity models — is written in a vocabulary I do not natively speak.

Please do the following. First, restate the Ellsberg paradox and the main theoretical responses to it in the vocabulary I am fluent in: training data versus deployment distribution, prior strength, parameter uncertainty, distribution shift, distributionally robust optimization, minimax decision rules, and regularization. Be precise about where the urn setup corresponds to a well-defined statistical object in my world and where the correspondence is only loose. Second, give me a residue report: which features of the paradox translate cleanly (for example, whether a minimax rule over a family of candidate loss distributions captures the Ellsberg pattern or only imitates it), which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in statistical practice at all — I suspect the violation of Savage's sure-thing principle, understood as a fact about preferences rather than about estimation, may be one of these, but tell me if I am wrong. Third, apply the translation to my actual situation: state what the ambiguity-aversion literature, restated in my vocabulary, would say about my pricing margins in the new lines, and identify which parts of my unease survive translation as defensible caution and which parts the translation exposes as something stranger — a preference that no amount of additional modeling discipline would dissolve. Where the residue resists translation, say plainly whether you take that to be a defect of the statistical vocabulary or a sign that the original philosophical claim was doing less work than it appeared to.
