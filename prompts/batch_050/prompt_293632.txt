Quick bit of context for where I'm coming from: I've been reading around the Libet literature for a reading group I'm in, and after a few months of it I'm genuinely confused about what the disagreement even is anymore. The setup everyone knows: in Libet's 80s experiments people flex a wrist whenever they feel like it, report when they first felt the urge using a clock, and the readiness potential in the brain shows up a few hundred milliseconds before that reported time. Then Soon and colleagues in 2008 used fMRI to guess which button someone would press, at better than chance, several seconds before the person reported deciding. On the other side, Libet himself thought the last couple hundred milliseconds left room for a conscious veto, and more recently Schurger and coauthors have argued the readiness potential isn't really a decision signal at all -- just noisy spontaneous fluctuations that happen to cross a threshold, which looks like a ramp only because you're averaging backwards from the movement. Forty years on, both camps are still publishing, and as far as I can tell nobody has ever been convinced out of their position by any of this.

My working hypothesis, which you should feel free to push back on: the neuroscience part of the debate is either settled or at least settleable, and what's actually doing the work is a disguised conceptual disagreement about what a "decision" is supposed to be. The debunkers treat the whole action episode, ramp-up included, as the relevant unit; the defenders only care about the final consious moment. Same timing data, different verdicts.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this (a) a genuinely empirical disagreement about what the readiness potential measures, which better experiments could close, (b) a conceptual disagreement about what counts as "my decision" as opposed to "brain preparation," or (c) two groups answering entirely different questions -- one about the causal origins of action, one about what agency feels like from the inside -- under the single banner of "free will"? Base it on what the sides actually argue in the technical papers, not the pop-science versions. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: an experiment, a formal result, a distinction? Or is the honest answer that nothing could end it, and if so why?

One thing I especially want addressed, because it seems to be the crux nobody talks about cleanly: suppose the Schurger-style critique is completely right and the readiness potential is an averaging artifact. Does that dissolve the whole problem, or just relocate it? Because a threshold crossing at time T still happens before the reported urge at T+200ms either way. If the "decision" is just noise crossing a line, is that better news for free will or worse?
