I teach a first-year course on legal method, and I keep catching myself giving inconsistent-sounding answers about when a court should follow the words of a statute and when it should follow its evident point. I would like to know whether my instincts add up to anything, so I am going to report my gut verdicts on six stylised cases and ask you to find the principle behind them, if there is one. Please write in British English.

As a frame, take Blackstone's account in the Introduction to his *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (the section "Of the Nature of Laws in General"). He proposes, in effect, an ordered series of signs for discovering the legislator's will: begin with the words in their ordinary sense; if they are ambiguous, turn to the context, the subject-matter, and the effects and consequences of competing readings; and only where the literal sense is absurd or empty, resort to the reason and spirit of the law — the mischief that moved the legislator to enact it. I want to know whether my verdicts follow his ordering or quietly violate it.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

(a) A municipal bylaw provides that no vehicles shall be permitted in the public gardens. An ambulance drives in to reach a collapsed jogger. My verdict: no violation. The words cover it; the point does not.

(b) A criminal statute punishes whoever carries a firearm during and in relation to a drug trafficking offence. The defendant kept an unloaded pistol locked in the glovebox of his car throughout; the drugs were weighed and sold from the boot. My verdict: guilty. "Carries" does not require that the gun be accessible or loaded, whatever the provision was aimed at.

(c) A nineteenth-century statute penalises anyone who assists the immigration of a foreigner under contract to perform "labour or service of any kind". A congregation pays the passage of a clergyman engaged to minister to them. My verdict: no violation. The mischief was imported manual labour, and "service" should shrink to fit it.

(d) A statute requires that factory chimney emissions be tested annually. The regulator tests a factory twice in one calendar year and not at all in the next. My verdict: the statute was breached. "Annually" means once in every period of twelve months; administrative tidiness is not an answer.

(e) A statute sets the penalty for polluting a watercourse at "a fine not exceeding £50". Every neighbouring section, the bill as introduced, and the parliamentary record indicate £5,000, and at £50 the provision is a dead letter because compliance costs far more than the fine. My verdict: read it as £5,000. This is correction of an obvious slip, not legislation.

(f) A statute grants tax relief to registered charitable organisations. A genuinely admirable mutual-aid society, which does everything a charity does but has never registered, claims the relief. My verdict: no relief. I will not stretch the words even for a deserving claimant.

I can already see the tensions: in (b) I hold the text to a defendant's detriment while in (c) I shrink the text in a defendant's favour; in (e) I rewrite the words while in (f) I refuse to extend them by a hair. My questions:

1. Is there a single principle, stated precisely, that delivers all six verdicts? If not, give me the two best candidate principles and say which clauses of each are motivated by which cases.
2. Whichever principle you favour, which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon? I would rather be told to drop a verdict than be offered a principle gerrymandered to save all six.
3. Is the surviving principle better described as textualist, purposivist, or as evidence that this dichotomy is doing less work than the jurisprudential literature assumes? Relate your answer to Blackstone's ordering: does my pattern treat the reason and spirit of the law as a last resort for absurdity, as he does, or as a standing invitation?
4. Test the principle on a case I have not listed. A bylaw forbids "amplified music in the market square after 11 p.m.". At half past eleven a busker performs with no electrical equipment but is joined by a full brass band, producing more noise than most amplifiers. What does your principle say, and what does Blackstone's method say, and do they agree?
