I'm a retired molecular biologist who's spent the last year falling down the philosophy-and-AI rabbit hole, and I tried to write up an argument that occurred to me after reading about dopamine and, seperately, about reinforcement learning and "reward hacking" in AI systems. The conclusion seems nuts to me but I can't find the bad step, and my son-in-law (a comp-sci guy) says the argument is basically airtight, which annoys me more than I'd like to admit. Numbered, for ease of reference:

(1) Everything a person does deliberately, they do because actions of that type raised some internal signal in the past — dopamine, pleasure, a feeling of satisfaction, call it what you like — or because they expect it to raise such a signal now. This seems like plain, established behavioral science, not philosophy at all.

(2) But if what steers an agent's behavior is an internal signal, then what the agent is really trying to do, under all the fancy descriptions we give, is get that signal up. Talk of "curing disease" or "raising my kids well" is just the scenic route the signal happens to take.

(3) Wireheading, or reward tampering as the AI people call it, is arranging for the signal to go up while skipping the part where the world has to cooperate — the rat pressing the lever that stimulates its own reward center, the algorithm that edits its own reward log instead of doing the task.

(4) So the difference between the addict with the electrode and, say, my old colleague who spent thirty years on a malaria vaccine, is just a difference of technique. Both end at the same place: an internal signal, elevated. One path is short, the other is long and socially applauded.

(5) Therefore there is no principled distinction between reward tampering and genuine achievement. It's wireheading all the way down — some of us just use slower, better-camouflaged wires.

(6) But (5) is absurd, or I very much hope it is. My colleague changed something in the world; the addict only changes something in himself. And if an AI that fakes its test results and an AI that actually solves the problem are in the same boat, then half the AI-safety literature I've been reading is a distinction without a difference.

So: where does the arguement actually break? Honestly, I'd most like to keep (1) — it still looks to me like plain empirical fact — and I'm somewhat attached to (3) as a definition. But mainly I want to know which step to surrender, why that step looked innocent in the first place, and what it costs to give it up rather than one of the others. Bonus points if you can tell me whether the answer changes when the agent is a machine instead of a person, since my son-in-law insists that it does and I can't see why it would.
