Here's the question I set:

Spinoza argues in Part I of the Ethics that nothing in nature is contingent and that things could have been produced by God in no other way or order (1p29, 1p33). Explain what this necessitarianism amounts to. Then explain how, given it, Spinoza nonetheless makes room for human freedom in Parts IV and V. Finally, what is the intellectual love of God, and in what sense, if any, is it reciprocal?

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Spinoza's necessitarianism is the view, defended across Part I, that everything that exists follows from the divine nature with absolute necessity. Since God is the only substance and everything else is a mode of his attributes, the whole order of nature unfolds from God's essence the way the properties of a triangle follow from its definition. So there are no might-have-beens: contingency is just our ignorance of causes. Importantly, Spinoza is not saying God is constrained by anything external. God acts from the necessity of his own nature alone, and that is what freedom means for Spinoza: a thing is free when it exists and acts from the necessity of its own nature rather than being determined by something else. So God surveys the possible orders of nature and is bound to actualize the most perfect one, which means even God lacks free will in the libertarian sense.

Human freedom has to be understood on the same model. We are modes, so we cannot be free in the sense of being undetermined. The belief in free will comes from our being conscious of our desires but ignorant of the causes that determine them. Freedom for us is a matter of degree: the more we act from adequate ideas, i.e. from reason, the more we are the adequate cause of our own actions, and the less we are passive to the affects. Bondage is passivity; freedom is activity through understanding.

This culminates in Part V's doctrine of the intellectual love of God. Through reason, the second kind of knowledge, we come to understand things under a species of necessity and form an adequate idea of God as the cause of all things, and this knowledge is accompanied by a joy together with the idea of God as its cause, which is just what love is for Spinoza. Because this love arises from rational knowledge, which is common to all minds and eternal, the intellectual love of God is itself eternal and is our highest blessedness.

Is it reciprocal? In a qualified sense, yes. Spinoza says the mind's intellectual love of God is part of the infinite love with which God loves himself (5p36), so our love of God is literally God loving himself through us. God does not love us back the way a person would, but insofar as our love is a mode of his own self-love, the love runs in both directions."
