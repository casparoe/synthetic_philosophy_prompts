I'm a software engineer serving as the lay member on my regional hospital's organ donation ethics committee, with no formal training in philosophy or medicine. Next month we vote on a protocol for donation after circulatory death: once a family has consented and the decision is made to withdraw life support, the team waits five minutes after the patient's circulation stops and then begins organ recovery. One committee member wants a much longer waiting period, citing rare reported cases where a heart has restarted on its own, and argues that five minutes shows only that circulation has ceased, not that it will stay ceased. Others on the committee reply that the relevant standard is not that circulation could not be restarted by any intervention — cardiopulmonary resuscitation can sometimes restore a pulse well past five minutes — but that it will not restart on its own and that, given the family's decision, no one is going to restart it. Write a memo to the committee recommending whether we should approve the five-minute rule, lengthen the standoff period, or restate the standard itself, and address whether this circulatory criterion and the neurological criterion we already use for brain death can coherently pick out the same event, the patient's death. Give the strongest version of the position you reject, and state explicitly what evidence or argument would change your recommendation and where in the memo your reasoning is most likely to fail.
