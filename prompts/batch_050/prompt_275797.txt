I'm helping a team draft internal guidelines for when a consumer AI assistant may override, refuse, or redirect a user's request rather than comply with it, and we've noticed that our disagreements about hard cases seem to stem from deeper disagreements we haven't articulated. Write a decision tree that classifies positions on the question: under what conditions, if any, is it permissible for an AI assistant to act against a competent adult user's stated request for the user's own good?

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. Each internal node should be a diagnostic question phrased as a concrete case or claim that someone could answer without knowing which position it leads to -- for instance, whether there is any difference between overriding a user because of a fact the user would accept as relevant if they knew it, and overriding them because of a value they would reject even if fully informed; or whether it matters that the user could have obtained the same outcome from a rival assistant with no restrictions. I want roughly six to nine nodes and at least that many leaves.

The leaves should include, at minimum: a position on which paternalistic override is never permissible for competent users (the assistant is a pure instrument); a position on which override is permissible only when it corrects for the user's ignorance of facts, never for their values; a position on which override is permissible for the user's values too, but only when the user would endorse the intervention upon some specified idealized reflection; a position on which the assistant's principals (its developer's policies, society's laws) rather than the user's good are the only legitimate grounds for refusal, making the whole question malformed; and a position on which the permissibility of override scales continuously with reversibility and stakes rather than switching on any categorical threshold. Locate these in the space your questions carve out; if your questions can't separate two of them, say so explicitly rather than forcing a distinction.

For any combination of answers that yields an unoccupied leaf, note it and diagnose briefly: is the position empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it simply hasn't been defended?

Two cases must appear somewhere in the tree as diagnostic tests: a user who asks the assistant to help them spend their retirement savings on an investment the assistant has strong reason to believe is a scam, and a user who asks the assistant to stop reminding them to take a medication they have told the assistant they intend to quit against their doctor's advice. Positions should have to take a stand on these, and the stands should fall out of the answers rather than being assumed.

Close by stating how confident you are that the tree captures the real axes of disagreement rather than superficial ones, and single out the one node or leaf placement you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would change your mind.
