I'm a software engineer who helps moderate a large history discussion forum, and I've been drafting posting guidelines — eventually I want to partly automate this — for separating fringe-but-legitimate amateur history from pseudohistory. My first pass was the obvious criterion I picked up from pop philosophy of science: a claim counts as scientific if it is falsifiable, so a theory of history that sticks its neck out with risky, checkable predictions gets a pass, and only explain-anything theories get flagged. When I tested this against the content we actually deal with — ancient-astronaut claims, Atlantis literalism, Fomenko-style new chronology — it sorted badly. Several of the worst offenders happily make concrete, checkable claims, while some honest amateur speculation is nearly unfalsifiable. A friend who studied philosophy told me the criterion I was using is a crude version of Popper's and that the demarcation problem is messier than I had assumed, but she didn't tell me how, so I'm asking here.

Construct a thought experiment to help me see the structure: invent a fictional fringe theory of history — not a real one, please — engineered so that the crude falsifiability criterion delivers the wrong verdict: the theory passes as "scientific" by that criterion, yet is unmistakably pseudohistory. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic — the theory is promoted through self-published books and a lecture circuit by, say, a retired engineer, with no time machines, no actually-functioning alien artifacts, no magical evidence of any kind. The theory must be genuinely falsifiable in form: at least one concrete, risky prediction about evidence (inscriptions, radiocarbon dates, coin hoards, whatever you like) that could simply come out false, and this must not be a trick of wording or a prediction rigged to be untestable in practice. The whole thing — the theory plus a sketch of its community of defenders — should fit in one paragraph.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping. State what the crude falsifiability criterion says about the theory and why. Then state what a better criterion says and why — I'd like you to articulate that better criterion yourself rather than have me hand it to you, though I suspect it will turn on how the theory's defenders respond to failed predictions, or on some asymmetry in the standards of evidence they apply to mainstream versus fringe claims. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed — does the case collapse back into honest error? Finally, give the best counterattack available to a defender of falsifiability-as-demarcation; I can imagine one line being that the criterion was always meant to classify statements, not the conduct of the people who hold them, and I'd like to know whether that rescue succeeds. One last thing: I will have to defend these guidelines to users who read the same pop philosophy I did, so please separate what is genuinely settled in this area from what remains contested among people who work on it, and don't present any single demarcation criterion as the consensus view if there isn't one.
