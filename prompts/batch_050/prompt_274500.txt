I want a second opinion on an argument I cooked up, because the conclusion feels nuts but I cant find the step I'd actually be willing to give up. Background: I got into it with my brother in law about whether its ever OK to "trade" on moral questions. In my friend group there's this real thing where two of us disagree about politics, and instead of both voting and cancelling each other out, we agreed neither of us votes and we each put that time into a charity we both respect. Both of us honestly think the world is better off than if we'd both voted. Seems obviously fine to me.

So here's the argument:

(1) If a deal leaves everyone involved better off than the no-deal alternative by their own moral lights — meaning each person sincerely judges the outcome better than what would have happened otherwise — then the deal is at least permissible.
(2) Honest moral compromise between people who disagree is a deal of exactly that kind. (Hence the voting pact being fine.)
(3) Nothing in (1) cares whose values are doing the judging. Better off "by their own lights" is defined by whatever the other person's values happen to be.
(4) So a deal with someone whose values I think are monstrous is permissible whenever it passes the (1) test. Say I help some movement I hate get a small win, in exchange for them abandoning a much bigger win they were otherwise going to get. By their lights and by mine, the deal beats the alternative.
(5) And whether the "no-deal alternative" is something I'd be creating shouldn't matter either. If I threaten to do something bad unless I'm paid (in concessions, donations to my causes, whatever) to not do it, that's just proposing a deal whose alternative is me doing the bad thing. Accepting leaves everyone better off by their own lights, so moral extortion is fine provided the threat is credible.

(4) and (5) seem crazy. Helping a hateful cause get anything, even inside a trade, feels like becoming complicit in it, and the extortion one is obviously a racket. But I can't see which numbered step does the damage. If I have to pick what to protect, I fight hardest for (1) and (2) — I really do think the voting pact is fine, and that this kind of compromise is genuinely good and not just a sad necessity.

So: where does it go wrong? Name the step, tell me why it looked innocent, and what it costs me to reject it — especially whether fixing (5) forces me to give up anything I care about in (2). And one meta request: say what would change your mind about your diagnosis. Is there some deal structure or counterexample that would flip your verdict on which step is the culprit? I want to know where your answer is most likely to be wrong, not just where mine is.
