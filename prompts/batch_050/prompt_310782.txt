So I'm a retired chemist who's been reading philosophy of maths in my spare time, and last week two friends of mine (both engineers, which may explain it) got into an argument over coffee that I couldn't settle. One of them, Dave, said that whether 2 is an element of 4 is just a math question with a math answer: in the standard set-theoretic construction of the naturals, 2 is {0,1} and 4 is {0,1,2,3}, so 2 ∈ 4, end of story, and anyone who says otherwise is being mystical. His friend Alan said that's clearly wrong: nobody before the set theorists thought numbers had elements at all, Caesar isn't an element of anything, and whatever the set construction is doing it's picking one representation among many (he mentioned Zermelo had a different one where it comes out false), so the 'fact' Dave cites is an artifact of the encoding, not a fact about numbers. Dave came back with: fine, but you still need *some* objects to do arithmetic with, and if the truths about numbers don't care which objects you use, then the structural facts are all there are -- so 'is 2 in 4' is meaningless, not false, and Alan shouldn't say it's not a fact about numbers but that there was never a question there to begin with. Alan said that position makes hash of ordinary mathematical practice, since mathematicians happily identify the natural 2 with the real 2 and the complex 2 without anyone fussing about which sets they are.

Please adjudicate the moves, who's right about what. And since I'm new to this literature and liable to be snowed, please also tell me what would change your verdict -- which consideration, if it turned out to hold, would flip you to the other side, and where your reasoning is most likley to go wrong.
