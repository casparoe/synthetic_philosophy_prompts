I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. Most of my work involves building systems that make decisions under uncertainty -- demand forecasting, capacity planning, some reliability tooling -- and about two years ago I stopped modeling uncertainty with single probability distributions and switched to what's usually called imprecise probabilities. Instead of committing to one prior, you represent your state of belief as a set of distributions, let an event's probability be an interval rather than a point, and make decisions with rules like interval dominance or maximin over the set rather than maximizing a single expected value. My reasons were roughly the standard ones: a single number smuggles in information I don't have; people seem to rationally distinguish situations where odds are known from situations where they're merely guessed, in a way one distribution can't express; and in safety-relevant contexts, "I don't know, so call it 50%" strikes me as strictly worse than an explicit statement of how much is unknown.

I've now built enough on this view that I'd like it stress-tested. Not the mathematics -- the thesis: that sets of probabilities are the right representation of rational belief under severe uncertainty, and that single-distribution Bayesianism is at best a special case that only applies when you have a lot of symmetric information. Tell me what would count against that thesis.

Concretely, I'd like the following.

First, a map of the defeaters. Which arguments, formal results, or observations -- if they held up -- would show that sets of probabilities are a mistake, or at least no better than one distribution plus auxiliary machinery? I'm aware of some of the usual pressure points: cases where conditioning on new information strictly widens your interval even when that information was guaranteed to arrive; problems in sequential decision-making where an imprecise agent can apparently be walked into sure losses or into refusing free information unless they bolt on extra rules; and the complaint that aversion to ambiguity is a preference about bets rather than a fact about belief, and therefore belongs in the utility function rather than the epistemology. Tell me which of these actually land, which are merely superficial, and what's missing from my list.

Second, for each defeater: what would the world, or the mathematics, have to be like for it to succeed? And separate what a committed defender of imprecise probabilities would concede counts against the view from what only its critics treat as counting against it.

Third, what does the view predict that precise Bayesianism doesn't, and vice versa? Is there any decision-theoretic or observable difference that would let me tell which is right, or do the two become empirically equivalent once you hand the Bayesian a rich enough space of hypotheses?

Fourth, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Every time a problem case shows up, the literature seems to respond with another rule -- an updating convention, a tie-breaking decision criterion. At what point does that stop being refinement and start being insulation?

For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is not a clever counterexample but a demonstration about practice: that in sequential decisions of the kind my systems actually make, the imprecise approach either collapses into the precise one (with the intervals doing no real work) or produces systematically worse outcomes. And if, on inspection, nothing could in principle move me, say so plainly -- that would itself be an answer worth having.
