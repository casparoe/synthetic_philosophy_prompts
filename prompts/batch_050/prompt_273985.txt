I retired three years ago after a career in microbiology and have lately been reading philosophy of history. I keep encountering a tension: some writers treat pathogens, climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions as genuine historical actors, while critics object that this slides into environmental determinism and erases human agency. To force myself to be precise, I devised two scores for any candidate non-human cause: (1) counterfactual weight — how differently the following century plausibly unfolds if the event does not occur, holding human decisions roughly fixed; and (2) human mediation — how far the event's impact runs through institutions, prior vulnerabilities, and choices rather than through the physical shock itself. Please rate each of the following on both scores from 0 to 1, with brief justification: the arrival of the Black Death in Europe (1347–1352), the eruption of Mount Tambora (1815), the introduction of smallpox to the Americas after 1492, and the Little Ice Age (roughly 1300–1850). Then say whether any of them merits the label of historical actor in a sense stronger than metaphor, and identify where, within your own scoring, the threat of determinism actually arises rather than merely being alleged. Throughout, distinguish clearly between what historians and climate or disease specialists broadly agree upon and what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
