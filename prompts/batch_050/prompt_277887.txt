I TA an upper-level metaethics seminar, about 14 students, one 90-minute session. Design an activity that makes them *feel* the Frege-Geach problem for expressivism instead of just nodding at it on the page. Here's the rough idea I have in mind, build on it or replace it with something better: early on the group agrees to run a stripped-down "normative language" where evaluative utterances are pure expressions of attitude -- literal thumbs up / thumbs down, booing, cheering, that kind of thing, with an explicit rule that these utterances are NOT truth-apt claims. Then somewhere in the middle they should be forced to actually reason in this language: embed their attitude-expressions in conditionals, chain them into arguments, and watch modus ponens either break or smuggle in something they weren't allowed to have. I want them to trip over the inconsistency themselves before anyone names it -- the moment where the same sentence means one thing unembedded and something else inside an if-clause, and their own earlier moves convict them.

Give me: concrete rules and materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, what the facilitator is allowed to say along the way (ideally almost nothing), and a debrief that maps each moment of the breakdown onto the actual moves in the literature (the negation problem, the embedding issue, why just adding more booing doesn't fix it). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- e.g. students quietly treating the thumbs as truth values because it's just easier -- and how to design the rules so that cheating of that sort is visible. And be honest about which part of the problem the game can't reproduce, because I suspect the real bite is about compositional semantics and not about what happens at a table.
