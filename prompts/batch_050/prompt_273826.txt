I'm the in-house stats person at a small company that prices weird one-off risks -- prize indemnity stuff (will someone sink a hole-in-one at this golf tournament), contest insurance, the odd bespoke contract nobody has loss history on. My bosses keep asking me to "just put a prior on it" when we have five or zero relevant observations, and I've been winging it, and last quarter two of us winged it on the same contract and got answers three orders of magnitude apart. So I need to stop winging it.

What I want is a written procedure, maybe a page or two, that any analyst here can follow to turn "here's what we genuinely know about this quantity" into a defensible prior distribution. I keep hearing that objective Bayesianism / maximum entropy is supposed to be the principled answer here -- pick the flattest distribution consistent with what you know -- but every time I try to actually use it I trip over stuff: the answer seems to depend on how I parametrize the problem, flat-over-the-thing isn't the same as flat-over-the-log-of-the-thing, and sometimes the "ignorant" prior spits out predictions that seem insane to me.

So: write me the procedure. Specifically:

- The questions the analyst answers in order (Is the quantity bounded? Positive only? Do we know a mean, a range, a symmetry? Do we know literally nothing but the scale?) and which prior each combination of answers points to.
- What to do about the parametrization problem. If the procedure just says "use maximum entropy," it needs to say on which variable, and what the rule is for choosing, because otherwise it's a coin flip dressed up as a principle. If the honest answer is that there is no rule and this is a judgment call, say that, but tell me how to at least bound how much the choice matters.
- Where Jeffreys-style invariance priors fit in, if they do -- do they belong in the procedure or do they conflict with the maxent step, and if they conflict, which do I use when?
- A step where the analyst has to stress-test the output: what sanity checks to run on the prior before it goes into the pricing model (implied probabilities of extreme events, behavior at the boundary, etc.), and what to do when the "objective" prior fails the sanity check. I suspect the honest move here is to admit the procedure has failed and fall back to eliciting an actual judgment from the underwriter, but I want that written into the procedure, not done ad hoc.
- Mark clearly which steps are genuine mechanical rules and which are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. Part of the point is being able to show an auditor which is which.

Then run the procedure on three of my cases:

1. The probability that a first-time entrant wins an esports tournament we know nothing about, where the contract pays out on a win. (This is the one I dread -- I suspect the procedure gives an answer nobody here will sign, and if so I want to see it give that answer and then say what to do about it.)
2. The expected number of attendees at a new annual festival, where all we know is the venue capacity and that the organizer guessed "a few thousand."
3. The failure rate of a component in a novelty vending machine, where the manufacturer gave us a mean-time-between-failures figure and nothing else.

Constraints on your end: the procedure has to be runnable in under an hour by someone with a stats degree but no philosophy background, the output has to be an actual distribution and not a gesture at one, and I'd rather have a procedure that says "give up and ask a human" at the right places than one that pretends to remove all judgment. If the deep problem here is that there just are no fully rational priors and maxent oversells itself, don't sand that down -- build the procedure around the limitation and tell me which steps are where the overselling happens.
