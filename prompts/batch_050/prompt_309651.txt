Next month I'm presenting to a political economy seminar a paper defending a position I'll call auction-based market socialism. My thesis has three parts. First, I concede to Mises that rational allocation of capital requires genuinely discovered prices for producer goods, and I concede to Hayek that most economically relevant knowledge is dispersed, tacit, and cannot be uploaded to a planner regardless of computational power. Second, I argue that both arguments indict central planning but not socialism as such: Lange was wrong to put the state in the business of setting prices, yet his mistake was one of institutional design, not of principle. Third, I propose a specific mechanism I call the capital ladder: consumption-goods markets operate freely; state-owned enterprises are controlled by dozens of legally independent management trusts that bid against one another, in iterative auctions run by state investment banks, for blocks of investment capital; interest rates emerge from these auctions rather than being administered; managers' compensation and continued tenure track realized returns, and chronically underperforming trusts are dissolved. My claim is that this reproduces the epistemic function of the capital market -- real bids, real losses, real exit -- without private ownership of the means of production, and that standard objections from the calculation debate therefore fail to touch it.

The audience: roughly a dozen people. Two are openly Austrian-sympathetic economists who know the Mises-Hayek literature deeply and are suspicious of anything that smells like Lange with better computers. One is an economic historian who cares about how actually existing socialist economies behaved and thinks the whole debate is settled empirically. Two are public choice theorists who will grant my economics for the sake of argument and attack the politics. One is a computational economist sympathetic to planning who may attack me from the left for conceding too much to Hayek. The rest are graduate students.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how damaging they would be if I handle them badly, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it, with the specific audience member likely to raise it. For each, give me what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, flagging where the reply is weakest. Distinguish objections against the economics of the auction mechanism from objections against my framing of the historical debate. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
