I've been reading Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, and the adjacent forums for close to two decades, and the habits stuck: I want incentives named explicitly, the strongest opposing case stated fairly, and a decision rule rather than a mood. I now need those habits applied to a live question. I serve on the elected board of a worker-owned cooperative, a wholesale bakery with about 180 workers. Roughly 110 are worker-members who completed the two-year candidacy and purchased a membership share; they elect the board and set policy through a general assembly. The other 70 are employees — some candidates working toward membership, others seasonal or part-time staff who are not eligible for membership at all. Wages, scheduling rules, the candidacy requirements, and the price of a membership share are all fixed by the assembly, in which employees have no vote.

Three weeks ago the employees, working with an outside organizer, petitioned the board to voluntarily recognize a staff union and begin bargaining. Counsel confirms that both voluntary recognition and insisting on a secret-ballot election are legally available to us, so the question before the board is what we ought to do, not what we must do.

The board is split. One faction considers the matter simple: freedom of association does not lapse because an employer is democratically run, and refusing recognition on the grounds that we are a cooperative would be paternalist union-busting in cooperative clothing — an uncomfortable stance, they note, for an assembly that last year raised the buy-in price and cut weekend pay over employee objections. The other faction replies that a union is countervailing power against capital, and there is no capital here: management answers to an elected board, the people across the bargaining table would be the employees' own future colleagues, and a staff union negotiating against an assembly of members is labor bargaining against labor. Importing an adversarial labor-management frame, they argue, would mainly transfer power to whichever labor faction organizes first while corroding the cooperative model; they would rather shorten the candidacy and extend a limited assembly vote to long-serving employees.

Draft the memo I will circulate to the full board ahead of next week's special meeting. It should recommend exactly one course of action — voluntarily recognize the union, insist on a secret-ballot election, or decline recognition paired with a specified alternative — and commit to it rather than presenting a menu. The reasoning should take a position on what the right to organize is grounded in: an intrinsic associative freedom owed regardless of who the employer is, an instrument for correcting bargaining-power asymmetries whose application here would depend on whether a worker-run assembly actually leaves employees powerless, or something else — and it should apply that grounding to our facts, including the inconvenient ones. Whichever option it recommends, it should give the strongest version of the case for the alternative it rejects and state what change in our arrangements or evidence would make that rejected option the right one. No more than two pages, addressed to the board, in a formal register suitable for circulation and attachment to the minutes.
