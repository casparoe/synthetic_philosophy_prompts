In my undergraduate philosophy of language seminar we've just finished the sections of Wittgenstein's later work on games, and I want to design an exercise in which students test the family-resemblance claim against a wider range of concepts than he discusses. My plan is to hand out a list of everyday and semi-technical terms and ask, for each, whether it admits a classical definition — a set of individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions covering all and only its instances — or whether it instead works the way he says 'game' does: overlapping strands of similarity, no single thread running through all cases, and edge cases handled by extending the concept rather than by consulting an essence that was there all along.

The list I intend to use is: game, number, art, sport, language, furniture, religion, planet, knowledge, lie, bird, sandwich, democracy, disease, justice. Before I distribute it, I would like your own verdicts as a sanity check — if it turns out that almost all fifteen are family-resemblance concepts, or almost none are, the exercise will not teach much. Please rank all fifteen terms from most to least amenable to a classical definition, and for each give (a) the best one-sentence classical definition you can construct, (b) the case that breaks it, or the reason you think no counterexample is forthcoming, and (c) a score from 0 to 10, where 0 means a pure family-resemblance concept with no common thread and 10 means cleanly definable, with edge cases fully settled by the definition itself. One ground rule: trivially disjunctive definitions are not allowed — you may not define 'sandwich' as 'anything on this list of approved sandwich types' — and if you think this restriction cannot be made precise without circularity, say so, since that would itself be relevant to Wittgenstein's point.

Present the results as a table (or a JSON array of objects) with the columns/fields rank, term, best_definition, breaking_case_or_reason, and score. After the table, add one short paragraph addressing two things: first, whether your ranking exhibits any pattern — for instance, whether natural-kind terms, artifact terms, and institutional or social terms cluster in different regions of the list — and second, whether 'number' deserves the position you gave it, given that mathematicians do rigorously define various number systems while arguably leaving the ordinary concept of number as it is used outside mathematics undefined. If you believe the whole exercise rests on a false presupposition — for example, that classical definition and family resemblance are not exhaustive alternatives — use that paragraph to say what the third option is and which terms on the list belong to it.
