I sit on the grants committee of a small family foundation, and one of my fellow trustees, a moral philosopher by training, holds views about weighing benefits that occur at different times which are consistent but, to me, elusive. Over several funding cycles I have quietly kept track of her verdicts. Offered a treatment that prevents a severe migraine today or an identical treatment guaranteed to be effective in twenty-five years, she is indifferent. Presented with two disaster-prevention projects, one averting a certain catastrophe next year and the other averting an equally severe catastrophe in two hundred years, where we stipulated the future disaster as fully certain, she is again indifferent, remarking that a catastrophe's date does not change its badness. Asked to value a project whose benefits in sixty years depend on the survival of a lunar settlement she judges fifty percent likely to persist, she values it at exactly half the equivalent certain project, discounting precisely by the probability that the beneficiaries will exist. Yet when a proposal would deliver benefits a century hence to people projected to be several times wealthier than we are, she would pay strictly less per unit of benefit than for the same benefit delivered to people today, justifying this by saying it seems perverse to ask comparatively poor present-day donors to sacrifice for people who will be far better off than they are.

This month we must choose between two proposals of equal cost, and we can fund only one. Project A would, with certainty, prevent a specified severe harm for people alive today. Project B would, with certainty, prevent an identical harm one hundred and twenty years from now, for a population whose existence is not in doubt and which is projected to be roughly twice as wealthy in material terms but markedly worse off in overall quality of life, owing to a predicted widespread chronic illness. Please predict her verdict between A and B, and give your reasons.

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the structure underneath it. Please identify at least two distinct decision rules that are each consistent with all four of her earlier verdicts, state what each rule predicts about the new case, and say which rule you take her to be running and why. Also tell me which well-known position on discounting her track record rules out entirely, which of the four earlier verdicts does the most discriminating work, and what further hypothetical case you would put to her to settle whatever ambiguity remains after Project A and Project B are decided.
