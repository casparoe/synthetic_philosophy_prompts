I'm putting together the final exam for my third-year applied ethics course and I want one essay question on the moral status of video game characters that I haven't been able to write well myself. We've done the standard moral patiency units (animals, future generations, marginally conscious humans) and the students all know the two stock answers to this topic: "NPCs aren't conscious so nothing you do to them matters," and the virtue-ethics line that torturing NPCs is bad because of what it does to the player's character. Both are fine as far as they go, but I want a question that can't be answered by picking one of those and running with it. So I'd like the question to force students to actually work out what has to be true for an NPC to matter *intrinsically*, and to grapple with a complication I'll seed: a game whose NPCs run on a deliberately crude pain-behavior algorithm versus a hypothetical game where the devs use a large learned model to drive NPC distress responses and nobody, including the devs, can rule out that something morally relevant is going on inside. The question should push them to distinguish (a) the instrumental/player-corruption answer, (b) the representational answer (the NPC depicts a person, so simulated cruelty wrongs someone the way an effigy might, or doesn't), and (c) the intrinsic answer that depends on contested theories of consciousness, and to say which theory of consciousness would have to be true for (c) to get off the ground and what our epistemic position toward it actually is. Extra credit territory: does it matter whether another real player is watching or controlling the character.

Please write the actual exam question as it would appear on the paper, aimed at a 45-minute answer of roughly 600-800 words, then a model answer of about 600 words, then a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with the marks for each, and for each point a one-line note on the typical way students fumble it (e.g. treating "NPCs aren't conscious" as an established fact rather than a claim hostage to a theory of consciousness nobody has). The marking scheme should make clear what separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of specific moves made or missed, not just "depth."

One more thing: in the model answer, have it say explicitly how confident it is in its overall verdict and flag the single point it's least sure of -- I want to model intellectual humility for the students, and honestly I'm not sure myself whether the epistemic-uncertainty-about-consciousness move is as strong as it sounds, so I'd rather the answer not pretend otherwise.
