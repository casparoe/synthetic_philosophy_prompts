So my roommate and I cooked up this argument for our moral psych reading group and it's been bugging me because it seems to prove way too much. Here it is:

(1) A person has a character trait only if that trait reliably predicts and explains their behavior across a wide range of very different situations -- that's just what a trait is, something stable about the person you can count on.
(2) But the experiments say otherwise. Whether someone stops to help a stranger depends more on whether they're in a hurry or just found a bit of spare change than on anything about their personality. Subjects in the famous obedience studies cranked the shocks up to the max just because a guy in a lab coat kept calmly telling them to continue. Even people who score high on honesty tests cheat in one setup and not the next.
(3) So behavior is predicted by situations, not by traits.
(4) So there are no character traits. Honesty, compassion, courage -- none of these exist as real stable features of people.
(5) But wait, the same form of argument generalizes to everything. A glass is fragile only if dropping it reliably leads to it breaking across a wide range of situations -- yet whether it breaks depends on the height, the surface, the temperature, all situational factors. So by parallel reasoning, fragility doesn't exist either, and neither does any disposition of anything, anywhere.
(6) Worse, the argument undermines itself: the one stable cross-situational pattern it attributes to absolutely everyone is "being the kind of creature whose behavior tracks situational factors" -- which sure looks like a trait of the very kind it says don't exist.
(7) So something has gone badly wrong, because the conclusion of (5) is absurd.

I'm pretty confident in (2), the studies are what they are, and I'd honestly like to hang onto the anti-virtue-ethics punch of (4) if at all possible since it was the interesting part. So I guess if I have to give something up I'd rather it be (1) or some later step. But I can't cleanly see where the mistake is, or whether it's the same mistake at every stage. Which premise or step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? Also: does rescuing fragility in (5) automatically rescue character traits, or is there a principled difference between the two cases the argument misses? And what's the deal with (6) -- is the self-undermining worry real or just a cheap gotcha?
