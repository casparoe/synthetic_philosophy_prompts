Two people in my grad program have been arguing about dreams for a whole year and it's gone nowhere, so I've ordered them to do an adversarial collaboration and write one joint statement. Neither trusts the other to draft it fairly, so I want you to draft it for them.

Side A is a "received view" realist: dreams are genuine conscious experiences that actually occur during sleep, mostly during REM, the dream really unfolds in time while you're out, and the morning report is an imperfect record of an experience that was already there before you woke up.

Side B is a skeptic in the Malcolm/Dennett lineage: we have no good evidence that anything is experienced during sleep at all. What we call dreams are better understood as stories or pseudo-memories assembled at or around waking (Dennett's cassette-theory idea), and the feeling that the dream "happened" last night is an artifact of waking up with a ready-made narrative in your head. Malcolm's line too: the dream report is the criterion of the dream, not a fallible description of some prior mental movie.

The document I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can actually sign. This should cover the shared data (sleep-lab awakenings and the REM correlation, lucid dreams where dreamers execute prearranged eye-movement signals, external stimuli like alarm clocks getting woven into dream narratives, people who insist they never dream, reports of dreams that seem to last hours vs. minutes of sleep) plus claims about what each theory owes an explanation of, plus a few classic arguments both agree settle nothing. Don't pad it with vacuous stuff like "dreams are puzzling" -- only things that do real work.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated precisely, with each side's position phrased the way that side itself would put it, neutral wording overall. No strawmen.

3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions where an answer would genuinely move at least one side. I suspect some are: whether the lucid eye-signal experiments show experience *during* sleep or only that a narrative gets constructed with fabricated timestamps; what, if anything, could distinguish "I experienced a long dream" from "I woke up with the memory of a long dream"; whether the skeptic owes a positive account of why the waking fabrication takes the form it does; and whether the mid-20th-century reports of mostly black-and-white dreaming (vs. mostly color reports now) tell us reports reconstruct rather than record, or just that reports are unreliable.

4. For each crux, a concrete finding or case both sides accept would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance -- e.g., what would each predict about "white dreams" (NREM awakenings where subjects report having been thinking but with no imagery or narrative), about dreams nobody ever remembers, about dramatic time-dilation dreams (a whole lifetime in one night), and about false awakenings.

Also: if you think part of their fight is purely verbal -- say, over what "experience" or "conscious" means here, or over what it would even mean for a memory to be false if there was never an experience for it to mismatch -- say which part and show it. But don't use that as an excuse to dissolve the whole debate; flag what's left as genuinely substantive after the verbal residue is scraped off.
