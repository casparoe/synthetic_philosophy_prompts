so i run a small reading group on philosophy of mind / AI and we just finished the section on learning machines in Turing's 1950 Mind paper, Computing Machinery and Intelligence. we got pretty stuck on the bit near the end where he floats the idea that instead of trying to programme an adult mind directly, you might build a child machine and then put it through an actual course of education, with rewards and punishments doing part of the teaching work, and where he also says a random element is genuinely useful in a learning machine because it helps when you're searching for a solution you don't know in advance. half the group read this as charming and prophetic, half read it as a bit ominous, like school but for something that can't leave.

i want to close our last session by reading a poem out loud, and i want a villanelle, but with one rule broken on purpose: the two refrain lines should NOT repeat exactly. each time they come back they should drift by a word or two, sometimes a small improvement, sometimes a small corruption, so that the form itself enacts what Turing is describing -- repetition plus reward plus a random element, converging on something. by the final quatrain, when the refrains finally stand side by side, they should have settled into something that reads almost like a stable, adult sentence, and it should be a little ambiguous whether that's a triumph or a loss.

write it in the voice of the child machine at school. don't name Turing or the paper in the poem, and don't use any obviously anachronistic AI jargon (no "training data", no "weights") -- someone who knows the passage should recognize it from the rewards, the punishments, the randomness, and the education, and someone who doesn't should just read a strange, slightly sad poem about being taught. definately keep it to the 19 lines, the drift is the whole point.
