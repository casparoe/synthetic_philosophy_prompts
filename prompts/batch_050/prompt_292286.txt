Exam question: "State Kripke's rule-following paradox as developed in his discussion of Wittgenstein, using the 'plus'/'quus' example. Explain why the dispositionalist response — that meaning addition is constituted by my disposition to give certain answers — fails. Then explain what Kripke's 'skeptical solution' consists in and in what sense it is skeptical."

Student's answer: "Kripke imagines a skeptic who notes that by 'plus' I might have meant 'quus,' where x quus y equals x plus y for numbers below 57 and equals 5 otherwise. Since all my past uses involved small numbers, they are compatible with either rule, so the skeptic's point is that I can never know which rule I was actually following. The dispositionalist tries to ground meaning in my dispositions to answer, but this fails for two reasons: my dispositions are finite and cannot cover infinitely many cases, and dispositions describe what I will do, not what I should do — if I'm disposed to make mistakes, the disposition can't capture the rule I meant. The skeptical solution concludes that there is no fact about what I mean, and instead that meaning is fixed by the community: an individual means plus rather than quus just in case the community would agree with their answers. So meaning-attributions are true or false according to communal consensus, which is why the solution counts as skeptical — it replaces private facts with public agreement."
