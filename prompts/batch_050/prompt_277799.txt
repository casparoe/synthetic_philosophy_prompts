In the legal philosophy seminar I help coordinate, our sessions on Nuremberg and Tokyo have stalled in a way I suspect is typical of the wider literature. Everyone in the room accepts the same historical record: that the London Charter defined crimes against humanity and crimes against peace in a way that had no clear precedent in positive international law, that the Kellogg-Briand Pact and the Hague Conventions did the defenders' work of showing some pre-existing legal materials, that the tu quoque problem was real (Soviet judges at Nuremberg, Allied area bombing, the selective immunity extended to the Emperor at Tokyo), and that the alternative of summary execution was seriously discussed at the time. Given all that shared ground, the group still splits into two unmoving camps. One says the tribunals violated the principle that there is no crime without prior law, and that dressing punishment up as law was worse, not better, than honest political violence would have been. The other says the retroactivity objection is overstated: the maxim protects against unfair surprise, and no defendant could plausibly claim surprise that mass murder and aggressive war were punishable.

My hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about Nuremberg at all, but a jurisprudential disagreement about what makes something law, with a further normative disagreement hidden underneath about whether trials are primarily instruments of justice or of legitimacy and historical pedagogy. But I may be wrong: perhaps there is a genuine, resolvable legal question here that both camps are circling. Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is it verbal, metalegal, or substantive? Which premises does each side actually need? And given your diagnosis, what could count as progress -- a sharper distinction, a domestic transitional-justice analogy, a Radbruch-style test -- or is the impasse itself evidence that 'was it lawful?' is the wrong question and should be retired in favour of better ones?
