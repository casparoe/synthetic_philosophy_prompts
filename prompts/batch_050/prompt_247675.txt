I'm drafting a survey chapter on epistemic injustice for an edited volume, and at this point my taxonomy feels exhaustively standard. Here is what it already contains, so you don't repeat any of it: (1) testimonial injustice in Fricker's original sense, where a hearer deflates a speaker's credibility because of an identity prejudice; (2) hermeneutical injustice, where someone cannot render a significant area of their experience intelligible, to themselves or to others, because the shared conceptual resources were formed without the participation of people like them; (3) testimonial smothering in Dotson's sense, where a speaker truncates or censors their own testimony in advance because the audience is demonstrably unlikely to receive it competently; (4) epistemic exploitation, where members of a dominant group demand that marginalized people educate them about oppression, treating them as on-call epistemic resources whose labor goes unrecognized; (5) contributory injustice, where the dominant group maintains biased hermeneutical resources and refuses to take up the alternative interpretive tools that marginalized groups have developed for their own experiences; and (6) distributive epistemic injustice, the unfair allocation of epistemic goods such as education, access to information, and platforms for being heard at all. I mention all this partly because a colleague told me, over coffee last week, that the field has settled into a closed canon of varieties and that nothing genuinely new has been added in years, and I want to prove her wrong, or at least find out whether she is right.

So: give me one further variety of epistemic injustice that is not on this list and is not a special case, a sub-type, a relabeling, or a mere combination of the items on it. It should preserve the spirit of the concept — a wrong done to someone specifically in their capacity as a knower, inquirer, or epistemic agent, with a structural or identity-linked dimension rather than a one-off interpersonal slight — while picking out a phenomenon the six items above leave untouched. Describe the phenomenon concretely, give it a working name, and supply a realistic example from ordinary institutional or social life. Then do two more things. First, locate it relative to my list: which existing variety does it come closest to, and what is the precise feature that prevents it from being absorbed into that one? Second, give me an honest verdict on whether it deserves to stand as a distinct variety or whether treating it as one stretches the concept of epistemic injustice past the point of usefulness — and if the latter, say what that suggests about where the concept's boundaries should be drawn and why philosophers have been tempted to keep multiplying varieties anyway.
