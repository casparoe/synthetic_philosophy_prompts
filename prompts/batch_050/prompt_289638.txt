Design a 90-minute classroom activity for a professional-ethics workshop (16 participants, mixed graduate students and mid-career professionals) on the distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. The activity should be built around a core mechanic I want participants to experience rather than be told about: persuasion that survives full disclosure versus persuasion that collapses under it. Specifically, structure the session around a repeated persuasion game with real (small) stakes — for example, advocates trying to shift deciders' choices about which of two real charities receives an actual pooled donation — in which advocates must operate using a fixed 'tactic deck' you design. The deck should be graded along an axis from clearly legitimate moves (presenting new evidence, clarifying a consequence the decider hadn't noticed) through contested territory (emotional vividness, anchoring, strategic ordering of information, social proof) to clearly manipulative moves (manufactured urgency, selective omission, flattery calibrated to insecurity). Include a third role — a registrar or observer — with a defined job that forces the group to track not just whether choices changed but what, internally, changed them.

The design must include a 'receipts' phase: after each round, deciders receive complete disclosure of every tactic used on them and may revise their choice, with the game's payoffs arranged so that influence which evaporates under disclosure is punished and influence that persists is rewarded. Participants should rotate through all three roles so that everyone experiences deploying a tactic they suspect is manipulation, being on the receiving end of one, and having to draw the line as an observer. Give me: the full rules and payoff structure, the composition of the tactic deck with at least twelve concrete cards, a minute-by-minute timeline, and rules for handling the predictable failure modes (participants who treat the game as a pure competition and go straight for the dirtiest tactics; participants who refuse to use anything beyond the first tier and learn nothing; rounds where nobody's choice moves at all).

Then write the debrief plan. It should map specific moments the game will reliably produce onto the philosophical questions the literature presses — whether manipulation is best understood as bypassing the target's rational capacities, as covert influence, as pressure that raises the cost of refusal, or as something that fails what we might call a post-disclosure endorsement test — and it should be honest about which reading the game's incentive structure covertly privileges, since building the receipts phase into the payoffs is itself a theoretical commitment. Finally, include a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce: which features of real-world manipulation (asymmetric expertise, ongoing relationships, stakes the target can't walk away from, manipulation by institutions rather than identifiable individuals) the game flattens out, and how the facilitator should supply those features in discussion without letting the debrief do the work the game was supposed to do.
