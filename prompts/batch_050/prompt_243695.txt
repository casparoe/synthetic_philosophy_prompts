I am a novelist, and next month I have the odd honour of delivering a keynote to an annual meeting of philosophers of science — invited, I suspect, as a curiosity. I intend to argue, seriously, the following thesis: scientific thought experiments yield knowledge by the same mechanism that novels do. When Galileo imagines two falling bodies chained together, or Einstein imagines chasing a light beam, nothing is observed and nothing is measured; what happens is that a carefully staged narrative forces background commitments into collision, the way a novel forces a reader's moral intuitions into collision with an invented case. On my view, the evidential work is done entirely by the background theory the reader already accepts; the narrative is merely a delivery device that makes the contradiction vivid and impossible to ignore. Thought experiments are therefore neither windows onto a realm of laws nor disguised arguments — they are a literary technology for making latent inconsistencies felt.

The audience will be roughly split between deflationists in the tradition of John Norton, who hold that thought experiments are just ordinary arguments decorated with picturesque detail, and a smaller but vocal contingent sympathetic to James Robert Brown's view that a successful thought experiment delivers a kind of rational, non-empirical insight into nature. There will also be a few working physicists and historians of science in attendance.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections I will actually receive from this particular audience — not the ones a general public lecture would draw — ordered by how damaging they would be to my thesis, each phrased the way that faction would genuinely phrase it (a Nortonian will not object the way a Brownian does). For each objection, give me what I should concede gracefully, what I should hold, and a reply I could deliver in under two minutes that leans on my home ground — what novelists actually know about how fiction manipulates a reader's commitments — rather than on my shakier command of the physics. Mark the point in each reply where I am weakest. I also want the one question I am hoping nobody asks: the objection, presumably from the Nortonians, that my account makes the narrative itself epistemically inert, and so cannot explain why a badly told thought experiment fails where a well told one succeeds even when both encode the same argument. Tell me honestly whether my thesis can survive that question, and if so, what the novelist's answer to it should be.
