The exam question was: "Dennett and Caruso both reject the idea that offenders deserve punishment in the basic-desert sense, yet they arrive at notably different practical positions. Set out each thinker's view on just deserts, explain how each proposes to justify incarceration (if at all), and identify the deepest point of disagreement between them."

Here's the student's answer:

"Dennett and Caruso both find the retributivist notion of just deserts confused, but from opposite directions. Dennett is a compatibilist: on his view, agents who are reasons-responsive, self-controlled, and capable of being moved by moral criticism are morally responsible for what they do, and because such agents freely choose their crimes, they can genuinely deserve punishment in the basic sense -- retribution is legitimate provided it remains proportionate. Caruso, by contrast, is a free will skeptic who argues that no one is ever morally responsible in the basic-desert sense, regardless of whether determinism is true, so retributive punishment is never justified. His positive proposal is the public health-quarantine model: the state may incapacitate dangerous offenders for the same reason it quarantines carriers of dangerous diseases, so long as detention is humane, lasts no longer than necessary, and is paired with rehabilitation, and the criminal justice system should also target the social determinants of crime the way public health targets the conditions that spread disease. And just as quarantine can be imposed on anyone who poses a sufficient threat to public health, Caruso's model licenses detaining people purely on the basis of predicted dangerousness, even where they have not yet committed any crime. The deepest disagreement between the two is therefore that Dennett preserves moral responsibility and blame while Caruso eliminates them entirely."
