I am a retired biochemist who, over the past two years, has been working my way through philosophy of language with a small reading group, and we have spent the last month on the problem of empty names and fictional truth. The question that keeps nagging at me is whether "Sherlock Holmes" refers to anything, and if not, how it can nonetheless be true that Holmes is a detective and false that he is a greengrocer. Here are the positions on this I have now catalogued, and I believe my list covers the standard options. First, Meinongianism: there are nonexistent objects, Holmes among them, and the name refers to one, which is how fictional truths get to be straightforwardly true. Second, the Russellian line: "Sherlock Holmes" is either a disguised description or a genuinely empty singular term, so apparently referring sentences about him come out false or truth-valueless, and the appearance of truth is explained away pragmatically. Third, Walton's pretense theory: fiction is a game of make-believe, utterances of "Holmes is a detective" are acts of pretense rather than assertions, and the name refers to nothing outside the game. Fourth, the abstract-artifact view associated with Thomasson and others: fictional characters are real, created abstract objects, so "Sherlock Holmes" refers to something that genuinely exists and genuinely has the property of being a character, though not of being a detective. Fifth, a loose cluster of free-logic approaches that admit empty singular terms and assign fictional sentences nonstandard truth-values.

What I want from you is one more position on empty names and fictional truth that is not on this list and is not merely a variant, subdivision, or hybrid of items on it. I realize my list is probably exhaustive of the textbook options, so I am quite prepared to be told that no further position exists in the literature and that the interesting move is instead to construct one -- if so, please construct it, state it carefully enough that I could defend it in my reading group, and say what it explains that my five options handle badly or not at all. If instead you think there is a genuine position I have missed, identify it, distinguish it from the nearest item on my list, and say what it adds.

I would also like you to be explicit about your own epistemic position in answering. I spent forty years in a field where one states one's confidence and one's exposure, and I would like the same here: tell me how confident you are that your answer really is distinct from the five listed positions rather than a relabeling of one of them, and state what consideration -- an argument, a case, a distinction -- would change your mind about that, or where your reasoning about fictional discourse is most likely to be going wrong.
