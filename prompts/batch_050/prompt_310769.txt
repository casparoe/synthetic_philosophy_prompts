I have no training in philosophy, but I restore old stonework for a living, and a job last year sent me down a rabbit hole about statues and the stuff they're made of. I keep coming back to the same cluster of cases, and I want to know whether there's a single principle underneath my reactions or whether I'm just inconsistent. Here are my gut verdicts:

(1) A sculptor takes a lump of clay and shapes it into a statue of a horse. If someone later squashes it back into a ball, the statue is gone, but the lump of clay is still there — the same lump that was sitting on the shelf that morning. So on Monday evening the statue and the lump occupy exactly the same space but are somehow two different things with different life stories. That feels right, if a little odd.

(2) A marble statue weathered badly, and over forty years of restoration every fragment of the original stone was gradually replaced, one patch at a time, so that today none of the original material remains. I still say it's the same statue. Nobody would accept that the museum lost it somewhere along the way.

(3) A bronze horse is stolen, and the owner commissions a perfect copy, cast in the same foundry from the same molds. It's a different statue. If the original turned up, there'd be two, and the copy would still be the copy.

(4) A clay horse is smashed, and the artist gathers up every last crumb of the clay and shapes it into a horse identical to the first. To me that's a new statue — a recreation, not the old one back.

(5) A cracked statue is carefully glued back together from its own large pieces. Same statue, barely a question.

Here's what keeps me up: (4) and (5) seem to pull against (2) and (3). In (2) I'm willing to let all the material go as long as the change is gradual, but in (4) I refuse to let the statue survive even when all its material comes back, apparently because the change was sudden and total. But then why does (5), which is also a total dismantling, get a pass? And (3) suggests identity rides on history rather than material or form, yet (2) suggests it doesn't ride on material, and (1) suggests the lump and the statue are distinct precisely because of their histories — which would make the statue nothing but a history, and that can't be right either, since in (2) the history is continuous only in some thin institutional sense.

So: is there a precise principle — about what a statue is, or about when a thing survives change — that delivers all five verdicts, or at most trades one away? If two principles each save a different subset, lay them both out and tell me which verdict each one sacrifices. Then tell me what the surviving principle says about two cases I haven't made up my mind on: a statue 3D-printed from a scan while the original still exists, and a statue whose marble is ground to powder and re-cast into an identical figure using every grain of the same stone.
