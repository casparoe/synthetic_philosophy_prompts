I'm a philosophy PhD student working on agency, and I've agreed to run one 75-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group of cognitive scientists and sociologists on “free-floating rationales” — cases where a practice seems to exist for a reason even though no individual or clearly unified group currently possesses that reason. Plan how I should teach the idea rather than just explaining it to me.

Build the session around this case: a hospital has used a triage checklist for forty years. Its original designers are gone, later clinicians added rules for local reasons, and nobody now knows the justification for the whole system, although many of its features can be reconstructed as responses to genuine constraints. Call this an “orphaned rationale.” Say what conceptual pieces have to be introduced first, in what order, and why the case can carry the session. Map the misunderstandings I should expect — especially that reasons always require a present reasoner, that the hospital must be a literal group mind, that a merely causal history is already a rationale, and that calling the rationale normative means the checklist is justified — and indicate where in the sequence to block each one. Finish with a good understanding-check question and one issue I should explicitly leave for another day. My group is smart but pretty suspicous of armchair philosophy.
