I am a novelist at work on a book set in a small republic whose founding charter rests on a single revision clause: any provision of the charter, that clause included, may be revised or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the Assembly, and no enactment counts as law unless its authority traces back to the charter. I stole the device freely from the game Nomic, in which players alter the rules that govern how rules are altered. For a crisis chapter I gave the opposition's legal adviser the following argument, and I can no longer tell whether he has proved something or merely performed something. (1) The charter constrains the Assembly only if there are things the Assembly is forbidden to do. (2) For every prohibition the charter contains, the Assembly can lawfully be rid of it: revise it away under the revision clause, then act as it pleases. (3) A prohibition that the very body it binds can lawfully remove imposes no constraint on that body. (4) Hence the charter constrains the Assembly in nothing. (5) And a charter that constrains nothing is a charter in name only, so the republic, for all its parchment and ceremony, has no constitution at all. The conclusion plainly proves too much, since amendment procedures do restrain real governments; yet my plot needs the adviser taken seriously, because the Assembly is about to invoke the clause twice over, first to declare the article protecting the courts irrevocable, then to amend the revision clause itself. Tell me which step he should be forced to surrender, why that step sounded innocent, what surrendering it costs his wider position, and whether the Assembly can in the end bind its own successors. He is proudest of step 3, and for the character's sake I would like as much of it as possible to survive. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
