So I'm a PhD student in political theory (dissertation is on epistemic democracy adjacent stuff) and I TA a small grad seminar on alternatives to electoral democracy -- 14 students, one 80-minute session, and I've got futarchy on the syllabus. Hanson's vote-values-bet-beliefs setup, where you adopt whichever policy prediction markets say will produce higher welfare.

I really don't want to lecture. I want a game that makes them FEEL the two objections that always come up and that even sympathetic write-ups kind of half-acknowledge:

1. Markets on policies that never get adopted never resolve, so betting on them is pointless, so nobody does, so their prices are garbage -- meaning the system is flying blind exactly about the options it isn't choosing.
2. The selection effect / winner's curse thing: if you adopt whichever policy the market rates highest, you're disproportionately picking the policies whose value happens to be overestimated, so adopted policies should systematically underperform their market price even when every trader is honest and reasonably informed.

Design me a runnable in-class game: the class "governs" some measurable class-level quantity over several rounds, picks one policy per round based on market prices, bets with fake money, and there's some scoring so forecasting skill is rewarded. The twist has to live in the rules, not in anything I announce -- they should notice mid-game that refunding the unresolved markets kills any incentive to investigate dark-horse policies, and at the end that their adopted policies kept landing below their market-implied expectations. Then a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the two problems and onto the standard replies (subsidized markets, conditional trading, whatever you think fits).

Practical constraints: no real money, no apps or laptops, just paper, tokens, and a whiteboard, and I can play facilitator/bank. Give me the actual rules, a payoff scheme simple enough to run with whiteboard arithmetic, a rough timeline for the 80 minutes, the debrief questions, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- someone WILL figure out that pumping a policy's price costs nothing if they expect it to lose, and honestly I kind of want that to happen, so tell me how to handle it when it does. Also one honest paragraph on what the game can't reproduce -- presumably the part where real traders have genuine private information about policy consequences, since in my game the "true" effects will just be dice or hidden cards I control.
