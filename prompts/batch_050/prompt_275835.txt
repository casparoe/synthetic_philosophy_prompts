I've been reading around the arrow of time and every author seems to be grading the others against a checklist they never state. Before I pick a side I want the checklist itself. Write the desiderata that any account of why entropy increases toward one end of time (or why it appears to) has to meet, with the case or argument behind each. Things I suspect belong on it: it has to survive Loschmidt's reversibility objection without hand-waving; it has to explain why there are records and traces of the past but not the future, without quietly assuming the very asymmetry it's meant to explain; it has to say something non-embarrassing about why the early universe had such absurdly low entropy, or else argue this isn't a legitimate thing to ask for; and it has to avoid what I'd call the double standard, i.e. using statistics to infer a low-entropy past from today's state while refusing to run the exact same inference toward the future. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp (the laws-must-be-symmetric people, the "direction of time is primitive" crowd, the boundary-condition folks). The part I care about most: if some subset of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied -- say, keeping the micro-laws time-symmetric while also getting the arrow out of the laws rather than out of boundary conditions, or something less obvious I haven't thought of -- say so explicitly, because then the list itself is the result. At the end, briefly, how the past-hypothesis approach, primitivism about temporal direction, and the it's-all-perspective view do against the list. Keep the scoring short though, the criteria are the point.
