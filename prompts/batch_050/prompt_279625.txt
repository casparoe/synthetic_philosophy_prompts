Since retiring from a career in experimental physics I have been reading, with the enthusiasm and confusion of a beginner, in the philosophy of history and social science. One debate keeps frustrating me, and I would like a diagnosis of it rather than another entry into it. The question is why some nations are rich and others poor. As an outsider, I see roughly three camps. One holds that institutions do the decisive work: inclusive political and economic arrangements reward investment and innovation, extractive ones strangle them, and the rest follows. A second holds that geography and ecology are fundamental: disease burdens, climate, access to navigable coasts, and the historically contingent distribution of domesticable species shaped everything downstream, institutions included. A third insists that culture -- trust, attitudes toward work and thrift, religious inheritance, norms of cooperation -- is the neglected variable that explains what the first two cannot. Each camp has its canonical cases, its regression tables, and its favorite episodes in which the rivals' variables were held constant and theirs still mattered.

What strikes me, coming from a field where I could simply run the experiment again with one parameter changed, is that history seems to offer exactly one realization. Every proposed instrument or natural experiment I have seen invoked -- settler mortality rates, distance from coastlines, the placement of colonial borders -- appears to be contestable in the same way: a critic argues the instrument also affects outcomes through the rival variable, so the identification fails, and we are back where we started. Worse, the three factors are plainly entangled causally: geography plausibly shaped institutions, institutions plausibly shaped culture, culture plausibly sustains institutions, and all three co-evolved over centuries. So my working hypothesis is that this is not really an empirical dispute at all, or not only one. It may be a dispute about what would even count as an answer -- about whether causal attribution makes sense for a unique, path-dependent historical process, or whether each camp is answering a different question (why income gaps emerged, why they persist, why they vary at a given level of development) under a single heading.

So here is my request. Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it substantive, partly verbal, or a case of irreducibly different standards of evidence? Do the camps disagree about facts, about what counts as a cause of a singular event, or about which comparisons are legitimate? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what progress could even look like -- a sharper conceptual distinction, a particular kind of evidence that could in principle discriminate between the camps, or a concession that the question as posed is ill-formed and should be replaced by smaller ones. If the answer is that the debate is stalled for principled reasons and no conceivable evidence could settle it, I would rather be told that plainly, with the argument for it, than be given another tour of the positions.
