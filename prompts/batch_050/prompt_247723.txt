My team was arguing about launch criteria for a recommender change, and a coworker who reads philosophy blogs gave me this summary of the “seperateness of persons” objection: utilitarianism treats everyone’s pleasure and pain as if it goes into one shared utility pool, so if torturing one contractor raises engagement for a million users, it counts as a win; critics say that’s confused because a benefit to Ana cannot literally compensate a loss to Ben, since they’re separate lives and nobody experiences the total. He also said this means utilitarians secretly deny that individual persons exist, that all ethical aggregation is basically dashboard averaging, and that once you admit tradeoffs you’ve already endorsed harvesting organs; therefore the only serious options are leximin-style priority to the worst off or hard deontology.

I get the engineering intuition: a +2 for lots of users doesn’t cancel one person’s −10 if the −10 is an actual ruined life. But “never aggregate” also sounds unusable for policy, triage, carbon budgets, even uptime vs. accessibility work. Is his summary fair? What would a careful utilitarian concede, what would they push back on, and where would a prioritarian or sufficientarian land?
