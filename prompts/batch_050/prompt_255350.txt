A friend and I fell into a long argument last weekend after she told me she's decided not to move back to her hometown now that her widowed father, who is seventy-one and increasingly frail, can no longer manage alone. She loves him, speaks to him weekly, and will help arrange and pay for good care nearby; she just won't upend her career and marriage to relocate. Her brother told her she owes their father more than that, given that he worked two jobs for a decade to put both of them through university. She and I reconstructed the dispute afterward and couldn't settle it, and I'd like a careful adjudication. The exchange ran roughly as follows. (1) Her brother argued that parental sacrifice of that magnitude creates a debt of gratitude, and that when the creditor's needs finally come due, an adult child is obliged to pay in kind -- with presence and care, not just money. (2) She replied, drawing on Jane English's friendship model of filial duty, that adult children owe their parents what friends owe one another: the duties track the current relationship, not a ledger of past sacrifices, so love and ongoing friendship ground obligations but unremembered or repaid sacrifice does not. Money and weekly calls are what she, as a friend at a distance, actually owes. (3) Her brother answered that this proves too much: it implies a child whose well-meaning, sacrificing parent simply isn't the sort of person she'd befriend owes essentially nothing, which gets the direction of fittingness backwards -- the sacrifices should generate the relationship's claims, not wait on the child's affection to license them. (4) She countered that treating parental care as a loan the child never agreed to borrow converts gift into debt and poisons the relationship, and that the brother's own view has no principled stopping point: if sacrifice is repaid in kind, she would owe her father her thirties and forties. Please go through the four moves in turn, saying who is right at each step and where the stronger version of each position would press next; and close by identifying what, if anything, both siblings are missing -- for instance, whether the friendship model can even handle a case like this one, where the relationship is intact and loving and the dispute is precisely about what intact filial friendship demands.
