Two semanticists have been arguing for a decade about the status of presupposition in natural language. Dr. Hale defends the lexical-semantic view: expressions like *stop*, *again*, *even*, the factive *realize*, and the definite article conventionally encode, in their lexical entries, requirements that the common ground already contain certain content, and the way these requirements survive or disappear under negation, questions, conditionals, modals, and attitude verbs follows from compositional semantics. Dr. Osei defends the pragmatic-scorekeeping view: nothing in the lexicon encodes such requirements; the relevant inferences arise because a cooperative audience accommodates whatever the speaker's move must be assuming on the conversational scoreboard, and the embedding facts follow from general principles about what it is and isn't plausible to accommodate, not from grammar.

Draft the joint statement the two could both sign after a serious, good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims: observations about the phenomena both accept, stated in vocabulary neither side owns. Include at least: which embeddings systematically let presupposition-like inferences through and which block them; the fact that conditional sentences sometimes seem to inherit only a weakened, conditional-shaped inference rather than the full one; the variable cancellability of these inferences across triggers and contexts; and the contrast between presupposed and asserted content under challenges and denials. Every claim must be one both could sign without mental reservation.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the issue; Hale's position, phrased as she would phrase it; Osei's position, phrased as he would phrase it; and a status column marking each row as a genuine disagreement or a verbal one. Where a row is verbal -- for instance, if the two have been using *presuppose* for different things, one for a property of sentences and the other for an act of speakers -- say so and state the disambiguation that dissolves it.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case, experiment, or argument both accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Include among the test cases the following constructed one, which neither has seen before: in a hypothetical language, a verbal suffix behaves like a trigger except that its associated inference projects through negation but not through yes/no questions, and it can be cancelled only when the speaker has just explicitly expressed uncertainty. State whether each theory predicts that such a morpheme could exist, and what each side would conclude if a language with exactly this profile were documented.

Close with a short section each author writes about the other: what result or argument would most seriously damage their own view.
