Philosophers Ingmar Persson and Julian Savulescu have argued that advances in science and technology have placed enormous destructive power in the hands of states, small groups, and even individuals, while human moral psychology remains adapted to life in small, face-to-face societies. To close this gap, they propose moral bioenhancement: the use of drugs, genetic interventions, or other biomedical means to strengthen dispositions such as empathy, a sense of fairness, and aversion to harming others. Because the costs of moral failure would fall on everyone, they suggest that such enhancement might, in principle, be compulsory.

In the debate over this proposal, one analogy does repeated work: compulsory moral bioenhancement is like compulsory vaccination. Just as the state may require vaccination because one person's refusal endangers third parties and herd immunity is a public good, so the state may require moral enhancement because one person's unenhanced aggression or partiality endangers third parties and a morally trustworthy citizenry is a public good. In both cases, the intervention is minor, the individual burden small, and the collective benefit large.

Audit this analogy. Provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the pathogen, the immune response, the herd-immunity threshold, the side-effect profile, and the act of administration. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion from those that do not. Consider at least: that vaccination targets a specifiable pathogen with measurable efficacy while "moral deficiency" has no biomarker and no agreed specification; that vaccines leave personality untouched while moral enhancement modifies dispositions partly constitutive of who a person is; that disease transmission bypasses the victim's agency while moral "transmission" runs through reasons and choice; and that the legitimacy of compulsory vaccination is itself contested, so the analogy may assume rather than establish the permissibility of compulsion. Identify what the analogy quietly imports about the nature of moral agency—treating dispositions as non-rational mechanisms like antibodies rather than as reasons-responsive capacities—and ask whether the case for compulsion survives once it must stand on the features the two practices genuinely share. Finally, if the vaccine analogy fails, propose a better candidate (mandatory civic education, quarantine, fluoridation, or another) and say what the choice among candidates reveals about which conception of moral agency the enhancement proposal presupposes.
