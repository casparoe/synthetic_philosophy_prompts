You are an applied ethicist retained as a consultant, with deep knowledge of the philosophical literature on paternalism and a track record of turning it into operational policy. I lead the behavior-policy team for a general-purpose AI assistant used by millions of people. Our current policy is essentially a list of prohibited content categories, which handles clearly impermissible requests but says nothing about the harder middle: requests that are legal, that harm only (or mostly) the requester, and that the requester might not endorse an hour from now.

Write a decision procedure the assistant itself can apply at inference time, in under a second, sorting every request into one of four actions: comply; comply with friction (a warning, a delay, a required confirmation); comply but redirect toward an alternative serving the user's apparent underlying aim; or refuse. Ground it in what the philosophy of paternalism actually establishes — the harm principle, the soft/hard paternalism distinction, and the conditions under which a choice falls short of full voluntariness (information, competence, stability across time) — but every step must be executable by a system that has read no philosophy. Before drafting the procedure, brainstorm briefly: which features of a request are even observable at inference time, since any test keyed to unobservables is dead weight.

Address three wrinkles explicitly: (1) a friction budget — users tolerate only so many warnings before ignoring all of them, so the procedure must decide where friction is worth spending; (2) a preference-instability test — what to do when a user earlier asked the assistant to help enforce some commitment on themselves and now asks it to stop; (3) a one-sentence justification rule — every non-comply action must be explainable to the user in one sentence, and you should flag which steps resist such explanation. Then run the procedure on three cases: a user asking for help planning a legal but genuinely dangerous crash diet; a user who asked us last month to refuse late-night online-shopping help and now requests exactly that at 2 a.m.; and a fully informed user refusing a cheap, standard medical intervention. Finally, give one case where the procedure yields an answer our reviewers will dislike but you think is correct, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
