Last week I got into a long argument with a friend about the 1997 rematch between Garry Kasparov and Deep Blue. My friend took the match as settled evidence that human cognition is, at bottom, just computation that machines can replicate and surpass — and dismissed the Lucas–Penrose line of argument as a relic. I defended the Gödelian anti-mechanist position, but I left the argument less sure of my ground than I'd like, so I want a careful analysis rather than another round of assertion.

Please take the Deep Blue match as a case study for the Lucas–Penrose anti-mechanist arguments and work the analysis in both directions. On the theory-to-case side: reconstruct the core Gödelian argument as carefully as it deserves (the move from a formal system's inability to establish its own consistency, or to assert its own Gödel sentence, to the claim that no such system can capture human mathematical thought), and then say what, if anything, the match actually bears on in that argument. My friend's move seems to be that Deep Blue's victory shows human-level cognition emerging from a machine, full stop. But Lucas and Penrose were arguing about minds as consistent formal systems and about mathematical understanding specifically, and Deep Blue is a brute-force search engine playing chess, not a theorem prover aspiring to consistency. So tell me: is the match even the right kind of evidence? Does the anti-mechanist get to say the episode is orthogonal, or is that a defensive retreat — if machines keep winning at everything we point to as distinctively human, at what point does the Gödelian argument lose its grip even if it was never formally refuted?

On the case-to-theory side: I think the match contains details that are awkward for both of us. For my friend, there's the fact that Deep Blue's architects, not the machine, did the understanding — the evaluation function encoded human chess insight, which sits oddly with the "mind is just mechanism" moral being drawn. For me, there's the uncomfortable fact that Kasparov was the inconsistent, error-prone party in the match (he even resigned game two in a position later argued to be drawn), while Lucas's argument seems to require the human to be the consistent reasoner who can "see" what the machine cannot. If human mathematicians and grandmasters are themselves inconsistent, does the Gödelian premise survive, or does the match expose that the argument was always about an idealized human who doesn't exist?

Close with a verdict: is the Deep Blue episode a confirmation of anti-mechanism (the machine won without understanding anything), a counterexample to it, or evidence that the whole debate was mis-posed because neither side had the right concept of what was being tested? Defend the verdict rather than hedging, and flag where either Lucas or Penrose has resources I haven't given them credit for.
