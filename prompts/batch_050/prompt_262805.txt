So I want a thought experiment built to order, about the common prior assumption and Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem. The point of the case is to pin down which premise of that theorem is really load-bearing, so don't just stipulate the verdicts -- they have to actually fall out of the setup.

Here's the spec. Construct a scenario with two perfectly rational agents who have a genuine common prior (stipulate it, but make it psychologically plausible, not magic) and common knowledge of each other's rationality, and who end up with *persistently different* posteriors on some yes/no question -- and the disagreement has to survive them talking to each other. The catch: you may not cheat by giving them different priors, or irrationality, or private evidence the other doesn't know exists. The intended escape hatch (and you should engineer the case around it) is that knowledge of each other's posteriors is mutual knowledge only to some finite depth: she knows his estimate, he knows she knows his estimate, she knows he knows she knows... and then the tower just stops somewhere short of full common knowledge.

Constraints: realistic setting (two epidemiologists, two market analysts, two chess coaches assessing the same position, something like that -- no infinite minds, no demons), the scenario itself should be tellable in a paragraph or two, and use concrete numbers -- I want the actual posterior probabilities each agent lands on, not just "they disagree."

After the scenario, do the verification:
1. State Aumann's result precisely enough -- spell out the actual premises (common prior, common knowledge of rationality, common knowledge of posteriors).
2. Show that your case violates exactly one premise and identify which.
3. Walk through at least two rounds of the agents updating on each other's stated estimates and show they *still* disagree, and explain why the usual convergence intuition (they keep exchanging estimates and have to meet in the middle) stalls when the knowledge tower has finite depth.
4. Say what happens if you upgrade the finite-depth mutual knowledge to full common knowledge -- does the disagreement collapse immediately, or does something subtler happen?
5. Honest verdict: does your case show the theorem is wrong, that its premises are too strong to ever hold in real life, or that common knowledge is a way bigger ask than plain old mutual knowledge?
