Two members of my philosophy-of-physics reading group have been arguing for a year about whether mathematical elegance is evidence for a physical theory. The first holds the view associated with Dirac, Weyl, and Chandrasekhar: that the mathematical beauty of a theory is a genuine, if fallible, indicator of its truth, and that physicists who trusted their aesthetic judgment were vindicated often enough that the practice is rational rather than superstitious. The second holds the skeptical position pressed by several physicists and historians in recent years: that beauty-guided theory construction has a poor recent track record (natural supersymmetry, grand unified proton decay, and the string program are her exhibits), that the celebrated successes are cherry-picked after the fact, and that aesthetic criteria are contingent products of training and fashion rather than windows onto nature's structure.

Draft the joint statement the two of them could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Concretely:

1. A numbered list of claims both accept. Candidates to include or improve upon: that "elegance" names several distinct properties — symmetry, simplicity of axioms, unification of previously separate domains, rigidity or inevitability of the mathematical structure — which need not stand or fall together; that the historical record contains both striking successes and striking failures of beauty-guided reasoning; that no aesthetic judgment has ever settled a question in the absence of empirical confirmation.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in a single sentence, worded so that the person who actually holds the view would accept the formulation as fair. Mark beside each disagreement whether it is empirical, methodological, or — if you find one — merely verbal.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I expect these to include whether the famous aesthetic successes were identified as beautiful before or only after empirical confirmation, and whether aesthetic criteria are themselves inductively answerable to past empirical success — in which case the whole dispute may collapse into an ordinary question about which inductions are licensed and over what base class of cases.

4. For each crux, a concrete test, case, or piece of analysis both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. One protocol I have proposed to them: a prospective registry in which working theorists score not-yet-tested theories for elegance on fixed criteria, so that the predictive value of aesthetic judgment can be estimated against a base rate rather than reconstructed retrospectively. State whether both parties should accept this protocol as probative, and if either should not, say what it fails to measure.

Two further requirements. First, say whether the joint statement can cover mathematics without equivocation, given that proof settles truth there — so that elegance in mathematics could at most be evidence of a theorem's depth, fertility, or explanatoriness, and the analogy to physics may be weaker than both parties assume. Second, if you conclude that some part of their dispute dissolves once beauty is disambiguated (for instance, into simplicity-as-prior versus harmony-with-existing-data versus generative inevitability), identify that part, and explain why the dissolution is not itself a victory for either side.
