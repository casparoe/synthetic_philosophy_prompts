I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and the professional vocabulary I am most fluent in is the economics and practice of forecasting: prediction markets, calibration and Brier scores, superforecasters and their updating discipline, market prices as consensus probabilities, information revelation through trading, and the failure modes of forecasters under incentive distortions. I am preparing a long essay on Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle in formal epistemology — roughly, the requirement that a rational agent's present credence in a proposition should match what she currently expects her future credence to be, given that her future self will be at least as well informed as she is now — and I want to present the entire debate to my readers without ever leaving the vocabulary of forecasting, markets, and scores.

Please do the following. First, restate the principle itself in that vocabulary: what would it mean for a forecaster, or a market, to satisfy or violate Reflection, and does the principle amount to anything beyond the familiar ban on foreseeably exploitable updating? Second, translate the standard argument in its favor — that violating Reflection exposes you to diachronic incoherence, roughly of the kind a bookmaker punishes — into the language of traders who can be front-run by those who know their updating habits, and state honestly whether anything in the argument survives that translation unchanged. Third, translate the principal counterexamples the literature leans on, cases where an agent rationally expects her future self to be worse situated rather than better — anticipated memory loss, the gradual erosion of an insight she currently possesses, expected interference from drugs or social pressure, and disagreement with epistemic peers whose track record she respects. Give each case a forecasting-market counterpart: say what it looks like when a market or forecaster is justified in discounting its own future prices, and whether that move is intelligible inside the forecasting frame at all.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Which claims in the Reflection debate translate cleanly into forecasting talk; which translate only by distortion — for instance, where the translation quietly replaces one agent trusting her later self with a population of traders aggregating information, and whether that substitution changes the subject; and which parts of the debate have no expression at all in the vocabulary of prices and scores. I suspect the untranslatable residue concerns something first-personal: a market has no self whose future judgment it must decide whether to respect. If I am right about that, say so and say what follows; if I am wrong, show me the forecasting-vocabulary expression I have missed.

Finally, take a position on the residue: is it a defect of my vocabulary, evidence that forecasting practice simply cannot capture a genuine norm of epistemic self-governance, or is it evidence that the philosophical debate treats as a substantive requirement something that is, in the end, just consistency of updating wearing a more solemn costume? Argue for your answer rather than merely gesturing at both options.
