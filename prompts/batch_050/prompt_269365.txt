So I'm in a reading group on philosophy of language, and last week we did Lewis on scorekeeping and the whole common ground / accommodation machinery. One guy in the group, Mark, who enjoys being annoying, came in with an argument that he says shows accommodation can't possibly work the way the textbooks describe — that it "proves too much" and would basically dissolve the difference between asserting something and presupposing it. None of us could find the exact move where it breaks, but we're all sure it breaks somewhere, because the conclusion is nuts. Could you play the role of a pragmatics specialist who's graded a thousand of these and tell us where it goes wrong? Think it through carefully rather than pattern-matching to the nearest textbook paragraph.

Here's his argument, as best as I can reconstruct it from my notes:

(1) If a speaker utters a sentence that presupposes p, and p isn't already on the conversational record, a cooperative hearer just quietly adds p to the record — that's accommodation, and it's what lets me say "my sister's visiting" to a friend who didn't know I had a sister.
(2) Accommodation requires nothing from the hearer beyond not objecting. If they let the conversation continue, the content is on the record, full stop.
(3) So a speaker can get absolutely any content onto the conversational record simply by packing it into a presupposition instead of asserting it — including content the hearer would flat-out reject if it were asserted outright.
(4) For instance: "I can't make it Saturday, I have to go collect my Nobel Prize," or "We should hire Dana, since even you admitted last week she's the strongest candidate," said to someone who admitted no such thing. On (1)–(2), if the hearer doesn't interrupt in time, "they have a Nobel Prize" and "you admitted Dana is strongest" go onto the record just as legitimately as the sister case.
(5) But if that's how it works, assertion is pointless. Anything controversial you want established, you presuppose; assertion is strictly worse because it invites challenge. Rational speakers would presuppose everything contentious, and assertion would wither away as a speech act.
(6) Assertion obviously hasn't withered away, and the presuppose/assert distinction genuinely constrains us — the Nobel Prize line is a joke precisely because it doesn't work.
(7) Therefore something is deeply wrong with the Lewis-style scorekeeping picture: either accommodation isn't real, or the conversational record isn't what he says it is.
