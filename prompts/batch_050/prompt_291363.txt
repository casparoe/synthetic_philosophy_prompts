I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece loosely about whether moral philosophy makes people worse at being people — the worry that thinking too hard about what's right can somehow spoil perfectly good loves and friendships. For a sidebar I want to dramatize the classic clash here, and I'd like you to write it as a dialogue. The setup: two philosophers, maybe late 50s, at a hotel bar after a conference panel on partiality and ethics has wound down and the grad students have finally left them alone. One is a committed impartialist — a consequentialist, or something in that family, your call on the exact flavor — who thinks what makes an act right ultimately comes down to agent-neutral value. The other is a Bernard Williams–type defender of integrity who thinks there's something deeply wrong with any moral theory that can't let a husband rescue his drowning wife over a stranger without first checking that morality permits it. The conversation should center on that rescue case (feel free to vary it — one life jacket, a burning building, whatever), but I want it to be about more than the case.

Non-negotiables, because I've read too many bad versions of this debate. The impartialist cannot be a robot. They should immediately concede that it would be creepy and alienating if the husband actually ran a moral calculus in his head mid-rescue, and then deploy the sophisticated reply: a criterion of rightness is not a decision procedure, the ideally good agent would be *disposed* to save his wife without the intervening permissibility thought, and the theory is describing what justifies the act, not dictating what must cross anyone's mind. The Williamsian has to engage that move directly, not pretend it wasn't made — press on whether a disposition can really stay stable when it's propped up by a justification the agent is supposed never to think about, or on what happens when the disposition and the theory's verdict come apart, say when saving the wife genuinely is impartially worse (ten strangers on the other boat, whatever). And the Williamsian shouldn't get a free pass either: make them answer the charge that the integrity talk licenses grotesque partiality, and the tu quoque that once you poke any theory hard enough it generates its own version of the one-thought-too-many problem — the Kantian double-checking her maxims, the partialist who first has to confirm that this really is *his* wife. I want real back-and-forth where each line responds to what was just said, roughly 800-1000 words, and it should end not with a winner but with the two of them locating, as precisely as they can manage over drinks, the actual crux they disagree about. Keep the dialogue naturalistic — interruptions, a little grumpiness, someone signalling for the check. If you want to gesture at the idea that some reasons only exist from the inside of a life, fine, but definately don't let it turn into a seminar with a bartender.
