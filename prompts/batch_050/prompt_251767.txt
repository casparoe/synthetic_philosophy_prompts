I'm a philosophy prof drafting a paper on whether individuals ever explain large-scale historical events, with Nazi Germany as the running case. A colleague in the history department read the draft and hit me with an argument I can't accept but also can't refute, and its wrecking the paper. Here it is, as charitably as I can put it:

(1) For any large-scale event, ask whether the background structural conditions were sufficient for an event of that kind. Either they were or they weren't.
(2) Suppose they were sufficient — Versailles resentment, the Depression, the collapse of the center parties, an authoritarian-leaning old elite willing to hand power to someone, anti-communist panic. Then Hitler was replaceable: if he'd stayed a painter, someone else (Strasser? a military clique? some movement we can't name because it never needed to exist) would have ridden the same wave. Hitler explains nothing; he's just the name we give the foam on the wave.
(3) Suppose instead the conditions weren't sufficient. Then Hitler's presence made a difference only the way a coin flip makes a difference: the outcome was underdetermined by anything structural, so what did the work was contingency, and contingency isn't agency. Crediting Hitler with *explaining* Nazism on this horn is like crediting one particular butterfly with a hurricane.
(4) Either way, Hitler doesn't explain the Nazi seizure of power.
(5) The schema is perfectly general — run it on Caesar, on Lenin, on Napoleon — so no individual ever explains a large-scale event.

And here's where it seems to prove too much: run the same dilemma on the structural factors themselves. Was WWI sufficient for the collapse of Weimar democracy, or not? On the colleague's own schema, either it necessitated the collapse (so the particular war is replaceable, some other shock would have done) or it didn't (so it's butterfly stuff). Apply recursively and *nothing* explains anything; explanation just bottoms out in "conditions obtained." A schema that dissolves every candidate explanans can't be right.

He accepts the regress and bites the bullet — says narrative explanation is mostly post hoc storytelling, Tolstoy had it basically right, and so on. I can't follow him there, but when I press on the argument each premise looks fine in isolation.

What I'd most like to keep: that structure does the heavy lifting for 1933 itself (I don't want to end up a Great Man romantic), and that explanation is a real relation rather than storytelling. I'm willing to sacrifice the butterfly analogy if that's the weak link.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If you think the argument is actually sound, tell me which premise is cheapest to drop and why. And be honest about your own epistemic position — say how confident you are in the diagnosis, and name the specific point in your answer where you're least sure of yourself.
