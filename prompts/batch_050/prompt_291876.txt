I'm putting together the final for my upper-level undergrad seminar on collective responsibility and I need one essay question on complicity. I want it built around a case I made up, so students can't just regurgitate a summary of the complicity literature. Here's the case, feel free to polish it a bit but keep the essential structure:

Mara works as a data annotator for a defense contractor, labeling satellite images that feed a targeting algorithm. Two things are true by design. First, every image is labeled by three annotators and a consensus system, so her individual labels never change any output -- if she mislabels or skips an image, the other two carry it. Second, the company deliberately overstaffs the annotation pool so that no single worker is ever pivotal; if Mara quit tomorrow, throughput would be completely unaffected. Mara has no enthusiasm for the project, she does it for the rent money, and she sometimes tells herself her sloppy labeling might even be slightly sandbagging the system, though she has no evidence of that.

The question should force students to answer: is Mara complicit in what the targeting system does, and does the answer change given that her participation is causally redundant twice over? I want them to actually have to choose between and apply (a) counterfactual difference-making accounts of complicity, (b) Kutz-style accounts based on participatory intention -- acting with the intention of contributing to a collective project, even if your contribution is redundant -- and (c) an account where complicity is about flouting a normative expectation not to lend assistance, regardless of intentions. The twist I want them to grapple with is what I'm calling "redundancy by design": the firm has engineered things so that the difference-making account apparently lets everyone off the hook, and that looks like a feature for the firm, not a bug. A good answer should notice that this structure generalizes (voting, emissions, consumer boycotts, "if I don't do it someone else will") and say something about whether complicity can survive total causal redundancy and what that implies for whether resignation is morally required or merely supererogatory.

Please write:
1. The exam question itself, phrased the way it would appear on the paper. 75 minutes, open-book but no notes-sharing, worth 40 of 100 marks.
2. A model answer of around 600 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 40 that names the specific moves an A answer must contain vs. a B answer -- e.g., a B answer correctly describes all three accounts but applies them loosely to the case, while an A answer has to do specific things like flag that Mara's "sandbagging" hope tests whether participatory intention requires actually trying to advance the project, and engage seriously with the generalization worry rather than name-dropping it. Also list the two or three most common mistakes you'd expect (like conflating complicity with blameworthiness, or assuming redundancy automatically exculpates) and how many marks each should cost.
