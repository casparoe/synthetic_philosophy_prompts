I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about work and leisure by going on gut verdicts about cases. Tell me whether a single principle underwrites all of them, and if not, which verdict I should surrender.

My verdicts:

1. A former colleague quit to build his own product and now works seventy-five-hour weeks, cheerfully, answerable to no one. Fine — admirable, even.
2. Karoshi cases — salaried workers who die of overwork at firms where leaving before the boss is unthinkable — are a grievous wrong, even though each worker "chose" to stay late every night. The consent doesn't settle it.
3. A statutory cap like France's thirty-five-hour week strikes me as legitimate when it functions as a default that teams can collectively adjust, but paternalistic and wrong if it would forbid my former colleague from choosing his own hours.
4. One week in six I carry a pager for a small on-call stipend. Most weeks it never goes off. I still count those evenings as work: I'm not free to drink, travel, or make firm plans. Constrained availability is labor even when it's never used.
5. Quiet quitting — doing precisely what your job description says and nothing more — is entirely legitimate, and the pejorative term is management spin.
6. Yet the teammate who went home at 5:30 while production was down and three of us debugged until midnight did something wrong, contract notwithstanding.
7. A burned-out volunteer maintaining an open-source library that several large companies depend on is being exploited, full voluntariness notwithstanding.
8. Rest framed as recovery so you can perform — the wellness industry's version — corrupts leisure. Leisure needs no justification in terms of output. But sleep and exercise are legitimately instrumental, so the principle can't simply ban instrumental rest.

The pairs I can't reconcile: 1 versus 2 (voluntariness legitimates in one case and not the other), 1 versus 7 (both freely chosen, yet one is admirable and the other exploitative), 5 versus 6 (contractual limits decisive in one case, no defense in the other).

What I want: a principle — or two rival principles — stated precisely, clause by clause, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Where no principle saves every verdict, say plainly which one has to go and why that one rather than another. Then tell me what the surviving principle predicts for cases I haven't decided: a four-day week at full pay where output holds steady; a legal right to disconnect applied to the founders of a two-person startup; whether caregiving for a child or an aging parent counts as work, leisure, or a third thing; and whether my on-call arrangement would be fixed by a larger stipend or is wrong in principle regardless of pay. Finally: does the work/leisure dichotomy itself survive, or does the principle force a third category (availability, caregiving, instrumental rest) — and if so, which of my verdicts was already implicitly relying on it?
