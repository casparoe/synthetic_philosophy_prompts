I'm hoping you can settle an argument I've been watching unfold between two friends in my reading group on behavioral economics. They asked me to referee, and I'd rather outsource it. The dispute is whether loss aversion -- in particular the endowment effect -- is a cognitive bias or a legitimate preference. Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

Martin opened: loss aversion is simply a bias. In the classic experiments, people randomly handed a mug demanded roughly twice as much to part with it as non-owners offered to buy it. Since allocation was random, there is no information, sentiment, or history that could justify the gap -- the same object, valued differently depending only on who happened to receive it. A valuation that flips with a coin toss is not a preference but an error, and the same error is what keeps people holding losing investments and staying in jobs they hate.

Clare replied that this is too quick. Ownership is not a random label: once you possess something, you build plans and expectations around it, and losing it disrupts those plans in a way that merely failing to acquire it never does. The disutility of losing what has been woven into your life legitimately exceeds the utility of gaining what has not. On her view, the endowment effect records a stable and intelligible asymmetry, and calling it a bias simply presupposes a standard of rationality -- that only an object's intrinsic features may enter its valuation -- which the experiments were meant to test, not to assume.

Martin countered that a deep preference would not be so trainable. In field studies at collectibles markets, experienced dealers show a markedly smaller gap between buying and selling prices than novices do; and in the laboratory, the gap shrinks substantially when subjects are first given practice rounds with the trading mechanism. Something that largely melts away with a little experience looks like confusion or an unexamined habit rather than a principled valuation.

Clare answered that preferences are formed by experience, so showing that experience alters them does not show they were fake -- no one argues that a trained palate's taste in wine is not a real preference because training produced it. The dealers' smaller gap may reflect learned market norms rather than the disappearance of the underlying asymmetry; offer to buy those same dealers' own long-held possessions and the gap would return. She also charged Martin with conflating two distinct theses: that the endowment effect is an artifact of experimental procedure, and that loss aversion as such is irrational. Refuting the first, even if successful, would leave the second untouched.

Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the four contributions, state what it gets right and where it overreaches; then identify anything both parties are missing; and close with an overall verdict on whether loss aversion is better understood as a bias or a legitimate preference. Keep the entire response under 400 words.
