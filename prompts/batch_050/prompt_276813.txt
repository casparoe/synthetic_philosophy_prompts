Our TA laid out the two standard objections people raise when they say the case doesn't generalize to most pregnancies: first, that the violinist's dependence is nobody's fault (you were kidnapped), whereas in most pregnancies the woman voluntarily did something that foreseeably led to the dependent person existing and being dependent; and second, that unplugging is letting someone die whereas abortion is actively killing them. Ok, fair enough. Constraints: no science fiction, no trolleys, no perfect-predictor stuff, it has to be physically possible in an ordinary hospital or household setting, and the scenario itself should be one paragraph at most. Then say what happens if you strip that feature out too — does the intuition finally flip, or does the case just become the original pregnancy question? And finish with the best attack a Thomson critic could mount against your construction: what would they say has been smuggled in, and does the smuggling matter?

Format-wise: answer as exactly five numbered items with no introduction or conclusion — item 1 is the scenario paragraph, item 2 is why the permissibility intuition survives (a) and (b), item 3 is the remaining asymmetry doing the work, item 4 is what happens when you remove it, and item 5 is the critic's best counterattack.
