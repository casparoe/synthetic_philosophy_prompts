Ok so here's one I've been chewing on since a phil of religion seminar. Analyze the Miracle of the Sun at Fátima, October 1917, as a test case for Hume's argument against believing miracles on testimony. Quick recap of the facts as usually reported (correct me if I'm off on these, they're part of the setup): three shepherd children had been predicting a public sign; a huge crowd, tens of thousands, showed up in the rain; many people reported the sun appearing to spin, zigzag toward the earth, change colors, and their soaked clothes and the wet ground drying fast; but some people standing right there saw nothing unusual at all; and both devout believers and hostile skeptics were in the crowd.

Run this in both directions. First direction: what does Hume's machinery actually say about this case? His points about proportioning belief to the evidence, about testimony needing an otherwise-unimpeachable track record when it contradicts uniform experience, about rival miracle traditions canceling each other out, and his observation that religious wonder tends to corrode ordinary standards of testimony. Which of those bite here and which don't? Mass simultaneous testimony by thousands seems like exactly the kind of case Hume's argument was built for, but I'm not sure he ever imagined this scale, or witnesses who were there and saw nothing.

Second direction: what does the episode show about the argument? Is it a confirmation (expectation, crowd psychology, and the fact that observatories recorded nothing all doing exactly what Hume would predict)? Or does it expose that his standard is too crude — that he lumps all testimony into one bucket when the interesting structure here is conflicting reports from the same event? And a sharper question: does Hume's argument rule out believing this happened, or only rule out believing it on testimony strong enough to carry a religion — those seem to come apart and I'm not sure which conclusion the Fátima case pressures.

Tell me how confident you are in each part of your analysis, and flag the single point you're least sure about.
