I'm a high school junior and the co-captain of my school's debate club, and last week a teammate presented an argument that has been bothering me ever since. We had been reading Plato's Euthyphro in my philosophy elective, and he claims to have shown that the dilemma Socrates poses there -- does what is pious please the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it pleases the gods? -- can be generalized into a proof that nothing can ever ground or explain anything. Here is his argument, as close to his wording as I can reconstruct it.

(1) For any claim about what makes something right, there are exactly two possibilities: either the thing is right because the proposed authority approves of it, or the authority approves of it because it is right.

(2) If the thing is right because the authority approves of it, then its rightness depends on an attitude that could have been otherwise, and so its rightness is arbitrary -- had the attitude differed, the opposite would have been right.

(3) If the authority approves of it because it is right, then citing the approval explains nothing about what makes it right; the real ground, whatever it is, lies elsewhere.

(4) So no appeal to anyone's approval can ground rightness. (This much is just the Euthyphro, and I accept it.)

(5) Nothing in steps (1) through (3) is specific to gods, approval, or morality. For any fact F and any candidate ground G, the same fork applies: either F obtains because of G, or G holds because of F.

(6) On the first horn, G could have been otherwise, so F's obtaining is arbitrary in exactly the sense condemned in (2).

(7) On the second horn, citing G explains nothing, for exactly the reason given in (3).

(8) Therefore no fact ever has a ground, and nothing ever explains anything.

(9) But plainly some things do explain others: the bridge held because of its cables, water boils at 100 degrees Celsius because of the behavior of hydrogen bonds, and I failed the quiz because I did not study.

(10) Since (8) is absurd and the reasoning is valid, my teammate concludes that the true lesson is that we should stop demanding grounds for anything -- moral or otherwise -- and that the arbitrariness objection in (2) was never a genuine objection at all.

I strongly suspect something goes wrong between (5) and (8), but when I pressed him, he insisted that the generalization is perfectly smooth: whatever makes the fork work for divine approval must make it work for every purported explanation. I cannot say whether he is smuggling something in or whether one of the earlier premises has to die.

If I had to protect something, I would most like to keep (9) -- I do not want to live in a world where nothing explains anything -- and I would be reluctant to concede that arbitrariness is never a legitimate objection, since that seems like the whole engine of the original Euthyphro.

So here is what I am asking: identify the precise step where the argument fails (or, if you think it is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and why). Explain why that step looked innocent -- my teammate is not stupid, and the move felt natural when he made it. Then price the alternatives: if the flaw is in the generalization at (5) or (6), what does it cost to restrict the Euthyphro to cases involving contingent attitudes, and does that leave divine command theories untouched on other grounds? If the flaw is an equivocation on "explains" between the two horns, explain the distinction the argument is conflating and why the distinction does not save approval-based moral theories anyway. Finally, say whether my teammate's conclusion in (10) could be defended on independent grounds even if this particular argument for it collapses.

Please answer in American English.
