I have been reading Wang Yangming's Chuanxilu (the Instructions for Practical Living) and I keep circling his doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action. The famous passage, roughly paraphrased, runs like this: people say of some man that he knows filial piety and knows fraternal respect, and yet he does not act on them; Wang Yangming says this shows the man never knew them at all. Knowing filial piety without practicing it is not knowledge of filial piety. He extends the point to the senses: you cannot know the badness of an odor without disliking it, or the beauty of a color without loving it. Knowledge that fails to move you was, on his view, never genuine knowledge but only words.

I want to take this seriously, so I have been testing it against my own verdicts on cases. Here is where I land:

The chain-smoker who sincerely assents that smoking will likely kill him, believes the statistics, and keeps smoking: I say he does know. It seems perverse to deny that he knows the medical facts.

The son who lectures beautifully on filial piety but neglects his mother: I agree with Wang, he does not really know filial piety. His "knowledge" is verbal decoration.

The club chess player who can recite every positional principle and then violates all of them over the board: I say he does not know the principles, whatever he can recite.

The man who knows casual cruelty is wrong, feels genuine compunction each time, and is cruel anyway under social pressure: I say he knows it is wrong. Weakness of will strikes me as a real phenomenon, not an illusion produced by misclassifying knowledge.

The medical resident who aced the relevant exam but freezes and cannot act when the emergency arrives: I waver. I want to say she has partial or incomplete knowledge, but I am not sure "partial knowledge" is a coherent middle position or an evasion.

The recovering alcoholic who says, after years of white-knuckling it, "now I finally understand": I find this a natural thing to say and it seems to vindicate Wang completely.

The scrupulously honest man who never lies, but only because he fears punishment and reputational ruin, not because he grasps what makes lying wrong: I say he knows lying is wrong (he gets every case right) and yet, intuitively, he does not know in Wang's sense. I cannot tell whether that is one kind of knowledge evaluated twice or two kinds of knowledge.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the smoker and the unfilial son. Both sincerely assent, both could pass any written examination on their respective subjects, and neither acts. My verdicts treat them oppositely, and I cannot see a principled difference between knowing a medical fact about one's own body and knowing a moral fact about one's own mother, though Wang's own example forces me to confront exactly this.

So here is my request. State, as precisely as you can, the strongest principle (or two or three candidate principles) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible: some criterion that sorts genuine knowledge from mere verbal or propositional knowledge. For each clause of the principle, tell me which case motivates it. Then apply your principle to two cases I have not listed: first, the climate scientist who flies to conferences twelve times a year; second, a person who reliably acts well but can articulate no reasons at all when asked. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. If saving all seven requires an intolerably complex principle, tell me which single verdict is cheapest to surrender and what picture of knowledge and action remains after the surrender. I would also like to know whether the result is a substantive thesis about what knowledge is, or merely a recommendation to reserve the word "knowledge" for what moves us, and whether those come apart.
