I am a junior in high school, and next month I will present a proposal at our school's annual Town Hall forum, where students argue for changes to school policy in front of a panel and an open audience. My proposal is to abolish elections for our twenty-seat student council and instead fill the seats by lottery, drawing names at random from the entire student body. I have three modifications to a pure lottery: anyone drawn may decline, in which case another name is drawn; terms are staggered so half the council rotates each semester; and outgoing members must run a two-week handover and training period for their replacements.

My core argument is this: elections select for popularity, confidence, and free time rather than for judgment or representativeness. Our council is consistently drawn from the same small social stratum, and members spend much of their energy on getting re-elected. A lottery would make the council statistically resemble the student body it governs, and members who owe their seats to chance rather than to a campaign would have no incentive to posture. I already know the broad history here (selection by lot in classical Athens, modern citizens' assemblies, juries), so I do not need a literature review; what I need is to survive the question period.

The panel will consist of four current council members who won competitive elections and understandably take random selection as an insult to their own legitimacy; our AP Government teacher, who has said publicly that student elections are the school's main instrument for teaching democratic citizenship; the principal, who is risk-averse and cares mostly about discipline and about the school not being embarrassed; and a reporter from the school newspaper looking for a story.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each could do to the proposal, each phrased in the voice of the person who would raise it (a council member who campaigned for her seat will not put the objection the way the principal will). For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, what I should concede outright, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest. I also want you to stress-test my own design rather than only the audience's objections: for instance, I have a nagging worry that letting students decline their seat quietly reintroduces the same self-selection by the already-engaged that the lottery is supposed to eliminate, and I suspect the Government teacher sees this too. Tell me how to handle that tension and any others you find in the proposal itself, including the objection I should be hoping nobody asks and what to say if someone does.
