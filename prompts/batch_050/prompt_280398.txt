I'm a PhD student (political theory, so epistemology is adjacent-but-not-my-thing) and there's a pattern I keep noticing in ordinary life that I don't have vocabulary for. Two recent instances. My flatmate is sure the pharmacy on our street closes at 7, she's been there at 6:50 before, and when I casually ask whether she *knows* it closes at 7 she says yes without blinking. But last week her kid needed a prescription filled that evening and she suddenly insisted on calling ahead to check, and when I teased her and asked "so did you actually know it closes at 7 or not?" she got weirdly hesitant, like the answer had changed. Second instance, from my own work: I'll happily tell a colleague I know a certain quote is on a certain page of a book, but the night before a deadline I always re-verify before it goes in the footnote, and at that point it feels wrong to say I *knew* it before checking -- even though nothing about my memory or evidence changed in between, only what would happen if I was wrong.

So here's what I'm wondering. Sometimes it looks like the belief stays exactly the same and only the willingness to *act* on it shifts; other times the person seems to genuinely retract the claim to know. Are those the same phenomenon or two different ones that get lumped together? Is there an established name (or names) for any of this? I have a vague sense there's a literature about whether what you know can depend on what you'd lose if you're wrong, and a seperate literature about how the word "knows" behaves depending on the conversational setting, and I honestly can't tell whether those are rival theories trying to explain one phenomenon, or descriptions of two distinct phenomena that philosophers keep conflating. If there's a standard taxonomy here I'd love to have it laid out, including where exactly the boundaries between the positions are supposed to run.

One more thing: I sat in on a talk where someone claimed that when you actually test ordinary people, the effect mostly doesn't show up, or shows up in weird places -- like people judge differently based on what's at stake for the *person making the judgement* rather than the person with the belief, or something like that. I don't know how much of that is solid. So in your answer, please be explicit about what's genuinely settled in this literature versus what's still contested among the people who work on it -- I don't want to cite something in a dissertation chapter as "the view" if it's actually one contested position among several, and I especially don't want to repeat an empirical claim that didn't hold up.
