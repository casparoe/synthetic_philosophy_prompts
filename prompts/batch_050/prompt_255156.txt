Hi, bit of background first: I spent about 35 years as an experimental condensed matter physicist, retired a few years back, and since then I've fallen down the philosophy of physics rabbit hole. It's fun but humbling. Here's something that's been nagging at me and I can't find a satisfying writeup of anywhere.

So in stat mech we learn the usual story about entropy: for a given macrostate there are vastly more microstates corresponding to higher entropy than lower, so an isolated system wandering through phase space will almost surely move toward equilibrium. Fine. But here's the thing that keeps me up at night. The counting argument seems completely time-symmetric. Take a system in some non-equilibrium state right now, say my half-melted ice cube in a glass of water. The very same counting argument that says its future is almost surely higher entropy ALSO says its past was almost surely higher entropy. Because nothing in the phase-space volume counting cares about which temporal direction you integrate in. So the argument "predicts" the ice cube spontaneously froze itself out of a lukewarm glass five minutes ago, which is absurd. We all know the actual past was lower entropy. But that's not something statistics gave us, it's something we just know from records and memory.

The resolutions I've seen all seem to boil down to: well, the actual explanation of the arrow isn't the statistics at all, it's that the universe started in an extraordinarily low-entropy state (the big bang being oddly smooth and uniform gravitationally, or whatever the right way to state that is), and everything else follows from that boundary condition plus the statistics. Ok. But this strikes me as deeply weird and I'm not sure how to think about it:

1. Is it right that the statistical argument by itself, without the low-entropy boundary condition, gives you literally nothing asymmetric? Or am I missing something and there IS some intrinsic asymmetry in the dynamics/statistics I've overlooked?

2. What IS this low-entropy-past postulate, metaphysically speaking? It's not a dynamical law like Newton's or Schrodinger's equation, those are time-symmetric. But calling it a mere contingent fact, like the fact that my coffee cup is on the left side of my desk, also seems off, since apparently EVERY thermodynamic regularity in the universe hangs off it, including all of geology, biology, memory, and the possibility of records at all. I've seen people gesture at the idea that it should count as a law in some non-dynamical sense, maybe because the best summary of the universe includes it, but I find that slippery.

3. Maybe most confusingly for me: if every belief I have about the past (photos, fossils, my own memories) is only reliable because of this one cosmic boundary condition, does that mean the entire epistemic basis of history and science rests on a single unprovable posit? Or is there some way to break out of that?

Now here's the important part: I don't want a survey of positions. I've read enough "some philosophers say X, others say Y" to last a lifetime. Pick a side. Tell me what you actually think the answer is, especially on question 2 (law vs brute contingent fact vs "you're confused, it's neither"), and defend it. If you think one of my questions is malformed, say so plainly and tell me what the right question is. I'd much rather get a committed answer I can push back on than a hedged one.

Thanks in advance, and sorry if this is all old hat to you philosophers.
