Here's how I lean on a bunch of promise cases, and I want to know what principle (if any) is doing the work, or whether some of my takes have to go.

- My friend makes me promise not to tell anyone about her engagement, then her whole family finds out anyway from her brother. I figure I'm released from the promise. The point of it is gone.
- I promise my dying grandfather I'll scatter his ashes in Ireland. He dies. I still think I'm bound, even though he'll obviously never know whether I did it.
- My coworker says "promise you won't be mad" and makes me promise before telling me he broke my laptop. I say the promise doesn't bind me — he extracted it blind, specifically so I couldn't react.
- I promise my kid I'll take her camping this weekend, then a genuinely great opportunity comes up for me that conflicts. I think I still have to take her camping, and it bugs me when people say "well the opportunity outweighs it" — if it just gets weighed like everything else, what was the point of promising?
- I'm leaving a party and say "I'll text you when I get home!" to four people, then forget. I feel mildly bad but not like I wronged anyone, even though it has the form of a promise.
- I promise to help a friend move, and later find out he's moving because he got evicted for dealing from the apartment. I think I can back out — what I promised isn't quite what he led me to picture.
- Someone promises me they'll return a book by Friday, I completely forget they ever borrowed it, and they return it three weeks late. I feel like they didn't wrong me at all, since I never even noticed.

The pair that worries me: in the ashes case I hold myself bound even though the promisee is dead and can't be wronged or disappointed, but in the book case I let the promiser off partly because *I* didn't notice. So either I need a principle where what matters is something other than the promisee's expectations (but then the engagement case and the book case get weird), or I have to drop one of those two verdicts. Also the camping case seems to sit badly with the moving case — in one I insist the promise can't be traded off, in the other I let new information dissolve it, and I'm not sure "but the info was about the thing promised" is doing real work there.

So: what principle captures most of these? State it precisely enough that it also tells me what to say about cases I didn't mention (a promise to someone who's now in a coma; a promise repeated every year out of habit). And tell me honestly which of my verdicts the cleanest version of the principle has to sacrifice, rather than bolting on epicycles to save all seven.
