I'm a PhD student in formal semantics, and this term I'm TAing a joint linguistics-philosophy graduate seminar on conditionals. The instructor has asked me to draft one essay question for the take-home final, along with the materials we'll use to grade it. The seminar covered the standard landscape -- the material conditional and its paradoxes, Stalnaker-style possible-worlds semantics for counterfactuals, the restrictor view, and the epistemic readings associated with indicative conditionals -- and the instructor's worry is that a lazy student can earn a pass simply by rehearsing the famous contrast between the indicative and subjunctive Oswald/Kennedy pair and declaring that mood tracks truth conditions. She wants a question that forces students to take a position on a harder issue: whether the indicative/subjunctive distinction marks two semantically different conditional constructions, or a single conditional whose felicity and interpretation shift with the speaker's epistemic relation to the antecedent.

The question should be built around a small battery of data that resists easy treatment, and I'd like you to supply it: (1) a fresh indicative/subjunctive minimal pair in the spirit of the Oswald case, but chosen so that neither sentence is straightforwardly false; (2) a case of the Sly Pete type, in which two differently informed speakers can each felicitously assert indicative conditionals whose consequents contradict each other, and which puts pressure on any semantics giving indicative conditionals invariant truth conditions; and (3) at least one case in which a speaker uses a subjunctive even though they believe the antecedent may well be true (or an indicative despite suspecting the antecedent false), to test whether mood really is controlled by the speaker's credence in the antecedent. Students should have 2.5 hours and write roughly 900-1200 words.

Please deliver three things. First, the exam question itself, phrased the way it would appear on the paper, including the example sentences. Second, a model answer of about 600 words that a strong student could plausibly produce under time pressure -- it should take a definite position rather than survey options, but flag where the position is doing real work versus where it is stipulative. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 marks, built around five must-hit points with their individual values, plus a one-line note for each on the specific way a mediocre answer typically fumbles it (e.g., treating the two speakers in the Sly Pete case as simply equivocating, or assuming that a subjunctive with a possibly-true antecedent is ungrammatical rather than conversationally marked). Please also state explicitly what separates a first-class answer from a merely good one in terms of the moves made, not the literature cited.

Finally, I'd like you to be upfront about the epistemic status of what you write: for each major claim in the model answer, indicate how confident you are that it represents the settled state of the literature rather than one live position among several, and single out the single point in your own model answer where you are least sure you have it right.
