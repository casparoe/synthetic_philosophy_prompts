So I just got out of a truly ridiculous three-hour argument with my friend Marcus about acausal trade. He thinks the whole idea is incoherent -- if you can't communicate with someone and there's no causal channel between you, there's no trade, end of story, anything else is magic. I tried to explain the standard setup: two agents who know of each other only through reasoning about what sorts of agents would exist, each reasoning about the other's decision procedure, each cooperating because they predict the other is doing the same symmetric reasoning, and it "works" without any messages ever being sent. He said that's just two people independently doing what they'd do anyway, and I couldn't quite articulate why that's wrong. Frustrating.

Anyway, I want to settle this in the most productive way possible, which is apparently by commissioning fiction. Write me a short story (maybe 800-1500 words) that actually dramatizes an acausal trade, or something close to one, and lets the reader feel the weirdness of it rather than just asserting it works. Some seeding you can use or ignore: I was picturing two civilizations (or maybe two AI systems, or one of each) that can never interact causally -- separated by something that genuinely blocks all signals, not just distance, so there's no cheating via hidden communication. Maybe each has evidence of the other's existence only through indirect means, like reasoning about what a universe like theirs probably contains. The story should show the actual reasoning -- the part where an agent has to model a stranger well enough to predict what the stranger predicts about them, and has to decide whether to "cooperate" on that basis -- and ideally it should include some friction: an asymmetry between the two parties, or a moment where the reasoning threatens to unravel (what if the other side doesn't run the same algorithm? what if they predict you'll cooperate and then defect anyway, since defection costs them nothing causally?).

A couple requests. First, don't make it a dialogue where characters lecture each other about decision theory -- show the dilemma through choices and consequences. Second, don't resolve it by having one side turn out to be stupid or evil; the interesting case is two competent, honest reasoners. And if you want, end it in a way that would make Marcus at least admit the scenario is coherent, even if he'd still deny it counts as trade.
