Suppose John Stuart Mill were alive today, fully briefed on contemporary institutions and evidence, and asked to advise a legislature on the regulation of sex work. His briefing describes three regimes in neutral terms: (1) the Nordic model, under which selling sex is legal but purchasing it is a criminal offense, brothel-keeping and procuring are criminalized, and the state funds exit services and frames the law as reducing demand in order to protect sellers; (2) legalization as practiced in Germany and the Netherlands, under which sex work is a lawful occupation subject to licensing, zoning, and registration requirements, brothels operate as licensed businesses, and ordinary labor and tax law applies; and (3) decriminalization as practiced in New Zealand, under which adult consensual sex work is governed by no special criminal statute at all, ordinary law covering assault, public health, and employment applies, and small owner-operated brothels are permitted.

Write a reasoned reconstruction of Mill's advice. The reconstruction must be built from positions Mill actually held, and must engage at least the following: the harm principle and its corollary that self-regarding conduct among consenting adults lies beyond the reach of law; his hesitation in On Liberty over whether acts that must be tolerated when performed (he instances fornication and gambling) may nevertheless be punished in those who profit by facilitating them (pimps and gambling-house keepers); his denial that freedom includes the freedom to sell oneself into slavery, and his reasons for that denial; his opposition to the Contagious Diseases Acts, which subjected women suspected of prostitution to compulsory medical inspection while leaving their clients untouched; and his utilitarianism, with its consequence that his policy views are hostage to empirical outcomes.

Address the following questions in order:

(a) Does the Nordic model's asymmetry — punishing one party to a transaction the law expressly permits the other party to perform — violate the harm principle, or can Mill coherently reconstruct the buyer as harming the seller notwithstanding her consent? Consider in particular whether his voluntary-slavery argument offers him a route to the claim that consent to sell sex is precisely the kind of consent a free society need not honor, and assess whether he would take that route given his general anti-paternalism.

(b) Where does his ambivalence about pimps and procurers land him on the regimes' divergent treatment of third parties: licensed brothels in Germany, small owner-operated brothels in New Zealand, and criminalized procuring under the Nordic model? Is the licensed brothel-keeper simply the pimp over whom On Liberty hesitated, or does Mill's reasoning admit a distinction between facilitation and exploitation that tracks one of the three regimes more closely than the others?

(c) How does his record on the Contagious Diseases Acts bear on the registration and inspection requirements of German-style legalization? Would he regard the registration of sex workers as a recurrence of the one-sided police surveillance he opposed, or as an even-handed application of general law of the sort he could accept?

(d) Since he is a utilitarian, specify what empirical findings — concerning violence against sellers, trafficking, health outcomes, or the broader bargaining position of women — would have to hold for his ranking to shift. In particular, identify the evidence that would move him from his predicted first choice to each of the alternatives.

Conclude with an overall prediction: which regime Mill would endorse, which he would rank last, and the single point on which the evidence of his own writings is genuinely too thin to settle the matter — a point you should defend as indeterminate rather than merely confess as uncertain.
