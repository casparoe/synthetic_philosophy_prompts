I'm a program officer at a mid-sized private science funder. Every year we recieve about forty renewal applications from groups we've been funding for three-plus years, and I recommend renew / renew-with-conditions / defund to our director, who signs off. Two things keep going wrong: my reviewers (generalist scientists, not specialists in each field) throw around "this programme is degenerating" and "the core is still fertile" as pure vibes, and whatever we decide has to be written up as reasons that survive an appeal to the board.

I've been reading Lakatos on research programmes -- hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristics, progressive vs degenerating problemshifts -- and it feels like exactly the right machinery, except he never had to make a funding call in March. He seems pretty comfortable saying a programme can look dead for years and then revive. I can't wait ten years.

So: turn his framework into a procedure my reviewers can actually run in about two hours per application. Tests in order, what counts as a passing or failing answer to each, and operational criteria for the mushy bits -- especially (i) how a generalist is supposed to tell a genuinely novel confirmed prediction from a post-hoc patch, and (ii) how many cycles of degeneration are too many, given Lakatos gives no cutoff. Say which of his distinctions you're keeping and which you're dropping as unusable at our level, and mark the steps where judgment can't be eliminated and the reviewer just has to write a paragraph defending the call. Also say whether any finding should be an automatic stop, or whether everything is a flag. The procedure has to handle two tricks I've seen: teams that quietly rewrite the hard core while claiming continuity (so the grant stays continuous), and teams that present replications of their own old results as if they were new confirmations.

Then run it on three cases from my desk, including B, which I personally like and was planning to recommend for renewal -- be honest about what the procedure says even if I won't like it.

Case A: blood-biomarker group for a neurodegenerative disease. Confirmed a blood marker five years ago. Three attempted extensions since, all failed, each handled by narrowing the claim (only early-onset patients, only one ancestry group, only in combination with a second marker). Last year they predicted, before the data existed, that the marker would track progression in a related disease, and an independent lab confirmed it.

Case B: microbiome-and-depression programme. Enormous publication count, high-profile team. Every flagship association has failed outside replication; each failure handled by adding a covariate or redefining the phenotype. No pre-registered novel prediction ever confirmed. Their forward plan is basically: collect more data, recluster.

Case C: a small mathematical-biology programme, quiet for four years, almost no papers, no confirmed novel predictions on our criteria -- but there is no rival programme in this area at all. Defunding it means the field has nothing.

Close with a section on what this procedure can't do, then a summary table with one row per case and exactly these columns: case; hard_core_status (intact / quietly-swapped / unclear, one line why); belt_moves (progressive vs degenerating moves last cycle); novel_predictions (count, and for each whether it was used to build the theory or merely accommodated after the fact); rival_exists (yes/no); verdict; judgment_calls (which steps needed a person); appeal_risk (low/medium/high).
