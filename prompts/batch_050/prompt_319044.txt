I am currently taking an upper-level undergraduate seminar on convention and the emergence of social norms, and we have spent the first half of the term on David Lewis's account of convention and on the mechanisms by which conventions and norms stabilize in a population. My seminar leader has been training us in classification: she describes a practice and we must say whether it counts as a convention and defend the answer. Her own verdicts so far have been: driving on the right-hand side of the road — a convention; the fact that English speakers use the word dog for dogs — a convention; shaking hands at introductions rather than bowing — a convention; tipping roughly fifteen to twenty percent at sit-down restaurants — a convention; serving customers in the order they arrived — a convention; refraining from lying to one's friends for personal gain — not a convention; stopping at red lights even at deserted intersections late at night — not a convention; and the fact that most people in this country eat their largest meal in the evening — not a convention.

She has announced that next week we will get a harder case, and I want to work out her likely verdict in advance. The case: in this country, brides almost universally wear white at their weddings. The practice is historically recent, other colors would serve every practical purpose equally well, and a bride who wore bright green would reliably attract comment and mild disapproval. At the same time, no guest's choice of attire depends on what the bride wears, and no bride's choice depends on what other brides do; each bride conforms because of how her own deviation would be received, not in order to coordinate with anyone.

Please do the following. First, predict her verdict on the white-dress case and give your reasoning. Second, state as precisely as you can the rule she appears to be applying — for instance, whether she is running a strictly Lewisian definition requiring a genuine coordination problem with multiple available equilibria, or a broader account on which any arbitrary regularity sustained by a conditional preference to conform given others' expectations counts as a convention. Third, identify which of the eight earlier verdicts do the real constraining work and explain how; in particular, say what the red-light and evening-meal verdicts rule out. Fourth, give at least one alternative rule that is equally consistent with all eight verdicts but delivers the opposite verdict on the white-dress case, and explain why the track record cannot decide between the two. Fifth, describe a further hypothetical case that would discriminate between your two candidate rules, and say what each rule predicts about it. Finally, in a short closing paragraph, connect this to the course's other theme: if the practice turns out not to be a convention on her rule, what kind of norm is it instead, and what keeps it stable — and does its historical origin as an arbitrary innovation that then became socially enforced tell us anything about whether arbitrariness or coordination-dependence should be the load-bearing condition in a definition of convention?
