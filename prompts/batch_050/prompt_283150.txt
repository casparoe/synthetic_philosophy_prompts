ok, putting together my position statement for the aesthetics grad seminar and I want someone to stress-test it before the students do. Here's where I stand on aesthetic testimony:

1. Nobody can know that a poem is beautiful (or a painting serene, a symphony triumphant) purely on someone else's say-so. You have to experience the work yourself. Testimony gets you a justified belief at most, not aesthetic knowledge.
2. It's perfectly rational of me to form the belief that a new restaurant's food is delicious on the strength of one trusted friend's review, and to act on it. No acquaintance needed there.
3. Someone blind from birth, who's read deeply in the best art criticism and knows the consensus of competent judges, can genuinely know that Vermeer's interiors have a quiet luminosity. I'd defend this in a heartbeat, it seems like the obvious anti-elitist verdict.
4. Whether an interpretation of a poem is correct (say, that it's ironic rather than sincere) is something you can straightforwardly learn from an expert's testimony.
5. Aesthetic properties supervene on non-aesthetic ones, and trained critics are, by and large, reliable detectors of them.
6. No matter how unanimous the critics are, my own careful experience of a work can rationally override their verdict. I'm not epistemically required to defer.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? Rank the tensions by how serious they are, and for each one tell me what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs. And be honest about whether (3) survives contact with (1) and (5), because I have a bad feeling about it.
