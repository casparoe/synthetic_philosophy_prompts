I'm the secretary of our community hall committee (eight of us plus a chair) and something odd happened at last month's meeting that I've been chewing over since. We were deciding whether to replace the boiler this year. We voted on three things seperately:

- Is the boiler likely to conk out before next winter? Six said yes.
- If it's likely to conk out, should we replace it now rather than wait? Five said yes.
- Right, so should we replace it now? Only three said yes.

So as a committee we'd apparently agreed the boiler is on its way out, agreed that means replacing it now, and then voted not to replace it. The chair said the third vote was the decision and moved on, and nobody complained, but it's been bugging me ever since and I can't work out whether we did something wrong or whether that's just how voting goes.

I've never studied philosophy or politics or anything, but here's roughly where I've landed on how group decisions ought to work, and I'd like to know if the package hangs together:

1. Taking each question on its own and going with the majority is the only fair way to run things. Once you start combining questions or weighting some votes differently you're rigging it.
2. A committee, same as a person, should never be committed to a contradiction. If we've voted the boiler's going and voted that that means replacing it, we simply can't turn round and vote not to replace it. Whatever else happens, the group has to come out making sense.
3. Everything the group "thinks" should boil down to what the actual members think. There's no committee mind floating above the table with its own opinions.
4. It shouldn't be able to matter what order the questions get asked in or how the chair bundles them up. Same views in the room, same outcome.
5. Secret ballots are fairer than a show of hands because people vote honestly when nobody's watching.
6. The decision that actually gets enacted should be one a majority of members genuinely endorse, not something cobbled together out of other votes they cast on different questions.

So: which of these are actually in tension with each other, and which only look like they are? Please rank the genuine tensions by how bad they are rather than listing them all as equally problematic, and for each one say what the cheapest fix would be and what it would cost me. If there's one single change that would clean up the most, tell me which it is. And going back to the boiler vote: given my commitments, was the chair right to treat the third vote as the decision? I have a feeling my own views give two different answers to that, so tell me straight which commitments are doing the pulling.

One more thing -- if any pair I've listed seems like a problem but actually isn't once you look properly, say so and explain why, because I suspect at least one of them is fine really.

Please answer in British English.
