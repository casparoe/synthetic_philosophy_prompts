I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, and my dissertation chapter has basically committed me to defending the moral equality of combatants -- the Walzerian idea that just and unjust combatants stand in the same moral position on the battlefield, that they're equally permitted to kill each other, and that jus in bello runs independently of jus ad bellum. But I keep getting stuck on something in the older tradition. I was rereading Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.40 a.1, and the way he gets the just war off the ground is through a straight-up policing analogy: "just as it is lawful for them to have recourse to the sword in defending that common weal against internal disturbances, when they punish evil-doers [...] so too, it is their business to have recourse to the sword of war in defending the common weal against external enemies." So for Aquinas, war is punishment, continuous with the magistrate punishing criminals. And it strikes me that this is exactly the picture the revisionists (McMahan and co.) use against Walzer -- cops and robbers are not moral equals, so if war is like policing, unjust combatants are in trouble. Which makes me wonder whether the equality thesis is fighting the whole tradition from Aquinas on down, or whether Walzerians can just absorb this passage by denying the analogy transfers.

So: what would count against the moral equality of combatants? I don't just want a summary of McMahan's liability argument, which I know. I want a map of the thesis's actual exposure. Which defeaters would be fatal (e.g., would showing that unjust combatants can never satisfy in bello proportionality sink it? would a convincing account of symmetrical self-defense do it?), which would merely be awkward, and which supposed objections does the view survive easily? Also: what would Walzerians themselves concede as counting against the position, as opposed to what their critics say counts -- those two lists seem to come apart interestingly here. And is the view hostage to any empirical claims, say about soldiers' duress, their epistemic situation re: the war's justice, or what conscription does to responsibility, such that psych research could in principle dent it? Or has it been qualified into something that risks nothing, and if so is that a defect for a thesis in this area?

Finally, on the Aquinas passage specifically: tell me honestly whether a Walzerian can accomodate the punitive framing (maybe equality holds between combatants even under the policing model, since ordinary criminals still get to defend themselves against punishment in some respects?) or whether conceding the analogy really does force the revisionist conclusion. Please flag how confident you are in each part of the answer, and single out the one claim you're least sure of.
