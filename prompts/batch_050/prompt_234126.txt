In Section X (Of Miracles), Part I, of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, David Hume writes: "Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony. A man delirious, or noted for falsehood and villany, has no manner of authority with us."

Analyse the 'Miracle of the Sun' at Fátima on 13 October 1917 through the lens of this passage. The salient facts, as commonly reported, are these: a crowd estimated in the tens of thousands had gathered because the apparitions had been announced in advance; many present said they saw the sun spin, change colour, and plunge towards the earth; some present, including journalists and sceptics, reported striking experiences, while others saw nothing unusual; and no astronomical observatory recorded any anomaly. I want the analysis to turn on the quoted criteria rather than on Hume's better-known maxim. What verdict do tenacity of memory, inclination to truth, and liability to shame actually deliver about the Fátima witnesses, and is Hume entitled to assimilate a crowd of tens of thousands to his delirious man? Where does his framework fit the episode well — for instance, the heterogeneity of the reports and the prior expectations of the crowd — and where is it silent or overconfident, given that some witnesses expected nothing and reported something anyway? Finally, say what the episode shows about the theory: does it confirm Hume's account of testimony, or does it expose a gap in it, and what would Hume have to add or concede to handle mass, simultaneous, public testimony to a physically unconfirmed event?
