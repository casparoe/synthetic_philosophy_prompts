I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on motivated reasoning, and a dispute broke out at my reading group last week that I couldn't settle. Two colleagues, Maya and Jonas, were arguing about a mutual friend, Dan. Dan had come across some genuinely worrying signs that his wife was having an affair -- unexplained absences, a pattern of evasiveness -- and responded by quietly disabling the location-sharing they both used, avoiding a particular colleague of hers, and steering conversations away from the topic whenever it drifted close. A year later, he sincerely and spontaneously describes his marriage as rock-solid and seems surprised anyone ever worried. Maya called this a textbook case of self-deception: Dan intentionally arranged his environment so as to end up believing something he had strong evidence against. Jonas denied that anything deserving the name deception occurred. I'll reconstruct the exchange as best I can.

(1) Jonas opened with the classic paradoxes: deception requires a deceiver who believes p and a victim who comes to believe not-p, so self-deception seems to require Dan to hold contradictory beliefs at once, which is incoherent; and even spread over time, Dan cannot intentionally set out to believe what he currently believes to be false, so the intention condition fails too. (2) Maya replied that the intention condition is satisfiable at one remove: Dan's evidence-avoidance only makes sense as a strategy if he knew, at some level, that looking closer would disconfirm what he wanted to believe, and that foreknowledge does the work of the deceiver's knowledge -- nobody needs to believe a contradiction at any single moment. (3) Jonas countered that on her loosened standard, self-deception collapses into ordinary motivated reasoning and wishful thinking, and we lose any grip on why deception talk seemed apt; he offered a thought experiment of his own: imagine a "confidence pill" that makes you fully convinced, for the rest of your life, of whichever proposition you are sincerely entertaining when you take it -- someone who pops the pill while thinking of their spouse's fidelity is plainly not deceiving themselves but merely manipulating their own brain, like self-administered hypnosis, and Dan's slow version differs only in mechanism. (4) Maya said the pill case actually supports her: it shows self-deception is possible without simultaneous contradictory beliefs, since the only difference between the pill-user and Dan is speed, and if we deny that the pill-user is self-deceived we have to say something bizarre -- namely, that whether you've deceived yourself depends on how long the trick takes. (5) Jonas's last move was to insist the pill case shows the opposite: that at the limit, the concept of self-deception breaks down precisely because there is no persisting "deceiver" who retains the truth, and Dan's case is interesting only because he might retain it -- which is exactly the homuncular picture Maya needed to avoid.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each of the five moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or partially succeeds, and why. Then give a verdict on the two underlying questions -- whether Dan is genuinely self-deceived, and whether the pill-user is -- and identify anything important that both parties are missing.
