I spent most of last Saturday arguing with a friend about whether a government could ever justify running itself on prediction markets, and we kept circling the same two or three moves without either of us landing a clean hit. She thinks decision markets would be systematically exploitable and morally blind; I think elected officials are worse on exactly the dimensions she cares about. I want to see the argument done properly, so: write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors — call them Mara and Dev — debating whether a country should adopt futarchy, understood as the proposal that policies be chosen on the basis of conditional prediction markets forecasting some agreed-upon measure of national welfare, with markets rewarding accurate forecasts and policies implemented only when the market predicts they improve the measure.

Give each side the strongest version of their case and make them actually engage with each other's points rather than reciting prepared speeches. Mara should press the objections I found hardest to answer: that a welfare metric narrow enough to be priced invites gaming and Goodhart-style drift, that wealthy actors can move prices and thereby set policy (so the scheme quietly converts money into political power), and that conditional markets can't generate meaningful prices for policies nobody expects to be enacted anyway, so the system goes silent precisely on the bold or unpopular options where guidance is most needed. Dev should concede what genuinely needs conceding but press back: existing democracies already let wealth buy influence without the discipline of losing money for being wrong, voters face no comparable penalty for forecasting badly, and the metric problem is a problem for every form of governance, not just this one — his proposed design is a limited version, applying only to policies with measurable short-to-medium-term effects and fenced off from constitutional rights, and Mara should make him say exactly which real-world category of decision survives that fence. Around 1,200 words; at some point each character should name a specific, concrete development that would cause them to change their mind.

Since a dialogue can't issue a verdict of its own, append a brief author's note after it: state how confident you are that each side is presented at its genuine strongest rather than as a plausible-sounding simplification, and single out the one attribution or argumentative move — on either side — that you are least sure a sophisticated proponent would actually endorse.
