Alright, so I spent forty years as a field biologist and only picked up philosophy in retirement, which means I keep bumping into problems that feel like they should be easy but aren't. Here's the one keeping me up this week. In my old life, if I found a rare beetle on the first leaf I checked, I'd say: hmm, maybe beetles like this aren't so rare. Seemed like solid inductive practice. Now I read that some philosophers think the same move applies to *me*: the sheer fact that I exist as an observer is evidence for universes (or multiverses, or whatever) containing enormously many observers, and against theories where observers are scarce. And then I read that other people think this leads to absurdity -- apparently a philosopher can sit in her armchair with no equipment and become nearly certain the universe is teeming with life, before anyone even points a telescope at anything. The "presumptuous philosopher," I saw it called. In my day you at least had to *look* at a leaf.

So here's what I want. Give me your actual credence -- a number -- that the self-indication style of reasoning is basically correct, i.e., that my existing as an observer genuinely counts as evidence favoring observer-rich cosmologies over observer-poor ones, even before any astronomical observation. But don't just give me the number. Decompose it the way I'd decompose a messy population estimate: how much rides on whether there's even a well-defined "reference class" of observers I could have been sampled from (as a biologist I want to know what the *population* is supposed to be), how much on whether the presumptuous-philosopher case is a fair test of the principle or a rigged example, how much on whether the rival approach -- the one where I reason as if randomly sampled from among actual observers in my reference class, whatever that is -- fares any better or just exports the weirdness elsewhere (I gather it has its own embarrassments, something about doom arriving sooner than it should). Rough probability for each sub-question, one line of justification each, then how they combine into your final credence.

Then tell me which term in your decomposition is doing the most work -- the one where, if a smart person disagreed with you, the final number would swing the hardest -- and what sort of argument or thought-experiment would actually move you on it. And one more thing: my gut as a scientist says the armchair-telescope result is a reductio of the whole approach, but my gut also once told me continental drift was nonsense, so please say plainly whether "the conclusion feels presumptuous" is doing any legitimate epistemic work at all, or whether it's just a vibe.
