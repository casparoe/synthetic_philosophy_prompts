I run a reading group / informal seminar on social epistemology and legal theory at my uni — mostly final-year undergrads and a couple of masters students, about 13 of us in total, meeting for 75 minutes. We've been circling around the discursive dilemma (List and Pettit and so on) and I can tell it isn't landing. They nod along at the part where a group can be individually consistent and collectively inconsistent, but it stays very abstract — nobody seems to feel why it matters that a court or committee gets a different answer depending on whether it votes on the premises or on the conclusion. One student basically said "well just do whichever procedure gives the sensible outcome," which is exactly the complacency I want to shake.

So: design me an in-class activity where the group has to actually run both procedures on the same decision and watch them diverge with their own votes. I'm picturing something like a mock tribunal deciding a fictional case where liability turns on two or three separate doctrinal questions — a duty existed, a breach occurred, the breach caused the harm, that sort of thing — so that the "vote on each premise" route and the "vote on the bottom line" route can genuinely split. But I'm open to a different setup if you think there's a better one. Key thing: the participants should not know in advance that the two procedures can conflict. They should construct the votes themselves, be made to commit to outcomes under both rules, and only afterwards realise what just happened to them. Please spell out the case materials I'd need to write, the roles if any, a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, and exactly how the votes should be collected and revealed (order matters a lot here, I suspect — I don't want people anchoring on the first result).

Also: once they've seen the divergence, I'd like a second phase where they feel the next layer of the problem — e.g. that once you know how aggregation works, individuals can vote strategically on premises they actually accept, or that the choice of procedure is itself a political decision somebody has to make. Then give me a debrief plan that maps what happened onto the actual theory (premise-based vs conclusion-based procedures, where the majority inconsistency came from) and that pushes back on the "just pick the sensible outcome" line. Be honest about what will predictably go wrong — people spotting the trick, ties, someone refusing to vote sincerely, the discussion contaminating the individual judgements — and what to do about each. And finally, tell me which feature of the real dilemma the activity can't reproduce in a room of 13 people who all just heard each other argue, and what I'll need to supply by hand.
