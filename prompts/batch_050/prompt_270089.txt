I want to put two practices side by side and ask what each of them presupposes, because on the surface they seem to embody opposite attitudes towards the same problem: how we should reason about minds we cannot directly inspect.

The first practice is a methodological rule of comparative psychology, associated with the Victorian psychologist C. Lloyd Morgan and usually called Morgan's canon. Roughly paraphrased, it forbids interpreting an animal's behaviour as the product of a psychologically higher process whenever it can be adequately interpreted as the product of a lower one. So a dog's apparent guilt, or a crow's apparent planning, must be redescribed in terms of associative learning, instinct, or trial and error, wherever such a redescription is available. This rule has shaped over a century of experimental design and remains a live default in the field.

The second practice is a recent development in animal welfare law. The United Kingdom's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 formally recognises animals as sentient beings, and — following a government-commissioned scientific review — its scope was extended beyond vertebrates to cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans: octopuses, crabs, lobsters. For these animals the behavioural evidence of pain is suggestive but nowhere near conclusive, and the rationale for including them is openly precautionary: where there is a serious possibility of suffering, protect now rather than wait for proof.

My questions are these:

1. Parsimony principles in science are usually principles of theoretical economy, not claims about what is probable. Does Morgan's canon merely regulate how psychologists should talk, or does it smuggle in a substantive prior — that simpler psychological explanations of animal behaviour are more likely to be true? If the latter, what could justify that prior, given that evolution is not obviously committed to psychological minimalism?

2. The canon is conspicuously asymmetric: it prohibits higher interpretations where a lower one suffices, but says nothing against lower interpretations where a higher one would suffice. Applied to suffering, it therefore counsels withholding the attribution of pain whenever a pain-free explanation is available. What weighting of the costs of error does this presuppose — that false positives (crediting the insentient with sentience) are worse than false negatives (denying it to the sentient)? And is that weighting an empirical claim, a methodological convention, or a disguised ethical judgement?

3. The precautionary legislation appears to invert exactly this weighting, treating false negatives as the graver error. What epistemology of other minds does it thereby presuppose? Does it commit its drafters to the view that absence of proof of pain is not proof of absence — and if so, are the two practices simply rival epistemologies, or can they be reconciled?

4. Could one coherent position underwrite both practices at once — for instance, a division of labour in which something like the canon governs scientific assertion while precaution governs action under uncertainty? Or does the rationale behind the canon, if sound, extend to policy as well, so that a legislator who accepts it ought to demand proof of sentience before regulating?

5. Throughout, please distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where the practices are philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several rival views would license them equally well. Finally, sketch what each practice would have to look like if the other's presuppositions were adopted: comparative psychology run on a precautionary footing, and welfare legislation run on a strict application of the canon.

Please write your answer in British English.
