I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy (with some classics background), and my advisor has roped me into covering one 80-minute session of her undergrad course on ethics and narrative. The topic: whether you can learn anything morally from reading a life, with Plutarch as the case study. The students have read a bit of Aristotle on habituation but zero Plutarch, and my guess is most of them arrive assuming biography is either entertainment or thinly veiled propaganda.

Don't teach the session for me — plan how I should teach it. Concretely:

- The order the ideas have to come in, and the dependency structure. My own sense is that the hagiography question can't even get going until they have Plutarch's own stated method — in his prefaces he says he's writing lives rather than histories, that a small gesture or offhand joke can reveal character better than the famous battles, that a life can work as a kind of mirror for the reader's own conduct, and that he deliberately included some bad lives so readers learn what to avoid, not just what to copy. Tell me what has to be in place before what.
- In my notes I've been using a rough distinction between a template reading of a life (what should I copy?) and a diagnostic reading (what does this life show about how certain traits actually play out under pressure?). Where in the sequence should that distinction be introduced, and does it dissolve the hagiography worry or just relocate it?
- The one life to build the whole session around, and why. I'm torn between Cato the Younger (admirable but arguably too rigid to imitate, which seems pedagogically useful) and one of the negative lives like Antony. What should the carrying example have and what must it lack?
- The misconception map. I'm expecting, roughly in this order: (1) exemplarism means picking heroes and copying what they did; (2) if Plutarch moralizes or smooths things over, the Lives are just hagiography and worthless for moral learning; (3) the negative lives are just cautionary scare stories. Tell me where in the arc each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact.
- One check at the end that would show they got the question rather than just enjoyed the anecdotes.
- What I should openly refuse to cover at this level.

One more thing: in your answer, keep seperate what's broadly settled among people who work on Plutarch (e.g., that he understood his project as moral rather than strictly historical) from what's genuinely contested (e.g., how knowingly he reshapes material for edification, whether exemplarist moral education works psychologically at all). Don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
