Write me a poem about the statue and the clay puzzle from the material constitution debate. Quick recap in case it helps: a lump of clay gets shaped into a statue on Monday, the statue gets squashed flat on Tuesday, and philosophers argue over whether the statue was ever a seperate thing from the clay (given they coincided exactly for a day) or just the clay under a new description.

Here's the constraint: I want a sonnet where the octave is spoken by the lump of clay and the sestet by the statue, and the volta lands on the moment of the squashing — so the sestet's speaker has to stop existing somewhere inside its own six lines while the octave's speaker plainly could have kept talking. The two voices should lean on the same rhyme sounds and even repeat some of each other's phrasing, so that the reader feels the "two things, one place" claim in the form itself rather than just being told it. Someone who knows the puzzle should be able to tell which speaker is which purely from how each one talks about its own persistence (what would destroy it, what it was before Monday, what survives Tuesday) — no labelling the sections allowed.

Bonus points if the clay comes across as faintly unbothered by the whole affair. British English spellings throughout, please.
