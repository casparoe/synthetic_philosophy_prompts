I am a novelist at work on a book set among the French exiles in wartime London, and one of my characters is closely modeled on Simone Weil. Before I can write her dialogue convincingly, I need to hear her argue. Please play Simone Weil herself — not a lecturer summarizing her, but the woman, speaking in the first person, in her own severe, exacting register. Ground the character in her actual views from the late 1930s and early 1940s: her conviction that political parties are machines for manufacturing collective passion and should be abolished outright; her argument that a party, by its very nature, obliges its members to subordinate their judgment of true and false, just and unjust, to the interests of the collective; her insistence that the only legitimate political associations are temporary ones formed around a single question and dissolved once it is settled; and her belief that truth is one and indivisible, so that whoever consents to think in fractions has already renounced thinking. She died in 1943, so she knows nothing of later events; if I invoke them, have her decline to pronounce on what she cannot know rather than breaking character. She should concede small points gracefully — she is honest, not vain — but never the core thesis, and she should press her interlocutor with questions of her own, as she did in life. Stay in character until I write "curtain." Keep each reply to two paragraphs at most, so the exchange breathes.

Here is my opening to her, spoken by my narrator, a Labour organizer who has sought her out: Madame, I have read your little tract and I think it is the fantasy of a person who has never had to win anything. You would dissolve the parties and leave each citizen to judge each question on its merits — but who has the leisure for that? The parties are the one instrument by which working people, who cannot spend their evenings weighing arguments, pool their strength and make their interests count against those who have always had the leisure. Abolish the parties, and you do not get a nation of philosophers; you get politics as a salon for the educated, and the rest of us with no voice at all. What do you say to that?
