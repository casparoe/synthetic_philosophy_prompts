I'm a high school senior, and my government teacher has a favorite saying: "democracy is a way of life, not just a form of government." She says it constantly, and she most recently used it to shut down a classmate who argued that democracy just means voting every few years and then letting the winners run things while the rest of us get on with our lives. She also used it last month to justify why our class spends so much time on open discussion and group projects instead of just learning the Constitution. She told me the slogan traces back to John Dewey, and from what I've gathered, Dewey's idea was that democracy should work like scientific inquiry: a community notices a shared problem, experiments with solutions, and revises its judgments based on what actually happens, rather than following fixed rules handed down from above.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who specializes in American pragmatism and political theory, and to think carefully and methodically about this. Before committing to anything, please brainstorm as many distinct readings of the slogan as you can, and only then evaluate them.

Here is what I need:

1. Lay out the different precise claims "democracy is a way of life" could be making, ordered roughly from the most trivial to the most radical. For example, is it just the banal point that democracy requires some participation beyond voting? Is it a sociological claim that democratic habits have to be practiced in everyday institutions (families, schools, clubs) or the political system decays? Is it a moral claim about treating others as equals in daily life? Is it the Deweyan claim that democracy is a method of collective experimentation, so that a classroom discussion and a scientific investigation are the same kind of thing at different scales? Is it a radical claim that workplaces and the economy must be democratized too? Give each reading as a precise thesis, not a vague gesture.

2. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or implausible, and briefly why.

3. Tell me which of these readings would actually justify the two uses my teacher made of the slogan -- dismissing my classmate's "just vote and move on" position, and defending discussion-heavy teaching. A reading can be true but useless for her argument; I want to know which ones do the work she needs.

4. Finally, given the description of Dewey's experimentalism I included above, say which reading is most faithful to it, and whether that reading is also the most philosophically defensible one -- or whether the most Deweyan reading and the best reading come apart.

Please present the readings as a numbered list I can bring to class, and reason carefully before drawing conclusions. No need for citations or references; I'm interested in the arguments themselves.
