Two people I play five-a-side with had a proper row in the pub last week and it's still bugging me, so I want a referee.

Background: our team organiser, Rob, is chronically late to everything and twice this season forgot to book the pitch, leaving fifteen of us standing around in our boots. Last week a new lad turned up twenty minutes late and Rob tore into him in the group chat about respecting other people's time.

Mate one (call her Sophie) said Rob had no business saying a word. Her argument, roughly: (1) the criticism itself was fair, the new lad was in the wrong, but Rob had lost his standing to blame because he does the same thing constantly. (2) Blaming someone isn't just registering that they did wrong, it's demanding they answer to you, taking up a kind of authority over them, and you can't do that under a norm you won't apply to yourself. (3) She said she'd happily distinguish here: Rob is perfectly entitled to think the lad was late and even to note it factually, but the finger-wagging performance, the demanding an apology, that's what hypocrisy rules out.

Mate two (Dan) pushed back hard. (4) He said "who are you to talk" is literally a textbook fallacy, tu quoque, and if the criticism is accurate then the target benefits from hearing it regardless of the source's track record. (5) He also said Sophie's view makes standing almost impossible to come by, since everyone who blames anyone for lateness has surely been late themselves at some point, so the upshot of her position is that nobody can ever call anyone out, which seems obviously wrong. (6) And he pressed her on a case: what if Rob had opened with "look, I'm a nightmare for this myself, but..."? Either the hypocrisy vanishes with one sentence of self-awareness, which makes standing weirdly cheap, or it doesn't, in which case standing is about your entire moral biography and no living person qualifies.

Sophie's last move (7) was to say the caveat doesn't fix it, because the problem isn't what Rob says, it's that he's not genuinely committed to the norm he's enforcing, and saying "I'm bad at this too" while changing nothing just proves the point. Dan (8) replied that this makes standing depend on unknowable inner states, whereas the new lad has no way to inspect Rob's "genuine commitment", and surely what can or can't be demanded of you shouldn't hinge on something invisible.

Who's right about which points here? I'd like a move-by-move verdict rather than just who wins overall, and flag anything both of them seem to be missing. Also, since some of this strikes me as genuinely contested ground, please say how confident you are in your verdicts and single out the point you're least sure of.
