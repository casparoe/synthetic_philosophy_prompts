So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last week something happened that I keep chewing over. I was finishing a piece on deep-sea mining and, being conscientious for once, I re-derived a figure I'd taken from a consultancy report -- the estimate of how much nickel the clarion-clipperton zone deposits could yield per year. I got a number, checked it twice, felt good about it. Then a colleague of mine, who is honestly about as numerate as I am and who I trust on exactly this sort of thing, did the same sum independently and got something different. We sat down, and it turned out she'd misread one of the assumptions in the methodology, so in that instance I was right. But for about an hour there, before we found the discrepancy, I just... kept believing my number. And afterwards I couldn't decide whether that hour of stubbornness was a failure of rationality or actually the sensible response, given that I knew things about my own checking process that I couldn't have quickly conveyed to her.

Anyway this maps onto stuff I've been reading for a possible piece -- the whole literature on peer disagreement and higher-order evidence, and whether it's ever rational to believe something while simultaneously believing that the evidence available to you doesn't support believing it. Epistemic akrasia, I think it's called. Here's what I actually want: your credence that epistemic akrasia is ever rational -- that a person can be fully rational in holding a belief while rationally judging that their total evidence, including the higher-order stuff about disagreement and their own fallibility, tells against it. Give me a number, but I care more about the decomposition. Break it into the sub-questions you think the answer rides on: something like whether the 'split the difference' view of peer disagreement is correct, whether first-order evidence can legitimately screen off higher-order evidence or whether the two get lumped together and the higher-order always wins ties, whether rational belief and rational belief-about-your-own-belief can even come apart without the concept of rationality just fragmenting into two different notions. Probability for each, a line of justification, and then how they combine into your final number.

Two more asks. First, tell me which term in your decomposition you're least confident in, and what argument or evidence would shift it -- this is genuinely useful to me because I need to know who to ring for the piece. Second, say what your view implies for my hour of stubbornness: was I being irrational before we found her mistake, or was I fine? If it matters, my own credence that akrasia is sometimes rational is maybe 0.65, and the friend I argued about this with down the pub is at like 0.1 -- so if you can, diagnose where our disagreement most likely lives in your decomposition, because neither of us could figure it out.
