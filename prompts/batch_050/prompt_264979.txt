A friend who has been reading about the units of selection debate walked me into this argument, which seems to prove too much, and I cannot locate the exit. (1) Cumulative natural selection can build adaptations only at a level of organization whose variants are replicated with high fidelity; if a fitter variant is not faithfully transmitted, selection cannot accumulate anything at that level. (2) Organisms are not replicated: an organism's offspring are not copies of it, and in sexual species each organism is a one-off combination dismantled by meiosis. (3) Groups fare no better, since their membership is continually reshuffled. (4) Genes, by contrast, are copied across generations with extremely high fidelity, so the gene is the only genuine unit of selection, and talk of individual- or group-level selection is at most a bookkeeping convenience. (5) But recombination breaks genes apart too: crossing over occurs within genes, so what is actually transmitted intact is only ever a fragment of no principled length, and mutation guarantees that even fragments are not copied perfectly. (6) By the standard of (1), then, no level of organization replicates its variants with the required fidelity, so cumulative selection can build adaptations at no level at all. (7) Yet adaptations plainly exist. (8) So (1) must be weakened or rejected -- but if we weaken it enough to avoid the absurdity, it is no longer clear why it excluded organisms and groups in (2) and (3), and the case for the primacy of the gene collapses with it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced to choose, I would rather keep the idea that genes are special in some sense than the strict fidelity requirement, but I want to know what I am paying for it.
