So I'm a retired geochemist, forty years in labs and field work, and about two years ago I fell down a philosophy rabbit hole and landed on C.S. Peirce (which I now know is pronounced like purse, after embarrasing myself at a book club). I run a little reading group at our public library -- mostly other retirees, a couple of curious neighbors, one very sharp teenager -- and I've volunteered to do a session on Peirce's pragmatism. Not the whole system, god no. Just three connected things: the pragmatic maxim (meaning of a concept = the conceivable practical effects of its object), the business about how belief gets fixed and why inquiry starts from real doubt rather than pretend Cartesian doubt, and the fallibilism -- the idea that we should hold even our best findings as revisable.

Here's the thing: I don't want you to explain Peirce to me, I've read the two big essays twice and argued about them plenty. I want you to plan how I should explain him, in about an hour, to people whose entire exposure to the word pragmatist is politicians calling themselves pragmatic when they mean unprincipled. Tell me:

1. The order the pieces have to come in. My instinct is doubt-and-belief first, then the four methods of fixing belief (tenacity, authority, a priori, science), and only then the maxim as the payoff -- but maybe the maxim should come first as a hook? Tell me what depends on what and why, and push back on my ordering if it's wrong.

2. One carrying example. I was going to use his own bit about a diamond being hard -- that being hard just means it would scratch things, would resist, even if it never gets tested -- because as a chemist I love it. But is it the right one for this crowd, and if so what does the example need to have (and need to NOT have) to carry both the maxim and the fallibilism? If you think something homelier would work better -- my group knows nothing of mineralogy -- say so and pick it.

3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll form. I'm bracing for at least three: that pragmatism means truth is whatever works for you, that the maxim is just the verificationism the logical positivists did later, and that fallibilism means we can't know anything so why bother. For each one, tell me where in the session to head it off, and how, before it hardens.

4. One question I can put to them at the end that shows whether the idea landed -- something where a wrong answer is diagnostic, not just wrong.

5. What to explicitly refuse to touch at this level (I'm assuming the semeiotic, the categories, scholastic realism) and the one-line way to say "that's real but not today" without sounding dismissive.

Keep it concrete -- I've sat through too many retirement-hobby lectures that are all names and dates. And no reading lists, my group barely finished the handout last month.
