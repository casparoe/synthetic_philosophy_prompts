Last week I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about whether any explanation of past events can be trusted, given that we always explain from the vantage point of knowing how things turned out. Her position was that all such explanation is storytelling: we take the outcome as fixed, sift the prior facts for whatever leads up to it, and then congratulate ourselves on having understood something, when in fact we would have constructed an equally confident story had events gone the other way. I think she's overstating it, but my defense kept collapsing into "some explanations feel legitimate and others don't." So here are my verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them.

1. After my team's product launch failed, our post-mortem concluded that the failure had been overdetermined: we'd misread churn data, mispriced the offering, and ignored a competitor's move. All true, and all visible at the time, though at the time each signal was ambiguous and had plausible innocent readings. My verdict: the post-mortem is legitimate, though I'd discount it somewhat.

2. A historian argues that by 1914 the alliance system, mobilization timetables, and arms race had made a general European war very likely, whatever the specific trigger turned out to be. My verdict: genuinely explanatory, maybe the paradigm of a good historical explanation.

3. A biography of a successful CEO argues that childhood adversity forged the resilience that explains her triumphs. My verdict: this feels like a cheat, because the identical childhood would have been deployed to explain her failure had she failed, and the biographer would have sounded just as convincing.

4. A political analyst says the election was decided by the state of the economy, citing a pattern across dozens of past elections. My verdict: legitimate, partly because the claim issues predictions forward, not just a story backward.

5. After a plane crash, investigators conclude the airline's safety culture made an accident "bound to happen sooner or later." My verdict: I'm torn. It rings true, but the near-misses that didn't end in a crash generated no such narrative, so the conclusion seems to be doing its work only after the fact.

6. The claim that had the archduke's driver not taken a wrong turn, there would have been no First World War, strikes me as wrong, while the claim that had the ambulance arrived five minutes later, the patient would have died, strikes me as fine. Both are counterfactuals about single events; I can't say why one is permissible and the other isn't.

The obvious pressure points: case 1 and case 3 look structurally identical (retrospective selection of facts that fit a known outcome) yet I want to acquit one and convict the other; and case 2's inevitability claim seems to sit awkwardly next to my rejection of the wrong-turn counterfactual in case 6, though I suspect those two actually cohere and it's something else that's in trouble. So: is there a single, precisely statable principle about what makes an explanation of a past event more than a just-so story that delivers most of these verdicts? If so, state it, motivate each clause by reference to the case that forces it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate -- I suspect I'll have to give up at least one of case 1 or case 3, and I'd rather know which than have a principle that saves everything at the price of vagueness. Finally, tell me what your principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: my grandmother's claim that her generation's character was formed by the Depression. It looks like the CEO biography transposed to a whole cohort, but it doesn't feel equally disreputable to me, and I can't tell whether that's insight or sentimentality.
