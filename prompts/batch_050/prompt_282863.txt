Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about the moral sufficiency of Pareto improvements, and we ended up further apart than when we started. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in normative ethics and social choice theory, and to settle where our disagreement actually lives. Please think carefully before answering: spend some time first brainstorming candidate counterexamples and the strongest version of each side, and only then compose the structured response specified below.

The proposition under dispute, call it P, is this: if an action makes at least one person better off and no one worse off, relative to the status quo, then that action is morally permissible. My credence in P is about 0.75. My friend's is about 0.15.

Her case against P rested on three examples. First, the undetected violation: secretly reading a stranger's diary, where the stranger never finds out and suffers no experiential cost, while I gain satisfaction. Nobody is worse off in any way the victim will ever register, she argued, yet the act is wrong, so permissibility cannot be read off welfare comparisons alone. Second, the windfall split: a surprise gift of money arrives for a household, and I give all of it to one child. The other child is no worse off than before the gift arrived, one child is better off, yet the distribution seems plainly unfair. Third, the complaint she finds most fundamental: P treats the status quo as morally privileged, since anyone currently disadvantaged cannot claim to be made worse off by an improvement that bypasses them, so P systematically licenses entrenching arbitrary or unjust baselines.

I replied that these cases equivocate on worse off: the diary victim is worse off in preference-satisfaction or autonomy terms even if she never notices, and the passed-over child is worse off relative to what she could reasonably expect once the gift exists. My friend countered that I was stretching worse off to mean whatever is needed to save P, rendering it unfalsifiable. She then offered a repair she calls the safety clause: a Pareto improvement is permissible only if it does not worsen anyone's position relative to what fairness, desert, or existing entitlements would otherwise recommend. I objected that this clause does all the work and reduces P to a triviality; she said that is the point, and that a correct principle about welfare improvements should be nearly trivial once the baseline is handled.

Please give your own credence in P, decompose the reasoning, and locate our disagreement. Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. Setup and disambiguation (roughly 150 words): state the most defensible reading of better off and worse off for purposes of evaluating P, and flag any remaining ambiguity that affects the verdict.
2. Your credence and headline reasoning (roughly 100 words): your number for P and a compressed statement of why.

Decomposition (roughly 400 words): the sub-questions your credence depends on — for example, whether moral permissibility supervenes on welfare comparisons alone, whether worse off can be given a non-trivial reading that survives the counterexamples, whether baselines carry independent moral weight, and how often P and rival principles diverge in practice — with a rough probability for each and an account of how they combine into your headline number.
4. Where our disagreement most likely lives (roughly 200 words): given credences of 0.75 and 0.15, identify which sub-question my friend and I most plausibly disagree about, and say which of us is better positioned on it.
5. The safety clause (roughly 150 words): assess my friend's repair. Does it save what is true in P, gut it, or both? Give your credence that some suitably hedged descendant of P is true.
6. What would move your number most (roughly 100 words): the single argument, case, or consideration that would most change your credence in P, and in which direction.
