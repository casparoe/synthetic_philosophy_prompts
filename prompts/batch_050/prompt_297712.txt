I work at a mid-sized AI lab and somehow ended up running the internal review for proposed safety mitigations before they go to leadership. The pattern at this point is depressingly predictable: someone proposes a mitigation (more red-teaming, refusal training, a capability eval before release, whatever), and within one meeting somebody says the magic words -- this creates an alignment tax, we'll ship later and worse than the labs that skip it, and the safety gain is moot because users just switch to the less careful model. Sometimes that's obviously true, sometimes it's obviously a lazy veto, and we have no consistent way to tell which is which. I've read the standard arguments about the competitiveness constraint (labs only adopt safety measures that don't put them at a serious disadvantage relative to rivals, so real safety work has to be cheap in capability/dollars/delay or it just doesn't happen), and I buy the logic, but it's a principle, not a method.

Can you turn the alignment-tax/competitiveness idea into an actual decision procedure my team could run on any proposal in, say, a 90-minute review? I want the questions in order: what we ask first to establish whether the claimed tax is even real (and how you'd measure a capability or time-to-market cost when both are contested), how to distinguish a temporary tax from a permanent one, when a cost can be neutralized by getting rivals to share it (standards, regulation, coordinated commitments) versus when that's cope, how to check the other side of the ledger -- whether the measure buys real safety or just legible safety -- and where the procedure has to stop and hand things to an actual value judgment because the tradeoff isn't empirical. Flag explicitly which steps can't be de-judgment-ed.

Then run it on two real-ish proposals: (1) a harmlessness finetuning pass that costs roughly 1-2% on the benchmarks customers look at and two weeks of delay, and (2) a mandatory pre-release dangerous-capability evaluation that would hold a frontier launch back six weeks and that no competitor currently runs. Ideally one of these should come out the opposite of what my team instinctively wants -- I'd rather see the procedure bite than rubber-stamp us. And tell me what the procedure fundamentally can't settle, so nobody cites it as having decided more than it did.
