My uncle has a habit of ending political arguments at family dinners with "my country, right or wrong." Last week he deployed it to shut me down when I said I was embarrassed by our government's foreign policy and didn't feel like defending it to my friends abroad. The annoying thing is that the phrase feels like it could mean anything from something completely innocent to something genuinely chilling, and I want to go into the next dinner with the ambiguities pre-mapped.

So: what are the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making? I want the full menu, from the trivial to the radical. For example, at one end it might just mean something descriptive like "this is my home and that's not conditional on its behavior," which seems like a platitude nobody would argue with. At the other end it might mean "I will support my country's policies even when they're unjust," which sounds monstrous. But I suspect there are several distinct readings in between that people blur together -- e.g. one where it's about what you say to *outsiders* vs. what you say at home (my uncle seemed to want that one), one where it's about staying vs. leaving, one where it's really about love being unconditional but obedience not being. And there's the folk-completed version of the line people sometimes add -- something like "if right, to be kept right; if wrong, to be set right" -- which seems to flip the meaning entirely.

Also, a lot of the work seems to be done by the word "my." What relationship is "my country" being modeled on in each reading -- a parent you're loyal to no matter what, a sports team you root for, a spouse, a joint project you're a stakeholder in, a body you're an organ of? Different analogies seem to license totally different conclusions, and I'd like them disentangled.

For each reading: state it precisely, say whether it's a platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or actually false/dangerous, and then tell me whether it would actually support my uncle's conversational move (shutting down criticism addressed to foreigners) or only some weaker use. Which reading do people who reach for this phrase probably mean, and is that the one that would justify the move, or is there a motte-and-bailey thing going on?
