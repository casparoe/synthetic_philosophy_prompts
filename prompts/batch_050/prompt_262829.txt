I teach a second-year epistemology seminar (about 18 students, one 75-minute session) and I want an activity that makes them feel the Condorcet jury theorem in their gut before they ever see a formula. Design a game or sequence of estimation/judgment tasks where each person's accuracy is honestly mediocre but the majority vote does noticably better than almost everyone individually. Then I want a second phase where you quietly break one of the theorem's assumptions -- I'm thinking correlated errors, e.g. a shared misleading hint that sways everyone the same way, or pushing competence below 50% -- so they watch the "wisdom of the crowd" flip into systematic folly and have to figure out what changed. Give me: the rules and materials, a scoring scheme that doesn't involve money, and crucially how to pick the tasks so individual competence really does land just above 50% (this seems like the fiddliest part, calibration is everything). Then a debrief that maps each phase onto the theorem's assumptions (individual competence, independence, sincere voting) and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce -- with 18 people the effect is modest, and real juries deliberate, which breaks independence in ways a voting game hides. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
