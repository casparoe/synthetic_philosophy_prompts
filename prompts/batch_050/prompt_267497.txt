Here's an argument a friend in my cohort put together after we covered rational herding in a reading group, and it's been bugging me for weeks because the conclusion looks absurd but I can't find the leak. The set-up is the standard sequential model: a panel has to decide whether to approve something (say, whether a new drug is safe enough to licence). Each member recieves a private signal about the drug's quality — signals are independent conditional on the truth and all equally reliable — and the members announce approve or reject one at a time, in a fixed order, each seeing every previous announcement but nobody else's signal. (1) A rational agent announces whatever the balance of all the evidence available to them favours. (2) Observing a predecessor's announcement is evidence about that predecessor's signal; since everyone's signals are equally reliable, one announcement is worth roughly one signal in the scales. (3) So once approve-announcements outnumber reject-announcements by two or more, your own signal — whatever it says — cannot tip the balance, because even the strongest contrary signal offsets at most one announcement. (4) From that point on, every rational agent announces with the majority of predecessors regardless of what they privately know; later agents can work this out too, so their announcements carry no new information, and the imbalance never corrects itself. (5) Hence the panel's final verdict contains at most the informational content of the first couple of members' signals; the private evidence of everyone else is, quite correctly, thrown away. (6) But adding more experts with independent evidence to a decision process cannot leave the group with less information than it started with — information can't hurt. (7) So at least one of (1)–(5) must be wrong, and the sequential procedure can't really be discarding nearly everything the panel knows.

The thing making me say it proves too much is that the same reasoning seems to licence some mad institutional conclusions — that voting by seniority is perfectly sensible, that a committee of thirty is worth its first two speakers, that all the fuss about aggregating dispersed knowledge is overblown — while (7)'s denial of that seems like something no sane model could deliver. If I had to choose, I'd most want to keep (1) and (6), since both feel close to definitional. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked compelling in the first place, and what rejecting it actually costs — and if a single premise is doing different work under two different readings, say which reading the argument needs and whether it can have it. If you think the argument is sound and (6) is the one to swallow, I'm open to that, but then I want it argued rather than asserted, with the cost made explicit. And if my whole framing rests on a confusion somewhere, say so plainly instead of politely playing along. Please answer in British English, and finish by naming the premise or inference you'd reject in the form \answer{...}.
