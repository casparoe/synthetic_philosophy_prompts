Having retired from a career in population biology, I have recently become interested in the philosophy of social kinds. I am accustomed to treating cohorts as scientifically respectable when they support prediction and causal explanation, yet I remain unsure whether labels such as Boomer or Gen Z pick out anything comparable, or whether they merely carve a continuous population at administratively convenient points. Construct a thought experiment that isolates this question. Imagine two societies whose present-day populations are empirically indistinguishable in every ordinary respect: the same distributions of age, health, attitudes, habits, incomes, and social outcomes, and the same correlations between those variables. In one society, however, membership in a particular birth interval is unified by a shared causal history that leaves a latent, counterfactually significant disposition; in the other, the corresponding interval has been drawn arbitrarily and has no such causal unity. The difference must not arise from self-identification, collective memory, institutional discrimination, or the social effects of the generation label itself, and the scenario should avoid extravagant science fiction.

Present the thought experiment in no more than one page, making the relevant causal histories vivid without stipulating in advance which society has a genuine generation. Then analyse it from both sides. Explain why a modest realist about social kinds might say that the first birth interval constitutes a real generation because it supports explanation and counterfactual inference, while the second is only a classificatory convenience. Also explain the opposing objection: that a shared origin may create a historically bounded group, or perhaps a historical individual, without creating a kind that supports genuinely general truths. Identify precisely which feature of your scenario is doing the philosophical work, say what happens if that feature is removed, and indicate how someone could resist your preferred interpretation without denying the facts of the case. Conclude by explaining what the thought experiment suggests about familiar generational labels in actual societies, and what kind of evidence would be required to show that such a label tracks more than an arbitrary age band.
