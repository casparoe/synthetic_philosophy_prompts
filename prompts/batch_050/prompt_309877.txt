I host a podcast about how language shapes public life, and next week I'm interviewing a linguist whose new book argues that the most effective political messaging works through presupposed content rather than asserted content. Her central claim is that hearers accommodate presuppositions basically by default -- we just quietly add the assumed stuff to the common ground without scrutiny, whereas asserted content gets evaluated and often rejected. So smuggling a claim in as a presupposition is, on her view, a way of bypassing the listener's critical filter entirely.

I want questions that actually push on the theory, not book-promo softballs. A few things I'm hoping the sequence will do:

- Start by making her state the mechanism precisely. When someone says something that presupposes P and P isn't already common ground, what exactly has to go right for the hearer to accommodate it? I want to pin her down on the conditions under which accommodation *fails* -- cases where the audience balks, objects, or simply can't make sense of the utterance. Her book has a chapter claiming accommodation is nearly automatic, so I want to know what her theory predicts about the balk cases, and whether "sometimes people push back" is something her view can explain or has to explain away.
- Press her on the projection problem. Presuppositions famously don't survive every embedding -- sometimes they project out of conditionals, negations, questions, attitude reports, sometimes they don't, and the pattern is messy. If accommodation is the free, automatic process she says it is, why does it pattern so irregularly? I want a question that forces her to say whether projection facts are a problem for her publicity-friendly story or independent of it.
- Get at the evidence question. Her view implies presupposed content is processed differently from asserted content -- absorbed rather than weighed. What would she point to to show that's real and not just a nice metaphor? And what's the strongest result or phenomenon she'd concede cuts the other way?
- End with something about the ethics/design upshot: if she's right, does she think journalists (like me, awkwardly) are doing something manipulative every time we let an interviewee's presuppositions slide past unchallenged? And would she want, like, a norm that interviewers should make presuppositions explicit -- or would that be pointless because accommodation can't be consciously resisted anyway?

Give me a sequence of maybe 6-8 questions in the order I'd ask them, each with a follow-up written out for the reply she's most likely to give. Casual register is fine, its a conversational show, but the questions need to show I've done the reading.
