In my african american studies reading group we got to Du Bois's 1926 essay Criteria of Negro Art (the one that ran in The Crisis), and ever since, one member keeps ending every arguement with the slogan people quote from it: all art is propaganda. Last week she used it twice -- first to say a Black novelist we know has no business writing a book that's "just" a love story while things are this bad, and then to wave off someone who defended the novel on craft grounds. I haven't checked the exact wording of the essay myself, so maybe that's on me, but I suspect the slogan is doing more work than any single claim could bear. What are the different precise theses "all art is propaganda" could be expressing? I'm guessing there's at least: a descriptive claim that every artwork pushes some picture of how life should be whether it wants to or not, so apolitical art just propagandizes for the status quo; a normative claim that artists (maybe especially Black artists) ought to put their work in service of the cause; a claim about criticism, that art should be judged partly on its social effects rather than purely on craft; and maybe something stronger about there being no such thing as disinterested creation at all. For each reading, tell me whether it's actually plausible, whether Du Bois probably meant it (the same essay seems to care an awful lot about beauty, which sits oddly with some of these), and whether it would actually license telling the novelist off -- because I suspect the reading that's true isn't the reading she needs. Give me the menu with verdicts, not just whichever interpretation you find most charitable.
