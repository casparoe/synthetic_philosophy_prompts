I am drafting a literary political novel about a divided parliament debating whether to suppress a provocative serialized book after a riot. I need a classification instrument that will let me give the major characters genuinely different philosophies of expressive liberty rather than merely different temperaments.

Construct a taxonomy of positions on when speech may justifiably be restricted, centered on Mill’s harm principle but extending beyond its textbook form. Present the taxonomy as a decision tree with eight to eleven branch nodes. Phrase every branch as a question a character could answer without already knowing the name of any theory. The branches should distinguish, at minimum:

- whether expression receives special protection or is treated as ordinary conduct;
- whether harm, offense, indignity, moral corruption, and the risk of future harm constitute different kinds of reasons;
- how direct, probable, or imminent the causal connection between speech and injury must be;
- whether diffuse harms produced by accumulation, repetition, or technological amplification count;
- whether truth, speaker intent, audience rationality, and the availability of counterspeech matter;
- whether the same standards bind legislatures, courts, publishers, digital platforms, employers, and private associations.

Give the tree eight to ten leaves. Use descriptive names for recognizable positions—for example, classical harm-only liberalism, probabilistic-risk liberalism, speaker-autonomy liberalism, audience-autonomy liberalism, democratic self-government, dignity or anti-subordination, offense-balancing, legal moralism, paternalism, emergency consequentialism, and institution-sensitive contextualism—but do not force every position into this list if the logical space suggests a better division. If two recognizable views cannot be separated by the tree, leave them together and explain what further question would separate them. Also identify any coherent-looking combinations of answers that have no obvious defenders, and say whether each is unoccupied because it is incoherent, morally unattractive, or simply neglected.

Then test the tree against six incidents from the novel:

1. A pamphlet names several officials as traitors and urges readers to make them afraid to leave their homes.
2. A satirical novel portrays a minority as parasitic but advocates no action; afterward, online mobs use passages from it to organize harassment.
3. During an epidemic, a popular commentator knowingly circulates a false rumor that a medicine is contaminated.
4. A journalist truthfully publishes a politician’s private correspondence, gravely humiliating the politician’s innocent family.
5. A blasphemous play causes profound offense and social anger but no credible threat of violence.
6. A publisher’s recommendation system repeatedly steers readers from moderate grievance literature toward militant material, substantially increasing aggregate danger even though no individual recommendation is inflammatory.

For each incident, state which leaves permit, prohibit, or suspend judgment on restriction, and identify what further facts would alter the classification. Treat restriction broadly enough to include criminal punishment, civil liability, compelled removal, refusal to publish, and deliberate reduction of algorithmic circulation, while making clear where these measures diverge morally.

Conclude with a dramatic stress test: select two pairs of positions that ordinarily resemble one another but sharply diverge on at least one incident, and explain how each disagreement could generate a revealing confrontation between characters.
