Textbooks, survey courses, and museum timelines almost universally mark 476 CE as the year the Roman Empire fell: the year Odoacer deposed the boy-emperor Romulus Augustulus and declined to install a successor in the West. Yet the same books that draw this bright line teach, a chapter later, that the Roman Empire did not end until 1453, that the people of Constantinople called themselves Romans throughout those intervening thousand years, that Odoacer sent the western imperial regalia to Constantinople and formally ruled Italy in the eastern emperor's name, that the Roman Senate continued to meet and consuls continued to be appointed for decades after the supposed fall, and that Charlemagne was crowned Roman Emperor in 800 without anyone apparently finding the title absurd. Contemporaries, by most accounts, did not experience 476 as a cataclysm; it became one retrospectively.

What does this standard periodization presuppose? If it presupposes that a state persists so long as some continuous institutional and self-identifying successor exists, then "Rome fell in 476" is simply false on the convention's own terms, and the real date is 1453. If instead it presupposes that a political community's life is tied to a particular territory or capital, then the eastern empire was never Rome at all -- which its inhabitants, its laws, and its institutions would have denied. Or does the convention quietly presuppose a retrospective criterion, on which what counts as the end of a civilization depends on what later developments the historian wants to explain -- the emergence of medieval Western Europe, say -- so that 476 is a fall for the West and a non-event for everyone else?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of political identity and historical rupture behind the textbook convention, and say which rival theories it rules out. Is the convention even internally consistent, given the other facts the same textbooks teach? Could several different theories of state persistence license the same periodization, leaving it philosophically underdetermined? Consider, too, the alternatives on offer: Henri Pirenne's thesis that the ancient Mediterranean world survived the Germanic invasions intact and was broken only by the Arab conquests of the seventh century; the "transformation of the Roman world" paradigm associated with the study of Late Antiquity, on which there was no fall to date at all; and the older Gibbonian narrative that ends the story in 1453. What would have to change in our periodization -- and in what we take periodization to be -- if we adopted each of these? Finally: is drawing a line at 476 really a claim about when Rome ceased to exist, or merely a pedagogical device -- and if the latter, does a mere device presuppose anything at all?
