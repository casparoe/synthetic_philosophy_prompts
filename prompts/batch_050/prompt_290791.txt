I write a weekly science-and-ideas column and frequently have to decide whether new research justifies describing an animal, artifact, model, organization, or collective system as intelligent. I need a reusable editorial procedure I can run in about twenty minutes before proposing wording to an editor. It should not attempt to settle what intelligence ultimately is. It should determine what degree of intelligence-attribution the available evidence supports and what wording is responsible.

Please operationalize this working principle, without treating it as a settled scientific definition: intelligence is a defeasible attribution warranted when flexible success persists across relevant variations of a problem and is not better explained by memorization, a fixed behavioral routine, chance, external scaffolding, or an interpreter’s generosity. The procedure should preserve what is important in that principle while making it usable by a nonspecialist. In particular, distinguish intelligence from consciousness, sentience, agency, moral status, human-likeness, and mere computational power; identify the entity to which the attribution is being made; distinguish a single achievement from a stable capacity and task-specific competence from broader intelligence; and say when distributed performance can support an attribution to a collective without implying that any individual member possesses it.

Give the questions in the order they should be asked, what would count as an adequate answer to each, where an inadequate answer stops the process, and where the procedure must hand the decision to an editor because it concerns communicative values rather than evidence. Include rules for handling missing evidence, conflicting tests, anthropomorphic language, training-data contamination, human assistance, and failures on superficially altered versions of a task. The final output should be a small set of publishable labels, such as demonstrated task performance, adaptive behavior, bounded intelligence, or general intelligence not established, together with rules for when each may be used. If those labels are inadequate, replace them with better ones.

Apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases: an octopus that solves several familiar containers and then opens a novel latch after one demonstration; a language model that exceeds the human median on a major benchmark but performs much worse on paraphrased problems and has been exposed to similar material during training; and an ant colony that reliably finds and repairs efficient routes after disruptions although no individual ant appears to represent the overall map. At least one application should reach a conclusion that would be uncomfortable in ordinary science coverage, whether by withholding intelligence from a strikingly successful system or by granting a limited form of it to an unintuitive one. Do not include citations or a bibliography; I will supply any sources separately.

Use exactly these sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths: Editorial Principle, 200–300 words; The Twenty-Minute Procedure, 600–800 words; Labels, Wording, and Sign-Off, 250–350 words; Worked Applications, 650–850 words; Where the Procedure Fails, 250–350 words; and Confidence Assessment, 120–180 words. In the final section, state how confident you are in the procedure’s main distinctions and conclusions, and single out the specific point about which you are least confident.
