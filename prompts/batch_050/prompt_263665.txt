You are an experienced philosophy teacher and workshop designer, familiar both with classical Buddhist metaphysics and with the practical realities of running seminars. I teach an undergraduate course on Buddhist philosophy at a British university — a mixed group of about 18 second- and third-year students, most of whom have done some Western philosophy of personal identity (Locke, Parfit) but keep treating anatta as if it were merely the claim that personality changes over time. We have one 75-minute seminar slot. I want an in-class activity or game that makes them feel the force of the no-self doctrine in their own deliberation before I ever put the term on the board: something where each participant curates a 'self' — a set of memories, dispositions, commitments, perhaps a name — and where the rules then quietly redistribute, copy, or erase these elements across rounds, so that by the end nobody can say, with any confidence, who in the room is whom, even though everything has run smoothly and nobody feels that anything has gone missing. Crucially, I'd like the game to carry the further Buddhist puzzle about moral responsibility: if points or small prizes are earned in early rounds, who deserves them in the final round once the traits that earned them have migrated elsewhere? Please think carefully about the design before writing, and then give me: the full rules and materials (nothing requiring preparation beyond slips of paper and envelopes); a minute-by-minute timeline; the payoff or incentive structure, which must work without real money; a debrief script that maps specific moments of the game onto the two-truths distinction — the sense in which the players functioned perfectly well at the conventional level while no persisting bearer turned up at the ultimate level — and onto the karma-and-responsibility problem; a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time (students colluding, refusing to trade, or treating it as mere make-believe) and how to handle each failure; and, finally, an honest note on which feature of the doctrine the activity structurally cannot reproduce — I suspect the first-person givenness of experience is the hard bit, but I'd like your own view. Please write in British English throughout.
