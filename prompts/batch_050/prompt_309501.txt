I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the unity of consciousness, and I want a decision tree that takes a student from the classic split-brain findings to a named position on what is going on in the commissurotomy patient. Start the tree at this shared commitment: when different stimuli are shown to the two hemispheres, the patient's behavior (the left hand drawing what the right hand cannot name, the verbal report covering only half the field, cross-cueing tricks like tapping a cheek to smuggle information across) is best explained by appealing to conscious experience rather than purely unconscious processing. From there, branch on the questions that actually divide the literature: how many subjects of experience are present after the cut; if only one, whether that subject's consciousness at a moment is unified or fragmented; whether unity requires that all of a subject's experiences be jointly accessible for report and reasoning, or merely co-instantiated; whether the right hemisphere's responses establish its own perspective or only the one subject's divided access; and whether the experimental evidence settles any of this at all.

Each node should be phrased as a question the student can answer on the basis of the cases alone, without knowing which view it leads to — so questions about what the evidence shows or what unity requires, never "do you believe in two selves?" Please build the tree from the logical space first: list the combinations of answers your questions permit, then locate the real positions in that space. Leaves I want to see include: two subjects with two simultaneous streams of experience; one subject with two non-overlapping streams that attention switches between; one subject with a single unified stream whose behavioral disunity is merely access failure; a partial-unity view on which some unity relations hold and others fail; and a deflationary or underdetermination leaf on which the case shows our ordinary concept of a unified subject has no determinate application. Mark any combination of answers that no one defends, and diagnose briefly whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If two of your leaves cannot be separated by any question answerable from the behavioral evidence alone, flag that explicitly instead of papering over it.

Format: present the tree as an indented outline, each internal node a one-sentence question with its answer options, each leaf carrying the position's name, a one-sentence gloss, and its most awkward commitment. Keep the whole instrument under 800 words.
