I'm a high school senior, and this semester my history and civics classes have unexpectedly collided on the same question. In history we've been studying cases where governments have formally apologized for past wrongs -- for instance, official apologies issued to Indigenous peoples in countries like Australia and Canada, and apologies for wartime internment and for slavery. In civics, we got into a heated argument about whether such apologies mean anything at all. The class split into two camps, and our teacher suggested that instead of a debate, where each side just tries to win, we attempt an "adversarial collaboration" and produce a joint statement both sides could sign. I'd like help drafting that statement.

The two camps are these. The first holds that state apologies for historical wrongs are genuinely meaningful and sometimes morally owed: a state is a continuing institution, not just a collection of whoever happens to be alive, so it can own up to what it did, acknowledge victims as having been wronged, and commit itself to doing better. The second camp holds that official apologies are at best empty symbolism and at worst a substitute for real action: the officials speaking today committed no wrong, the citizens being apologized to on behalf of are not guilty of anything, many victims are long dead and cannot accept or refuse the apology, and words cost a government far less than compensation or policy change. For the record, I find myself pulled toward the first camp on some days and the second on others, which is exactly why I want the statement to be fair to both.

Please draft the joint statement our collaboration would produce. I want three sections. First, a numbered list of agreed-upon claims that both camps could sign without reservation -- for example, claims about what an apology is supposed to do when a private person offers one, about whether acknowledging historical facts is different from apologizing for them, about whether an apology can have value even when no living person is guilty, and about what would make an apology hypocritical. These should be substantive, not vague platitudes like "the past matters." Second, a disagreements table with three columns: the precise point of disagreement (each side's position phrased in words that side would actually accept, not a strawman), the crux underneath it, and what each side's view predicts. Third, a section on cruxes and tests: for each crux, describe a case, observation, or argument that both sides agree would bear on it -- for instance, whether it matters to either camp how survivors and their descendants in fact respond to official apologies, or whether the two camps differ on what it would show if an apology were followed by no policy change at all -- and write down, in advance, what each side predicts the case would show and what result would count against its own position. If you conclude that part of the dispute is really verbal -- that the camps are using a word like "responsibility" or "the state" in two different senses -- identify which part and explain why, in language both camps could accept.
