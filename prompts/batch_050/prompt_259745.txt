'In his 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, Milton Friedman settled the question of unrealistic assumptions once and for all. His argument is simple: a theory must be judged entirely by the accuracy of its predictions of future events, never by the realism of its assumptions. Indeed, the more wildly unrealistic a theory's assumptions are, the better the theory, since wildly false assumptions show how much the theory explains with how little. Friedman illustrated this with physics: Galileo's law of falling bodies assumes a vacuum that doesn't exist anywhere on Earth, and it works perfectly well. So whenever someone criticizes an economic model for assuming perfect rationality, perfect information, or infinitely lived agents, they're committing a methodological blunder Friedman exposed seventy years ago. The upshot: economics should aim at prediction rather than at understanding the actual mechanism, and models built on assumptions we know are false are often superior to realistic ones.'
