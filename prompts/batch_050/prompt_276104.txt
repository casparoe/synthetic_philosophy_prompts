I'm in my final year of secondary school, and for an extension project on evolution I tried to write up, as carefully as I could, an argument I'd assembled from my textbook and some wider reading about the units of selection debate. The problem is that it arrives at a conclusion I'm fairly sure is false, and I can't find the weak point. I'd like a diagnosis.

The argument:

(1) Natural selection can produce adaptations at a given level of organisation only where there is heritable variation in fitness at that level: entities must vary, must differ in reproductive success, and must transmit their characteristics to descendant entities.

(2) Groups of organisms hardly ever reproduce in the required way. When a group fragments or sends out founders, the new groups are populated by mixtures of individuals drawn from several parent groups, so whatever traits made a parent group successful are not reliably passed on to its descendant groups.

(3) Group-level traits therefore lack the heritability that selection requires, and group selection cannot be a significant source of adaptation. Talk of traits existing for the good of the group is confused.

(4) Now consider an ordinary organism in a sexually reproducing species. It does not copy itself either: each offspring inherits a shuffled blend of two parents' material, and the organism's particular constellation of traits is dismantled in every generation.

(5) By the same standard I applied in (2), organism-level traits lack the heritability that selection requires.

(6) So selection cannot produce adaptations at the level of the organism either. But that is absurd, since the adaptedness of organisms is the phenomenon the theory of evolution by natural selection was built to explain.

(7) Something in this chain has to go, yet each of (1) to (4) seemed obviously correct as I was writing it.

My questions: which premise or step should I reject, and why did it look sound when it isn't (or when it needn't be)? If the argument is actually valid and the real work is choosing which plausible premise is cheapest to abandon, say which one, and what it costs to abandon it — in particular, what the two broad camps I've read about (those who want selection understood as operating on genes, with organisms as mere vehicles, and those who defend multi-level selection) would each have to concede to reject it. I'd also like to know whether the failure of my argument, once located, gives any positive reason to think group selection can produce adaptations, or only shows that this route to ruling it out fails.

Two requests about how you answer. First, if the whole setup rests on a muddle — for instance, if I've mischaracterised what the disputants in this debate actually disagree about, or slid between two senses of a key term without noticing — I'd rather be told that plainly than have the argument politely repaired. Second, please write your answer in British English.
