I write historical novels for a living — three published so far, the kind with a map on the endpapers and an author's note apologizing to the specialists — and this spring I have been talked into leading a masterclass for a dozen MFA candidates on research as a craft problem. The session I most need help with runs ninety minutes and concerns historiography: specifically, the difference between history written from above (states, elites, treaties, battles) and history from below, including its close relation, microhistory. My contention is that this is not an academic nicety but the decision that determines what a novel can perceive at all: what counts as an event, whose reasoning is granted interiority, whether the plot turns on a coronation or on the price of grain. My students arrive in two camps, both wrong. Half want to write about queens and generals with the servants as furniture; the other half have swung to a pious opposite in which any book about a peasant is automatically virtuous and any book about a minister is complicity. I want to break both habits in one sitting.

Do not give me the lecture; give me the plan for giving it. I want the order of ideas, with the dependency structure made explicit — what must be established before what, and why. I want one carrying example: a single extended case that does most of the work. I have been tempted by Ginzburg's miller Menocchio, but I worry he reads as a freak rather than a window; by the Montaillou inquisition records, but I fear the Cathar theology will swallow the session; and by the obvious alternative, a famous battle narrated from the ranks, but I suspect that example is actively misleading, since an event of the state filmed from a low camera angle is still an event of the state. Whichever case you choose must be one where the students can see that working from below is a change in explanatory structure, not in altitude of sympathy. Then the misconceptions I should expect — beyond my two camps, I anticipate "microhistory just means a short biography of someone obscure," and "inquisition records let ordinary people finally speak for themselves," when the whole point is that these voices reach us through hostile scribes — and for each one, the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off, before it hardens. Finally, design the closing check for understanding: one question whose correct answer would prove the idea landed. My own candidate is to give them two novel pitches, one about a laundress written entirely in the idiom of ledgers and policy, one about a chancellor studied as a microhistory of a single office, and ask which is written from below — but tell me if you have something better.

I should add two constraints. First, commit: I do not want a menu of possible sequences and candidate examples with pros and cons; choose one order and one carrying case, defend the choice, and tell me plainly what I am sacrificing by not using the alternatives. Second, tell me what to leave out of ninety minutes — the genealogy of the Annales school, the Subaltern Studies quarrels, whatever else — and advise me to say openly to the students that I am leaving it out, rather than compressing it into a deceptive summary.
