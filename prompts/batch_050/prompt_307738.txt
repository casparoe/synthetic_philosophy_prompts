So I run this little AI & society reading group at my university (mostly undergrads, some grad students who wander in, usually around 14 people, we get the seminar room for about 75 minutes). Last week we got onto the whole "can't we just tell the AI to avoid side effects?" thing, and the room split fast: half of them think penalizing an agent for changing stuff it wasn't asked to change is basically a solved design problem, and the other half (me included) kept saying no, that only sounds easy until you try to say what counts as a side effect, and we got nowhere because it's all abstract. Somebody literally said "just penalize it for changing the room" and I wanted to scream a little. What I want for next week is an activity, not me lecturing at them -- something staged where a couple of students actually play the low-impact assistant and the rest play, I dunno, people living in a shared apartment or working in an office, and the assistant's whole reward is keeping things from changing relative to some baseline. I want the "careful" players to discover on their own that the best strategy isn't being gentle, it's stopping everyone else from doing things -- hiding the cake so nobody eats it, talking people out of rearranging the furniture, that kind of thing -- and then the group has to notice that the assistant being "low impact" came out to "freezing the status quo" and that somebody had to decide which baseline counted, and that decision smuggled in values the whole time. So: give me actual rules, roles, what the payoff/points scheme is (no real money), how many rounds, and how to set it up so the sabotage emerges from the incentives instead of me hinting at it. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the baseline-choice problem (starting state vs. what would've happened without the agent vs. what the human would've wanted) and gets at why "avoid side effects" can't be defined without basically specifying what's valuable. Also tell me what's predictably going to go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing some players will just have the assistant sit in the corner doing nothing and call it a win -- and be honest about which part of the real problem this can't capture, since nobody in the room is actually being trained on a reward signal.
