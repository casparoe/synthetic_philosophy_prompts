I've been going down a rabbit hole lately comparing how different countries handle personal information, and I can't figure out whether the differences are just cultural quirks or whether they reflect genuinely different underlying philosophies. Some examples: in Germany, you can demand that Google blur out your house on Street View, data collection is opt-in by default under their privacy regime, and there's a general attitude that personal data basically belongs to the person it's about. In the US, it's almost the opposite -- arrest records and mugshots are public, court filings are open to anyone, voter registration data is semi-public, and the default assumption seems to be that transparency is good and if you want something hidden you need a special reason. And then China has social credit style systems where the state itself aggregates tons of behavioral data to score or nudge citizens, which neither Germany nor the US would ever tolerate.

Here's my question: what does each of these regimes presuppose philosophically? Like, what theory of privacy, or of the relationship between citizen and state, does each one only make sense under? I have some half-formed guesses -- maybe the German model presupposes something like privacy as a facet of personal dignity or identity, so that information about you is partly *constitutive* of you, whereas the American model presupposes privacy as a kind of property or liberty interest that you can trade away, and publicity as a check on state power (you can't have secret courts if trials are open). And the Chinese model seems to presuppose that a person's trustworthiness is a legitimate object of state measurement, which sounds almost behaviorist to me. But I'm probably projecting. Do these three systems presuppose rival views of what a person is, or could they all be reconciled under one theory of privacy with different practical tradeoffs? And where is each system just philosophically underdetermined -- i.e., where would several very different theories of privacy license the same practice, so we can't read any deep commitment off of it?

Please answer in American English, and feel free to push back on my framing if you think I've mischaracterized any of the three systems.
