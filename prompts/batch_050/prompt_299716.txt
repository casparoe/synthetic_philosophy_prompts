I'd like your credence on a historiographical question, with the reasoning decomposed. Consider the contrastive question at the heart of the Great Divergence debate: why did sustained industrial growth begin in late-eighteenth-century Britain rather than in, say, the Yangzi Delta? Historians disagree not only about the answer but about whether the question is well posed -- whether picking Britain and China as the contrast pair smuggles in assumptions (that nations are the right units, that there was a single divergence event to explain, that the counterfactual of a Chinese industrial breakthrough is coherent) that distort whatever explanation follows.

Please state your credence, between 0 and 1, that this question is well posed in the following specific sense: that a correct and non-misleading causal explanation of the divergence could in principle be phrased as an answer to it, at some level of abstraction the historian gets to choose. Then decompose your number into the sub-questions it depends on: for example, the probability that the nation-state framing (rather than regions like England and the Yangzi Delta, or Europe and China as wholes) materially biases explanation; the probability that there is a determinate contrast to explain at all rather than a range of outcomes underdetermined by the evidence; and the probability that counterfactual claims about Chinese industrialization are evaluable enough to constrain answers. Give a rough probability and one line of justification for each, show how they combine, identify the term your final number is most sensitive to, and say what historical evidence or argument would move it most.
