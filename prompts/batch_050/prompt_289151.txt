I’m a journalist covering science and ideas, currently working on a long feature about public trust in expertise — why people accept what scientists, doctors, and reporters tell them, and why that trust has frayed. As background reading, I picked up a popular book on the psychology and philosophy of belief, and I want to make sure I don’t launder its simplifications into my piece. Here is how it summarizes a debate among epistemologists about testimony, the ordinary business of learning from what other people say:

“Philosophers who study how we learn from others divide into two camps, a division that goes back to David Hume on one side and Thomas Reid on the other. Reductionists hold that testimony never produces justification on its own: to be justified in believing what someone tells you, you must be in a position to check the claim for yourself, at least in principle — otherwise you are simply taking their word on faith. Anti-reductionists reply that we are entitled to believe whatever people tell us, because understanding and trusting speech is built into what human beings are. The dispute is really about how gullible a rational person is permitted to be. And since no one could actually live as a reductionist — you would have to personally re-verify everything from your own birthday to the shape of the Earth — reductionism collapses into absurdity, and anti-reductionism wins by default.”

My instinct is that both positions are being made to look sillier than any careful philosopher would defend: the reductionist as described seems committed to an impossible standard of individual verification, and the anti-reductionist as described seems committed to believing obvious lies. But I can’t locate precisely where the caricature enters, and some of it may be perfectly fair. So: which parts of this summary accurately represent the two positions, which parts distort them, and does the conclusion — that one side wins by default — survive once the distortions are removed? Finally, a professional question: does the actual philosophical dispute, properly understood, bear at all on the practical judgments I make daily about how much trust to place in sources, or is it about something else entirely?
