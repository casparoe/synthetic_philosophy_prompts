I chair the peer-review and credentials committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital, and next week we must decide the case of Dr. Elena Marsh, a general surgeon with fourteen years of unblemished practice. On an overnight shift last March, covering for a colleague who had been sent home ill, Dr. Marsh proceeded with a scheduled bowel resection without waiting for a final set of laboratory values that hospital policy makes mandatory before incision. Her reasoning, which she does not dispute, was that postponement would have meant a six-week rebooking delay given our surgical backlog, that the patient appeared stable, and that she judged the risk acceptable. The patient suffered a serious but nonfatal complication that the missing results would very likely have flagged. The case comes to us for an individual disposition: no action, formal censure with supervised practice and a defined remediation period, or suspension of privileges.

Dr. Marsh contests none of the facts. Her defense is that singling her out for blame is unjust. She was working a double shift because we are chronically understaffed; she faced a backlog we created and throughput pressure we imposed; and, as she puts it, any competent surgeon placed in exactly her situation, with her training and her conscientiousness, would have made the same call. Her decision, she argues, was the inevitable product of circumstances the institution controlled; responsibility for it lies with the system, not with her, because she could not, in any meaningful sense, have done otherwise.

The committee is split. One faction agrees with her: blame requires genuine alternatives, and where an action follows necessarily from the agent's situation and character, sanctioning the individual is scapegoating; we should sanction the staffing model instead. The other faction replies that Dr. Marsh deliberated, knew the rule, and chose; acting from one's own deliberation without external compulsion is the only freedom responsibility has ever required, and waiving the sanction would gut a policy to which every other surgeon is held. A senior member, hoping to break the deadlock, circulated a passage from Section VIII, "Of Liberty and Necessity," of David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding:

"I hope, therefore, to make it appear, that all men have ever agreed in the doctrine both of necessity and of liberty, according to any reasonable sense, which can be put on these terms; and that the whole controversy has hitherto turned merely upon words."

His covering note claims the two factions agree on every fact and differ only over the word "free," so we should stop philosophizing and simply vote on what the hospital needs.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting, roughly 700–900 words excluding the passage. It should: (1) recommend a concrete disposition, with a time frame and any conditions, and say what, if anything, the committee should separately demand of the hospital administration; (2) interpret Hume's claim — what he means in saying both parties have always agreed, once the terms are taken in a "reasonable sense" — and test it against our actual dispute: if the disagreement in this case is not merely verbal, locate precisely where the substantive disagreement lies (for instance, in what sanctions are for, or in what kind of "could have done otherwise" blame presupposes); (3) say whether accepting Hume's reconciliation would change the right disposition here, or whether the case turns on questions his argument leaves open; and (4) give an honest account of the strongest consideration favoring the disposition you reject, including Dr. Marsh's point that the institution engineered the very choice it now proposes to punish.
