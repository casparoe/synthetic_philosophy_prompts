My coworker wrote up some notes for our AI safety reading group and one section is on 'corrigibility'. Here's his summary of it:

"Instrumental convergence means that basically any sufficiently capable agent will resist being shut down, because a switched-off agent can't achieve its goals, so self-preservation shows up for free even if nobody programs it in. The standard fix people discuss is to make the agent indifferent about shutdown — you carefully balance the utility it assigns to staying on against the utility of being turned off, so it has no incentive to grab the off switch or defend it. An agent set up like this is corrigible: it will obediently do whatever its operators tell it, including letting them modify it or turn it off. Since indifference removes the incentive problem, the remaining difficulty is basically just engineering — getting the utility numbers right."

He presents this like it's all settled textbook stuff, but the "just engineering" line seems way too confident to me, and I have a nagging feeling that "corrigible" doesn't actually mean "obedient". Can you go through it and tell me which parts are accurate, which are reasonable simplifications, and which are actually wrong or conflating different things? Specifically: does the utility-indifference trick actually work as advertised, or are there known problems with it? Is corrigibility the same as being obedient to your operators, and if not, why would the people working on this deliberately avoid just building obedient agents? And if the summary needs correcting, does his conclusion that the problem is basically solved in principle survive, or does it fall apart?
