I am a retired molecular biologist, new to philosophy, and I am trying to state my position clearly before a reading group discusses W. V. O. Quine’s “Two Dogmas of Empiricism.” I keep returning to his sentence: “Things had essences, for Aristotle, but only linguistic forms have meanings. Meaning is what essence becomes when it is divorced from the object of reference and wedded to the word.” My working commitments are these: I accept that Quine is right to move essence-talk from objects to words; I still think that, in science, some properties are non-negotiable for being the thing in question, as atomic number is for gold; I accept confirmational holism for mature theories, namely that sizeable bodies of theory rather than isolated sentences meet experience; I regard elementary logic and arithmetic as unrevisable in principle; I expect radical translation to leave meaning underdetermined, yet I trust field linguistics to settle objective synonymies well enough for practical work; and I use an analytic/synthetic ledger in the laboratory, marking some statements as definitional housekeeping and others as genuinely vulnerable to experiment. Audit this set rather than lecturing me: identify which commitments genuinely cannot stand together, which merely appear to conflict, what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost me, and say whether the quoted passage forces the hard choice or only exposes it. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
