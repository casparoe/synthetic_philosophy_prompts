Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about how far expected-value reasoning should be trusted when probabilities or stakes become extreme. I accused her of inconsistency; she insisted she was following a principled line and challenged me to find it. Here are her verdicts on the four cases we discussed. In every case, the probabilities are stipulated to be objective and known, and the payoffs to be exactly as described — so no rule of the form "distrust the person making the offer" is available as an explanation.

Case 1: a certain $10,000, or a 1% chance of $1,050,000. She takes the gamble.

Case 2: a certain $1,000,000, or a 50% chance of $2,100,000. She takes the gamble.

Case 3: a certainty of saving one life, or a one-in-a-billion chance of saving a trillion lives. She takes the gamble, remarking that declining would mean throwing away a thousand expected lives.

Case 4: a certainty of saving ten lives, or a one-in-10^30 chance of an outcome that she agrees would, if realized, be 10^40 times as good as saving a life. She declines, and says she would be a fool to do otherwise.

I plan to put a fifth case to her tomorrow: a certainty of saving one hundred lives, or a 1% chance of an outcome 10^30 times as good as saving a life.

Please do the following. First, predict her verdict on the fifth case and give your confidence. Second, articulate at least two decision rules, each consistent with all four earlier verdicts, that diverge on the fifth: I suspect both a floor below which probabilities are treated as negligible and a ceiling on the payoff magnitudes she is willing to take seriously would fit her record, and I want each rule stated precisely enough that its prediction is unambiguous. Third, say which rule she is most plausibly running, and defend the choice — including whether the ceiling, if that is your pick, is really bounded utility in disguise or something structurally different. Fourth, describe the sixth case that would best discriminate between your two leading candidate rules, and explain what each possible verdict on it would establish. Finally, if her pattern of verdicts strikes you as simply incoherent rather than rule-governed, say so and defend that verdict instead.
