I run a little philosophy-and-politics podcast, and my guest next week has a new book out on Hannah Arendt's essay Lying in Politics (the one she wrote in the wake of the Pentagon Papers). Her book's central claim is that for Arendt, what makes modern political lying distinctive isn't that officials deceive the public — it's that the liars deceive themselves first, swallowing their own image-making until facts get replaced wholesale by a self-consistent fiction. She calls this the real scandal Arendt spotted, and she thinks everyone who treats the essay as just a denunciation of government spin has missed the point.

Draft 8–10 interview questions for me. I don't want a tour of the essay — my listeners can read. I want questions that push on the parts of her reading that seem most shaky or most interesting. A few angles I definately want covered somewhere in the set:

- Whether her "self-deceived liar" is even coherent as lying — if the deceiver genuinely comes to believe the fiction, hasn't he stopped lying and become something else (and doesn't Arendt's own framework need the liar to keep a grip on the truth he's displacing)?
- How her reading handles the obvious comparison with Harry Frankfurt's bullshit — the guy who's indifferent to truth altogether rather than at war with it. She insists Arendt's liar is importantly different; I want to know if the difference survives the self-deception point.
- Arendt's claim that factual truth is fragile and politically homeless in a way rational truth isn't — whether that still looks right now that we have instant fact-checking and it doesn't seem to help.
- The status of the truth-teller: Arendt seems to make truth-telling a kind of political action, but doesn't that drag facts into the arena of persuasion and power, which is exaclty where Arendt thought facts shouldn't live?
- Whether the Pentagon Papers really support the self-deception reading, or whether Arendt (and my guest after her) is being too charitable to people who may just have been deceiving the public while knowing better.

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not caught flat-footed. And end the sequence with a question about what textual or historical evidence would actually count against her reading — she tends to treat counterexamples as "the traditional misreading," and I'd like to pin down what could in principle dislodge the interpretation.
