I want a thought experiment built to order, on the topic of thought experiments themselves. Quick background so we're on the same page: there's a standing fight over how thought experiments in science manage to teach us anything new about the world. On one side, the "it's just an argument" camp (Norton-style): every good thought experiment can be reconstructed as an ordinary argument from premises we already accept on empirical grounds, so no mysterious new faculty gets invoked. On the other side, the rationalist camp (Brown-style): at least some thought experiments give us a kind of direct rational insight into how nature must be, where the conclusion outruns anything derivable from previously accepted premises.

Construct a fresh thought experiment in physics that these two camps are forced to disagree about. Specifically: a case the Brown camp counts as a genuine knowledge-yielding thought experiment, but which the Norton camp can't wave through as a disguised argument without doing serious reconstructive work. Constraints:

- it must be original, not a variation on the greatest hits (so no Galileo on falling bodies, no elevator, no chasing a light beam, no Maxwell's demon, no chain draped over a triangle)
- only physics an undergrad knows: mechanics, E&M, thermo. No rockets-and-relativity setups and no quantum measurement stuff
- describeable in one paragraph, without idealizations so extreme the empiricist can just dismiss the whole thing as fiction (frictionless surfaces fine, infinitely rigid bodies pushing it)
- the conclusion has to be a substantive claim about the physical world, not a definitional truth you get by unpacking concepts

After the scenario, do the full check. First, sketch what the Norton defender's argument-reconstruction would look like and say where each premise is supposed to come from empirically. Then give the Brown defender's case that the scenario delivers more than any such reconstruction captures. Tell me which feature of your case is doing the seperating work, and what happens to the verdict if you remove that feature -- does it collapse into something both camps happily classify the same way? Finally, steelman the Norton camp's counterattack: where would they try to smuggle the empirical premises back in, and does that move succeed? Be honest if the case you built ends up actually favoring Norton in the end.
