I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a piece on the sunk cost fallacy: why otherwise sensible people keep funding failed ventures, stay in bad relationships, and finish books they hate. At a conference last month, a decision theorist I interviewed pressed me with an argument that goes well beyond the usual advice to quit. If she is right, the standard injunction against honoring sunk costs does not merely tell us when to abandon failing projects; it appears to dissolve promise-keeping, retributive desert, and loyalty to our own past undertakings. I reconstructed her argument afterward, as fairly as I could, in numbered steps:

1. A rational decision at a given moment must be based solely on the prospective costs and benefits of the options currently available.
2. Resources already spent are gone no matter which option one now picks; no present choice can recover them.
3. Therefore past expenditures are irrelevant to a present decision, except insofar as they have altered the prospective payoffs of the available options (a half-built bridge is cheaper to finish than to rebuild, but that is a fact about future costs, not about the past).
4. Honoring a sunk cost just is letting past expenditures count in favor of continuing a course of action when the prospective payoffs alone would not justify it.
5. Therefore honoring a sunk cost is never rational.
6. But the same reasoning generalizes: a promise made yesterday is as unrecoverable and unalterable a past event as money spent yesterday.
7. So if (5) holds, the bare fact that one made a promise cannot by itself give a reason to keep it, apart from forward-looking considerations such as reputation and trust.
8. Likewise, the fact that a wrongdoer committed a crime is past and unchangeable, so desert, as opposed to deterrence, can give no reason to punish.
9. Therefore, if the reasoning behind (5) is sound, promise-keeping and retributive desert are rational only when independently justified by future consequences.
10. Yet at least one backward-looking consideration seems genuinely binding on its own: I take the fact that I promised to be a reason to act even when breaking the promise would go entirely unnoticed.

She concluded that I must either abandon (1) or accept that promises and desert are useful fictions propped up by their downstream effects. If forced, I would rather keep (10) than (1), but I am not confident the argument even survives as far as (7): something about the move from spent money to made promises feels slippery, though I cannot name the slip.

For the column I need a precise diagnosis, not a pep talk about quitting. Tell me which numbered step you would reject first and explain why that step looked plausible, since I want to present her position fairly. Then price the alternatives: if one instead rejects (1), or (4), or the analogical steps (6)–(8), what does each rejection cost, and does any of them preserve the everyday advice that one should walk out of a bad film one has already paid to see? In particular, say whether the analogy between expenditures and promises actually holds, or whether some feature of promises — perhaps that their whole point is to bind future action — breaks it, and whether that feature is one a purely forward-looking theory can accommodate without circularity.

Please end your answer with \answer{N}, where N is the number of the single premise or step you ultimately recommend rejecting, or \answer{sound} if you conclude that the argument is valid and its premises are all true.
