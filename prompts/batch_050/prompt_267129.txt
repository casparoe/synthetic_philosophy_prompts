Ok so my friend Dan and I got into a massive arguement last night that went til like 1am, and about two hours in we realised we'd basically re-invented the Raz vs Waldron fight about the rule of law from scratch. Here's the hypothetical we kept circling. A city replaces all its low-level adjudication — parking fines, benefit eligibility, permit refusals — with an automated system. The rules are published in advance, they're clear, they're applied identically to everyone, and every decision comes with an exact readout of which rules and which facts generated it. No discretion, no bribes, no clerk in a foul mood. But also: no hearing, nobody you can explain your situation to, no human who could actually be persuaded. Appeals go back to the same algorithm running the same rules.

Dan's position: this is the rule of law close to perfected. Raz's whole thing is that the rule of law is about law being capable of guiding conduct — prospective, clear, stable, predictable — and that it's an instrumental virtue, sharp knife style, fully compatible with the regime being awful in other ways. And the system aces Fuller's eight desiderata better than any human bureaucracy ever has, especially congruence between the declared rules and what officials actually do, which Fuller thought was the hardest one to get.

My position: it's the rule of law hollowed out into a spreadsheet. Waldron's thing is that the rule of law isn't mere regularity, it's the dignity of being treated as someone who can be given reasons and can answer back — courts, hearings, argument, a decision-maker who has to listen. Predictability isn't answerability. A perfectly reliable sorting machine doesn't govern you through law, it just processes you.

Write our argument out as a proper dialogue, except make us both sharper than we were at 1am. Real back-and-forth: each of us responds to the other's actual last point, nobody talks past anyone, no strawmen, and it shouldn't wrap up too neatly. Pressure points I want genuinely tested:

- Dan pressing me on whether I'm smuggling substantive justice into the rule of law, which Raz specifically warned turns the ideal into mush.
- Me pressing Dan on the widow whose documents were destroyed in a flood: a human system can do equity, his machine can only apply the rule.
- A fight over who actually gets Fuller — Dan treats the desiderata as a checklist his system passes, but Fuller's inner-morality-of-law idea might cut my way.
- Whether a fake hearing (you may submit a statement; a human reads it; it changes nothing) would be better than nothing or somehow worse.
- Whether we can actually locate the crux — instrumental vs. constitutive value of procedure? — or whether one of us has to concede something.
