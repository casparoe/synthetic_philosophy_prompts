I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and while reporting a piece on whether culture can explain economic outcomes, I interviewed two social scientists who ended up arguing with each other for the better part of an hour. The subject was Weber's old claim that an ethic of disciplined, worldly work helped cause the rise of modern capitalism. Here is the exchange as I recorded it:

(1) The first scholar argued that culture cannot be a cause in the required sense at all. Economic outcomes are produced by incentives and constraints, she said, and what gets called a "culture of hard work" is just the stock of rational responses people have made to their circumstances. Change the incentives and the values follow; so the values are doing no explanatory work of their own.

(2) The second replied that this treats internalized norms as epiphenomenal, when in fact they determine which incentives agents even perceive. A culture that prizes honor and one that prizes commerce can face identical price signals and respond completely differently, and the difference traces to the values, not the prices.

(3) The first answered that this is exactly why the Weber thesis is unfalsifiable. Protestant regions that prospered count as confirmation; Catholic regions that prospered, and Protestant regions that stagnated, get handled with auxiliary stories. A thesis compatible with every outcome explains none of them.

(4) The second conceded that the thesis as Weber stated it was loose, but said its modern descendants are testable: comparing otherwise similar communities on opposite sides of historical religious borders, or measuring how long differences persist after institutions converge, separates the effect of values from geography and formal rules. The first countered that persistence can run entirely through institutions — schooling systems, guilds, legal traditions — so such studies identify institutional effects, not values. The second's final move: institutions are, so to speak, values in durable form, and the institution-versus-culture distinction her opponent was leaning on does no real work.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication: who is right at each of the four moves, what considerations both parties are missing, and who is more right overall about whether a value system can be a genuine cause of economic outcomes. End your response with a final line of the form Verdict: [a short phrase naming who is more right overall].
