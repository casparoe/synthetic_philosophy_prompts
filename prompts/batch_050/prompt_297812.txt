For a term paper in my philosophy of science seminar I have been developing an argument, and I have come to suspect it proves too much, since its conclusion is one I cannot seriously accept. The argument starts from a passage in David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (Section IV, Part I), where Hume writes:

"Adam, though his rational faculties be supposed, at the very first, entirely perfect, could not have inferred from the fluidity, and transparency of water, that it would suffocate him, or from the light and warmth of fire, that it would consume him. No object ever discovers, by the qualities which appear to the senses, either the causes which produced it, or the effects which will arise from it; nor can our reason, unassisted by experience, ever draw any inference concerning real existence and matter of fact."

Taking this passage as my foundation, the argument runs as follows.

(1) Hume is right: no exercise of reason unaided by experience can establish any matter of fact. Therefore nothing beyond the observed instances themselves is available to support any projection from observed to unobserved cases.

(2) It follows that two general hypotheses which agree on every observed instance are equally well supported by the evidence gathered so far, since by (1) there is no further evidence to which either could appeal.

(3) Call an object "grue" just in case it is either examined before some fixed future time t and green, or not so examined and blue. Every emerald examined to date is grue, since each was examined before t and found green.

(4) The hypotheses "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" agree on every observed instance. By (2), they are equally well supported.

(5) These two hypotheses make incompatible predictions about emeralds first examined after t. So our total evidence equally supports incompatible predictions.

(6) The standard ways of breaking the tie all fail. Privileging "green" on the ground that green-like predicates have projected successfully in the past is itself an inductive inference of the very kind under scrutiny, and so circular. Privileging it on a priori grounds such as simplicity or naturalness violates (1). And treating the choice between predicates as a linguistic convention grants convention evidential force, which nobody accepts.

(7) Therefore no inductive projection is better supported than any rival, however bizarre: reading tea leaves is as well supported by evidence as celestial mechanics. Call this conclusion inductive nihilism.

I cannot accept (7), but each step looks defensible when I examine it in isolation, which is exactly what makes me suspect the argument proves too much. Please tell me where it goes wrong, if it does.
