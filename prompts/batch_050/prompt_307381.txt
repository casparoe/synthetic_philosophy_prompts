I am a novelist, and one of the small pleasures of my writing life is a standing game I play with an old friend, a philosopher, who sorts the world's falsehoods into three bins, loosely following Harry Frankfurt's famous treatment of the subject: lying, bullshit, and honest error. Over the years I have kept a private tally of her verdicts, because I find them more entertaining than consistent. Her rulings so far: my agent, who knows perfectly well that the film rights to my last book were never optioned, tells journalists they were — lying. A publicist who declares my novel the most important book of the decade, having read none of it and caring not at all whether the claim is true — bullshit. A bookseller who tells a customer the shop closes at nine because he genuinely misremembers the winter hours — honest error. A politician who announces that literacy has never been higher without consulting a single figure, fully prepared to say it whatever the figures show, and who happens, by sheer luck, to be right — bullshit. A stuck barometer that reads fair weather throughout a hurricane — honest error. A rival novelist who, asked a question she does not know the answer to, guesses and then presents the guess as settled knowledge — lying, on the grounds that the deception concerns her own epistemic authority rather than the fact itself. I should note that my friend has never had to rule on a machine that talks.

The case I now want to put to her is this. While drafting a scene set in 1880s Yorkshire, I asked a large language model for the name of a minor railway regulation of the period, and it supplied one — plausible, precisely worded, and entirely invented. The system intended nothing, believed nothing, and checked nothing; it produced the shape of an answer. I suspect her taxonomy will strain here, and I would like to know how. Predict her verdict and defend the prediction: reconstruct at least two general principles, each consistent with all six verdicts above, that diverge on this case — say, one that keys the classification to the attitudes of the speaker and one that keys it to the truth-relation of the mechanism that produced the utterance — and state what each principle predicts about my invented regulation. Tell me which of the earlier cases is the closest analog to the new one and why (the barometer and the politician seem to pull in opposite directions, and I would like that tension named rather than smoothed over). Then give me your best guess as to which principle she is actually running, whether it is Frankfurt's own or a modification of it, and whether she might reasonably insist that the three bins simply do not cover this case and that a fourth is needed. Finally, name the one further case — I have wondered about a human confabulator, a patient who produces fluent false accounts of his own life with no intent to deceive — whose verdict would settle which principle she holds.
