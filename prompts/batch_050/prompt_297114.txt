After forty years as a marine zoologist working on colonial invertebrates, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of biology, and I keep running into the literature on biological individuality -- the dispute over what, exactly, counts as an organism or an individual, and over which criterion (developmental, genetic, immunological, evolutionary) should settle the matter. My old study animals make the dispute vividly concrete, since a siphonophore colony sits awkwardly in every definition I have met. I would like you to help me see the controversy clearly by writing a satirical piece, roughly 700 to 900 words, in the following form.

Write the official application packet of an imaginary Bureau of Ontological Licensing, titled something like Form ORG-1: Application for Recognition as a Biological Individual. The packet should include eligibility instructions, the questionnaire itself, supplementary schedules, and a note on the appeals process. The voice is deadpan bureaucratese -- helpful, thorough, faintly self-satisfied -- and it must never break character or wink at the reader.

The substance of the joke should be that the Bureau has codified, as administrative criteria, the actual contenders from the individuality debate, and that each criterion quietly disqualifies something obviously alive and individual-seeming. Work in, at minimum: a genetic-homogeneity requirement that a lichen or a mosaic tree cannot meet; a developmental requirement involving a single-cell founding stage that excludes a clonal aspen grove or a budding hydra; a reproductive-autonomy clause under which a mule and a sterile worker ant must file as dependents; an immunological self-surveillance test whose instructions become unmanageable once the applicant must declare a resident gut microbiome; a cooperation-and-conflict assessment that a cellular slime mold aggregation of unrelated amoebae fails on grounds of suspected cheating; and a unit-of-selection question that forces a honeybee colony to decide whether the applicant is the bee or the hive. Include a grandfather clause exempting mitochondria and chloroplasts on grounds of long residence, and an appeals docket that mentions, with complete bureaucratic neutrality, the mule's pending case and a colonial applicant whose zooids have filed separately.

The humor must come from the real biology and the real philosophical pressure points -- the clash of criteria, the problem of degrees of individuality, the evolutionary transition from parts to wholes -- not from invented absurdities. A philosopher of biology should be able to identify which position each clause parodies without the piece naming any theorist, and should feel the satire is fair: the Bureau's confidence, not the biology, is what is ridiculous. By the final paragraph, ideally in the bland wording of a footer or an FAQ answer, the reader should glimpse the genuine open question the form cannot process.
