I'm a high school student in an advanced biology elective, and I need help preparing for a class debate. Please take on the role of a philosopher of biology who is also good at explaining things to a bright teenager. Think carefully before answering, and start by brainstorming the plausible definitions of life that could fit the case record below before you commit to a prediction.

Our teacher, Ms. Okafor, has a running classroom game where she presents borderline cases and gives her verdict on whether they count as alive. So far her verdicts are:

- An ordinary dog: alive.
- A lit candle flame: not alive, even though it consumes fuel and oxygen, grows, and can "spread" to new candles.
- An influenza virus: alive, on the grounds that viruses mutate and adapt to their hosts over time.
- A mule: alive, even though mules are sterile and can never reproduce.
- A bacterial spore that has sat completely dormant in dried soil for fifty years: alive, even though it does nothing at all during that time.
- A single mitochondrion extracted from a cell and kept functioning in a nutrient bath: not alive.
- A salt crystal that "grows" in evaporating seawater and can seed new crystals that resemble it: not alive.

Next week she has promised to rule on a new case, and the class is split on how she'll go. The case is a computer simulation in which short programs copy themselves into free memory, occasionally with random errors in the copying; the variants compete for limited memory and processing time, and over thousands of generations the population changes as faster and more robust copiers displace slower ones. Nothing in the simulation is made of chemicals at all -- it is just patterns in silicon and electricity.

Here is what I'd like from you:

1. Lay out at least two general rules or definitions of life that are each consistent with all seven of Ms. Okafor's verdicts so far. For each rule, show briefly how it delivers each of her verdicts.
2. Explain where the two rules diverge on the simulation case, and what verdict each one gives.
3. Predict which verdict Ms. Okafor will actually give, and explain which rule she seems to be implicitly running, judging from her track record -- especially her reasoning on the virus, the mule, and the mitochondrion, which I suspect are the most diagnostic cases.
4. Describe one additional borderline case, different from all of the above, that would best tell the competing rules apart -- that is, a case where the rules give clearly different verdicts, so that her ruling on it would reveal which principle she is really using.

Please reason through the rules and their predictions before giving your final prediction, and flag honestly where her past verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer rather than forcing a clean story onto them.
