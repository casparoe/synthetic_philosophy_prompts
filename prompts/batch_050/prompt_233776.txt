I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and my dissertation touches on the debate between realists and instrumentalists about representations in artificial neural networks. I've been following recent work in mechanistic interpretability, and I want to make sure I'm not missing something before I commit to a chapter structure.

It seems to me that this debate has by now been settled empirically in the realists' favor. Researchers can extract directions in a language model's activation space — for instance using sparse autoencoders — and then show that clamping or amplifying a single such direction produces reliable, semantically coherent changes in the model's output (say, making it talk about a particular landmark or concept far more often). If intervening on a direction causes predictable, concept-specific behavioral effects, doesn't that just demonstrate that the network itself was using that direction as a representation of the concept? What exactly is left for the instrumentalist to deny — that the intervention works? It plainly does. So I find myself unsure what position the anti-realist is even supposed to occupy anymore, short of denying the experimental results.

Is my reasoning here sound — does successful causal steering settle the metaphysical question — or am I conflating two claims that need to be kept apart? In answering, I'd ask you to be explicit about which points are genuinely settled among people who work on these systems and which remain contested, rather than presenting either realism or instrumentalism as the obvious consensus view.
