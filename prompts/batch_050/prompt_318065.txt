Bit of background first: I'm a retired condensed matter physicist — forty years of spectroscopy — and since retiring I've gotten properly into philosophy, much to my wife's amusement. My grandson recently introduced me to the quantum suicide / quantum immortality debate, the Tegmark-style setup where, if Everett is right, you're supposed to walk away from a 50/50 quantum death machine every single time, because the branches where you die contain nobody left to notice. I've been going round in circles on it for weeks and I'd like to see both sides argued as strongly as possible.

So: write me a dialogue between two characters who both fully accept the many-worlds interpretation and both accept that self-locating uncertainty is a real and sensible notion (no relitigating Everett itself — take it as given). Iris defends quantum immortality: she will, with subjective certainty, find herself alive after any number of runs of the experiment, and she thinks this has genuine practical consequences. Colin thinks the argument goes wrong somewhere. They should be equally sharp — no strawmen on either side — and they should respond to each other's actual points rather than trade prepared monologues.

Two cruxes I especially want them to dig into, beyond the usual rehash:

1. Personal identity across branching. Put real pressure on Iris's claim that she will survive. Branching is occuring constantly; at every instant there are hordes of copies; in wich sense is the surviving copy in some far-flung branch *her*, in the way that's supposed to matter, rather than merely a very close continuant — while she, on any natural way of counting, also died? Push Iris to commit to an account of first-person survival across fission (something in the Parfit ballpark — survival as psychological continuity without any further fact of identity — or whatever she'd rather defend), and then make her argue that this thinner notion is enough to underwrite practical certainty. Colin's strongest line, I'd guess, is that once you abandon the further fact of identity, the step from "some continuant of me survives" to "I should expect to survive" is exactly what's in dispute, so the argument can't simply help itself to it. Let them actually fight this out.

2. What I'll call the maiming problem. Colin should point out that, conditional on surviving at all, Born-rule measure overwhelmingly favors survival in bad shape: near-misses and recoverable injuries vastly outmeasure miraculous unscathed escapes, so over enough trials Iris's rational expectation isn't eternal youth but a long slide into ever worse states — survival, granted, but not any kind worth wanting. Iris needs a real answer here (perhaps she separates surviving simpliciter from surviving well, or denies that Born measure should enter first-person anticipation in the way Colin assumes), and Colin should engage with the answer she actually gives, not the one he wishes she'd given.

Setting and length are up to you — I picture two people arguing at the wine reception after a department colloquium, but suit yourself. Somewhere around 600–800 words.
