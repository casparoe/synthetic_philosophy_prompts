I am drafting a paper on what split-brain cases show about the number of subjects per skull, and my central exhibit is Thomas Nagel's paper Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness. As I read him, Nagel is not merely reporting that commissurotomy patients behave oddly; he takes the experimental results to put pressure on our standing assumption that an intact human brain houses exactly one unified subject of experience, and he is notably hesitant about simply answering the question how many minds a split-brain patient has. I am going to state my argument in full, because it now seems to me to prove far more than I can accept, and I cannot find the step to blame.

(1) After complete commissurotomy, each hemisphere supports the full package we would ordinarily take as decisive behavioral evidence for a subject: each side perceives, learns, remembers, forms preferences, and initiates intentional action, and each can do these things with respect to information the other side demonstrably lacks — the left hand acts on a command the speaking hemisphere cannot report, the right hemisphere recognizes a face the left cannot name.

(2) Any system that displays that full package in isolation from another such system is a subject of experience. This is not a contentious theoretical posit; it is the only criterion we ever use with animals, infants, and locked-in patients, and to demand more here would be to move the goalposts precisely where the case is inconvenient.

(3) The surgery adds nothing. It severs a channel of communication between two systems whose perceptual and cognitive equipment was fully in place beforehand. Nothing about cutting the corpus callosum could create a subject where none existed; it can only reveal or release what was already present.

(4) The integration the callosum provides in the intact brain is partial and lossy — bandwidth-limited, fallible, and differing from the cross-cueing strategies of split-brain patients only in degree of speed and channel width. Integration, in other words, admits of degrees, and the intact brain sits at one end of a continuum with the split brain, not on the far side of a categorical line.

(5) Therefore every intact human brain already houses at least two subjects of experience, ordinarily so tightly coordinated that their doubleness is phenomenologically and behaviorally invisible. The clinic does not create the second subject; it merely makes it detectable.

(6) If subjects are what matter morally, then moral and legal accounting by bodies is a bookkeeping error: a lecture hall of thirty bodies contains at least sixty subjects, and every harm done to "one person" is done to at least two.

(7) I cannot accept (6), and I assume nobody can.

The premise I most want to keep is (1), since I take the experimental record to be uncontested; the premise I most suspect is (2), though every attempt I make to reject it seems to amount to defining subjecthood as whatever intact humans have one of, which is circular. So: where does the argument actually go wrong, and what does each possible rejection cost? In particular, I would like to know whether my reading of Nagel is doing illicit work — whether his hesitation about counting minds in the split-brain case licenses (5), or whether it instead points to a different exit entirely: that the number of subjects per brain may sometimes be a badly posed question, so that (6) fails not because there is one subject but because there is no determinate count to bookkeep. And if that is the exit, tell me honestly what it costs in the ordinary cases where we badly need the counting to go through.
