Two Hume scholars in my department have spent years on opposite sides of the causation debate, and I have asked them to settle at least the terms of their dispute. One defends the traditional regularity reading: Hume's considered view is that causation, as far as anything in nature is concerned, just is constant conjunction and priority in time, and his talk of necessary connection picks out nothing beyond an impression-derived feeling of expectation in the mind. The other defends the sceptical-realist (New Hume) reading: Hume believed there are genuine, mind-independent necessary connections in objects that ground the regularities, but held that our faculties can never grasp their nature, so that his official definitions capture only what causation is for us, not what it is in itself.

Draft the joint statement the two of them should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Specifically, the document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: points about Hume's text and method that both parties accept, such as what the copy principle does and does not license, what both of Hume's two definitions of cause actually say, the distinction between what exists and what we can conceive or know, and the role of Hume's scepticism in setting limits on any interpretation. Include only claims each side would affirm in its own voice, not concessions extracted under pressure.

2. A disagreements section in which each residual dispute is stated in a single formulation that both sides accept as neutral, followed by each side's position phrased so that side would endorse the wording. Cover at least: whether the talk of secret powers and ultimate springs is to be read as committed belief or as the vulgar's error; what the second definition of cause is for; and whether the regularity reading collapses Hume's own distinction between the relative and absolute ideas of cause.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, identify the specific texts or arguments both parties accept as bearing on it (you may refer to passages in the Treatise and the first Enquiry by location, but do not quote them), and record in advance what each side predicts a fair reading of those passages shows, so that neither can adjust after the fact.

4. A final short section in which the two state whether any part of their disagreement is merely verbal — for instance, whether they differ over what Hume believed, or only over what his positive account amounts to — and justify the verdict.

Tone it as an actual collaborative document: measured, precise, and free of victory claims.
