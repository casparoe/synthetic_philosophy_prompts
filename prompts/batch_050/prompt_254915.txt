For years I have noticed the same defensive move in arguments about institutions, and I want to know whether it has an established name. The pattern: someone criticizes a rule or custom as pointless; the critics actually do the homework the Chesterton's-fence heuristic demands, unearthing the real history and showing that the original purpose is defunct or was never respectable; and the defenders, rather than conceding, reply that the practice may still serve some function nobody has yet identified, and treat that bare possibility as sufficient reason to keep it. Two recent instances. A city council proposes scrapping a licensing requirement for street performers; the records show it was created in the 1930s to settle a feud between two theatre owners, both long dead; opponents of scrapping it argue that licensing may quietly filter out problem performers, though no one has ever checked whether it does. A firm has a custom of never scheduling meetings on Fridays, traced to a founder's religious observance the firm no longer shares; defenders argue that Friday-free weeks may have undiscovered benefits, and that a custom surviving this long probably does something. The move is not always foolish — food traditions really can encode invisible functions; the alkaline processing of maize (nixtamalization) prevented pellagra for centuries without anyone knowing why, and populations that adopted maize without the processing got the disease. But in the cases I keep seeing, the investigation has already happened, the known purpose is discredited, and the defense has shifted to functions that are merely possible and never stated in a way anyone proposes to test. It is not plain status quo bias, since an argument is offered rather than mere inertia; it is not the fence heuristic itself, which governs what to do before you investigate; and while it resembles the argument from ignorance and the biologists' complaint about just-so stories, I am unsure whether this exact move — resting a defense on an unspecified, untested hidden function — has a name of its own. I also want to know whether the strong version (it survived a long time, so it probably does something) is a different move from the weak one (you cannot prove it does nothing), or one move in two strengths.

Please structure the answer in four sections, in this order: Nearest concepts (three or four established ideas the move resembles, with a couple of sentences each on how the move differs; roughly 80 words per concept); Best fit (which of these, if any, actually covers the move, and where it falls short; about 120 words); A name for it (if nothing fits, coin a term, define it, and mark its boundary cases — in particular, what separates the maize case, where the hidden function was real, from uses that immunize a practice against any possible refutation; about 150 words); Two versions or one (whether the survival-based argument and the bare appeal to untested possibility deserve a single label; about 100 words).
