Having retired last year from a career in condensed-matter physics, I have been spending my newfound spare time reading philosophy, and I keep returning to a puzzle that arose not in a book but in my attempts to contribute to Wikipedia. I would be grateful for a careful analysis of it.

Wikipedia's editorial community instructs its members to assume good faith: when another editor makes a change one regards as wrongheaded, one is to presume that the editor was trying to improve the encyclopedia, however misguided the attempt. The rationale usually given is that motives are invisible, and that attributing bad intent poisons collaboration. Yet the same community maintains a parallel set of practices that seem to run in the opposite direction. Editors can be sanctioned for what is called tendentious editing, where the case against them is assembled precisely from a pattern of behavior — the topics they return to, the sources they favor, the talk-page remarks they make — and where the inference from pattern to disposition is doing all the work. There is also a widely endorsed maxim to the effect that competence is required, under which an editor may be removed for persistent error even when everyone concedes his sincerity.

My question is what these practices, taken together, presuppose. What view of good faith, and of our access to other people's motives, does the assumption-of-good-faith norm make sense under? Does it quietly presuppose that sincerity is the morally relevant variable in assessing a contributor, or is it merely a useful fiction adopted for instrumental reasons, and can one tell which from the outside? Conversely, what do the tendentious-editing sanctions and the competence requirement presuppose — that dispositions are legible from behavioral traces in something like the way a phase transition is legible from bulk measurements, and that this legibility suffices for judgment even where intent remains opaque? Are these two sets of presuppositions consistent with one another, or does the institution operate on a divided theory of mind, charitable in one room and actuarial in the next? If the latter, I would like to know whether some single account of disagreement — say, one on which most conflict stems from honest differences in judgment rather than from malice or incompetence — could reconcile them, and what would have to change in the community's actual procedures if one replaced the implicit theory with a more thoroughly behaviorist one, or with a more thoroughly intent-focused one.

Finally, two requests about the manner of your answer. Please indicate how confident you are in each of its main claims, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. And please single out explicitly the one point in your analysis that you are least sure of, and say why.
