I'm drafting a position statement for a graduate seminar on the foundations of welfare economics, and before I circulate it I'd like a consistency audit. Here is the set of commitments I've arrived at:

1. Utility is nothing over and above a representation of choice. To say that someone prefers A to B just is, in the idealized limit, to say that they would choose A over B. There is no further psychological magnitude — pleasure, felt satisfaction, an inner degree of wanting — that utility is supposed to track, and talk of utility beyond its role in systematizing choice is a reification.

2. The intransitive chooser is making a mistake. Someone who pays to trade A for B, B for C, and then C for A again has been money-pumped, and that shows the pattern is irrational — it is not evidence that the person simply has cyclic preferences which deserve the same respect as acyclic ones.

3. Menu effects are biases, not revelations. When adding an irrelevant third option flips someone's choice between the original two, the right response is debiasing, not the conclusion that preferences are menu-relative all along.

4. Interpersonal aggregation is meaningful. It makes sense to say that one policy produces more total welfare than another, and cost-benefit analysis is a legitimate, if imperfect, instrument of public decision-making.

5. Expected utility theory fully captures rational attitudes toward risk: risk aversion just is curvature of the utility function, and there is no need to posit a separate attitude toward risk over and above the shape the function happens to take.

Two things I want from you. First, the audit itself, ordered by severity rather than given as a flat list: for each genuine tension, name the commitments involved, explain exactly why they pull against each other, and state what would have to give on either side. I suspect that some of these conflict only apparently — if so, identify the pair and show why the tension dissolves on inspection rather than flagging everything indiscriminately. Pay particular attention to whether any of my commitments are quietly borrowing resources from a picture of the mind that one of my other commitments forbids.

Second, I want a committed verdict, not a menu of options. Tell me which single revision would do the most to repair the set, what it costs me in terms of the overall picture, and whether what remains is still recognizably the revealed-preference program or has quietly become a return to the mentalism I started out rejecting. If you judge that the set hangs together as it stands, defend that verdict directly — but do not split the difference.
