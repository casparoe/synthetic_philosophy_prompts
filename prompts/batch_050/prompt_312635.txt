So I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind (dissertation is on introspective reports, broadly) and I keep going back and forth on Dennett's Chase and Sanborn cases from Quining Qualia. Quick setup in case it matters: both are coffee tasters. Chase comes to dislike the taste of coffee over the years; Sanborn does too. Chase says he thinks the coffee tastes the same as it always did but he no longer likes that taste, while also allowing he might be wrong -- maybe the taste itself changed and his memory of the old taste is distorted. Sanborn is sure the taste itself has changed and his memory of how it used to taste is accurate. Dennett's point is supposed to be that nothing -- not behavioral tests, not physiology, not the subjects' own say-so -- settles which of them is right, and he takes that to show there's something deeply confused about the idea of qualia as ineffable, intrinsic, private, immediately apprehensible properties of experience.

Here's what I actually want from you: your credence that the Chase/Sanborn cases successfully establish that qualia, in roughly Dennett's four-condition sense, are an incoherent posit -- i.e., not just that we can't know facts about them in these cases, but that there are no such facts to know. Give me a number and decompose it. Things I'd expect to see in the decomposition, though feel free to restructure: your probability that the cases really are underdetermined by all possible evidence (as opposed to there being a clever third-person test or a future neuroscience that settles Chase vs. Sanborn); your probability that, granted underdetermination, the verificationist-ish move from "undecidable" to "no fact of the matter" is legitimate here rather than just question-begging against the realist; and your probability that Dennett's four conditions actually capture what careful qualia realists are committed to, versus being a strawman -- plenty of people seem happy to drop "incorrigibility" or even "ineffability" and keep the rest. State how these combine into your final number, and tell me which term your credence is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would move it most.

One more thing: please be careful to separate what's genuinely settled in this literature from what's still live and contested. E.g., I don't want "philosophers have shown that qualia are incoherent" presented as consensus if it isn't (I strongly suspect it isn't), and same in the other direction -- don't present "the cases obviously fail because they presuppose verificationism" as the received view if that's actually contested too. Flag which sub-claims of yours are mainstream and which are you taking a side on.
