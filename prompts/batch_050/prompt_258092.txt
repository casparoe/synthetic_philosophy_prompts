Three months ago I discovered that a colleague at my company has been quietly inflating the numbers on grant reports, and since then I've been going around in circles about what I owe to whom. Rather than ask what I should do in my own case, I'd like to work out what principle I actually hold, by giving you my verdicts on a set of cases -- some real, some hypothetical -- and asking you to find the principle, if there is one, that unifies them.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A nurse notices her hospital is systematically overbilling Medicare. She reports it to federal regulators, knowing the hospital will likely be fined and some colleagues may lose their jobs. Clearly right -- not merely permissible but admirable.

2. A man learns that his brother, an accountant, once helped a client evade taxes, years ago, at a different firm, with no ongoing conduct. He reports it to the licensing board anyway. Clearly wrong -- vindictive, even, though nothing he reports is false.

3. A teenager tells her parents that her older sister, home from college, has been smoking marijuana in the garage. Technically true information given to people with a legitimate interest, but it feels like a betrayal.

4. An engineer at an aircraft manufacturer goes to the press about a known defect after internal channels fail. Right, and the fact that she exhausted internal channels first seems to matter to how right it is.

5. A woman tells her best friend's husband, who has asked her directly, that the friend is having an affair. Wrong -- she should have refused to answer or lied. But: if the husband were about to cosign a large loan with the wife, and the affair was with the financial adviser who proposed the loan, telling him starts to feel right. The stakes being raised seems to flip it.

6. A civil servant leaks documents showing his agency lied to the public about a policy's costs. Right. But I don't think it would be right if the documents instead showed only that the agency's internal deliberations were chaotic and personal -- same act, same method, same breach of his employment obligations, different content.

7. A soldier reports his own unit's war crime up the chain, and when nothing happens, to journalists. Right, and I don't feel the hesitation I felt in case 3, even though the bonds within a unit are supposed to be at least as thick as family bonds.

Now, the pair I cannot reconcile on my own: in case 2, I think the man's fraternal loyalty trumps the public interest in the integrity of the accounting profession, but in case 1 I think the public interest trumps the nurse's institutional loyalty, even though the institutional bond is weaker and therefore, you'd think, easier to set aside. And yet in case 7 the military bond -- the strongest of all -- seems to give way most easily. So it isn't the strength of the bond, or at least not only that.

What I'd like from you: state, precisely, the single principle (or the smallest set of jointly necessary conditions) that you think best captures these verdicts, and commit to it. Don't give me a menu of candidate frameworks -- pick one, and say which case motivates each clause of it. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, and which verdict you'd have me drop rather than complicate the principle; I want you to choose, not to lay out the trade-off and leave the choice to me. Finally, apply your settled principle to my own case -- a mid-sized nonprofit, a colleague I like, inflated grant reports, conduct ongoing, internal channels not yet tried -- and tell me what it directs, including whether it matters that reporting may cost people besides my colleague their funding.
