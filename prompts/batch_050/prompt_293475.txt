---STUDENT ANSWER BEGINS---
Quinn imagines a device implanted in a person's body with a dial that can be set to any position from 0 to 1000. At each higher setting the device delivers a slightly more intense electrical stimulus, but the difference between adjacent settings is imperceptible to the subject. For each notch the dial is turned up, the subject receives a payment of $10,000. At setting 1000, however, the accumulated pain is excruciating — so bad that the subject would clearly prefer never having started, i.e., prefers setting 0 to setting 1000 overall, even though 1000 comes with far more money.

The puzzle arises because the subject seems to have a rational preference, for every n, of setting n+1 over setting n: the extra $10,000 is a genuine benefit, and the pain increase is by hypothesis one he cannot even notice. But since he prefers 1 to 0, 2 to 1, and so on up through 1000 to 999, transitivity of preference delivers that he prefers 1000 to 0 — contradicting his clear and rational preference for 0 over 1000. So at least one of the apparently rational preferences in the chain must fail to be rational, or else rational preference is intransitive.

In my view the puzzle is at bottom a sorites paradox and should be treated as one. The reasoning trades on the vague predicate "noticeably more painful," and as with the heap, there is no determinate point at which the pain becomes worse, so the chain of comparisons has no sharp breaking point. Once we recognize the vagueness infecting the setup, the paradox dissolves: the subject's preferences are fine as far as they go, and the appearance of contradiction comes from applying a transitive logic to vague predicates.

Strictly speaking, moreover, the subject is indifferent between adjacent settings, since by stipulation he cannot tell the stimulus levels apart. Indifference is well known to be non-transitive — you can be unable to distinguish a long series of pairs of shades while easily distinguishing the first from the last — so the intransitivity displayed here is of a familiar and harmless kind and poses no real threat to rationality.

If one nonetheless wants a resolution that preserves the strict-preference reading, the most defensible option is to deny that rational preference must be transitive. The cost is that intransitive agents are vulnerable to money-pump exploitation: someone whose preferences cycle can in principle be induced to trade around the cycle, paying a fee at each step, until bankrupted. I conclude that transitivity denial is available but expensive, and that the vagueness diagnosis above is the better account of what goes wrong.
