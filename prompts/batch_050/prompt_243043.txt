Okay so I'm writing a piece on periodization -- whether things like "the Middle Ages" and "the Scientific Revolution" are real joints in history or just labels historians slapped on afterwards. My editor keeps leaning on an analogy: historical periods are like the colors of the rainbow. The spectrum is continuous, there are no actual bands in the light, we carve it up wherever we find it useful, and that's fine -- the carving isn't wrong, it's just ours. He uses this to argue the whole framing of my piece is misguided, since asking whether the Renaissance "really happened" is like asking whether orange really exists.

But a historian I interviewed pushed back and said the rainbow picture quietly assumes the carvers are always standing outside, looking back -- whereas historical actors sometimes name their own period while living in it, and then act on the name. She thinks that changes everything; I'm not so sure it changes anything.

You're a philosopher of history who's thought carefully about this stuff. Before you answer, spend a bit brainstorming every way the rainbow analogy could bend or break, then give me the audit: what maps onto what (spectrum, bands, the person doing the carving -- what's the historical version of each?), which disanalogies actually matter for my editor's conclusion and which are pedantic, and whether "periods are retrospective inventions, so stop asking if they're real" survives once the self-naming point is on the table. If the rainbow is the wrong metaphor, what's a better one?
