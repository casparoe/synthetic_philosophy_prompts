I'm a longtime basketball fan who has been reading Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia for a political philosophy reading group, and I've become genuinely puzzled about what my own favorite institution is committed to. Consider how the NBA actually allocates its most important resource, playing talent. Players entering the league do not auction their services to the highest bidder; they are assigned to teams through a draft in which the worst teams pick first. Team payrolls are capped, individual contracts are capped, spending above the cap triggers a punitive luxury tax, and trades must satisfy salary-matching rules. A superstar willing to play for less than his market value, and a team willing to pay him more than the rules allow, are both forbidden from striking the deal they want. The league's stated rationale is competitive balance: the arrangement exists precisely to prevent the concentration of talent that unrestricted bargaining would produce, and to keep the distribution of wins across teams within a range the league judges appealing to fans.

Here is what puzzles me. Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument, as I understand it, says that once holdings are justly acquired, voluntary exchanges will disrupt whatever distributional pattern you started with -- fans happily pay a little each to watch the star -- so any end-state or patterned principle of justice can be maintained only by continuously interfering with the voluntary transactions of consenting adults. The NBA looks like a working laboratory for exactly that thesis: the league really does continuously interfere with voluntary transactions, and it really does so in the name of a pattern. So: what theory of justice in holdings does this whole apparatus presuppose? Does it rest on treating the league as a single owner allocating its own property internally, so that nothing is redistributed at all, or on a genuinely patterned principle in which the distribution of talent is an object of justice in its own right, or on some third view -- say, that the product being sold is the competition itself, so the fans' entitlement runs to the spectacle rather than to any particular assignment of players? And here is the complication I can't resolve: every one of these restrictions was agreed to by the players' union in collective bargaining, so by Nozick's own lights the cap seems to arise from just steps and should be unobjectionable -- yet its entire function is to bind future transactions between parties (a free agent, a capped team) in ways those parties would not choose. Does consent at the institutional level launder interference at the transaction level, or does the argument cut the other way?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice as precisely as you can: which parts of it strictly presuppose a patterned or end-state conception of justice, which parts are compatible with an entitlement view given the consent story, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined such that several rival theories would license it equally well. Then tell me what would concretely have to change -- abolishing the draft, uncapping individual contracts but keeping team caps, dissolving the league into independent clubs, or something subtler -- if the league took the entitlement theory fully seriously, and whether anything worth caring about in the practice would be lost.
