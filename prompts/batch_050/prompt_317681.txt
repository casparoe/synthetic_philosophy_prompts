I'm a graduate student in philosophy who runs a small reading group on rational choice theory, and for three sessions in a row we've circled the money pump argument without moving anyone an inch. The setup is familiar: an agent who prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A can apparently be induced to trade in a cycle, paying a small amount at each step, and so end up exactly where she started minus the payments. Half the group takes this to show that intransitive preferences are irrational, full stop, and that rationality must therefore be diachronic, since only the pattern across time reveals the defect. The other half replies that every individual trade is perfectly rational by the agent's own lights at the moment she makes it; that a foresightful agent can simply decline to enter the pump, walk away partway through, or adopt a plan and stick to it regardless of her moment-to-moment preferences; and that the supposed irrationality dissolves once the agent is allowed to care about things like not being exploited or following through on her intentions. At this point both sides are essentially repeating their opening moves with more elaborate examples.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about money, exploitation, or even transitivity at all, but a disagreement about what rationality assesses: single choices evaluated one at a time, or extended patterns of choices stretched across time. And I suspect this deeper disagreement may be one where neither side has anything non-question-begging to say, because each side's notion of what counts as a "cost" of irrationality already presupposes its own answer.
