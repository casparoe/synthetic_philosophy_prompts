Write the desiderata for a theory of objectivity in historical inquiry. The theory should serve working historians rather than merely define truth in general: it must explain how one historical account can be objectively better than another even when the evidence underdetermines selection, emphasis, scale, and narrative form. Use this test case throughout: working from the same archive concerning a nineteenth-century textile strike, one historian centers union organizers, another centers women performing unpaid household labor, and a third reconstructs how participants understood legality and respectability; all handle their sources responsibly, yet their causal explanations and narrative structures differ. Return exactly ten numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1–8 should each state a distinct requirement, explain the problem that makes it a requirement, and classify it as non-negotiable, revisable, or primarily camp-specific. Across those eight items, address evidential constraint and source criticism; selection and scale; the distinction between factual error and questionable emphasis; causal explanation and counterfactual reasoning; present concerns and the danger of anachronism; archival silences and unequal power over what was recorded; revision, disagreement, and epistemic progress; and the historian’s reflexive awareness of standpoint. Item 9 should identify the two requirements most difficult to satisfy jointly and explain the resulting trade-off. Item 10 should apply the completed list to the test case and assess whether Rankean reconstruction, Carr’s socially situated selection, and Hayden White’s emphasis on narrative form each identify a genuine constraint or mistake one component of historical inquiry for the whole.
