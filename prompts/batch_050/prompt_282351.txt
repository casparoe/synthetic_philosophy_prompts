After forty years as a molecular biologist, I retired and promptly fell down the philosophical rabbit hole, as retired scientists seem fated to do. I now help run a small colloquium series at my old university, and next month our guest is a Nietzsche scholar whose new book defends a strong reading of the second Untimely Meditation, On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life: namely, that Nietzsche meant his critique of historical culture to apply with full force to any scholarship that models itself on the natural sciences, and that his standard of "life" is neither a biological metaphor nor a disguised moral standard but a genuinely distinct criterion that a culture can apply to itself. I expect a room split between humanists and scientists, and I would like the scientists not to leave thinking the essay is mere nineteenth-century vitalism.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, sequenced so they build on one another, that do the following. Open with the taxonomy itself: press her on whether monumental, antiquarian, and critical history are really distinguished by the needs they serve rather than the methods they use, and follow up on the reply she is most likely to give, which is that the same archive can be raided in all three modes. Include a question on the famous "plastic power" of a person or culture: as someone who spent his career measuring things, I want to know whether she thinks this capacity admits of degrees in any principled way, or whether Nietzsche's talk of health and sickness in a culture is diagnosable only in retrospect. Include a question on the unhistorical and the suprahistorical, and on whether the latter sits in tension with Nietzsche's later hostility to anything that stands above or beyond life; I suspect she will say the essay is an early work he partly outgrew, so prepare a follow-up asking what in the essay survives that outgrowing. Include a question that puts my tribe on the hook: if striving for detached, objectifying knowledge enervates a culture's capacity for action when historians do it, why does it not do the same when biochemists do it, and is her answer a difference in kind or merely in what the knowledge is for. Include a question on critical history's self-reference problem: a people that drags its own past before the tribunal must judge from some standing point, and if that point is "life," ask whether the criterion is doing real work or simply ratifying whatever the judges already want. And close with a question about what evidence or example she would accept as counting against the book's central reading, since I have found that this separates scholars from advocates.

For each question, give me a one- or two-sentence note on the answer I should expect from a careful defender of her view and the best follow-up if she gives it. Keep the questions civil but pointed; the room will include people who revere this essay and people who think it is an extended category error, and I would like both groups to feel the questions were fair.
