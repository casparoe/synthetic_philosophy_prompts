I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm prototyping a side project I call an ethics linter: a tool that takes a description of a proposed action and flags it if it fails Kant's universalizability test. Before I write any code, I need a specification, and that is where I need your help.

Act as a moral philosopher specializing in Kant's ethics. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming a wide range of candidate requirements before settling on the final list. Then write the list of requirements an operationalization of the universalizability test must meet to count as a genuine test of universalizability rather than something else in disguise. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one interpretive camp.

Make sure the analysis engages at least these problems, which I already suspect are the hard parts:

(a) The maxim-description problem: the test's verdict seems to flip depending on how specifically the proposed action is described, and descriptions that are too general and too specific appear to break the test in opposite directions. What should a spec demand here?
(b) The distinction between maxims that cannot even be conceived as universal laws and maxims that can be conceived but not consistently willed -- and whether an automated procedure can draw that line at all.
(c) False positives: the test should not condemn clearly harmless policies, such as playing tennis at an unpopular time of day.
(d) Evasion by indexicals: the test should not be trivially passable by maxims that name the agent or a unique circumstance.
(e) Whether the test must be purely formal, or may build in substantive assumptions about what rational agents necessarily want.

The deliverable I most want: if some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and explain why, because that determines what my tool could even in principle deliver. You may briefly sketch how the standard readings of the test fare against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than the scorecard. Finally, state which argument or consideration would most change your verdict about which requirements are non-negotiable, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
