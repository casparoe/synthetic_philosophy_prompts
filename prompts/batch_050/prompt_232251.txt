So there's this analogy that comes up constantly in tech circles, and honestly I've used it myself: a country should be run like a company, and the president is basically the CEO. Voters are the shareholders, taxes are revenue, GDP is the growth metric, congress is the board, and a successful businessperson would obviously fix government the way a good CEO turns around a failing firm. I've heard it used to argue we should elect more founders and fewer career politicians, and also that government agencies should be judged on efficiency metrics the way divisions inside a company are.

I got into an argument about this with a friend who studies political science and she claims the analogy doesn't just have rough edges, it breaks in exactly the places people lean on it hardest. One thing she said that suprised me: the CEO analogy actually fits parliamentary prime ministers better than US-style presidents, because a parliament can fire a PM with a no-confidence vote the way a board fires a CEO, whereas a president has a fixed term and the "board" mostly can't touch him (impeachment being this rare, weird, quasi-criminal thing rather than a performance review). So the people using the analogy are usually picturing the wrong system of government for it.

Can you audit this properly? Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of shareholders, the board, customers, competitors, quarterly earnings, firing underperformers -- and then where the mapping breaks. Some things I've been chewing on: citizens can't just sell their shares and leave (you can emigrate, but that's nothing like dumping stock), the "company" can't fire its least productive people because those people are also the shareholders, the government doesn't get to choose its customer base, and a CEO's authority inside a firm is basically dictatorial compared to what a president can actually order anyone to do. Also companies can go bankrupt and it's sad but life goes on; countries can't. I don't know if all of these matter equally for the conclusions or if some are just nitpicks.

And then the payoff question: does either of the conclusions people use this analogy for -- elect businesspeople, judge agencies by efficiency metrics -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Or was the analogy doing all the work? If there's a better analogy for what a president actually is, I want that too. My friend's candidate was something like "chair of a committee where half the members hate you, none of them can be fired, and your main job is getting them to agree," which, having sat through a lot of cross-functional planning meetings, honestly tracks.
