I've been a reader of rationalist blogs and forums for years, and a recurring theme there is treating institutions as "solutions looking for their problems" — Chesterton's fence reasoning, the idea that a practice that survives for centuries is probably doing cognitive work we haven't made explicit. A discussion with friends recently pushed this onto a topic I realized I couldn't reconstruct: constitutional monarchy in otherwise serious democracies. The UK, the Scandinavian countries, and Japan all rank near the top of every index of democratic quality, and all of them maintain a hereditary head of state in a political culture that officially endorses equality of opportunity and consent-based legitimacy. The cases are interestingly varied: Sweden has stripped its monarch of essentially all formal powers, the British monarch retains notional reserve powers whose actual use would trigger a constitutional crisis, and Japan's emperor occupies a purely symbolic position under a postwar constitution. And the edge cases are instructive — in 1990, Belgium's King Baudouin refused on conscience grounds to sign an abortion law, and the government solved the problem by declaring him temporarily unable to reign, enacting the law in his absence, and reinstating him the next day, which seems to establish that the monarch's signature is required but the monarch's assent is not.

So here is what I'd like worked out. What does this institution, as actually practiced, presuppose about the nature of political legitimacy? On a naive consent-theoretic picture — authority flows upward from the people, offices are justified by election or at least by open competition — a hereditary, untouchable, state-funded ceremonial office looks not just unjustified but embarrassing, a surviving contradiction. Yet these are the polities that arguably function best, and their publics keep declining to abolish the monarchy. Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice only make sense if legitimacy can come from continuity and habituation rather than consent, if a polity needs a locus of authority that is deliberately insulated from democratic contestation, if symbolic and effective power ought to be held by different people so that neither can claim the whole? I'm particularly interested in whether the institution strictly presupposes any one of these or is compatible with several incompatible theories at once — perhaps different defenders (a Bagehot-style functionalist who values the separation of dignified and efficient government, a Burkean who trusts evolved institutions, a pure path-dependence skeptic who just fears the transition costs) would each license the practice for different reasons, and I'd like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined versus where it quietly rules positions out. Does it, for instance, rule out a strict proceduralist view on which only democratic procedure can confer legitimacy, or can the proceduralist absorb the monarchy as merely one more procedurally maintained rule?

Finally, run the counterfactuals, since I think they're diagnostic. Suppose one of these countries abolished the monarchy and replaced it with (a) an elected ceremonial president performing literally identical functions, or (b) a citizen chosen by lottery every seven years, again with identical functions. On each hypothesis you've reconstructed, is anything lost, and what? If your reconstruction says nothing is lost under (a), I'd take that as evidence the reconstruction is missing something, since elected-figurehead republics do not in fact reproduce whatever monarchies are doing — but tell me if you think that's wrong. And then complete the exercise: if the presupposed theory of legitimacy were replaced with a pure consent theory tomorrow, what concretely would have to change about these states beyond the obvious (the title, the succession)? Would anything follow about judicial legitimacy, about national anthems and founding myths, about the status of unwritten constitutional conventions generally? I want the implicit theory, not a verdict on whether monarchies are good.
