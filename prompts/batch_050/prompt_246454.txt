So I'm putting together a position statement for our practical ethics reading group (we're doing priority vs equality this term) and I want to stress-test my package of views before I get torn apart in the session. Here's where I've landed:

(1) I'm a prioritarian. A unit of well-being matters more morally the worse off the person who gets it, where "worse off" means their absolute level of lifetime well-being, not how they compare to anyone else. Overall betterness of an outcome is the sum of a concave function applied to each person's well-being.

(2) I think the leveling-down objection is basically decisive against telic egalitarianism. There is nothing whatsoever good — not even a tiny good that gets outweighed — about making better-off people worse off when doing so benefits nobody.

(3) I think what's bad about inequality, when it is bad, is the complaint the worse-off person has: they can point at the better-off and ask why they should have less through no fault of their own. The strength of that complaint tracks the gap between them — the bigger the gap, the stronger the complaint.

(4) I think the claims of unaffected bystanders shouldn't matter to a choice. Whether I should benefit A rather than B can't depend on how well off some third person C is, when C gets nothing either way.

(5) Numbers count: if I can save one group or another, everyone involved is equally badly off, and each person would gain the same amount, I have to save the larger group — and any adequate distributive view has to deliver that verdict.

Question: does this all hang together? Go through it pairwise and tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are at first glance. Rank the real tensions by severity — don't just flag everything as equally bad. For each real tension, say exactly what makes them pull apart and what the cheapest fix would be. And then tell me which single revision would clean up the most overall.

One thing I'm particulary unsure about is whether (2) and (3) can co-exist, but I've also had a nagging feeling that (1) and (2) might conflict, since priority weighting seems like egalitarianism by another name. Prove me right or wrong.
