I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a long explainer about a debate in philosophy of religion that most readers have never heard of: how God is supposed to know the future without making human freedom fake. The piece centers on Molinism -- the view that before deciding what to create, God knew what every possible free creature *would freely do* in every possible circumstance -- and on the main objection to it, the so-called grounding objection.

Can you write the heart of the explainer for me, aimed at a smart lay reader? Here's what I need covered:

1. The three-way division of God's knowledge (natural, middle, and free knowledge), and where middle knowledge sits in the logical order of things -- i.e., after natural knowledge but before God's creative decree. Explain why that ordering does all the work: it's supposed to let God exercise complete providential control over history without causing anyone's free choices.

2. The grounding objection, stated as strongly as it can be stated. A counterfactual like "if Eve were placed in circumstance C, she would freely eat the fruit" is supposed to be true before God creates anything at all. So it can't be made true by Eve's actual action (in many cases the circumstance never arises, and in some the person never even exists), and it can't be made true by God's will (then her choice would be determined by God, which kills the libertarian freedom Molinists insist on). So what makes it true? It looks like a brute fact floating loose from both God and creatures.

3. Why the analogous problem supposedly *doesn't* arise for ordinary counterfactuals we lean on all the time, like "if you offered this dinner guest decaf, she'd turn it down." I believe the idea is that if there's determinism humming along in the background, the counterfactual gets grounded in the person's actual character plus the laws of nature, whereas the Molinist has denied themselves that resource by insisting on libertarian freedom. Please make that mechanism explicit, because I think it's where the real action is and most pop treatments skip it entirely.

4. The best Molinist replies -- e.g., denying that every truth needs a ground, saying the counterfactuals are grounded in the agents themselves, or pointing out that we rely on such counterfactuals constantly in ordinary deliberation -- and how the objector pushes back on each.

Ground rules: no citations, no bibliography, no reading list (this is a magazine piece, not a lit review), and please don't put any invented quotations in the mouths of Molina, Plantinga, or anyone else. Use one concrete running example (the decaf guest works, or pick your own) and keep it going through the whole piece. Aim for roughly 800-1000 words.

After the piece, give me a table I can hand to my fact-checker with these columns: "Type of knowledge" | "What it covers" | "Where it falls in the logical order" | "What (if anything) grounds its truths" | "Standard objection to it". Rows: natural knowledge, middle knowledge, free knowledge.
