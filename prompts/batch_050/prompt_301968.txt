I am preparing a seminar on what adult children owe their parents, and I keep encountering two unhelpful extremes: debt-talk, according to which parental sacrifices generate a standing obligation to repay, and the friendship model associated with Jane English, according to which the relevant duties arise from the character of the present relationship rather than from a literal bill for childhood benefits. Before assessing either view, I want an adequacy standard for a theory of adult filial duty. The theory’s purpose should be to guide private deliberation—what an adult child has reason or obligation to do—not to determine what the state should legally compel. Treat the friendship model as the view just described rather than as an exercise in reconstructing English’s own article.

Write the desiderata such a theory must satisfy. For each requirement, provide a concrete case that shows why it matters and explain whether it is a genuine constraint or merely the prejudice of one camp. At minimum, address the following: parental sacrifice and gratitude; the difference between past benefits and an ongoing relationship; the fact that children do not choose their parents; negligent, absent, or abusive parents; severe parental need, including dementia or isolation; the adult child’s autonomy, resources, and responsibilities to others; the distinction between duty and supererogation; the allocation of burdens among siblings; the respective roles of family and public institutions; and whether obligations survive estrangement. Identify any desiderata that appear impossible to satisfy jointly—for example, unconditional concern for parental vulnerability versus protection against unlimited filial claims—and say which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which could reasonably be traded away.

After the list is complete, briefly test three approaches against it: a simple debt-or-gratitude account, the friendship model, and a role-based account on which occupying the parent-child relationship itself creates special obligations. Keep this scoring subordinate to the development of the criteria, and do not turn the answer into a literature survey. Finally, distinguish points on which there is broad agreement among serious participants in the debate from assumptions that remain genuinely contested, and do not present a contested premise—such as the possibility of special obligations independent of affection or present relationship—as though it were settled consensus.
