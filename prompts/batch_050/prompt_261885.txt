At a family dinner last week, my brother and his wife got into an argument about our uncle, a heavy smoker for forty years who finally quit, and who now delivers stern lectures to his teenage grandson every time he catches him vaping. My sister-in-law said: (1) He has no business lecturing the boy -- a man who smoked for four decades has forfeited any right to condemn someone else for the very same habit. My brother replied: (2) The advice is either sound or it isn't, and its soundness doesn't change depending on whose mouth it comes out of; you're confusing the message with the messenger. She answered: (3) He isn't merely advising, he's condemning, and condemnation is different: a private citizen may be entirely right that you parked illegally, but only the state may fine you; the authority to censure isn't earned just by being factually correct. He said: (4) That analogy backfires -- the uncle is subject to exactly the norms he invokes, and it's precisely his equal standing under those norms that entitles him to invoke them, the way any member of a community may call out a violation; his past failures are a stain on his character, not a defect in his title to speak. She said: (5) Then consider two cases -- one man who failed, openly admits it, and offers his past as a cautionary tale, and another who still vapes in secret while publicly shaming the boy. If we find the second man's lecture objectionable in a way the first man's isn't, and both say exactly the same true words, then something about the speaker, not the message, must be doing the work -- so your message/messenger distinction can't dissolve the problem. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and then say what, if anything, both of them are overlooking.
