I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of cognitive science, and every year we spend three weeks on the imagery debate -- Kosslyn's depictive or quasi-pictorial account against Pylyshyn's propositional or descriptionist alternative -- and every year I end those weeks more convinced that the debate, as usually staged, is not actually about what either side says it is about. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Here is the trajectory as I teach it. The early evidence was behavioral: mental rotation times scaling linearly with angle, scanning times across imagined maps scaling with depicted distance, interference between imagery and perception in the same modality. Pylyshyn's camp replied that all of this is compatible with discursive representations plus tacit knowledge of what perception would deliver, and leaned on demand-characteristic worries -- subjects know how long it takes to rotate a thing, and may simply be simulating what they believe the answer to be. Kosslyn's camp then moved to neuroscience: retinotopically organized activity in early visual cortex during imagery, scaling of that activity with imagined image size, selective deficits after lesions. The descriptionist reply is that spatial layout of neural activation does not by itself establish a depictive format -- a structured propositional system could be implemented in topographic tissue, and what matters is how the representations are processed, not where they sit. At that point both sides seem to agree about essentially every experiment and disagree about what any of it shows.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not over representational format at all but over what would count as evidence for a format claim in the first place: the depictivists treat isomorphism between represented spatial structure and representational structure, coupled with processing that exploits that isomorphism, as sufficient, while the descriptionists treat any functional behavior as compatible with any format given suitable auxiliary operations, which makes format claims empirically idle unless some additional constraint on processing cost or architecture is imposed. In other words, the debate stalled because format without a theory of processing is underdetermined, and neither side will state the processing theory that would make their format claim falsifiable.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is something else doing the work? Possibilities I want weighed: that the disagreement is genuinely verbal (the two sides mean different things by depictive); that it is substantive but about architecture-level commitments that behavioral and neuroimaging data cannot in principle arbitrate; that it reduces to a dispute about which cases count as data (phenomenology versus performance versus neural activation); or that recent work on aphantasia and hyperphantasia has quietly changed what the debate is about by turning it into a question about individual differences rather than about the representational currency of cognition. Whichever diagnosis you defend, tell me what progress would look like under it -- a distinction, an experimental design, a formal result about when format claims become empirically meaningful, or a case that no such progress is available and the question should be retired or replaced.
