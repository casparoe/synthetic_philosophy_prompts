I've been a reader of the rationalist blogs for well over a decade, and one slogan I've absorbed almost by osmosis is that priors wash out: given enough shared evidence, honest Bayesians converge, so persistent disagreement must trace back to dishonesty, differing information, or irrationality. Recently, though, I started reading up on the actual convergence results — the merging-of-opinions theorem, Aumann's agreement result, and so on — and I was struck by how many conditions they quietly assume. I now suspect the slogan is an overextrapolation from the theorems, but when I try to state my own position I just have a pile of gut verdicts on cases. I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them, and which verdicts a coherent principle would force me to abandon.

My verdicts: (1) Two Bayesians with wildly different priors about a coin's bias, who agree on the likelihoods and watch ten thousand flips together, will end up in practical agreement — their initial disagreement was rational but temporary. (2) Two Bayesians who disagree about which of two hypotheses holds, where the hypotheses are observationally equivalent — same predictions on every possible stream of data, like certain metaphysical or interpretational disputes — will never converge no matter how much evidence accumulates; this disagreement is rational and permanent. (3) Two people who watch the same study get published but disagree about how much evidential weight a study of that kind carries can rationally diverge further after seeing it; polarization on shared evidence isn't necessarily a reasoning failure. (4) Two agents with a common prior who honestly and repeatedly exchange their posteriors must end up agreeing; if full mutual transparency hasn't produced agreement, at least one of them is failing as a Bayesian. (5) And yet: a friend and I have argued politics for years, we know each other's arguments and track records intimately, I regard us as roughly equally intelligent and informed, and we still disagree — and I cannot bring myself to conclude that one of us is simply being irrational. (6) Finally, if both agents are certain the data come from one of two models but the truth is a third possibility neither has considered, I think they may converge — but their convergence vindicates nothing, since they might be converging on a shared falsehood.

What I want is the principle, or the small set of principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible — presumably something about shared likelihoods, identifiability, and model misspecification doing the load-bearing work, but I'd like it stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case is motivating. In particular, verdicts (4) and (5) seem to be in direct tension, and I'd like an honest verdict on which has to give: is the right move to say Aumann-style results have preconditions real humans never meet (no common prior, no common knowledge of rationality, no exhaustive exchange of unverifiable private evidence), or to bite the bullet on (5) and accept that my friendship contains at least one irrationalist? I'd also like the principle's pronouncements on cases I haven't given a verdict on: say, two forecasters trained on the same data who disagree only because they weight base rates differently, or an agent who rationally discounts evidence from a source it has independent reason to distrust.
