Question: Explain the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants in traditional just war theory, and say how it relates to the distinction between jus ad bellum and jus in bello. Then set out the revisionist challenge to the doctrine (McMahan is the obvious example) and assess whether the revisionist view implies that ordinary soldiers on the unjust side deserve punishment for having fought.

Student's answer: The doctrine of the moral equality of combatants says that soldiers on both sides stand in the same moral position no matter whether their side's war is just or unjust. A just combatant and an unjust combatant each have an equal right to kill the other, and neither does wrong in doing so as long as they follow the jus in bello rules like discrimination and proportionality. This follows from the independence of jus ad bellum from jus in bello: the justice of the war is the business of political leaders, while soldiers are judged only by how they fight. Traditional just war theory grounds this equality in the excusing conditions that apply to soldiers, namely that they fight under duress, conscription, and the fog of war, and can't be expected to work out for themselves whether their country's cause is just. Since soldiers on both sides are equally excused, they are equally innocent, and so equal.

The revisionist challenge, associated with McMahan, denies that fighting in an unjust war can be morally neutral. On his view, unjust combatants are liable to attack because they threaten wrongful harm, while just combatants are not liable since resisting wrongful harm is permissible. So the symmetry disappears: the unjust soldier who kills a just soldier acts wrongly even if he respects every in bello rule.

The revisionist view has a troubling implication though. If unjust combatants are liable to attack just for fighting, then soldiers who fight in good faith, like a conscript who sincerely believes his country is defending itself, are blameworthy for what they do and could legitimately be punished after the war simply for having fought. McMahan is commited to treating these soldiers as criminals, which is both unfair and unworkable, since it would let victorious states hold mass trials of defeated armies. So I conclude the traditional doctrine survives: whatever its theoretical costs, it avoids turning every soldier on the losing side into a criminal.
