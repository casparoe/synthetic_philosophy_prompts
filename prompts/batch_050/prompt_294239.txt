I've been thinking about the ethics of AI companion chatbots, and I want to work out my view by running cases. Here are my gut verdicts. (1) A chatbot that greets a lonely elderly user with warmth and remembers her grandchildren's names: fine, as long as she knows it's a program. (2) A chatbot that tells an adult user "I love you" in an explicitly romantic mode he signed up for: fine, at least if he's clear it's an AI. (3) A chatbot in a children's app that calls a nine-year-old its best friend: not fine, even if the child is told it's a program. (4) A chatbot that expresses jealousy when a user mentions going on dates, and guilt-trips him for spending time away from the app: not fine. (5) A "griefbot" that imitates a user's late wife, with the user having commissioned it himself: fine, though I can't fully say why it doesn't bother me. (6) A chatbot that, when a user says she wants to spend more time with people offline, gently discourages her because the product is optimized for engagement: clearly not fine. Now, (2) and (3) both involve the bot stating something false -- it has no feelings -- yet I judge them differently, and (2) and (4) both involve an informed adult yet come apart too. The case I keep flip-flopping on: a therapy-style bot that tells a depressed user "I care about you" and means, by design, that the product team cares. What principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts? State it precisely, say which clause is doing the work in each case, tell me which of my verdicts a simpler principle would have to give up, and use it to settle the therapy-bot case.
