I'm a biostatistician, and I've ended up in a reading group with some philosophers of biology. We've been going around in circles on the classic tautology objection to natural selection -- the charge that if the "fittest" are simply those that survive and reproduce, then "survival of the fittest" reduces to "survival of the survivors" and explains nothing -- and on the standard reply, the propensity interpretation, which says fitness is not actual reproductive output but a probabilistic disposition of an organism or type, something like its expected reproductive success given its traits and environment, so the theory makes a substantive claim rather than a definitional shuffle. I keep feeling that I already have the tools to adjudicate this, but I can't get the philosophers' vocabulary and mine into contact.

So here is what I want. Restate both the objection and the propensity reply in the vocabulary I actually work in -- random variables, conditional expectations, estimands and estimators, parameter identification, the distinction between fitting a sample and predicting out of it -- as precisely and as charitably as you can. Do not prejudge the outcome by translating in a way that makes one side trivially right: I want to know, for instance, whether the tautology objection is really the complaint that fitness is an unidentified parameter, inferred post hoc from realized offspring counts and then invoked to explain those very counts, in the way a model with more free parameters than data "explains" anything you feed it. And I want to know whether introducing fitness as a propensity fixes that the way positing a latent variable with independent measurement channels fixes identification, or whether it just renames the problem -- since in most actual cases we have no independent access to the propensity except through the realized outcomes it was supposed to explain.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate translate cleanly, which parts you had to distort, and which have no expression in statistical vocabulary at all? In particular, I'm unsure how to render one thing the philosophers seem to take seriously: a propensity is defined over an expectation across hypothetical replicates, but each lineage is a single realized draw with no ensemble to average over, and it's not obvious what work the expectation does for the one history we actually observe -- say whether that worry survives translation or dissolves in my framework. Finally, give me your verdict on the residue: does the untranslatable remainder point to something my statistical vocabulary is genuinely missing (and if so, what concept would I need to add), or does it show that part of the philosophers' dispute is less substantive than it appears?
