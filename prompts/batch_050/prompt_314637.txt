Two friends of mine run a small giving circle (about 15 people, maybe $40k a year total) and they had this drawn-out argument about how to pick between causes, using that importance/tractability/neglectedness framework. I stayed out of it but I want an outside verdict.

They were comparing two options: (A) a big well-known global health intervention where the evidence is strong but lots of funders are already active, and (B) a weird little cause almost nobody funds, where the problem is arguably huge but no one really knows if money helps.

Round one: Mara said just score each cause on the three factors and multiply. Jonah said multiplication is bonkers here, because it means any factor scored near zero vetoes the whole cause. B is maybe genuinely low-tractability, he said, so multiplying zeroes it out even if it's enormously important and totally neglected, and that can't be right — a framework that systematically rules out longshots is biased, not neutral. Mara said that's a feature not a bug: important-but-intractable is exactly the black hole that eats altruists' money, and the whole point of having tractability as a factor is to stop "this problem is huge" from winning every time.

Round two: Jonah said fine, but then tractability needs discounting for unreliability, because it's the factor we're worst at estimating — importance and neglectedness you can at least roughly check, but "will money help" for an unexplored cause is basically a guess, and you shouldn't let your noisiest input dominate the decision. Mara replied that this is backwards: if you can't estimate tractability you should assume it's mediocre-to-bad, because most possible interventions don't work, so ignorance is evidence against.

Round three: they fought about neglectedness. Jonah said the right measure is what's already going into the field, and A is clearly less neglected, end of story. Mara said that's the wrong counterfactual for them specifically: their $40k is a rounding error next to the big funders in A, and if a big funder would eventually cover the gap anyway, their marginal dollar in A accomplishes less than the raw "money in the field" number suggests — and actually by that same logic the crowd-out concern makes A look *worse* than its neglectedness score, so he was accidentally helping her side. Jonah said no, the counterfactual reasoning cuts the other way: in B, their $40k might be a huge share of the field, which means their decisions there are load-bearing and unreplicated, and one wrong guess wastes everything, whereas in A their mistake is diluted.

Round four: Jonah finally said the whole framework is just a heuristic stand-in for expected value per marginal dollar, and whenever the scoring conflicts with a direct EV estimate, EV should win and the scores should be thrown out. Mara said that abandons the framework precisely where you need it, since direct EV estimates are the least trustworthy exactly in cases like B.

Neither conceded anything. Can you go through each of the four rounds and say who's actually right on each specific point, and whether there's something both of them are missing? Don't give me a both-sides-have-a-point shrug — I want you to commit to a verdict per round with reasons, even where it's a close call.
