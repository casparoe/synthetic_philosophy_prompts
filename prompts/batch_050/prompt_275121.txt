Exam question: In 250–350 words: (a) state the prototype theory and the exemplar theory of concepts, and for each give one phenomenon it explains well; (b) state the theory theory and give one consideration that motivates it over similarity-based accounts.

Student's answer:

"The prototype theory holds that a concept is a summary representation of a category's central tendency: a set of features weighted by how characteristic they are of the category. Categorizing an item is a matter of computing its similarity to the prototype, and because similarity comes in degrees, membership itself is graded — a robin is simply a better bird than a penguin. The theory's flagship success is the typicality effect: people verify 'a robin is a bird' faster than 'a penguin is a bird,' rate robins as better examples, and produce them earlier when listing birds, all of which follows from differential similarity to the prototype.

The exemplar theory rejects summary representations altogether: the concept is a store of remembered category members, and classification proceeds by similarity to the retrieved exemplars. Because nothing is averaged away, the view retains information about a category's variability and its atypical members; it therefore handles, better than a single prototype can, categories whose boundary is disjunctive or whose exceptions matter — encountering an ostrich changes your later judgments in a way the prototype view has difficulty registering.

The theory theory holds that concepts are constituents of intuitive, domain-level theories — miniature bodies of causal and explanatory knowledge — and that categorization is a kind of inference within such a theory. A central motivation comes from transformation cases: a raccoon dyed and surgically altered to look and behave exactly like a skunk is still judged to be a raccoon. On the theory-theoretic diagnosis, this is because subjects' biological theory tells them that origins and insides, not surface appearance, fix kind membership. Overall similarity to stored exemplars of raccoons still favors this judgment, and that is what sustains it.

Finally, typicality data themselves favor the exemplar view over the prototype view. People rate 4 as a better even number than 106 even though evenness is exactly defined. A prototype representation cannot arise for a classically defined category, while stored exemplars predict graded goodness for any category at all — so the exemplar theory explains why the gradient persists where no prototype exists."
