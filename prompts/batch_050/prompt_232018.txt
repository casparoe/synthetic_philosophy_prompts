I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long essay on how fiction handles the question of whether psychopaths genuinely know right from wrong. I want to anchor the piece in No Country for Old Men -- the Coen brothers' film, though you may draw on Cormac McCarthy's novel where the two diverge. Please read the film through the debate about psychopathy and moral knowledge.

Anton Chigurh is the obvious case study, but I want the reading to resist the lazy version, in which he is simply evil and knows it. His coin tosses, his talk of fate and accountability, his visible irritation when his victims refuse to play along with his little procedures -- all of this suggests a man operating under some kind of normative code, however alien. So the philosophical question the essay needs answered is this: does Chigurh understand that what he does is wrong, in whatever sense matters? Psychologists have long reported that psychopathic offenders can state moral rules fluently while seeming unable to feel their force, and there's a live dispute about what that means -- whether moral knowledge requires the relevant emotional responsiveness, whether the failure is really one of understanding rather than motivation, and whether someone who reliably mouths the right judgments but is unmoved by them counts as knowing anything at all. Map Chigurh onto that dispute carefully. Which account of moral knowledge does his character presuppose, and which does it embarrass?

Then complicate the reading with Sheriff Bell. Bell knows exactly what is right and spends the film discovering that this knowledge does nothing for him -- he cannot comprehend Chigurh's world, cannot act effectively in it, and retires. If the film gives us a psychopath who may possess a counterfeit of moral knowledge and a decent man whose genuine moral knowledge is impotent, what is the film saying about what moral knowledge is for? Finally, be honest about where the philosophical lens distorts the film: Chigurh may be less a portrait of clinical psychopathy than a device or a symbol, and I'd rather have my essay acknowledge that than pretend otherwise.
