I run a fortnightly political philosophy reading group at my university -- roughly sixteen regulars, a mix of undergraduates and a couple of keen postgrads -- and we are about to spend a session on the boundary problem: the puzzle of who gets to be part of the demos that democratic procedures are supposed to aggregate, given that any vote on the membership question would itself need a prior demos to hold it. We have done the reading, and everyone can state the problem fluently, but I get the sense it feels to them like a tidy logical curiosity rather than something with real bite. I want an activity, runnable in about ninety minutes, that makes the group *live* the problem rather than recite it.

Design me a structured exercise in which the participants, as a group, have to make some genuine collective decision with small but real stakes (you may assume I can supply a modest budget -- say, a pizza order for the end of term, or the power to set next term's reading list and expel non-contributors), and in which the rules force them to confront the question of who counts as a member *before* any decision can be taken. Crucially, the mechanics should be arranged so that different plausible inclusion principles -- everyone affected by the decision, everyone subject to the group's coercive rules, everyone physically present, everyone who has shown sustained commitment -- deliver *different* voter rolls, and so that any attempt to settle the disagreement by voting merely reproduces the problem one level up. Participants should stumble into the regress themselves before I ever name it. Please give me concrete rules and roles, a timeline, and a plan for how to seed the group so that the boundary dispute arises naturally rather than being announced.

Include a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the theoretical options (the all-affected-interests principle, the coercion or all-subjected principle, and the claim that the problem is simply insoluble from within democratic theory and must be settled by something prior to it). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover. And be honest about which feature of the real-world boundary problem the activity cannot reproduce -- I suspect the fact that my students can simply walk out of the room, whereas actual political membership is thrust upon people, is one such feature, but tell me if there are others.
