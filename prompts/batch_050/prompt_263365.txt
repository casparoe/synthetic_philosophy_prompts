I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course, and for my seminar presentation I've been assigned Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" Rather than present the familiar trilemma, I want to defend a narrower and more contentious thesis: that Bostrom undersells how much philosophy of mind the argument actually needs. Early in the paper he writes: "We need only the weaker assumption that it would suffice for the generation of subjective experiences that the computational processes of a human brain are structurally replicated in suitably fine-grained detail, such as on the level of individual synapses. This attenuated version of substrate-independence is quite widely accepted." My paper argues that this move is not as innocent as it looks. The attenuated thesis, as stated, licenses the claim that a sufficiently detailed simulation would be conscious, but the probability argument downstream requires something more: that my current observer-moment could just as easily be one of the simulated ones, i.e., that simulated and biological observer-moments form a single reference class over which an indifference principle may be applied. I claim that gap is real — that one can coherently accept the weak thesis about what would generate experience while denying the strong indifference claim about which experiences I might be having — and that this is where the argument is most vulnerable, not in the empirical premises about posthuman computing power.

The audience matters a lot for how this lands, so here is who will be in the room. Our seminar has about twelve people: two committed functionalists who treat substrate-independence as close to obvious and will think I'm manufacturing a distinction without a difference; one student who took a course with a Searle-influenced professor and keeps raising biological-naturalist worries that I think are beside my point but that I can't dismiss rudely; one very sharp formal epistemology student who has brought up the Doomsday argument and self-locating belief in every session and will surely see the indifference principle as the real target; a couple of quiet attendees who mostly follow the functionalists; and our instructor, who knows the paper well and likes to ask the question the student presenter is least prepared for.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, each phrased the way that specific audience member would phrase it — the functionalists' version of "you're just denying that simulated people count," the Searle-adjacent student's version, the formal epistemologist's version — with an honest assessment of how much damage each does to my thesis, what I should concede up front (I suspect I have to concede something about how fine-grained "structural replication" needs to be), what I should hold, and a two-minute reply for each, flagging where each reply is soft. I especially want help with the distinction at the heart of my paper: how do I state the difference between "simulated minds would be conscious" and "my observer-moment is exchangeable with simulated ones" crisply enough that it doesn't collapse, under pressure, into either the functionalists' framing or a generic skeptical worry? Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I have a suspicion it involves whether my objection, if sound, would equally undermine the Doomsday argument and other applications of self-sampling assumptions, and I don't know whether I'm committed to that.
