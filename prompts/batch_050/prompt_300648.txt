Exam question:
"Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness holds that phenomenal properties -- the supposed intrinsic, qualitative features that it is 'like something' to instantiate -- do not exist, and that our introspective representations systematically misrepresent our brain states as having such properties. (a) State the view more precisely, using Frankish's version as your exemplar, and explain how it differs both from eliminativism about experience and from standard reductive physicalist accounts of consciousness. (b) Explain how the illusionist appeals to the meta-problem of consciousness as motivation for the view. (c) Consider the objection that the illusion itself must seem a certain way to the subject, so illusionism merely relocates phenomenal consciousness rather than eliminating it. Does this objection succeed? Defend a verdict."

Student's answer:
"(a) Illusionism, as developed by Frankish, says that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective illusion. Our brains run internal monitoring processes that represent our own states in a schematic, edited way, and these representations depict sensory states as having intrinsic qualitative properties -- phenomenal properties or 'qualia' -- that they do not in fact have. On this view, since phenomenal properties are what make an episode an experience, illusionists conclude that we do not actually have experiences at all; what we call experience is just the brain's misleading self-model. This distinguishes illusionism from reductive physicalism: the reductive physicalist accepts that phenomenal properties exist and tries to identify them with, or explain them in terms of, physical-functional properties, whereas the illusionist denies there is anything to reduce. It also distinguishes illusionism from eliminativism, since eliminativism rejects the folk-psychological notion of experience wholesale, while the illusionist keeps the notion but treats its phenomenal content as fictional.

(b) The motivation comes from the meta-problem of consciousness, Chalmers' name for the question of why the hard problem of consciousness cannot be solved by standard empirical methods. If the hard problem is genuinely intractable, the best explanation is that the thing it was trying to explain does not exist. The illusionist argues that a theory of the meta-problem -- an account of why we say and believe the things we do about phenomenal consciousness, in purely functional terms -- discharges our only real explanatory obligation. Once we explain why we are disposed to report qualia, there is nothing left over.

(c) The objection says that an illusion of phenomenal consciousness would itself have to seem a certain way to me, and that seeming is itself phenomenal, so the view regresses or collapses. The illusionist replies that the seeming is exhausted by our dispositions to judge, report, and reason, which require no phenomenal properties. This reply succeeds, because the objection simply assumes that seemings must be phenomenal, which is exactly what the illusionist denies, so the objection begs the question. Therefore the regress objection fails and illusionism is the most defensible position."
