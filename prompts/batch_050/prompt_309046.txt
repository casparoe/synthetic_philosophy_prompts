Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend over dinner about why people find things funny, and the argument ended in a stalemate I would like you to break. Her claim was that the four established families of humour theories, taken together, leave nothing out: anything genuinely amusing is explained by at least one of them, and the long history of the subject has simply failed to produce a fifth. I suspected this was suspiciously tidy, but in the heat of the exchange I could only offer stray examples, each of which she calmly absorbed into one of her four. I am writing now, in cooler blood, to find out whether she was right.

For the record, the four, as we understood them:

1. Superiority theory (Hobbes): amusement is a kind of sudden glory in our own eminence, delight at the infirmities or absurdities of others by which we feel ourselves exalted. The slip on the banana skin, the pompous man deflated, the rival's mishap.
2. Incongruity theory (Kant, Schopenhauer): amusement arises when an expectation is defeated or a concept is suddenly fitted to an object that does not sit under it — a mismatch between what we anticipated and what arrives.
3. Relief theory (Spencer, Freud): laughter discharges accumulated nervous or psychical energy; jokes smuggle forbidden or painful material past our inhibitions at a saving of effort, and humour is the economy of feeling.
4. Benign violation theory (McGraw and Warren): something is funny when it is appraised simultaneously as a violation — of a norm, of dignity, of good sense — and as benign, safe, all right. Tickling, wordplay and transgressive jokes all live at this crossing.

Please act as a professional philosopher working in aesthetics and the philosophy of cognitive science, and think carefully rather than quickly. Your task: propose a fifth theory of humour that is genuinely new relative to these four. I want to be strict about what counts as new. It must not be a relabelling, a special case, or a hybrid of items on the list; a theory that merely re-describes incongruity in cognitive language, or superiority in evolutionary language, does not count. It must also be a theory of what makes things amusing — of the conditions under which we experience something as funny — and not merely a claim about the social functions of laughter, unless you can show that the functional claim itself constitutes a rival account of the amusing.

You may brainstorm several candidates before settling, but I want a committed answer, not a menu: select exactly one theory, state it as precisely as you can, and defend it. In doing so, please (a) show point by point why it is not a variant of any of the four; (b) test it against hard cases the standard four handle awkwardly, such as why we cannot tickle ourselves, why jokes decay so fast on repetition, why some people are amused by jokes at their own expense, and why nervous laughter occurs at funerals where nothing seems funny at all; and (c) give an honest verdict on whether your own proposal is actually any good — where it is strongest, where it strains, and whether it genuinely explains something the four explain worse.

Finally, adjudicate the original dispute: does the existence of your fifth theory refute my friend's exhaustiveness claim, or does she survive on a technicality because your theory collapses into one of hers under scrutiny? Commit to a verdict. Please write in British English.
