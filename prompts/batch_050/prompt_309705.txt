Ok so I'm in a political philosphy seminar on the ethics of immigration and our class keeps going around in circles on what I'll call the house analogy for border control. The exclusion side (including a paper we read) says: a country is like a house. The homeowner may not have done anything special to deserve the house, but it's theirs, and nobody thinks you're morally required to let a stranger move in just because they'd be much better off inside -- so a state can exclude immigrants even when exclusion leaves those immigrants badly off. The open borders guy in my discussion section fires back that a country is more like a public commons that a few people happened to fence off first, so exclusion is what needs justifying, not entry.

Can you actually audit the house analogy for me instead of just vibing about it? I want the correspondence table first: who plays the homeowner (the state? current citizens jointly? does that include citizens who want to invite people in?), what plays the house (the territory? the institutions? the infrastructure everyone's taxes paid for?), what's the door, the lock, the mortgage, who counts as the stranger knocking. Then where the mapping breaks. Some disanalogies I half-noticed but can't articulate well: the state is the very thing that creates and enforces property rights, so using private ownership to ground the state's authority to exclude feels circular; nobody built the country the way you build or buy a house; the scale is totally different (turning away one guest vs. a policy binding on millions); and refusing someone your house leaves them the whole rest of the world, whereas every state excluding someone leaves them nowhere. I don't know which of these actually matter for the argument and which are just pedantic point-scoring.

Main thing I want to know: does the conclusion -- states have a presumptive right to exclude, end of story -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion the whole time? And if it breaks, what's the better analogy, if there even is one? A members-only club? A condo association? An inherited estate full of tenants? Our prof mentioned in passing that some philosophers defend exclusion through freedom of association rather than property, so also tell me whether that version fares better or just relocates the same problem.
