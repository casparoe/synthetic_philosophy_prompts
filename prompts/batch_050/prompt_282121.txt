You are a historian of economic thought with particular expertise in Marx's manuscripts and their reception. Please think carefully about the question below before answering; I would like you to begin by briefly brainstorming the plausible diagnoses, and only then give your considered analysis.

For several decades now, interpreters of Marx have argued over a short stretch of the Grundrisse usually called the Fragment on Machines, in which Marx sketches a hypothetical future where direct labour time shrinks relative to automated production and, he suggests, ceases to be an adequate measure of wealth. One camp -- running from Italian operaismo through to recent accelerationist and post-capitalist writers -- reads this passage as Marx's genuine, if underdeveloped, theory: automation undermines the law of value from within, so the development of machinery makes capitalism self-abolishing, and the political task is to push that tendency through. The opposing camp replies that the fragment is a speculative aside in an unpublished notebook, that Capital's settled view -- machines transfer value but do not create it, and automation intensifies relative-surplus-value extraction rather than dissolving value -- supersedes it, and that the first camp mistakes a thought experiment for a theory. Both sides cite the same handful of passages, the exchange has been essentially stationary for years, and nobody seems to concede anything.

My hypothesis, which you should feel free to reject, is that this is not really a disagreement about what the fragment says but about two deeper matters neither side states openly: first, which Marx has interpretive authority -- the exploratory notebooks or the published critique -- and second, whether 'value' names a historically specific social form that can internally collapse or a category that persists so long as production is for exchange. If that is right, the surface argument about a few pages of text is doing no real work.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it philological (a dispute over textual status and translation), theoretical (a hidden dispute about the logic of the value form), empirical (a disguised bet about whether automation is in fact reducing the role of labour time), or verbal? Or is it, as I suspect, a case of two groups answering different questions -- one about what Marx hoped, one about what his theory entails -- under a single heading? Ground your diagnosis in what each side actually argues rather than in a survey of positions. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a piece of archival or philological evidence, an empirical finding, or a reframing of the question -- or argue, if you think it warranted, that the impasse is permanent and explain why.

Throughout, please distinguish clearly between what is settled among serious readers of these texts and what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any contested reading as the consensus.
