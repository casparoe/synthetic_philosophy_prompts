So I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the recent debates over whether AI systems could ever deserve moral consideration -- you know, people arguing about whether a sufficiently advanced chatbot might genuinely suffer, plus the parallel stuff about shrimp welfare and insect farming suddenly being treated as serious causes. My editor wants a historical hook, and I pitched Jeremy Bentham, since he famously pushed the question away from whether a being can reason or talk and toward whether it can suffer, back when that was a radical thing to say even about animals.

Here's what I need from you: a reconstruction of what Bentham would most likely make of the current moment, written carefully enough that I can build the column around it. A few specific things I want the piece to get right:

- Would his criterion -- capacity for suffering, full stop -- actually let AI systems in, or does it quietly assume something (nervous systems? biology? evolutionary continuity with us?) that rules them out from the start? I want the honest answer, not the flattering one.
- Bentham was writing when animals were legally property and kids worked in mines, and he still took animal suffering seriously. Would he see today's concern for shrimp and maybe-machines as the same kind of move he made -- the circle of concern expanding to a case that strikes most people as absurd -- or would he think the analogy breaks somewhere? Where?
- How would he handle the sheer uncertainty? With animals you could look at behavior and anatomy and feel fairly confident. With an AI, the thing *says* it suffers, which for him would presumably not settle it either way. Does his framework have the resources to act under that kind of doubt, or does it just stall?
- Bonus: he had that whole thing about pushpin versus poetry and ranking pleasures. If digital minds turned out to be real and cheap to produce in huge numbers, each having mildly pleasant experiences, does his view commit him to something weird there?

Please don't just give me "he'd say X" pronouncements -- show the reasoning from his actual commitments, and flag the spots where you're extrapolating versus where the texts genuinely pin him down. Also flag anywhere the answer surprises you, since suprising the reader is kind of the point of the column.
