Last week I got cornered after our metaethics reading group by a friend who thinks he's found a kill-shot against every full-information account of value at once -- ideal advisor theories of wellbeing, coherent extrapolated volition as an AI alignment target, the whole family. His argument is that idealization is so plastic that it can never do real work. I've been turning it over for days and I can't find the step I'd actually deny, but the conclusion has to be wrong, or at least way stronger than he's earned. Here's his argument as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Take any preference a person actually has, no matter how parochial or weird -- my love of my hometown sports team, my distate for cilantro, my commitment to my particular religion. (2) For each such preference, there exists some idealization of me that preserves it: just run the "full information and flawless reflection" procedure on the parts of my cognition that don't bear on the preference, and leave the preference-forming parts as they are. (3) Nothing about the idea of idealization itself forbids this, because idealization is defined relative to which cognitive shortcomings it fixes, and every shortcoming has to be identified as a shortcoming by some standard. (4) That standard is either drawn from my actual values -- in which case the theory is circular, since it uses my un-idealized preferences to decide which idealization counts -- or it's supplied by the theorist -- in which case "what I'd want if I were ideal" is just a laundering device for what the theorist wants. (5) So idealization can be gerrymandered to ratify anything, and every full-information theory of value, CEV included, is either circular or covert imposition. (6) So we should stop talking about what idealized versions of people would want and just talk about what actual people want.

My instinct is that something goes wrong around (2) or (3) -- like he's treating idealization as modular when it isn't -- but I can't articulate it without hand-waving, and (4) honestly seems like a real dilemma to me. So: which step do you actually reject, why does it look plausible even though it's wrong (or if the argument is valid, which premise is cheapest to give up and what do I lose by giving it up)? If it matters, I'd rather keep the idea that there's a fact of the matter about what idealized-me would want than keep any particular story about how to extrapolate it. Also, please tell me what would change your verdict -- what premise, if I could defend it properly, would resurrect my friend's argument, or where your own diagnosis is most likely to fall apart. And write the answer in American English, please.
