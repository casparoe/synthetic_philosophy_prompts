I got into a dumb late-night argument with a friend of mine who TA's calculus at our university. His position is basically that Zeno's paradoxes were settled in the 19th century: convergent series, the epsilon-delta definition of a limit, done, case closed, and philosophers who keep writing about supertasks and the Arrow are just people who never learned analysis. My position (I'm the philosophy grad student in this friendship, so maybe I'm biased) is that this misses what the paradoxes were ever about. Anyway, he sent me this passage from Aristotle's Physics, Book VI, thinking it supports him:

"Moreover, it is plain that everything continuous is divisible into divisibles that are infinitely divisible: for if it were divisible into indivisibles, we should have an indivisible in contact with an indivisible, since the extremities of things that are continuous with one another are one and are in contact."

His reading: Aristotle says a line can't be built out of points, but Cantor and Dedekind showed a line literally is a set of points, so Aristotle's whole framework was wrong and the paradoxes died with it. My reading is murkier: modern math agrees with Aristotle that no point is "in contact" with another (there's always a line segment between any two), so maybe the set-theoretic continuum vindicates his intuition rather than refuting it, and the paradoxes were never really about infinite sums at all.

What I actually want from you is not a verdict on Zeno but a diagnosis of why this debate never dies. Mathematicians think it's over; philosophers keep publishing; both sides know the same math. Is this a substantive disagreement about what motion is, a verbal disagreement about what "solving a paradox" means, or two groups answering different questions (one about the mathematics of the continuum, one about the metaphysics of change) under the same heading? Does the Aristotle passage help decide which -- i.e., does the correct reading of it bear on whether the "standard solution" addresses the paradox or just changes the subject? Give me your diagnosis with reasons, and tell me what would change your mind: what argument or result would convince you the disagreement is really of a different kind than you conclude, or that my calculus friend is simply right and the philosophers are confused.
