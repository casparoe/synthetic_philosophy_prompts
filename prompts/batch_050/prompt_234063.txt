I recently had to choose between a guaranteed job offer and a riskier startup position, and while thinking it through I caught myself holding exactly the preferences Allais described, so I worked out the following argument to convince myself I was being irrational. It hasn't worked, which bothers me. (1) When two options share some feature with certainty — or with a probability unaffected by the choice between them — a rational evaluation should depend only on the respects in which they differ; otherwise identical features are doing work in one comparison and not another. (2) In the first Allais pair, the certain $1M and the gamble (89% $1M, 10% $5M, 1% nothing) share an 89% chance of $1M, so by (1) the choice between them reduces to the choice between the remaining 11% slices: 11% $1M versus 10% $5M plus 1% nothing. (3) But that reduced choice just is the second Allais pair, so anyone who takes the certain $1M in the first pair must take the 11% $1M in the second. (4) Almost everyone, including me, takes the certain $1M in the first pair and the 10% shot at $5M in the second. (5) Therefore almost everyone, including me, is irrational in a mundane choice about money. I find (5) nearly as hard to accept as the pattern itself is hard to shake — the 1% chance of walking away with nothing in the first pair seems to matter in a way it doesn't in the second, and I can't talk myself out of that. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to give something up, I'd rather abandon (5) than (1), but I want to know what (1) was actually buying me before I pay.
