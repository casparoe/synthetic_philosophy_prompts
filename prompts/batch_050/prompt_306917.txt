I serve on the legal affairs committee of a mid-sized city council in a country with no general duty-to-rescue law. The council has before it a proposed ordinance that would require any person who witnesses another person in imminent danger of death or serious injury to render assistance, provided doing so entails no significant risk to the rescuer and no more than trivial cost; a telephone call to emergency services would ordinarily suffice to discharge the duty. Breach would be a summary offence with a modest fine. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee in advance of next month's vote, recommending whether we should adopt the ordinance as drafted, adopt it in amended form, or reject it.

Two complications should occupy most of the opinion. First, several committee members are persuaded by the familiar philosophical argument that the intuitive duty to save a drowning child from a shallow pond, at the cost of ruining one's shoes, generalises alarmingly far: if proximity and eyewitness contact do no moral work, then each of us is already under a comparably stringent duty towards distant strangers we could save at similar cost, and an ordinance confined to emergencies one happens to witness looks less like a principled legal floor and more like an arbitrary concession to what happens to be visible. The opinion must say something honest about whether the ordinance's restriction to witnessed, physically present emergencies is defensible as law even if it is morally underinclusive, or whether that restriction undermines the rationale for the ordinance itself. Second, the draft leaves several design questions open, and the opinion should take a position on each rather than flagging them for later: whether the duty is genuinely discharged by a notification the bystander knows to be futile or redundant; whether a bystander who attempts a rescue and botches it through negligence should face liability for the attempt; how the trivial-cost ceiling should be understood in hard cases (an hour of one's time, a missed flight, a ruined laptop); and whether criminal penalties are the right enforcement instrument at all, as against civil liability or mere reporting duties.

The opinion should run to the length such documents actually run to, should end with a clear recommendation the committee can vote on, and should give the strongest version of the case for whatever it recommends against, since at least three of my colleagues are presently on the other side and will be reading it.
