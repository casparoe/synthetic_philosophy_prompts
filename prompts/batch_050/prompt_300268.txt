I run a little philosophy-of-science reading group (mostly grad students, mix of physics and phil backgrounds) and I want to make a "where do you actually stand on determinism and prediction?" quiz for our session on chaos. What I keep noticing is that people use "determinism," "predictability," and "chaos" as if they were a package deal, so the whole point of the quiz is to pry those apart. Please write me 8 to 10 questions where each one is a concrete scenario rather than an abstract question about labels. Good candidates for scenarios: a weather model whose error doubles every day or so, frictionless billiard balls on a table with slightly convex obstacles, a quantum measurement on a single atom, a coin flip midair, the orbit of Pluto over hundreds of millions of years, a computer program whose long-run behavior no shortcut can foresee (you just have to run it), a perfectly known roulette wheel that a casino still can't exploit. Feel free to swap or add your own, but keep everything answerable by someone who's never heard the vocabulary.

Each question should get two or three answer options phrased in plain language. Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto these leaves at minimum: the Laplacian view (determined and in-principle fully predictable), determinism with in-principle predictability but only practical unpredictability, determinism where unpredictability is somehow principled even given perfect measurement (chaos and computational irreducibility doing different work here is a distinction I want the quiz to be sensitive to), macro-determinism with genuine micro-indeterminism from quantum mechanics, and thoroughgoing indeterminism or the view that the whole determined/undetermined question is empirically undecidable. If your question set generates combinations of answers that no leaf covers, say so explicitly and take a guess at whether that spot is empty because it's incoherent or just unpopular. Also flag any pair of leaves your quiz ends up unable to tell apart rather than fudging the key.

Two more things. First, each leaf in the key should get one sentence naming its most awkward commitment (e.g. the Laplacian has to say a coin flip's outcome was fixed at the big bang, or whatever you think the real pinch is). Second, please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email to me: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and no numbered list for the questions either, just work through them in paragraphs with the options inline. I know that makes the scoring key annoying but I'd rather have it that way.
