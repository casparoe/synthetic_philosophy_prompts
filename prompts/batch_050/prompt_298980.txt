I'm a graduate student preparing the appendix of a dissertation chapter on slurs, and I need a classification instrument for the debate over what makes slurring terms offensive. Please write a decision tree that starts from the bare observation that a slur is offensive in a way its neutral counterpart is not, and that ends at a named theoretical position. Aim for eight to twelve leaves.

The tree must be built from questions, not from a list of theories: each branch node should be one diagnostic datum on which positions genuinely diverge, phrased as a question a reader could answer without knowing which theory it sorts them into. The diagnostics I want represented are these (use these formulations or equivalents; define each briefly at first use):

- Whether the derogation survives embedding: does a sentence like "There are no [slur]s here" still offend, and if so, does that tell us the derogation is truth-conditional content or something that projects?
- Non-displaceability: whether the offensiveness is tied to that specific word (you can't swap in another word with the same content and keep the sting), or whether it tracks something detachable like social taboo.
- Whether a speaker can slur someone without using any slur at all, for instance through tone, scare quotes, or a pointed use of a neutral group noun, and whether a theory that cannot generate that case is thereby refuted or merely incomplete.
- Whether offensiveness varies across audiences and eras in the way ordinary taboo vocabulary does, and whether such variation is evidence that the offense comes from a prohibition on the word rather than from its meaning.
- Whether the slur's content includes, essentially, the claim that the target group *deserves* contempt or mistreatment, and if so whether the term is thereby empty or false of everyone.
- How the theory handles reclaimed in-group uses, where the same word appears to lose its derogatory force without changing meaning.
- Whether the offense is a fact about the word's standing meaning or about the individual speaker's expressed attitude on a given occasion.

The leaves must include, at minimum: semantic content views (the derogation is part of what is said; include as a subtype the view that this content is partly institutional and therefore never satisfied), prohibitionism (the word is simply banned, and the offense follows from the ban), presuppositional or not-at-issue views (the derogation is carried but not asserted), expressivist views (the slur voices the speaker's contempt rather than describing anything), perspectival or ideology-indexing views (the slur signals allegiance to a derogating perspective rather than encoding any content), and speech-act views (uttering the slur *is* the derogation, whatever its semantics). You may add others if the question-space demands them. For each leaf, give the standard label plus one sentence stating the datum it handles worst.

Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question on the tree, say so explicitly and indicate what further datum would distinguish them. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, note it and diagnose whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or an unexplored position.

Please do not include citations or reading lists; gloss every position fully enough that the tree stands on its own. The intended reader is a philosopher of language who has not specialized in this literature, so keep the wording precise but self-contained.
