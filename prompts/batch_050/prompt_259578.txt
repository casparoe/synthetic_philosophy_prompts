I've gotten interested in reference-class forecasting — the idea that you should predict how your own project will go by looking at the base rates of similar past projects, instead of reasoning from the specifics of your situation (the "inside view"). I'd like to stress-test this idea against a real case: Athens' invasion of Sicily in 415 BCE, during the Peloponnesian War. The Athenian assembly, persuaded by promises of money from an allied city and by Alcibiades' arguments about Sicilian wealth and weakness, sent an enormous expedition to conquer Syracuse; by 413 BCE it had been almost entirely destroyed, and Athens never really recovered. Please analyze the episode through that lens: What reference class would the outside view have put this venture in, and roughly what base rate for success would it have implied? Which parts of the Athenians' reasoning look like textbook inside-view errors, and which parts — like the promises from their Sicilian allies — look like legitimately case-specific information that a base rate can't digest? Then go the other direction: the expedition came close to succeeding and was arguably sunk by contingencies (Alcibiades' recall and defection, the Spartan commander who slipped in, the eclipse that delayed the retreat) that no reference class could have priced in. Does the catastrophe vindicate reference-class forecasting, or does it show that base rates are silent on exactly the cases that turn on idiosyncratic events? And say something honest about the reference-class problem itself: the Athenians could have framed this as "besieging a great fortified city," "a democracy's overreach in a long war," or "an amphibious expedition far from home," and the class you pick does much of the forecasting work. Please write your answer in American English.
