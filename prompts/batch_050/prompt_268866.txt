I'm a philosophy prof and next month I'm giving the keynote at the public launch of a regional "century archive" project -- a community initiative where people deposit letters, recordings, seeds, etc. meant to be opened in 100 years. Funders, local families, and a handful of philosophers will all be in the room. Write me a 10-minute speech for the occasion. The spine of it should be Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the thought that our confidence that humanity will continue after we die is doing quiet work in making our current projects (including this archive) seem worthwhile, and that if we learned no new people would ever be born -- the Children of Men scenario -- much of what we do would lose its point, even if our own lives were unaffected. But don't just explain it. I want the speech to actually wrestle with the obvious reply: surely some things (love, a good meal, finishing the novel you're writing) would still matter to us even then -- so does the conjecture show that future generations are a condition of meaning, or just that they're one ingredient among several? The speech should end up saying something honest about what the archive contributors are actually doing, philosophically speaking, without pretending the conjecture settles it. Also: please be careful to distinguish what Scheffler actually argued from where interpreters of him genuinely disagree (e.g., whether he's giving a justification of our concern for posterity or just diagnosing it), and don't present any of the contested readings as settled.
