I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working up a piece on why Hume's essay "Of Miracles" (Section X of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding) is still the thing of his that non-philosophers actually argue about. My editor wants a sidebar on whether the standard objections to it are exhausted or if there's fresh blood in the debate.

Here's what's nagging at me. In Section I of the same book Hume splits philosophy into the easy-and-obvious kind and the accurate-and-abstruse kind, and he writes: "It is certain that the easy and obvious philosophy will always, with the generality of mankind, have the preference above the accurate and abstruse; and by many will be recommended, not only as more agreeable, but more useful than the other." The miracle essay seems to be Hume trying to have it both ways -- it's written in the easy, popular register, but the core argument is a piece of abstruse epistemic bookkeeping about balancing testimony against uniform experience. Keep that tension in mind; I want my sidebar item to turn on it.

Please act as a professional philosopher of religion with early modern expertise, think carefully, and brainstorm a handful of candidates internally before committing to one. Here are the objections to Hume's argument I've already collected for the piece:

1. The definition objection -- if a miracle is a violation of a law of nature and laws just are what uniform experience supports, then evidence for a miracle is impossible by construction and the argument is circular.
2. The Bayesian objection (Earman's line) -- a low prior isn't an insuperable one; strong enough likelihood ratios from testimony can push the posterior over the line, and Hume allegedly conflates the two.
3. The independent-witnesses objection -- Hume weighs testimony report by report, but genuinely independent converging reports compound in force in a way his balancing metaphor misses.
4. The "proves too much" objection -- Hume's principle would make it irrational to accept well-attested reports of anything unprecedented (the classic case being a tropical king refusing to believe the Dutch ambassador's report of ice), so something's wrong with the principle rather than with miracle-belief.

Give me a fifth objection that is genuinely new -- not a rewording, special case, or combination of these four, and not just "Hume was an atheist with an agenda," which is an ad hominem, not an argument. It has to be statable to my readers without a seminar, which is where the Hume passage comes in.

Format your answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro or conclusion: (1) state the new objection plainly, (2) show how it differs from each of my four, (3) give the strongest reply a good Humean would make, (4) say whether the objection survives that reply and how confident you are, (5) tell me whether the objection can be put in Hume's "easy and obvious" register or whether it needs abstruse machinery -- because if it's the latter, I want to note the irony that beating Hume requires the very style of philosophy he said most readers will never prefer.
