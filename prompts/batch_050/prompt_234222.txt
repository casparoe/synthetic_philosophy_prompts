I'm a novelist, and somewhere along the way I talked myself into the strong version of historical materialism: the economic base explains, in the last instance, basically everything else — law, religion, family structure, even which kinds of novels get written and sold. Honestly part of why I love it is that it's a great worldbuildng tool. But I want to know what would count against it. Not the lazy stuff ('communism failed, so...') — I mean: what would a case look like where some chunk of the superstructure demonstrably can't be traced back to the base, even in principle? How much causal feedback from ideas/institutions down to the economy can the view absorb before the base/superstructure metaphor stops doing any work at all? What does it predict that a Weber-style 'ideas drive history' view, or a mushy everything-causes-everything pluralism, doesn't? Also: has the 'in the last instance' escape hatch qualified the theory into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that actually fatal for a theory of history, or just how big theories of history are? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about which ones the serious defenders (Engels in his late letters, G.A. Cohen's functional-explanation version) would themselves concede versus wave away.
