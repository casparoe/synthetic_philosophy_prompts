I'm in a decision theory seminar this term and we've just spent two sessions on Ramsey's "Truth and Probability" (1926), where he argues that your degrees of belief have to hang together in something like the way logical consistency requires, since otherwise someone who knew your mind could offer you a set of bets you'd accept individually but which lose you money whatever happens. Our professor kept insisting that the interesting part isn't the betting, it's the idea that inconsistency is a practical defect that shows up in what you'd actually do, and this kicked off a huge row about whether the same lesson applies to preferences over time. She clearly has a distinctive line on money pumps and I want to work out what it is before I write my essay. Her verdicts so far: the classic pump victim — cyclic preferences, swaps A for B plus a fiver, B for C plus a fiver, C for A plus a fiver, round and round — is irrational, obviously. Someone with the same cyclic preferences who adopts a blanket policy of never trading is perfectly rational. Someone with cyclic preferences who sits down, plans to walk away holding B, and then sticks to the plan even when the next trade tempts her is also rational (she called this resolute choice and seemed to quite admire it). Someone with ordinary transitive preferences who keeps making plans in the morning and abandoning them by evening — resolves to skip pudding, orders it every single night — is irrational. And someone who simply can't rank two options and refuses to be forced into picking between them is fine, no irrationality there at all.

Here's the case I want her verdict on. An agent with cyclic preferences sees the whole pump laid out in advance, understands exactly where the sequence of trades ends up, and goes through with every swap anyway, paying the small fee each time, entirely cheerfully. Her defence: at each moment she genuinely prefers the trade she's making, the money was spent by earlier stages of her getting exactly what they wanted at the time, and there is no single standpoint stretched accross the whole sequence from which she counts as worse off, so nobody has been exploited and nothing has gone wrong. Predict my professor's verdict and tell me which rule she's implicitly running. I'd also like to know which other rules fit all five of her earlier verdicts just as well but land differently on this case (I can see at least two, maybe three), what each of those rules predicts here, and which further case you'd put to her to tell the rules apart. If you think her track record is just inconsistent rather than principled, say so, but I'd be surprised. Please answer in British English.
