I run a monthly philosophy circle at my local public library -- about a dozen adults, most with no formal philosophy background, and we meet for roughly 75 minutes. Last month we worked through a popular summary of the early books of Plato's Republic, and the part that stuck with everyone was Glaucon's challenge: the story of the shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible, and the claim that anyone -- just or unjust -- would end up behaving the same way once the fear of being caught was removed, since fairness is something we practice for its reputation and rewards rather than for itself. The group discussed it politely, but I could tell it stayed abstract for them, a story about a shepherd rather than a question about themselves. For next session I want an activity that makes each person feel the pull of that challenge in their own behavior before anyone names it, not a lecture followed by discussion.

Could you design a structured game or staged exercise for this group? It should involve real (though small) stakes that don't require actual money -- points, snacks, tokens, privileges within the session, whatever works -- and its core mechanic should let the facilitator toggle how visible people's choices are to the rest of the group, so that participants can later compare what they did when their actions were attributable to them against what they did under genuine anonymity. I want the rules specified precisely enough that I can run it without improvising: what materials I need, how the rounds proceed, how anonymity is guaranteed convincingly enough that people actually change their behavior rather than performing virtuously because they suspect a later reveal, and how to keep one enthusiastic person from spoiling it by loudly announcing that it's only a game and so they'll grab everything. Please include a timeline for the 75 minutes and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of Glaucon's argument -- the removal of detection, the convergence of the just and unjust person, the claim that justice is practiced involuntarily -- and that handles the participant who cooperates even when anonymous, since I suspect some will and I don't want the takeaway to be a flat "everyone is secretly selfish."

Finally, I'd like an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover, plus a frank note on which feature of the original challenge a parlor game structurally cannot reproduce -- I assume things like lifetime stakes, real punishment, and the difference between a concealed action and a lifetime of concealed character will be on that list -- so that I know what I have to supply through framing rather than through the activity itself.
