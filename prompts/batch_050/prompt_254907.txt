You are a political philosopher specialising in the ethics of parenthood and state regulation of the family, and you have been commissioned to design teaching materials for a postgraduate seminar. The seminar's organising question is one of consistency: in most liberal states we require would-be adoptive parents to pass rigorous vetting, we licence drivers, dog breeders and street vendors, yet we impose no test whatsoever on those who conceive and raise their own children. Some philosophers have argued that this asymmetry is indefensible and that parenting should be licensed like any other risky activity; others hold that procreation is a fundamental right that licensing would violate; still others ground restrictions not in harm prevention but in the child's right to an open future — a claim that parents must not so raise a child that most adult life-paths are foreclosed to them. Your task is to write a decision tree that classifies the positions in this debate by what their adherents actually believe, not by the labels they prefer.

Work carefully and in stages. First, brainstorm the logical space: list the distinct questions on which positions in this debate genuinely come apart, and note which combinations of answers are coherent. Candidate branching questions should include, but need not be limited to: whether parenting is best understood as a right, a privilege, or a fiduciary office; whether the justification for any licensing scheme rests on preventing harm to children or on protecting the child's future autonomy; whether the analogy between adoptive and biological parenting survives scrutiny; whether the familiar objection — that we do not licence marriage or friendship, though spouses can harm one another — succeeds against licensing or merely against bad licensing; whether enforcement worries (privacy intrusion, discriminatory administration, coercive interference with reproduction) defeat licensing in principle or only in practice; and whether an open-future rationale would licence parents themselves or only restrict what licensed or unlicensed parents may teach and do. Discard or merge questions that fail to separate any two real positions.

Second, construct the tree with between seven and ten branching points and between eight and twelve leaves. Every branch must be labelled with the claim or question that divides it, phrased as something a person could answer without knowing the literature. At each leaf, give the position a name (or mark it 'unnamed' if no one defends it), identify it with a real position from the debate where one exists — you should expect the tree to find homes for a harm-prevention licensing view, for a right-to-procreate objection, for the view that vetting is proper for adoption but not for biological parenthood, and for at least one view built on the open-future principle — and append one sentence stating the position's most awkward commitment. Third, audit the tree: note any coherent combination of answers that no philosopher occupies, and say briefly whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect; and flag explicitly any pair of named positions your tree cannot separate, explaining what further question would be needed to prise them apart.

Keep the instrument diagnostic rather than exegetical: a reader should be able to answer each question from their own convictions and arrive at a leaf. Conclude with a short reflection, in no more than a paragraph, on what the tree reveals about whether the adoption–procreation asymmetry is a deep disagreement about rights or a shallower disagreement about administrative feasibility.
