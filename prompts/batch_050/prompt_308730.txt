One guy is deep into the ergodicity economics literature — the Ole Peters, time-average-growth-rate stuff — and insists expected utility theory is a confused detour that only looks plausible because economists analyze ensembles instead of time. Our resident decision theory defender thinks this is rebranded log utility with extra steps, and that maximizing expected utility needs no ergodicity assumption whatsoever, it's just consistency axioms about preferences. After one too many circular arguements, I've told them they have to produce a joint statement, adversarial-collaboration style, and I want you to draft the document they should end up signing.

The thing that keeps coming up is a passage from Ole Peters' paper "The time resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox": "The final result can be phrased mathematically identically to Daniel Bernoulli's resolution, which uses logarithmic utility, but is derived using a conceptually different argument." Our ergodicity person reads this as: Bernoulli stumbled onto the right formula for the wrong reason, and the time-average derivation shows what log utility was really tracking all along. The decision theorist reads it almost as a confession — if the results are mathematically identical, then the "conceptually different argument" is philosophy rather than economics, and nothing follows about what any agent should actually do. Part of the joint statement should turn on this passage: is "mathematically identical but conceptually different" a substantive disagreement about rationality (e.g., about whether arbitrary utility functions are doing unearned work), or is it a disagreement about which story to tell while computing the very same thing?
