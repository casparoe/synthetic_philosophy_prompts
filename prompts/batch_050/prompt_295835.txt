I am a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience with a serious side interest in philosophy of mind, and I need to characterize illusionism about phenomenal consciousness accurately in my dissertation's introductory chapter before my committee reads it. A recent essay in a well-regarded science magazine summarizes the view as follows:

"Illusionists such as Daniel Dennett and Keith Frankish hold that consciousness is a user illusion: the brain constructs a simplified model of its own operations, and phenomenal consciousness — the inner, ineffable show philosophers call qualia — is part of that model rather than part of reality. On this view there is nothing it is like to be you: your conviction that you have private, intrinsic inner experiences is a fiction your brain generates, roughly the way a desktop interface presents files and folders where in fact there are only voltages. Introspection is therefore systematically mistaken about the very existence of experience, and once we see this, the hard problem simply dissolves — there was never anything further to explain beyond the brain's self-model and the judgments it issues. A welcome corollary is that a machine implementing the right kind of self-model would be conscious in exactly the sense we are, since consciousness was never a special inner glow to begin with."

I am inclined to find the view more defensible than its critics allow, but several of these claims feel stronger than what I recall from the primary literature, and I cannot pinpoint where the slippage occurs. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence, separating what a defender of illusionism would accept as accurate, what counts as a common and harmless popularization, and what misrepresents the position? In particular: is it right that illusionism denies there is anything it is like to be a subject, or does it deny only something more specific, and if the latter, what exactly? Does the view hold that introspection errs about whether experiences occur, or about what kinds of properties they present themselves as having? Does the hard problem genuinely dissolve on this account, or is it instead relocated into a different explanatory task — explaining why experiences seem phenomenal to us — that the illusionist still owes a solution to? Finally, the essay goes on to argue, first, that if illusionism is true then first-person reports have no evidential value in consciousness research, and second, that machine consciousness follows straightforwardly. Do these two conclusions survive once the summary is corrected, or do they quietly depend on its distortions?
