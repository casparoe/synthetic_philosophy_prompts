I'm designing a 90-minute session for a university ethics seminar of 16 students. The concept I want them to internalize is moral trade: the possibility that two people with genuinely different moral theories can reach agreements that each considers an improvement by the lights of their own theory, so that compromise need not mean both sides merely settling for less. I also want them to feel the tension this generates, because trading across moral commitments can feel uncomfortably like treating convictions as bargaining chips.

Design a runnable activity for this session with the following properties. The idea must emerge from what happens, not from anything I say beforehand, and the term moral trade must not be mentioned until the debrief. Give the students divided moral outlooks (assigned roles or secret commitment cards) and a series of small concrete decisions the group must collectively make, with some real but modest stakes (e.g., choices about actual class materials, snacks, a small pool of money, or agenda items). Build in a mechanism -- influence tokens, voting rights, or negotiation rounds -- such that students with different outlooks discover on their own that conceding on a decision they care little about, in exchange for winning one they care greatly about, leaves both parties better off by their own standards. At least one decision should be engineered so that a trade across decisions beats any within-decision compromise. The debrief should then name the concept, map specific moments from the activity onto it, and press the hard questions: when is trading across moral commitments rational from within each party's view, when does it feel like complicity in what one believes to be wrong, and does that feeling track a real moral difference or just a discomfort? Include an honest account of what will predictably go wrong when this is run -- students collapsing into majority rule, refusing to negotiate on principle, or trading insincerely because the stakes are low -- and how I should handle each. Finally, state frankly which feature of real-world moral trade the classroom version cannot reproduce (high stakes, parties who never meet, enforcement problems, or the sense of betraying a sacred value) and what I must supply in discussion instead.

Act as an experienced moral philosopher who also designs seminar pedagogy. Think carefully: before writing the final activity, brainstorm privately at least three possible mechanisms for generating the trades, pick the most robust one, and check that the payoff structure genuinely makes cross-decision trading dominant over within-decision compromise.
