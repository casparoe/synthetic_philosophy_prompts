At my lab we've got an AI assistant that drafts the analysis code and also writes up the reasoning for why it made certain modeling choices, and last week two of my coworkers got into a real argument about how we should be checking its work, and I got roped in as a neutral party and realized I couldn't actually settle it.

Dana's position: we should only ever evaluate outputs. Run the code, check whether the results replicate, sanity-check the conclusions against held-out data. Her argument was that auditing the assistant's written reasoning step by step is not just slow, it's actively misleading — the write-up is a story the model tells after the fact, and there's no guarantee it reflects whatever actually produced the answer. So rewarding "good-looking reasoning" just trains the system to produce reasoning that looks good to us, which is a different thing from being right, and in the limit you get beautiful justifications wrapped around garbage. She said outcome checks are the only ground truth we have and everything else is theater.

Marcus pushed back that pure outcome-checking only works while we can actually verify outcomes, and we're already near the edge of that. Half the time the assistant's code runs fine, produces plausible numbers, and the only way to catch a subtle mistake (a leaky normalization, a quietly dropped cohort) is to follow its reasoning or redo the analysis yourself, which defeats the point. He said process feedback is the only signal dense enough to catch problems early, and that if we wait for outcomes to be obviously wrong we'll only ever catch the dumb failures while the smart failures sail through.

Dana answered that Marcus's plan doesn't survive contact with a smarter system either: once the model is better than us at the domain, our judgments about whether each reasoning step is correct become worthless, and worse, we're then training it to persuade us step-by-step rather than to be right. Marcus said that's a reason to get better at process supervision now, while we can still tell good reasoning from bad, so the habit and the tooling exist before we need them. Dana's last move was that any legible-reasoning requirement caps capability — you're forcing the system to think in human-auditable steps when its best strategies might not decompose that way — and Marcus said a capability you can't check isn't worth having in our setting anyway.

They both seemed right about different parts and I couldn't figure out who won. Please go through the actual points made — the faithfulness problem, the verification-of-outcomes ceiling, whether step-by-step reward trains persuasion, the capability cap — and say who's right about each one, who's more right overall for our situation, and what both of them are missing.
