I'm in a bioethics reading group and last week we got stuck on this argument I sketched on the whiteboard, which seems to prove something none of us will accept. It starts from the idea that moral status is a matter of degree -- that there's no bright line, just a gradient running from thermostats and snails up through fish, chickens, chimps, and us, tracking psychological sophistication. Here's the argument as I wrote it up:

(1) How much moral status a being has is a function of its psychological capacities: richness of experience, self-awareness, the ability to have plans and relationships that a death would cut short.
(2) Those capacities vary continuously, not only across species but within them, and nothing in principle stops them from exceeding the normal human range -- think of what cognitive enhancement or a genuinely advanced AI might have.
(3) So moral status varies within the human species too, and could in principle rise above the ordinary human level.
(4) If status varies, then in trade-off cases beings with more status should count for more: other things equal, it's worse to kill the being with the richer mental life.
(5) So it is less bad to painlessly kill a severely cognitively disabled person than a typical adult, and it would be *worse* to kill a cognitively enhanced posthuman than one of us -- and if the gap ever got big enough, ordinary humans' interests could permissibly be sacrificed for enhanced beings the way we now think a chicken's can be for ours.
(6) But that's monstrous. (7) So (1) is false: moral status is not really a matter of degree, and we need some threshold that everyone past it clears equally.

I really don't want to give up (1), because the degreed picture is the only way I can make sense of why swatting a fly and kicking a dog feel so different without drawing an arbitrary species line. But (5) is a dealbreaker and the group spent an hour without finding the misstep. Which premise or move do you think is doing the damage, why does it look innocent, and what does it cost to reject it? I'd also like to know whether someone could honestly keep (1) through (4) and just deny that (5) follows -- if there's a principled way to say deaths differ in badness without rights or claims scaling the same way, that's the exit I'd prefer, but I can't tell if it's a real distinction or just a comfort blanket.
