I'm in a bioethics reading group and we got into a fight last week about the Jahi McMath case and nobody could actually say what the case proves, so I want to outsource the thinking to you. Quick recap of the facts as I understand them, so we're on the same page: in late 2013, Jahi, a 13-year-old in Oakland, had catastrophic bleeding after throat surgery, was examined multiple times and declared dead by neurologic criteria -- no brainstem reflexes, apnea, the whole battery, and this was confirmed by independent physicians and upheld in court. Her family didn't accept it, moved her to New Jersey (which has a religious-conviction exemption in its death statute, so she was legally alive there while legally dead in California), and she was kept on a ventilator for about four and a half years. During that time her body kept functioning with the support: it maintained temperature, fought infections, healed wounds, and reportedly went through pubertal changes. Her heart finally stopped in 2018 and a second death certificate was issued.

Now here's what I actually want analyzed. There's a standard philosophical defense of brain death -- call it the "organism as a whole" view, associated with the 1981 Uniform Determination of Death Act and elaborated by people like Bernat -- which says death is the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole, and that the brain is the master integrator, so total brain failure equals death even if individual cells or organs are kept perfused. And there's a dissenter, Alan Shewmon, who argues that cases like Jahi's are the empirical refutation of this: a body that maintains homeostasis, grows, matures, and heals for years is doing integrated somatic functioning, so the brain can't have been the sole integrator, so the organism-as-a-whole criterion was never actually satisfied.

Take the McMath episode and run it through this dispute in both directions. First the forward direction: what does the organism-as-a-whole view say the physicians in 2013 were detecting, how does it have to describe the four and a half years in New Jersey (a corpse being ventilated? a dead organism with lingering "biological life" of parts?), and is that description coherent or does it quietly redefine "integration" to immunize itself? Then the reverse direction: does Shewmon's argument from Jahi actually follow, or does it conflate integration *supported by machines* with the organism's own integrative unity -- and if the ventilator objection works against him, does the same objection threaten the original brain-death diagnosis, which also relied on machine support to even be observable? Then, the part our group really fought about: suppose you grant Shewmon everything. Does that show brain-dead patients with retained somatic integration are *alive*, or does it show that "the organism as a whole" was the wrong thing to be asking about and the real criterion was always something like the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, with brain death as a proxy? In other words, does the case vindicate a higher-brain account that almost nobody wants to defend, because of what it would imply about PVS patients? Finally, tell me where the conceptual analysis runs out -- what does the New Jersey/California split (same body, two legal statuses) show about whether "death" here is a biological fact being discovered or a social-legal line being drawn, and can the case itself even settle that, or does it just expose that the two sides were never arguing about the same thing?
