So I rewatched Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) last week — the one where a samurai is killed in the woods, his wife is assaulted, and the bandit Tajomaru, the wife, the dead samurai himself (via a medium), and the woodcutter each give a completely incompatible account of what happened, all framed by the priest and the cynical commoner sheltering from the rain under the ruined gate. On the rationalist internet this film is basically treated as the ur-text for "everyone lies, truth is socially constructed, man," which I've always found kinda lazy. So I want to try reading it through a totally different lens.

I've been reading about classical Indian epistemology, specifically the Nyāya school's theory of pramāṇas ("means of knowledge"). Quick sketch in case it's fuzzy: Nyāya accepts four independent sources of knowledge — perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda). The key claim for my purposes is that testimony is its own pramāṇa, NOT reducible to inference: you don't have to verify a speaker's track record inductively every single time; if the speaker is an āpta — someone who knows the truth firsthand and is disposed to convey it honestly — their word itself generates knowledge. Meanwhile the Buddhists of Dignāga/Dharmakīrti's lineage accept only perception and inference, treating testimony as a covert form of inference (we trust the speaker because we infer reliability). And one wrinkle I love: memory (smṛti) is not a pramāṇa for the Naiyāyikas, because it produces nothing new — it just replays whatever a real pramāṇa already delivered.

Now run the film through this. Some things I'm hoping you'll address:

1. Everything we the audience get is śabda about smṛti — testimony about memories — i.e. exactly the double mediation Nyāya should be MOST suspicious of. Within the fiction the witnesses presumably have pratyakṣa of their own deeds, but we never do. Does the framework give us any principled way to rank the four accounts, or does it just throw up its hands?

2. The āpta condition. Every speaker has enormous incentives to distort — honor, shame, the stolen dagger. Can Nyāya handle systematically interested testimony without smuggling inference back in? Like, if "is this speaker an āpta" is itself something you establish by inference from their behavior and motives, then śabda collapses into anumāna and the Buddhists win. Does Rashomon force that collapse, or is there a Nyāya response?

3. The ending, where the woodcutter takes in the abandoned baby and the priest's faith in people is visibly restored. The standard cynical reading is that this is a sentimental cop-out bolted onto a bleak film. Is there a reading where it's doing actual epistemic work — testimony can only function as a pramāṇa in a community with background trust, and the final scene re-founds that trust? Or am I over-reading?

4. Where does the film RESIST the Nyāya reading? Be honest — I don't want a Procrustean fit. Nyāya is staunchly realist: perception is mostly veridical, error is always explicable, and they have arguments that universal skepticism undermines itself. But Rashomon flirts with something stronger, that even perception itself is motivated. Can a realist epistemology absorb this film, or does the film secretly side with the skeptics?

5. Finally, what does the film add that the framework lacks? My candidate: self-deception. The scariest possibility isn't that the witnesses are lying to the court — it's that they're sincerely reporting memories that have already been edited. Nyāya's āpta is defined by competence plus sincerity, but Rashomon suggests competence and sincerity can coexist with total unreliability. Does that break the āpta condition, or just complicate it?
