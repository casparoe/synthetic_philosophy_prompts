The Milgrom-Stokey no-trade theorem says, roughly, that if all traders are rational and this rationality is common knowledge, then there can be no trade based purely on differences in information. The mechanism is adverse selection: if you're rational and you offer to sell me a security at price p, I know you must believe it's worth less than p, so I should refuse; and this reasoning is transparent to both of us, so no mutually acceptable offer ever gets made. It's the Groucho Marx point -- you don't want to be in any club that would accept you as a member. Aumann's result that agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree is really the engine behind this: since rationality implies shared posteriors once beliefs are common knowledge, there's no room for a bet both sides endorse.

So the load-bearing assumption of the theorem is common knowledge of rationality. Given that, rational agents will never trade with each other on the basis of private information.

Does this mean observed trading volume is evidence of irrationality? Yes, I think it does. The theorem proves that rational agents would never trade, full stop -- trade is a zero-sum game (ignoring transaction costs, which only make it worse), so any trade I accept must be one you expect to lose. The billions of shares changing hands daily therefore show that markets are populated largely by noise traders and overconfident speculators who think they know more than their counterparties. Grossman and Stiglitz independently arrived at a similar conclusion, showing that markets can't exist in equilibrium because prices reveal everyone's information and nobody is compensated for gathering it.

That said, there are escape routes. First, if some agents trade for exogenous liquidity reasons -- needing cash now, say -- then informed traders can hide among them, and Milgrom and Stokey themselves extended their model to show trade resumes in that case. Second, if agents have different prior beliefs rather than merely different information, they can rationally bet against each other, since the agreeing-to-disagree result no longer applies. So the theorem's practical upshot is narrower than its statement suggests, but its core message stands: pure information-driven trade among rational agents is impossible.
