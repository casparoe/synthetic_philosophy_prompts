My behavioral econ reading group has two members who have been bickering all semester about the sunk cost fallacy, and I want to force them to actually produce something. One of them takes the textbook line: sunk costs are gone, only future costs and benefits matter, so any choice that treats a past unrecoverable payment as a reason is a decision error, full stop. The other thinks this is massively overclaimed. Her view is that lots of what gets labeled "sunk cost reasoning" is perfectly sensible: the fact that you paid for something is information about how much you once expected to value it (you knew things then that you've now forgotten), finishing what you start is the only way people with self-control problems ever finish anything, and a visible habit of honoring sunk commitments does real work in relationships and organizations. She also complains that the classic experiments are rigged -- if someone tells you you've bought a ticket to a play, the natural inference is that the play is probably good, so going isn't honoring a sunk cost, it's using a cue.

I want you to write the joint statement these two would produce after a genuine adversarial collaboration, i.e., a document both could actually sign. Specifically: (1) a numbered section of claims they both accept -- things like the normative correctness of marginal reasoning given fully known preferences, the empirical reality of the pattern (people do throw good money after bad), whatever they agree the lab studies do and don't show; (2) a disagreements table where each remaining dispute is stated in wording each side would accept for its own position -- the key one being, I think, whether "don't waste" is a bias or a decent heuristic, and another being whether abandoning paid-for projects undermines the kind of personal commitment technology that hyperbolic discounters need; (3) for each disagreement, a crux -- some experiment, field observation, or formal result both agree bears on it, with each side's advance prediction recorded. For example, they should be able to agree on what would happen in a study that cleanly separates the money-is-gone signal from the I-once-thought-this-was-worth-it signal, and what each of them expects it to find. If part of their fight turns out to be verbal (say, over what counts as "irrelevant" in "sunk costs are irrelevant"), say which part and why.

Format-wise, start the document itself right away -- no preface, no "here is the joint statement" framing. Open with the numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions.
