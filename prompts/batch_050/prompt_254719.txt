I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been invited to give the closing talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on philosophy of mind and AI. The audience will be roughly half philosophers of mind -- several of them broadly Dennettian, at least one or two holdouts sympathetic to Searle -- and half researchers from an AI lab who build language models and tend to regard the whole intentionality debate as scholastic. My thesis: Dennett's two-bitser reply to Searle -- the vending machine designed to take quarters in the US that, shipped to Panama, takes quarter-balboas, its 'aboutness' inherited from our intentions -- does not straightforwardly settle the case of current language models. Derived intentionality, I'll argue, requires transmission from a source with a reasonably determinate purpose, and a model trained on the mass of human text has no such single source: no designer intends any particular thing by its outputs, and its users vary by the million. So the model's apparent intentionality is neither original nor derived in the classic sense, and I'll defend calling it a third thing. Prepare me for the question period: the objections I'll actually get from this particular mixed audience, in roughly the order they'll come and phrased the way a Dennettian philosopher versus a lab researcher would each put them, with what I should concede up front, where I must hold the line, and a two-minute reply for each with a note on where the reply is soft. Include the objection the Searle sympathizers will raise that cuts against my argument from the other direction, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
