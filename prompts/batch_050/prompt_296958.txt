I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and my dissertation is on compromise between deeply conflicting value systems -- think a secular welfarist and a religious traditionalist hammering out a shared city budget, where both sides walk away with something. At our department workshop last week I presented an argument I was pretty proud of, showing that refusing to compromise is often irrational, and someone in the audience got me to admit it also proves something I find repugnant. I've been banging my head against it since and I can't find the step to cut. So: please put on your hat as a moral philosopher who has thought hard about bargaining, value disagreement, and where economic reasoning legitimately runs out in ethics. Think carefully, and maybe sketch where the pressure points are before you commit to a verdict.

Here's the argument. (1) Whenever an outcome O is ranked above the status quo by party A's own values and also above the status quo by party B's own values, moving to O realizes more of what A values and more of what B values than staying put. (2) If you genuinely care that what you value actually gets realized -- as opposed to caring that your values be the ones in charge -- then you have reason to bring about outcomes that realize more of what you value. (3) So in deep moral disagreements there is often decisive reason to compromise: bundle several issues together, and let each side concede on the questions where its own concern is weaker. (4) Notice that this reasoning nowhere uses the content of either party's values; it only needs the existence of an outcome both rank above the status quo by their own lights. (5) So it applies just as well when the other party's ends are monstrous. Imagine a magically enforceable, one-shot deal with someone who wants innocents to suffer: you arrange for them to get a little of what they want, and in return they use levers only they control to deliver something you enormously value. Stipulate no enforcement problems, no reputation or incentive effects, fixed preferences, nobody affected beyond the deal's terms. By (1)-(4) you have decisive moral reason to take the deal. (6) But there is no moral reason, decisive or otherwise, to hand someone who wants innocents to suffer any part of what they want -- that's just helping evil, and wrapping it in the language of gains from trade doesn't change it. (7) So gains-from-trade reasoning is not a source of moral reasons in value disagreement at all; compromise is only ever prudential, never morally called for.

The problem is that (7) guts my dissertation, since I wanted (3) to be a moral conclusion, not a prudential one, and I also refuse to give up (6). If you forced me I'd rather keep (6) than (3), but I'd really like both. So tell me: which step do I reject, why did it look fine when I wrote it, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? In particular, if the culprit is (2), does rejecting it mean I never have reason to want my own values realized, which sounds absurd? And if it's (1), am I committed to saying something weird about deontologists, like that someone whose values come as side-constraints literally cannot compromise? And is there any patch that saves (3) for the welfarist and the traditionalist while blocking (5), or does any fix strong enough to block the monster deal also kill ordinary moral compromise? Don't just gesture at the stipulations being unrealistic, by the way -- I know the monster case is artificial, the question is what the artificial case shows about the logic.
