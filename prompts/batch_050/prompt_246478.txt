Last weekend a close friend and I spent the better part of six hours arguing about whether a mutual acquaintance had acted badly in a matter I won't rehearse here. Neither of us persuaded the other, though we did each adjust our views a little along the way. She ended the evening by invoking Aumann's agreement theorem: if two people are rational, share a common prior, and each knows the other is rational, then once their estimates become common knowledge they cannot sincerely disagree. Her conclusion was that our continued disagreement, after six hours in which everything either of us knew was laid on the table, proves that at least one of us is irrational. She was kind enough to leave open which one.

I couldn't find a flaw in this at the time, which has been bothering me all week. So I'd like some help working through the practical upshot. Given that the theorem establishes that one of us must be failing at rationality, what is the correct procedure for identifying which one? Is there some diagnostic a neutral party could apply? And does the theorem prescribe how two people in our position should proceed from here -- for instance, must we keep talking until we fully converge, and if we stall at partial convergence (as we did), does that tell us anything further about where the irrationality lies? I'd also like to know whether the size of our remaining disagreement matters: if we've narrowed from a wide gap to a small one, is the irrationality correspondingly small?

A careful answer would be much appreciated; I'd like to have something better prepared before I see her again.
