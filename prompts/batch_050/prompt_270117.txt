So my ethics reading group meets at my place on Thursdays, and last week we got into a genuine fight about acting under moral uncertainty -- like when you're 60% utilitarian, 30% Kantian, 10% virtue ethicist or whatever, and the theories disagree about what to do. My friend Mara (who does decision theory for a living and is annoyingly good at cornering people) argued that the whole literature on this collapses, and that if you take it seriously you end up having to throw out ordinary expected-value reasoning too. I've reconstructed her argument below as best I can, and I can't find the exit. I'm hoping you can tell me which step to reject and -- this is the part I really want -- what rejecting it actually costs.

Here's the argument, roughly as she gave it. (1) When you're uncertain about empirical facts, rationality requires weighing outcomes by your credences and maximizing expected value; that's just what taking your uncertainty seriously means, as opposed to pretending you know. If you think it'll probably rain, you bring the umbrella. (2) Being torn between moral theories is just another species of uncertainty -- uncertainty about which considerations matter and how much -- so the same rule applies: maximize expected choiceworthiness, where choiceworthiness is the moral analogue of value. (3) Whatever has nonzero credence gets a vote in the expectation, weighted by that credence. (4) There are coherent moral views on which some available action is astronomically, even limitlessly, more choiceworthy than the alternatives -- a view on which one particular ordinary act is the only thing that matters morally and matters without bound, say, or on which certain small acts carry infinite disvalue. You only need to assign such a view some tiny nonzero credence, and refusing to assign it anything at all seems like dogmatism. (5) But then the expectation is swamped: for almost any decision, some microscopic-credence, astronomical-stakes theory dictates the answer, and you should do whatever that theory screams loudest about, every time. (6) A deliberative method that can be hijacked by any hypothesis anyone can describe, provided they attach a big enough number to it, isn't guidance -- it's a hostage situation. (7) So, Mara says, the cleanest place to cut is (1) itself: expected-value maximization was never rationally required, not even in the umbrella case, and it's just a useful convention we've mystified into a law of reason.

Now, I'm willing to follow her a long way, but (7) makes me want to scream. Surely you should still bring the umbrella; surely if a bet wins 51% of the time at even odds you should lean toward it. At the same time, I don't want to give up the thought that started all this -- that moral uncertainty should make us hedge in real life, e.g. that being unsure whether animals matter morally is itself a reason not to eat factory-farmed meat even if your leading theory permits it. So: which step do you actually reject? Why did it look fine when Mara said it out loud? And what do I have to give up to reject it -- because she swears every escape route costs me either the umbrella or the hedging, and I'd like to keep both if I can.
