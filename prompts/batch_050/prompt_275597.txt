I founded a small literacy nonprofit 25 years ago, and next month is our big anniversary gala -- black tie, hotel ballroom, string quartet, the works. Every year a couple of our younger staff and volunteers quietly (and not so quietly) point out that the gala costs a fortune and that the same money could fund tutoring for hundreds of kids. The awkward thing is I've read enough about costly signaling and the handicap principle to suspect the waste is kind of the point: donors give big at precisely these sorts of expensive, slightly ridiculous events, and a bake sale would raise less even though it's more "efficient." But I also suspect that if I stand up at the podium and say that out loud -- thanks for coming, the extravagance is a costly signal of how serious we are -- the spell breaks, the way it breaks if someone announces they're buying an expensive gift purely to prove commitment.

I'm giving the keynote. Write it for me, about 4 minutes. The room is mixed: half longtime donors (a few of whom are econ and bio types who'll know exactly what I'm threading), half staff and volunteers who think the gala is indefensible. I need to make the staff feel genuinely answered, not schmoozed, without standing up there and openly naming the mechanism to the donors in a way that defuses it. If you think that tension can't actually be navigated in one speech, have the speech do something smarter than pretending it isn't there.

After the speech itself, add one line starting "Bottom line:" with the single sentence I'd want a skeptical volunteer repeating on the drive home.
