I'm a poli sci PhD student and I've somehow been roped into giving a guest lecture in the econ department's grad seminar on rational choice and welfare econ. Forty minutes on Rawls's original position and the difference principle. The catch is the audience: these are economics PhD students who know Harsanyi's impartial-observer setup cold, so the usual undergrad misconceptions (the veil means nobody knows anything at all, the parties are selfish jerks, etc.) are the least of my worries. My real problem is that within five minutes they'll have translated the veil of ignorance into Harsanyi's equiprobability framework and concluded that Rawls is just utilitarianism with an inexplicable bolt-on of infinite risk aversion.

So don't explain Rawls to me -- plan how I should explain him. What order do the pieces have to come in given that this audience will reach for the wrong formal model at every step? What example should carry the argument (I'm guessing it shouldn't be income distribution, since that invites them to start computing expected utilities immediately)? Where exactly in the sequence do I defuse the 'maximin is just a risk preference' move, and the follow-up move that the parties would obviously gamble? What should I openly refuse to get into at this level -- reflective equilibrium? primary goods vs. welfare? -- and what's one question I can put to them at the end that would show they've grasped why Rawls thinks the setup is doing different work than Harsanyi's, rather than just that they've memorized two different conclusions?
