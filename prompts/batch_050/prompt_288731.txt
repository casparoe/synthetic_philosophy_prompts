I am finalizing a position statement on sortition for a political theory workshop, and before I circulate it I would like an audit of whether the positions I hold are jointly tenable. The statement takes its cue from a passage in Aristotle's Politics (Book VI, chapter 2, 1317b) in which he lists the institutional marks of democracy. In paraphrase, the items include: that magistrates be chosen by all from all; that all rule over each and each in turn over all; that offices be held briefly and rarely twice by the same person, military command excepted; and that all serve on juries drawn from all. The clause that matters to me runs, in the Greek, "τὸ κληρωτὰς εἶναι τὰς ἀρχὰς ἢ πάσας ἢ ὅσαι μὴ ἐμπειρίας δέονται καὶ τέχνης" — that offices be filled by lot, either all of them or all but those requiring experience and skill.

My commitments:

(a) The diagnostic embedded in that passage is correct: election is oligarchic in tendency, because it systematically favors the prominent, the wealthy, and the already-known, whereas the lot is the distinctively democratic selection mechanism. A polity that fills every office by election is at best a mixed constitution, whatever it calls itself.

(b) Political equality, properly understood, is an equal ex ante chance of holding office. Sortition secures this; no electoral scheme does or could.

(c) Lawmaking must be epistemically competent. An allotted chamber should therefore have expert staff, ample time to master its briefs, and a standing norm of deferring to technical expertise where the questions are technical.

(d) An allotted chamber's authority rests on descriptive resemblance: it is a portrait of the public in miniature, and it is this resemblance that entitles its decisions to speak for everyone.

(e) Structured deliberation inside the chamber strengthens that authority. A body that has heard evidence and argued its way to a conclusion speaks for the public with greater, not lesser, legitimacy than the same body polled cold.

(f) In institutional design, sortition should supplement rather than replace election: an allotted chamber with agenda-setting or suspensive powers alongside an elected legislature.

What I want is an audit, not a literature review. Identify which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, ranked by severity; for each, state what exactly generates the conflict, what would have to give, and what that revision would cost. Identify which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what has to be made precise for the dissolution to go through, rather than flagging every pair as equally problematic. Attend to the Aristotle passage itself while doing this: I take the qualification about experience and skill to sit comfortably with my position, but if you think it undercuts one of my commitments, or that I have misread the passage, say so plainly instead of auditing around the difficulty. Likewise, if any commitment as stated is too vague to generate a determinate tension, flag that rather than manufacturing a conflict. Finally, if you had to name the single revision that would make the package most coherent at least cost, name it and say what I would lose by it.

Please aim for six to eight hundred words; the passage and the commitments above do not count toward that.
