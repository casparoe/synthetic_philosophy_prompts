So I run a small reading group on value theory -- mostly grad students plus a couple of keen undergrads -- and we just spent two sessions on spectrum arguments (the long-sequence-of-almost-identical-options ones, where each step looks like an obvious improvement or a wash but the two endpoints clearly aren't). Everyone left confused in different ways and I want to hand out a flowchart at the next meeting called "which way out of the spectrum argument is actually yours?"

Please build me that decision tree. Some constraints:

- Start the tree at the shared premise everyone in the room accepts: there's a sequence of options where each adjacent pair is, by all appearances, either indistinguishable in value or related by a clear "at least as good as" -- and yet the first option is plainly much better than the last. From there, each branch node should be a diagnostic question about a concrete mini-case or a claim, not a question about labels. E.g. don't ask "do you think betterness is vague?"; instead give them a case like a long run of headaches each imperceptibly milder than the last and ask what they'd say comparing headache #1 to headache #50 directly, versus step by step. Nobody should be able to figure out which leaf they're heading for just from the questions.
- The leaves should be the actual live exits, located in the space of answers rather than just listed: transitivity deniers who think "better than" can fail to be transitive (Temkin-style, including the version where betterness is "essentially comparative" and depends on what's on the menu), people who say adjacent pairs are roughly equal or on a par and that these relations just don't chain, the vagueness/indeterminacy route, bullet-biters who accept that one end of the intuition has to go (and there are two directions to bite -- say which), and threshold or all-or-nothing views. If you think another named exit belongs, add it.
- Aim for about 8-10 nodes max so it fits on one side of a page.
- For any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely separate, say so explicitly and say what extra question would split them if you had room for one more node.
- Note any combination of answers in the logical space that no one seems to occupy, and briefly guess why -- incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? E.g. is there a coherent spot for someone who denies transitivity of "better than" but keeps it for "at least as good as," and does anyone actually live there?
- One thing I specifically care about: the tree should distinguish people who think the puzzle is really about the *metaphysics of value* from people who think it's about *what we can know or say about value* -- those two camps talk past each other constantly in my group and I want the chart to make that visible early on, high in the tree if the logic allows.

Tone-wise keep the node questions short and punchy, the kind of thing someone can answer in five seconds from the gut. That's the whole point: I want to sort people by their actual case-judgments, not by which paper they last agreed with.
