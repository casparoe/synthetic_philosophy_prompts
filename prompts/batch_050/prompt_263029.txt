I’m a high school senior in an ethics seminar, and for my final paper I need help reconstructing my advisor’s implicit theory of when pain itself is bad for the person who has it. We are separating the badness of the experience from the badness of injury, illness, lost time, medical bills, or other people’s reactions. By pain asymbolia I mean a case in which someone reports that a sensation feels intense and painful in the ordinary sensory sense, but does not find it unpleasant, is not motivated to avoid it, and remains calm rather than distressed.

Her verdicts so far have been: an ordinary migraine is bad in itself, not merely because of what it prevents; an asymbolic migraine with the same reported intensity, no dread, no urge to escape, and no loss of concentration is not bad in itself; a faint electrical hum before a debate can be bad in itself if it is hated and keeps invading attention, even though it is not intense; and the chosen pain of a marathon hill is still bad while it lasts even if the runner freely endorses it and later calls the race worthwhile, because meaning can justify pain without erasing its momentary badness.

Now consider the new case. Noa has asymbolia and feels a maximal-intensity pain without dislike, panic, or any wish for it to stop; she would even accept it for a sufficient reward. Yet while it lasts, the pain monopolizes attention in a way she does not control: she misses a friend’s distress cues, cannot follow a conversation, and afterward remembers little of what happened, though she insists none of this bothered her at the time.

Predict my advisor’s verdict about whether Noa’s pain is bad in itself. Please do not just give a yes or no: identify at least two plausible rules that fit all four earlier verdicts, state what each rule implies about Noa, give your best prediction and why, and describe one further case that would most cleanly distinguish the leading rule from its strongest rival.
