I'm teaching a unit on self-deception in a philosophy of mind seminar and before we read any particular theory I want to give the students the spec sheet first: what would an account of self-deception actually have to do to count as adequate? The two classic paradoxes are the obvious starting point -- the static one (the self-deceiver seems to have to believe p and not-p at once, which looks impossible) and the dynamic one (to deceive yourself you'd seem to have to know what you're doing, which should blow your own cover) -- but I want the full desiderata list, not just "dissolve the paradoxes." Things like: it has to distinguish self-deception from plain wishful thinking, from being in denial in the colloquial sense, from ordinary motivated reasoning that never quite reaches belief, and from delusion proper. It should say something about why motivation seems essential (nobody talks about accidentally self-deceiving into an unwelcome belief) without making the process so intentional that the dynamic paradox comes back. It has to handle the standard cases -- the jealous spouse who sees all the evidence and sincerely insists there's nothing going on, the parent who can't accept their kid is guilty -- and it should have something to say about whether self-deception is a defect or sometimes a feature (the athletes-and-confidence literature suggests it can help performance).

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which ones you'd call non-negotiable vs which are just the hobby horses of particular camps (intentionalists vs deflationists vs the "it's not really belief" people). Most important: if some subset of these desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable -- e.g. if respecting the dynamic paradox forces you to give up on genuine belief, or vice versa -- flag it explicitly, because that trade-off is basically the whole debate and it's the part I most want the students to see. Then at the end, very briefly, score the main families of accounts against the list (intentional model, partitioning/divided-mind views, deflationist/motivationalist accounts like Mele's, and the revisionist line that self-deception isnt a species of deception at all). Keep the scoring short -- a few lines each, I can do the rest in class. The criteria are the deliverable.
