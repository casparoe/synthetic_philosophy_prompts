I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and next month I'm running a single ninety-minute seminar for biology graduate students on the species problem. They will arrive assuming that species are among the few categories in their discipline that are unproblematically real, and that any remaining disputes are just curatorial housekeeping. I want to open the session by putting this passage from Chapter II of Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) on the board before I say anything about species concepts:

"Those forms which possess in some considerable degree the character of species, but which are so closely similar to some other forms, or are so closely linked to them by intermediate gradations, that naturalists do not like to rank them as distinct species, are in several respects the most important for us. We have every reason to believe that many of these doubtful and closely-allied forms have permanently retained their characters in their own country for a long time; for as long, as far as we know, as have good and true species. Practically, when a naturalist can unite two forms together by others having intermediate characters, he treats the one as a variety of the other, ranking the most common, but sometimes the one first described, as the species, and the other as the variety."

Don't give me the seminar content; give me the plan for running it. Specifically: what has to be in place conceptually before the Darwin passage reads as a problem rather than as a grumble about messy taxonomy; the one case to build the session around (a ring species, an asexual lineage, a hybridizing oak complex, or something better), what it must illustrate, and which complications it should avoid carrying; the misconceptions this particular audience will form -- I'm expecting "Darwin is denying that species exist" and "this dissolves once we have enough genomic data" -- and exactly where in the sequence I should head each off; the check at the end that would show they've understood the passage rather than merely absorbed a list of definitions; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (phylogenetic concept technicalities, species pluralism?) while saying so out loud. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the plan's main bets, especially the carrying case and the misconception map, and single out the one point where you are least sure you've predicted how working biologists will actually respond.
