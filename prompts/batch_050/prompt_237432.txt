Teaching my decision theory seminar and a student cornered me after class with this argument about the Pasadena game. I told him it proves way too much, but when he asked me which line he should cross out I realized I wasn't quite as sure as I sounded. Help me before Thursday's session.

Setup, for anyone rusty: a fair coin gets tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads comes on toss n, the ticket pays (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars. So: +$2 with probability 1/2, −$2 with probability 1/4, +$8/3 with probability 1/8, −$4 with probability 1/16, and so on forever.

His argument, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct:

(1) The fair price of a gamble is its expected value, when it has one.
(2) When a gamble has infinitely many possible outcomes, its expected value is just the sum of the probability-weighted payoffs, i.e. the sum of the series.
(3) Here the weighted payoffs are 1, −1/2, 1/3, −1/4, ..., which is the alternating harmonic series, and that sums to ln 2, about 69 cents.
(4) So the fair price of the ticket is about 69 cents.
(5) But the very same gamble can be written with its outcomes listed in a different order: take the +1 and +1/3 terms first, then the −1/2, then +1/5 and +1/7, then −1/4, and keep that pattern up. That series sums to (3/2) ln 2, about $1.04.
(6) Reordering the list of outcomes doesn't change the gamble. Same coin, same possible results, same payoffs attached to the same results. Nothing about what can happen or how likely it is gets touched.
(7) So the fair price is also $1.04.
(8) And by picking other orders (he appealed to Riemann's rearrangement theorem by name, the show-off) the "fair price" comes out to literally any number you like — $10, −$50, whatever.
(9) A gamble can't have every fair price at once, so expected value theory assigns no coherent value here at all.
(10) And then the kicker: this problem arises for any gamble with infinitely many outcomes and both positive and negative payoffs, so expected utility theory is basically hopeless for realistic decision problems, which never come packaged with finitely many outcomes.

Now (10) is clearly where it proves too much. Plenty of infinite-outcome gambles with both positive and negative payoffs have perfectly well-behaved expected values (if the payoffs shrink fast enough nobody bats an eye), so he can't be right that infinity-plus-mixed-signs alone sinks the theory. Which makes me think the rot starts earlier, but every step from (1) to (8) looks annoyingly reasonable when I stare at it.

Two things I'd like to keep if at all possible. First, the verdict that the Pasadena ticket is strictly preferable to the Altadena ticket (same game, signs flipped), since the Pasadena pays strictly more in every single outcome — giving that up would be embarassing. Second, the ordinary St. Petersburg game should stay worth more than any finite price; I don't want a fix that drags it down too.

So: which numbered step do you actually reject, why did it look so innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? If the answer is that more than one step is doing damage, say which is the primary culprit and which is just along for the ride. And tell me what to say to him about (6) specifically, because that's the one he's most confident about and the one where my objections sound the most hand-wavy.
