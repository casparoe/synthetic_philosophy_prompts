I am an undergraduate taking a seminar in the philosophy of biology, and I am stuck on a reading assignment that combines Karl Popper with Charles Darwin, and I would like help thinking it through.

Here is the setup. Our unit on Popper's philosophy of science ended with his treatment of Darwinism. As I understand it, Popper argued in his intellectual autobiography Unended Quest that the theory of evolution by natural selection does not meet his criterion of falsifiability and functions instead as what he called a metaphysical research programme: a framework that usefully guides research without itself being open to refutation. A central part of his worry was that the principle looks tautological, because fitness seems definable only in terms of actual survival and reproductive success, so that a theory built on the survival of the fittest forbids nothing and therefore explains everything and nothing. Later, in a lecture he delivered at Darwin College, Cambridge, in 1977 and published in the journal Dialectica as Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind, Popper partly walked this back, conceding that the theory can be formulated in a way that is far from tautological and does make risky claims, though he continued to think its tests were weaker and less direct than those available in physics.

Our professor then handed out the following passage from Chapter IV of the first edition (1859) of On the Origin of Species and asked whether Darwin, in his own canonical statement of the theory, actually commits the error the early Popper diagnosed:

"Can it, then, be thought improbable, seeing that variations useful to man have undoubtedly occurred, that other variations useful in some way to each being in the great and complex battle of life, should sometimes occur in the course of thousands of generations? If such do occur, can we doubt (remembering that many more individuals are born than can possibly survive) that individuals having any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind? On the other hand, we may feel sure that any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed. This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection. Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic."

Three things about this passage have me stuck.

First, the sentence beginning "This preservation" reads as a stipulative definition: Darwin is christening a process, not asserting something that could turn out false. If that is right, then where exactly does the falsifiable content of the passage live? My best guess is that it resides in the surrounding premises, the claim that more individuals are born than can survive and the claim that heritable variations arise that are useful or injurious to their bearers. But I cannot tell whether the usefulness of a variation is identified independently of survival outcomes, or whether it is covertly defined by them, which is precisely what would make the theory circular.

Second, I noticed that Darwin never uses the phrase survival of the fittest here; that slogan is Herbert Spencer's, and Darwin adopted it only in later editions. He speaks instead of individuals having "any advantage, however slight." Does recasting the tautology objection against Darwin's actual wording change anything? It seems to me that the analogy to artificial selection, which the passage leans on with its talk of variations useful to man, might give advantage an independent anchor: a breeder can specify in advance which traits count as useful, without waiting to see which animals survive. But I am unsure the analogy transfers to nature, where nobody specifies anything in advance.

Third, I do not know what Popper's later partial retraction commits him to saying about this passage in particular. Which of its features would the later Popper count as supplying testable content? The final sentence about polymorphic species interests me here, because Darwin explicitly limits the reach of natural selection, saying that neutral variations would not be affected by it. A theory that declares some phenomena off-limits to its mechanism seems to be risking something, since selection could in principle be shown to act where Darwin says it does not. On the other hand, the hedged structure of the central claim, advantages "however slight" operating over "thousands of generations," seems built to absorb almost any observation. Does that make the passage unfalsifiable in Popper's sense, or merely difficult to test, and does Popper's demarcation criterion even mark a difference between those two verdicts?

Could you help me work through these questions? Please start with a plain-English paraphrase of the passage itself, then take the three difficulties in turn, and be explicit about where you think Darwin's wording helps him and where it hurts him. I am not looking for a bibliography or further reading; I want help reasoning through the text I have in front of me. Roughly 700 to 900 words, please, and please write in American English.
