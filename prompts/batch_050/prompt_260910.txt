I help run a small film-and-philosophy night with some friends from my old grad program, and next month we're doing Memento, which I volunteered to lead the discussion for. I want to read it through Dennett's Orwellian vs. Stalinesque distinction from the color phi stuff. For anyone rusty on it: in the color phi phenomenon two dots of different colors flash in sequence and people report seeing one dot move and change color midway -- before the second dot could possibly have been perceived. A Stalinesque story says the brain revises the experience before it ever becomes conscious, like a show trial where the evidence is faked in advance. An Orwellian story says you really did experience it one way and then your memory of it got rewritten after the fact, like the Ministry of Truth pulping old newspapers. Dennett's whole point, if I'm remembering right, is that once you push on it the distinction falls apart -- there's no fact of the matter about which revision happened, because there's no single moment or place where "what was experienced" is fixed.

So here's what I want from the reading. Leonard's self-deceptions seem to come in both flavors: the big one at the end, where he deliberately writes himself a note that will send his future self after Teddy, looks purely Orwellian -- he knows the truth now and engineers a false record for later. But the Sammy Jankis material, and that flash of a frame in the institution where Sammy is Leonard, seems more Stalinesque: his "memories" may never have been accurate experiences that got revised, they may have been confabulated from the start. Does the film actually respect that difference, or does it side with Dennett and treat the difference as illusory even while our intuitions keep wanting to classify each deception as one or the other? I'm especially interested in the structure: the reverse chronology forces us as viewers to constantly re-interpret scenes we've already watched, so are we put in the Orwellian position of revising our memory of what we saw, or does the film make us feel Stalinesque, like the "real" version was never on screen?

Finally, tell me where the reading strains. Memento is about a man with a damaged memory mechanism, and Dennett's argument is about normal brains and the metaphysics of consciousness, so maybe I'm mapping a clinical story onto a philosophical one and it doesn't fit. If the distinction does collapse the way Dennett says, does the film lose anything -- like, is there a moral or emotional difference between Leonard-the-Orwellian (a liar to himself) and Leonard-the-Stalinesque (a victim of his own wiring) that the film needs to keep alive even if the metaphysics won't support it? Where would you push the group, and what does the movie show about revision that the philosophy papers don't?
