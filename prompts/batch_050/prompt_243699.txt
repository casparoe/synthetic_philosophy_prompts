So I've been thinking about the nuclear arms race, roughly 1945 to 1991, and I want to run it through Hofstadter's superrationality and see what survives. The setup seems almost designed for it: two players in what looks like a prisoner's dilemma, both sides know the other side is staffed by highly rational people thinking about game theory (the RAND people on one side, whoever the Soviets had on the other), and the payoffs are about as symmetric as real life gets. A superrational player reasons: whatever conclusion I reach, a player enough like me will reach the same conclusion, so I should pick the move that does best conditional on us both making it. Applied here, that seems to say: don't build the next warhead, because they'll do whatever you do.

Here's what I actually want. First, map the episode onto the model properly -- who counts as the player (is it the USSR or the Politburo or the Soviet defense establishment?), what the actual moves and payoffs were at a few key junctures, and where superrational reasoning, if anyone was doing it, would have landed. Second, do the arms control successes -- the test ban, the ABM treaty, INF -- look like superrational coordination, or like something else (verification, iteration, domestic pressure), and how would you tell those apart from the outside? Third, where does the model break: my sense is superrationality needs you to be confident the other side reasons like you, and the whole tragedy of the Cold War is that each side's reading of the other was garbage, plus the US was pretty sure the Soviets weren't symmetric agents at all. Does superrationality just not get off the ground without that symmetry, or can a one-sided version still say something?

And run it in reverse too: does this episode count for or against superrationality as a decision procedure real states could use, or does it reveal that the theory silently assumes away exactly the hard part (knowing who you're playing with)? One more thing: please be upfront about what's actually settled versus contested here -- both on the history (e.g. how much Reagan's people were or weren't moved by this kind of reasoning) and on the theory side, where I gather people disagree about whether superrationality is even distinct from Kantian universalizability or just tit-for-tat in a trenchcoat. Don't present any of those takes as the consensus if there isn't one.
