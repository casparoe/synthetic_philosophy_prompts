I'm in a reading group on social epistemology, and over the last few sessions I've talked myself into a position that I now want stress-tested rather than defended. The position is this: photographs, and later audio and video recordings, came to function as a kind of backstop for public knowledge -- when testimony conflicts, when print can be doubted, when memory is unreliable, the recording was the thing that finally settled what happened. Cheap, convincing synthetic media destroys that backstop. And my stronger claim is that we will not merely revert to the pre-photographic situation, as if nothing had been lost; we will end up worse off than before recordings existed, because our epistemic habits, institutions, and expectations were all rebuilt around the assumption that footage settles things, and the collapse of that assumption leaves behind a generalized cynicism that pre-photographic societies never had. I usually state the harm as having two prongs: people will come to believe fabricated events, and -- more insidiously, I think -- anyone caught on genuine footage can now dismiss it as fabricated, so real evidence loses its force even when it is never actually challenged by any fake.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Some candidate defeaters I already have in mind, which you should engage but not limit yourself to. First, the empirical route: provenance infrastructure (cryptographic signing of captures at the camera, content credentials) might succeed well enough that authenticity becomes checkable again, or the detection arms race might stabilize in defenders' favor -- how much would each of those outcomes actually undermine the position, given that it only claims the backstop is gone, not that every individual recording is suspect? Second, the historical route: photographic forgery and staged imagery are as old as photography itself, and trust in photographs survived more than a century of that -- does that show the backstop is more robust than I assume, or does the difference in cost and scale make the analogy fail? Third, the conceptual route: perhaps recordings were never self-authenticating in the first place, and what really did the work was always chains of custody, trusted institutions, and witness testimony -- in which case nothing essential is lost, since those mechanisms remain. Fourth, the behavioral route: the limited evidence so far arguably suggests people disbelieve real footage more readily than they believe fake footage, or perhaps that neither effect is large -- which result, if it held up, would hurt my position more?

For each defeater, I'd like you to say whether a committed defender of the backstop view would concede it as counting against the position or dismiss it as compatible with it, and to be honest about whether that distinction reveals anything bad. In particular, I want you to press on a suspicion I have about my own view: that whenever I say trust is damaged even in a world with few actual deepfakes and functioning provenance -- because the mere possibility of fabrication is enough to let people dismiss real evidence -- I have insulated the position against any possible observation. If it has been qualified into something close to unfalsifiable, say so, and say whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind or simply the normal shape of claims about institutional trust. I'd also like, where possible, an indication of what prediction my position makes that the rival "business as usual, testimony was always primary" view does not, so that I know what to watch for. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, even though the ranking structure might tempt you toward a list.
