Data science adjacent rant incoming. I work on a policy analytics team for a city government. Last week I presented results from a big observational dataset: after controlling for everything we could think of, neighborhoods that adopted a certain program had measurably lower eviction rates two years later. My teammate shot it down with 'correlation is not causation', the room nodded, and we moved on. Annoying but fine. Then two days later the SAME teammate argued we should expand a different program based on a before/after comparison of the city budget line, which is... also correlational? When I called him out he said 'that's different, we watched the change happen.'

So help me arm myself. What could 'correlation is not causation' actually be claiming? I want a menu of distinct readings, from the near-tautological (an observed correlation doesn't logically entail a causal link, full stop) to the methodological (no observational data can establish causation, only randomized experiments can) to something stronger and weirder about causation itself (a Hume-flavored claim that all anyone ever observes is regular succession, so causation is never 'seen' in data at all). For each reading tell me whether it's a platitude, a live and defensible thesis, or just false. Then tell me which of those readings, if any, would actually justify dismissing my study — and crucially, whether any single reading justifies dismissing mine while accepting his before/after argument, or whether he's just moved the goalposts.

One more wrinkle, because I think this is where the slogan gets interesting. As I understand it, interventionist accounts of causation treat 'X causes Y' as a claim about what would happen to Y if we set X by hand, whereas counterfactual accounts treat it as a claim about what would have happened to Y had X been different, holding the rest of the world fixed. Suppose next year someone builds a device that lets us toggle the program on and off in randomly selected neighborhoods — a literally perfect intervention. Does the slogan lose all its force at that point, or is there a reading of it that survives even perfect interventions (e.g. worries about whether the toggled version is 'the same' program, or whether effects generalize)? I suspect answering that tells me whether the slogan was ever really about evidence quality, or about something buried in the concept of causation itself.
