Next month I'm presenting at a philosophy of physics colloquium, defending a thesis I call "reindexing finitism": actual infinities are mathematically unimpeachable but physically unrealizable, and the standard argument for this — Hilbert's hotel and its cousins — is a bad argument for a good conclusion. My claim is that every hotel-style absurdity (shifting all guests up one room, merging two full hotels into one) is at bottom just a relabeling, and relabeling is metaphysically free only in pure mathematics; once the rooms are physical, any reindexing operation carries real costs in time, energy, or information that grow without bound. So the paradoxes dissolve without giving up Cantor, and physical infinities are ruled out not by arithmetic embarrassment but by the impossibility of unbounded reindexing in a causal universe.

The audience is about fifteen people: two set theorists (one platonist, one formalist), several philosophers of physics already sympathetic to finitism about cosmology, a physicist who works on singularities and trusts spacetime math over philosophy, and one visiting ultrafinitist who will think I've conceded far too much by granting Cantor at all.

Write my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or so objections this particular audience will raise — not the generic ones from a math 101 crowd — ordered by how damaging they'd be to my reindexing argument specifically, each phrased the way that audience member would actually phrase it. For each objection, give me what I should concede, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and where that reply is soft. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
