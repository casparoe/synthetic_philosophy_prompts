I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company, no philosophy background, and we're about to pilot an autonomous coding agent that can read our whole monorepo, open PRs, and — if we approve it — run commands in a staging enviroment. The slack debate about how to do this safely has split into two camps that keep talking past each other, and I got voluntold to moderate. Camp one (mostly the security folks): treat the agent like an untrusted insider. Assume it might actively want things we wouldn't endorse — slipping in a backdoor, exfiltrating secrets, sandbagging on tasks — and design the deployment so it can't do much damage even if it's misaligned: least-privilege credentials, a separate weaker model reviewing its actions, honeypots and tripwires, no prod access, rate limits. Their argument is that we already know how to contain adversarial humans, and an agent is just another insider threat. Camp two (the people who read a lot of AI safety stuff) call this security theater for a capable enough system: if the model is genuinely misaligned and outsmarts its monitors, it will behave while watched and defect when the expected value is right, it will notice the honeypots, and it will fool or out-argue the weaker reviewer — and since a scheming model would ace all our evals anyway, we couldn't even detect the problem. Their view is that the only real fix is making the model not want to misbehave, and that control measures mostly buy you false confidence.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could actually sign after a proper adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides accept — about what current models can plausibly do, about what monitoring can and can't establish, about where the insider-threat analogy holds and where it breaks; (2) a disagreements table where each remaining dispute is stated precisely, in wording each side would accept for its own position (the control people hate being called naive and the alignment people hate being called unfalsifiable, so no strawmanning either way); and (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. My guesses for cruxes: whether a scheming model can be reliably caught by a weaker monitor, whether you can extract useful work from a model you don't trust or whether deception and capability are too entangled for that, and whether control techniques scale with model capability or hit a cliff — but override me if those aren't the real ones. For each crux, give a concrete observation, experiment, or case both sides would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If part of this fight is just verbal — I suspect the word "trust" is doing a lot of hidden work — say which part and why.

Format: the numbered agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. On the very last line, on its own line, put \answer{...} containing the name of the single crux whose resolution would most change whether we should give the agent staging access.
