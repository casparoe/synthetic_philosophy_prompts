---

DRAFT: "The repugnant conclusion is a sampling error"

Here's the setup as I understand it. Take population A: ten billion people, all with excellent lives. Now compare it to population Z: some astronomical number of people whose lives are barely worth living -- good enough that they'd rather live than not, but only just. Total utilitarianism says Z is better than A, because Z's total welfare, summed over that enormous number, exceeds A's. Parfit called this the repugnant conclusion and spent a lot of a very long book trying to avoid it and, as I read him, failing.

The argument that gets you there seems to go in steps that are each hard to refuse. Step one, "mere addition": take A and just add some extra people with lives worth living, while the original ten billion are no worse off. Call it A+. Surely A+ is not worse than A -- you haven't hurt anyone, you've just added happy people. Step two: redistribute. Imagine A+ has the extras living noticeably worse than the originals. Now even things out so everyone is at the same, middling level, keeping the total the same or a bit higher. Call that B. B seems at least as good as A+ -- it's more equal and has no less total welfare. Repeat this enough times and you slide down to Z. Each step looks fine, so by transitivity Z is better than A, which nobody wants to say.

Now here is where my old job kicks in. In stock assessment, the single most drilled-in lesson is that a total is not a thing you find in the water. A biomass estimate is a number you construct out of samples, and whether it's any good depends entirely on how the population is structured and how you sampled it -- age classes, distribution, clustering. Two stocks with the same total tonnage can be in completely different shape, and anyone who manages by the tonnage figure alone will drive a fishery into the ground. When I read population ethics, I keep having the same allergic reaction: "total welfare" is being treated like a tonnage figure. We sum across persons as if persons were interchangeable sampling units, and then we're surprised when the sum tells us something repugnant.

And here's the move I actually want to defend, which is related but different. Nobody finds it repugnant that a single person might rationally prefer a very long life of modest but positive days to a short life of peak experiences -- ten thousand decent years over one sublime decade. Within one life, we add across time-slices without flinching, and the "repugnant" temporal conclusion (a vast number of barely-okay days beats a small number of wonderful ones) is just... prudence, or at least an open question, not a scandal. So the scandal must come from the fact that in Z the welfare is spread across different people rather than different times. But that means the repugnant conclusion isn't really about quantity at all. It's about the separateness of persons -- the boundaries between sampling units, to put it my way. And if that's the real source of the repugnance, then the conclusion has been misdescribed: it's not a problem for the total view, it's a problem for anyone who thinks the boundaries between persons are morally thin, which is a view a lot of these same philosophers hold for other reasons.

So my proposal is: stop trying to avoid the repugnant conclusion by fiddling with the axiology (critical levels, average views, all the machinery I keep reading about) and instead admit that what it measures is how much weight you put on person-boundaries. If the boundaries are thick, the analogy with the single long life fails and Z really is worse -- but then say so, and give up the thin-boundary view elsewhere. If they're thin, accept Z and stop calling it repugnant. What's not available, I claim, is holding the thin view and treating the conclusion as an objection to summing.
