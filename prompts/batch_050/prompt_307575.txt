I'm in eleventh grade, and my ethics teacher has a running habit I want to understand better. Whenever a moral question comes up in class -- usually from something in the news -- she gives a quick verdict on whether a bystander or participant counts as complicit in some wrongdoing, meaning whether they share blame for it even though they didn't personally cause it. Over the semester I've written down five of her verdicts:

1. A student drives his friends to a rival school's parking lot knowing they plan to vandalize cars, waits in the driver's seat, and drives them away. Complicit.
2. A student buys new sneakers, at full price and with equally good alternatives available, from a brand that was just exposed for using sweatshop labor. Not complicit -- her reasoning was that no individual purchase changes anything for any worker.
3. At a club meeting, a member stays silent while the other nine members unanimously vote to play a humiliating prank on a younger student. His objection would not have changed the outcome. Complicit, at least somewhat -- she said he was in the room and part of the group.
4. A student buys the same brand of sneakers secondhand from a thrift store. Not complicit.
5. A voter supports a candidate in a national election, and the candidate, once elected, does something the voter never wanted or anticipated. Not complicit.

Now I want to ask her about a new case, but I'd like to predict what she'll say first. The case: a friend of mine has been offered a summer job at the local warehouse of the very sneaker company from cases 2 and 4. The job is ordinary packing work, he needs the money, he doesn't support the company's labor practices at all, and he'd be easily replaced if he turned it down.

My questions: First, what verdict will she most likely give, and why? I'm genuinely torn -- he isn't causing anything (like case 2), but he'd be inside the organization rather than just buying from it (more like case 3). Second, what are the two or three most plausible general rules that fit all five of her past verdicts, and what does each of them predict about the warehouse job? Third, which rule is she probably actually running, and what's the strongest evidence from the list? Finally, if her track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, what's one further hypothetical case I could put to her that would best reveal which rule she holds?
