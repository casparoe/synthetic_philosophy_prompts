Here's a question I'm workshopping for my history of ethics final and I want to road-test it on you before I unleash it on my students. Bentham died in 1832, so he never saw any behavioral economics: no mug experiments, no loss aversion, no prospect theory. Say someone sat him down and explained that when you hand people a coffee mug and immediately offer to buy it back, they demand roughly twice what they'd have paid for the same mug five minutes earlier, and that in general losses seem to hurt about twice as much as equal-sized gains feel good. What would he make of that?

I can feel two Benthams pulling apart. One says: the whole system runs on actual felt pleasure and pain, nothing else counts, so if losses genuinely hurt more then the felicific calculus just has to weight them more heavily, and a well-designed society should be reorganized around that fact (protecting settled expectations, making takings and disruptions especially costly, that sort of thing). The other says: Bentham was ruthless about dismissing feelings he took to be confused -- the principle of sympathy and antipathy gets a famously rough ride in the Introduction -- so maybe he'd call loss aversion a prejudice to be educated out of people rather than a datum to be built into the math.

So pick a side. Would Bentham treat the endowment effect as a legitimate preference that effectively doubles the disutility of losses, or as a bias that good legislation and good education should dissolve? In the modern fight over whether loss aversion is a cognitive error or a genuine value, who's side is he on? Commit to an answer, but also tell me where you think his actual views underdetermine the question and I'm asking him to settle something his framework simply can't. No need for chapter and verse, and definately don't fabricate quotations -- just reason from the general shape of the view.
