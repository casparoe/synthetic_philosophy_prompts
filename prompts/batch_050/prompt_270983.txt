I am writing a comic novel whose narrator, a retired schoolmistress of dry and settled tastes, delivers judgments on every joke the other characters attempt, and I need her verdicts to remain consistent with the palate I have already established for her. So far she has pronounced as follows. She laughed when the pompous alderman sat down on a freshly painted bench; she did not laugh at a pun inserted into a funeral eulogy; she laughed at a nonsense rhyme with no target at all, sheer verbal absurdity; she did not laugh when a clerk mocked a stammering apprentice before the whole office; and she laughed when the mayor opened his Christmas speech with a joke at his own expense. Now, in the chapter I am drafting, a group of pupils trick their elderly Latin master — a gentle, self-effacing man with no pretensions whatsoever, beloved by the whole town — into reading a page of gibberish aloud at assembly, whereupon the school erupts; he is briefly bewildered, then joins the laughter himself. My question is not simply whether she laughs, but what rule she is running. The verdicts above seem consistent with at least two distinct principles: one on which jokes succeed when their violation is suffered benignly and resented when it is not, and another on which humour licenses the deflation of the proud and the absurd but never the humiliation of the undeserving. These two rules diverge on my Latin master. Please identify whatever candidate rules fit her track record, say what each predicts for the new scene, give me your best guess at her verdict with your reasons, and tell me what further incident I might write into a later chapter that would settle which rule she truly follows.
