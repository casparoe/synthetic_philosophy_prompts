I'm a TA for an undergraduate philosophy of mind course, and this semester the imagery debate has become strangely personal: my roommate insists that when she mentally rotates a piece of furniture to see whether it will fit through a doorway, she is plainly manipulating a picture in her head, and that no amount of argument could make her doubt what she is doing. I suspect she is conflating the vividness of the experience with a conclusion about the format of the representation, but I have not been able to say, in a way she finds forceful, what the alternative explanation of her own performance would even look like. I'd like to settle this by staging the dispute in class rather than lecturing it: roughly 18 students, 75 minutes, and they should arrive at the two sides of the debate from their own behavior before either side is named.

Design a classroom activity that recreates the core of the Kosslyn–Pylyshyn dispute in miniature. The first phase should have participants perform some task that classically produces distance- or size-dependent response times when done "in the mind's eye" — scanning between remembered locations, verifying details of an imagined object, or something you consider better suited to a classroom without equipment — so that the group collectively generates the sort of data that seems to support the picture theory: the farther the mental journey, the longer it takes. The second phase is the important one: participants should then be made to seriously entertain, and ideally to construct themselves, the rival explanation — that their response times reflect tacit knowledge of how long the corresponding physical operation would take, plus a cooperative attempt to give the experimenter the expected pattern, rather than any literal traversal of an inner spatial medium. I want them to feel, in their own case, that the first-person obviousness of "I was scanning an image" does not discriminate between the two accounts. No lecture up front, and the names Kosslyn and Pylyshyn should not come up until the debrief.

Please give me the full working material: exact task instructions, a protocol for collecting the timing data with nothing fancier than phones and a whiteboard, and what results to expect if it works. Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the structure of the historical debate — including where demand characteristics and tacit knowledge show up in their own data, and what an actual experiment adds that our classroom version lacks. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (for instance, I worry some students will report "not visualizing at all" and others will insist the timing instructions made them fake it, and I need to know how to handle both). Finally, be honest about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce — my guess is that no classroom game can bear on whether the underlying representational format is depictive, as opposed to merely recreating the evidential impasse from the inside — and what I should say to my roommate, whose objection is not about data at all but about what it is like to be her.
