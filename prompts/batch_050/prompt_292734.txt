I teach a second-year module on war and political philosophy (about 24 students, mixed abilities, some very gamey types who will min-max anything you put in front of them). We're doing nuclear deterrence next month and every year I hit the same wall: students nod along to the idea that a deterrent threat has to be credible, and that credibility sometimes requires committing yourself to a response that would be insane to actually carry out, but they treat it as a curiosity rather than a genuine paradox. They don't feel how weird it is that rationality might demand you arrange things so you *can't* back down, or that deliberately losing a bit of control (leaving something to chance, as the brinkmanship literature puts it) could be a *strategy*. So I want a classroom game, 75-80 minutes, that makes them collide with this themselves before I ever say the words "commitment device".

Design the activity properly for me: actual rules, roles, payoffs (points or some trivial stake is fine, they're competitive enough that chocolate works), a timeline for the session, and how to run multiple rounds so the dynamics can evolve. The key thing is the structure should do the teaching -- e.g. players should discover that an unconditional threat gets ignored, then try to make it credible, and find themselves wanting to *remove their own options* or hand the launch decision to a die roll or a hot-headed teammate, and then feel queasy about it. I don't want a lecture up front and I don't want them told in advance what the game is "about". If there's a clever mechanical way to make escalation happen through a sequence of individually-tempting small steps rather than one big choice, even better, since that's the bit they find most counterintuitive on the page.

Also give me the debrief: which moments in the game map onto which concepts (credible commitment, burning your bridges, brinkmanship as manufactured shared risk, the tripwire idea, the so-called rationality of irrationality), what predictably goes wrong when you run this sort of thing (someone will try to break it, someone will refuse to escalate on principle, someone will treat it as pure poker and miss the point entirely) and how to handle each, and an honest note on what a classroom game *can't* reproduce about real deterrence -- presumably something about the stakes being annihilation rather than points, and maybe about playing iterated games with people you'll see next week versus states with no guaranteed tomorrow. If you think the premise is off somewhere -- e.g. if the "paradox" dissolves on closer inspection and I'm setting them up to learn a confusion -- say so plainly rather than designing around it. British English please, since they'll be seeing your handout.
