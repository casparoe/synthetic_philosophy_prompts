I'm in a second-year metaphysics course and two friends from my study group got into a long arguement about free will that's still bugging me, so I want a referee. Setup: they both granted determinism might well be true and argued about what kind of freedom would actually matter anyway.

(1) Maya said the freedom worth wanting is just acting on your own values without being coerced — if you choose med school because you genuinely care about patients, the choice is yours, whether or not the universe could've gone differently.

(2) Ben said that just pushes things back a step: your values come from your parents, genes, and random luck, so if determinism holds you're a very complicated puppet, and calling the strings "values" doesn't cut them.

(3) Maya said his standard makes free will impossible for anything, even in an indeterministic universe, because nobody can choose their values before they have any values to choose with — so a definition of "worth wanting" that nothing could ever satisfy is a defect in the definition, not a discovery about us.

(4) Ben said fine, maybe it's impossible — but that doesn't stop it being the one worth wanting; a consolation prize is still not the prize, and her view is consolation. He added that randomness doesn't help her either: an undetermined choice popping out of nowhere isn't more "mine" than a determined one.

(5) Maya said that move concedes the real question was never determinism at all, and once it's about ownership of your values, ownership comes in degrees — values you've reflectively endorsed are more yours, and determinism doesn't touch that.

Please go through the five moves and say who's right at each step, point out what both of them are missing, and give an overall verdict — whose picture of "free will worth wanting" should I actually take away from this?
