So I've been arguing with a colleague and taking a pretty orthodox Gricean line: what sets conversational implicature apart from every other way a speaker can communicate something -- entailments, presuppositions, conventional implicatures, raised eyebrows -- is calculability. On my view, a conversational implicature is one a hearer could in principle reconstruct from what's literally said, the maxims, and the context, and that's exactly why such implicatures are cancellable without contradiction ("some of the students passed -- in fact all of them") and non-detachable. I treat cancellability as a diagnostic symptom, calculability as the underlying essence.

Now I want to stress-test this before I put it in a paper. What would count against the view? I'm not asking for a survey of the literature (I'll dig up citations myself), I want the actual defeaters: which phenomena, cases, or arguments, if they hold up, would show calculability is neither necessary nor sufficient for being a conversational implicature. Please sort them from fatal to merely awkward. Things I'm already worried about: generalized conversational implicatures that behave like defaults and resist cancellation; the charge that the calculation is circular since its starting point ("what is said") is itself pragmatically inferred, so there's no neutral input to calculate from; and the possibility that any putative counterexample can just be relabeled a conventional implicature, which would make the view unfalsifiable. For each defeater, say whether a committed Gricean would concede it or bat it away, and what the world would have to look like for it to actually land. End with a final line of the form "Verdict:" followed by one sentence on whether calculability survives as the demarcation criterion.
