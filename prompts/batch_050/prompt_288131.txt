Contemporary liberal states run border regimes with a strikingly asymmetric structure. Would-be immigrants may be excluded almost at will, subject to narrow exceptions such as asylum. Yet citizens may not be prevented from emigrating, and citizenship is nearly irrevocable once held, revocable only in exceptional cases like naturalization fraud — while a number of the same states sell citizenship outright to investors through citizenship-by-investment schemes. Defenders of these arrangements typically justify them all under one heading: the political community's right to exclude. What conception of that right does the package actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory — whether membership is being modeled on property, on freedom of association, on collective self-determination, or on something else — and note where each familiar model fits badly: property owners may sell shares and evict, associations may expel members, yet states do neither to citizens, and selling citizenship sits oddly with defending exclusion as protection of the demos. Distinguish which parts of the practice genuinely presuppose a particular theory from those that are underdetermined and compatible with several, or whether the package presupposes no single theory at all. Finally, say what would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced with a strict property model or a strict association model, and whether the apparent absurdity of the consequences shows the existing practice to be principled or merely incoherent. Please write your answer in American English.
