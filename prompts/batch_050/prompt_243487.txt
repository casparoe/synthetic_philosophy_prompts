I'm a graduate student in philosophy of action, and for my second-year paper I'm attempting something my advisor is skeptical of: translating Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action into the vocabulary of contemporary analytic philosophy of action. I know the doctrine only at the level a non-specialist knows it, so let me say what I take it to be, and you should correct me where I've got it wrong. As I understand it, Wang held that genuinely knowing what is good is inseparable from doing it: someone who claims to know that one should be filial but behaves unfilially does not, strictly speaking, know filiality at all. Knowledge and action are not two things that ideally coincide but two aspects of a single process, and his stock examples are things like: one cannot be said to know a foul odor without disliking it, or to know pain without having suffered it. Where I'm stuck is the translation itself. The working vocabulary of my subfield consists of: propositional knowledge versus knowing-how (Ryle's distinction), occurrent versus dispositional states, intention as a sui generis practical attitude in the Bratman style, judgment internalism and externalism about moral motivation, and akrasia understood as acting against one's better judgment.

So: restate the doctrine in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Which parts come out as the claim that moral judgment is intrinsically motivating? Which parts come out as the claim that the relevant knowledge is really knowing-how rather than knowing-that? Is anything best rendered as a dispositionalist thesis about what it is to believe something at all — and does the foul-odor example suggest Wang was relying on cases where the 'knowledge' is perceptual or affective rather than propositional, which would make the doctrine much less radical than it sounds? Then give me the residue report: what does not survive translation? I suspect several things are lost — Wang's apparent redefinition of knowledge itself, so that only knowledge-realized-in-action deserves the name (which in my vocabulary looks like mere stipulation, though I'm not confident it is); the background metaphysics of the heart-mind and innate moral knowing; and the fact that for Wang this is a program of self-cultivation rather than an analysis of a concept. For each item of residue, tell me honestly whether it marks a genuine gap in the analytic vocabulary or whether it shows the original doctrine was trading on an equivocation that the translation usefully exposes. I'd rather hear that some piece cannot be said in my terms than receive a forced rendering that papers over the difference.
