I am a PhD student in political theory, and my dissertation chapter examines epistemic objections to futarchy: the proposal that a polity should settle on a measurable welfare metric by ordinary democratic means, then adopt whichever policy conditional prediction markets predict will most improve that metric. For a workshop whose audience will already know the standard objections, I need a counterexample that survives them. Construct a single scenario — set in a publicly traded company, a university administration, or a municipal government, not in some speculative polity — in which the futarchic mechanism selects policy A over policy B even though A is in fact causally worse for the agreed metric, and in which the error falls out of the mechanism's own logic rather than from any defect in its inputs.

Stipulations the scenario must respect, so that no cheap move is available: the markets are deep and liquid; traders are numerous, informed, and honest; no actor has anything to gain from manipulating prices; the metric is one all parties genuinely endorse, is measured accurately, and is assessed at a horizon that captures the policy's principal effects. The failure I want isolated is the gap between the quantity a conditional prediction market can price — the expected value of the metric given that a policy is adopted — and the quantity the decision actually needs, namely the causal effect of adoption, which can diverge when adoption itself is correlated with conditions that independently move the metric.

Deliverables, in order: (1) the scenario, one paragraph, with enough institutional texture that the confound arises naturally rather than by stipulation; (2) a demonstration that states precisely what the market prices estimate, what the decision requires, and why the two come apart here; (3) the verdicts — what the mechanism recommends and what a competent deliberative body with access to the same information could in principle conclude instead; (4) an identification of the single load-bearing feature of the case and what happens to the example if it is removed; and (5) the strongest response a defender of futarchy (Robin Hanson, above all) could mount — by appeal to randomized adoption, more carefully specified conditional contracts, or the claim that deliberation suffers worse confounds — together with your assessment of whether that response rescues the mechanism in this case. The chapter's claim is that the confound is structural, not incidental, so a scenario that merely stipulates the prices are wrong, rather than generating their wrongness from the mechanism, would be useless to me.
