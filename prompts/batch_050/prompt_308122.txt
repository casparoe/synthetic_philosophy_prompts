Draft the joint statement that a defender of naturalism about health and disease (think Boorse-style: disease is a failure of species-typical functioning, full stop, no values needed) and a defender of normativism (think: nothing counts as a disease unless we judge it bad for the person, and that judgment is ineliminably evaluative) could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. A few things I want in it:

- A numbered list of agreed claims, and make them non-trivial. E.g. both sides should be able to agree that drapetomania shows something about how value-laden disease labels can be smuggled in as science, and that the depathologization of homosexuality is a case study, but they should spell out what each side takes the lesson to be without pretending they draw the same one.
- At least one agreed claim about where the naturalist's "species-typical functioning" story has real teeth (e.g. why myopia or a broken leg seem easy) and one about where it strains (pick your own, but infertility in a 45-year-old vs a 25-year-old is a good stress test for the reference class problem -- functioning relative to *which* reference class: species, age group, sex? -- and if the choice of reference class is itself value-laden, has the naturalist smuggled normativity back in through the side door).
- A short "disagreements that turned out to be about words" section: at least one place where the two camps were using "function" or "normal" differently and the disagreement dissolves once that's flagged.
- Then a disagreements table with exactly these columns: Issue | Naturalist's position (in their own words) | Normativist's position (in their own words) | Crux question | Case or argument both accept as bearing on it | Naturalist's prediction | Normativist's prediction. Each side's position has to be phrased so that side would actually sign it, no strawmen. For the predictions, pick cases where the two views genuinely diverge -- e.g. a condition that impairs species-typical function but the person values (some deaf community cases, maybe), or something statistically normal in a population that everyone agrees is still pathological (like widespread gum disease or iron deficiency in some populations).
- One crux should be: if it turned out that laypeople and doctors apply "disease" in ways that track both dysfunction *and* disvalue, with neither being sufficient alone, who wins -- or is that a hybrid view (like Wakefield's harmful dysfunction thing) that both sides have to concede ground to? Say what each side would count as losing.

Keep the tone like an actual joint document, not a debate transcript. The whole thing should feel like both authors were slightly annoyed but acting in good faith.
