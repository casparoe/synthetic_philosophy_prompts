I'm a software engineer, and around the office "Chesterton's fence" gets thrown around constantly -- someone wants to delete a weird chunk of legacy code, someone else says hold on, that fence is there for a reason, figure out why before you tear it out. I've read the basic idea (if you don't know why a thing exists, you don't get to remove it until you do) and a bit of Burke on top of that, the whole thing about institutions encoding tacit knowledge that reformers can't see from the armchair. Then I came across the Chinese "smash sparrows" campaign from the late 50s and it kinda broke my brain, because it looks like the perfect real-life Chesterton's fence story, except the "fence" was a bird.

Quick recap of what I know so we're on the same page: during the Great Leap Forward, the government identified sparrows as one of the "four pests" because they ate grain, and mobilized the population on a massive scale to kill them -- banging pots so birds couldn't land, destroying nests and eggs, the works. Sparrow numbers collapsed, and shortly after insect populations (esp. locusts, which the sparrows had been eating) exploded and made the crop situation even worse in what was already becoming a catastrophic famine. Eventually the campaign was quietly dropped and sparrows were taken off the pest list.

So here's what I want: act as a political philosopher who knows both conservative political theory and 20th century history reasonably well. Take a moment to brainstorm where the analogy holds and where it strains before you write, then give me a careful analysis in both directions. First direction: read the campaign through Chesterton's fence and Burke -- is this actually what the principle is about? The sparrow population wasn't designed by anyone, it evolved, so is the "tacit knowledge encoded in the status quo" story even doing the same work here as it does for human institutions, or is something subtly different going on? Second direction: what does the episode do to the principle? My worry is that it proves too much. The same logic that would have saved the sparrows also seems to counsel against abolishing things that were genuinely rotten but whose "function" defenders claimed not to understand (people defended all kinds of awful institutions on "you don't understand the social order" grounds). Does the episode actually support strong Burkeanism, or only a much weaker procedural rule like "spend more time understanding before acting"? Where exactly does the fence principle break as an analogy for ecosystems, and -- since I live this at work -- does the codebase version of the argument survive the comparison, or is code different in a way that matters (we have version control, we can actually read the commit history)? I'd rather you tell me where the theory goes quiet than force it to have an opinion.
