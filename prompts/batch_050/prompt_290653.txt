Having spent my career in biology and only lately turned to philosophy in retirement, I find myself repeatedly frustrated by the literature on the concept of disease. Authors argue over whether some condition -- ageing, infertility, colour blindness, whatever -- is "really" a disease, and each side seems to be wielding a different implicit standard. Before I read any more of it, I would like the standard made explicit first.

Please write the desiderata for an adequate philosophical theory of health and disease: a specification of what any such theory must accomplish, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. In particular, address the following, and feel free to add requirements I have overlooked:

- The extensional cases the theory must get right, or must at least explain away plausibly: clearly central instances (infections, cancers, fractures), clearly central non-diseases (the effects of a boxing match, pregnancy, grief), and the contested middle ground (colour blindness, presbyopia, homosexuality as it figured in twentieth-century diagnostic manuals, and the 19th-century diagnosis of "drapetomania" concocted to pathologise enslaved people who fled). Which of these serve as data and which merely as tests of a theory's immunity to the values of its era?
- The distinctions the theory must not collapse: disease versus injury versus deformity; disease versus illness and sickness (the state, the experienced condition, the social role); being statistically abnormal versus being pathological; being unhealthy versus being disvalued.
- The constraints from the biology itself: a theory that presupposes fixed species essences or a designed "proper function" in any strong teleological sense is, to my mind, in conflict with evolutionary contingency; state whether this is a genuine desideratum or my own disciplinary prejudice.
- The reference-class problem: normal relative to what -- age, sex, species, environment -- and who decides?

For each requirement, give the consideration behind it and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp (naturalist, normativist, or hybrid). Two further requests. First, if some subset of these desiderata appears jointly unsatisfiable -- I suspect the demand for value-neutrality and the demand to match medical practice may pull apart -- say so explicitly, since the impossibility would itself be the most interesting result. Second, indicate which single requirement eliminates the most existing contenders, and close with a brief, informal assessment of how the main families of view (biostatistical naturalism, harmful-dysfunction accounts, thoroughgoing normativism) fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring; I can do the scoring myself once the standard is fixed.
