I'm a 3rd year taking a course called Paradoxes of Rationality (mixed phil + game theory students), and part of our participation grade is that each of us has to run one 50-minute discussion section for about 20 people. I got assigned "superrationality," which we covered last week, and I really don't want to just stand there and re-explain it. I want to run an actual live experiment on them.

Quick background of what the prof gave us, in case it's not familiar:

- The Platonia dilemma: an eccentric billionaire (Platonia) writes to 20 people saying: if exactly one of you replies to this letter, that person gets a billion dollars. If more than one reply, or nobody does, nobody gets anything. No communicating with each other allowed. The superrational thought (Hofstadter's thing) is that since everyone is reasoning in the same situation with the same info, everyone will reach the same conclusion -- so pure strategies are hopeless (if everyone replies, all lose; if nobody replies, all lose). The best you can do is everyone adopt the same probabilistic strategy: roll a 20-sided die and reply only if it lands on 1. Then there's a decent chance exactly one person replies.
- The Luring Lottery: a lottery where anyone can submit as many entries as they like, and the prize is $1,000,000 divided by the total number of entries submitted. Sending more entries raises your odds of winning but shrinks the prize for everyone including you. Superrationally everyone should send one entry or none -- but the "lure" is that from the inside, one more entry always looks like a good idea.

What I want from you: a runnable activity for the 50 minutes where the students actually *feel* this stuff -- the pull of "everyone else is reasoning exactly like me," the temptation of the lure, and how fast the whole thing collapses the moment one person stops cooperating. Some real but tiny stakes would be great (I can bring chocolate or offer bonus participation points, no actual money). Give me concrete rules, materials, timing, and ideally a structure where we play more than one round -- my guess is the round *after* they've talked about round one is where it gets philosophically interesting.

Then a debrief plan that maps what happened onto the actual concept, including the part I find hardest and want them to grapple with: whether "we'll all reason alike" is genuinely a *reason* to do anything, given that my die roll doesn't cause theirs. That seems to be where superrationality either does real work or turns out to be wishful thinking.
