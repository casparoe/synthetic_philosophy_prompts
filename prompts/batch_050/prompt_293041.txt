I direct the performance-psychology program at a conservatory. Roughly thirty advanced students a year come through my office with the same pattern: they play a piece flawlessly in the practice room and progressively worse as the audience grows, and the harder they try on stage to relax and let the music flow, the more wooden and error-prone they become. Telling them to stop trying does not help, for the obvious reason that obeying that instruction is itself a form of trying.

I want to build a coaching protocol out of the classical Daoist notion of wu-wei -- unforced action that nonetheless carries a kind of authority or presence (de) -- and out of the long-standing problem that one cannot aim at it directly. The tradition supplies both the materials and the dilemma. The Zhuangzi's exemplars -- the cook whose blade never dulls because he no longer perceives the ox as an obstacle, the ferryman who handles his boat as if it were nothing, the woodcarver who fasts until concern for praise and reward drops away -- all reach effortlessness only through long prior discipline. The Confucians objected from the other side that spontaneity worth having must be cultivated through deliberate practice and ritual, not achieved by letting go. So the protocol has to dissolve, in practice, the regress of trying not to try, and it has to keep cultivated effortlessness clearly distinct from relaxation, passivity, and underpreparation.

Write the protocol as something I can actually run in a sequence of six to eight weekly fifty-minute sessions: the stages in order, the concrete exercises at each stage, the observation or test that tells me a student is ready to move on, and the instructions a coach must never give because they reintroduce the paradox. For each stage, say which distinction from the underlying theory has to survive the translation into practice and which can safely be dropped.

Then apply it to three cases: a violinist who overpractices -- six hours a day, flawless in rehearsal, rigid on stage; a pianist who has adopted effortlessness as a reason to practice less and whose playing is deteriorating -- the protocol needs to catch this one rather than bless it; and a third-year student preparing for an orchestral audition in ten weeks, where the protocol may deliver an answer neither of us will like. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to my judgment -- for instance, distinguishing performance anxiety that is a coaching problem from one that needs clinical referral -- and state what the procedure cannot do at all.

Finally, say how confident you are in the main claims the protocol rests on, and single out the one step or assumption you are least sure of.
