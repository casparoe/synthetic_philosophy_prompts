ok so I TA a second-year political theory seminar and for the conservatism week I want to actually run something in class instead of just talking through the Burke extracts again. 14 students, one 80-minute session, no prep on their end. Design me a game or simulation that makes people *feel* the force of Chesterton's fence -- the idea that you shouldn't tear down a rule or institution until you understand what it was doing for you, because it might be load-bearing in ways invisible from the outside.

Some constraints, because I've seen bad versions of this:

- The lesson has to come out of what happens in the game, not from me lecturing after. I don't want Burke or the fence metaphor mentioned until the debrief. The mechanics should be rigged so that the room repeals something sensible-looking and then watches it blow up in their faces a few rounds later.
- BUT it can't just teach "never remove old rules," which is a vulgarization of the whole point. So build in at least one rule that is genuinely vestigial (repealing it is free, maybe even good) and ideally one whose hidden "function" turns out to be quietly funneling benefits to a subgroup, so the room has to fight about whether that one is a load-bearing fence or just a fence somebody profits from. The winning strategy should be investigate-before-repeal, not preserve-everything.
- Give me the actual runnable thing: the resource mechanics, rounds, event cards or whatever drives the hidden functions, roles if any, a minute-by-minute timeline, what small stakes to attach (chocolate or a sliver of pariticpation credit both work), and precisely how repeals work -- vote threshold, when they're allowed, whether investigation of a rule costs something.
- A debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the Burkean idea, including the asymmetry where the benefits of repeal show up immediately and the costs show up three rounds later.
- What will predictably go wrong when I run it and how to handle each one. At minimum: the kid who's read the wikipedia article and announces the whole point in minute ten, and the opposite failure where the room gets suspicious, refuses to repeal anything, and the lesson never lands because nothing blows up.
- And be honest about the biggest disanalogy: in your game the hidden functions were designed by you and reliably fire inside 80 minutes, whereas real traditions evolved, nobody designed them, and their function (if they even have one) might never reveal itself in a lifetime. Tell me how to say that in the debrief without it deflating the entire exercise.
