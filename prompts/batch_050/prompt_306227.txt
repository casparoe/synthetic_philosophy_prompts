You are a professional satirist with a doctorate in political philosophy and a specialty in the ethics of public office. Before drafting, brainstorm in a few sentences: list the central commitments and tensions of the "dirty hands" problem in politics (the claim that governing sometimes requires actions that are wrong even when justified; the question of whether guilt or moral residue survives the justification; the contrast between the consequentialist's briskness and the deontologist's anguish; the worry that willingness to get one's hands dirty becomes a self-congratulating pose). Then write the piece described below, making sure the brainstormed material is what generates the jokes.

The piece: a parody workplace health-and-safety manual, issued by an imaginary "Office of Ethical Hygiene" to all incoming cabinet ministers, titled something like *Safe Handling of Necessary Evils: A Guide for Employees*. The manual should treat moral dirty hands as a literal occupational hazard to be managed with procedures: personal protective equipment (the difference between gloves that keep the hands clean and gloves that merely keep the fingerprints off), permissible exposure limits (how many compromised principles per fiscal quarter before one must be rotated out of cabinet), incident report forms for unavoidable wrongdoing, decontamination protocols whose fine print admits that the residue never fully washes off no matter how justified the exposure was, and a whistleblower clause that punishes employees for reporting the very evils the manual instructs them to commit.

The humor must come from the real structure of the philosophical problem, not from invented absurdities: the manual's central tension should be that the Office insists simultaneously that these actions are necessary, that they remain wrong, and that proper procedure requires the minister to feel appropriately bad about them — with a schedule of recommended remorse durations appended. Include one section quietly savaging the consequentialist position (the Office offers a fast-track decontamination service that certifies hands "clean" if the outcome exceeded a utility threshold, and a footnote noting that no minister has ever requested the slow, honest wash instead) and one on the deontologist (a form for resigning in protest, available for pre-order in bulk).

Length: about 700 words, formatted as numbered sections with a title, a preamble, and at least one appendix. Keep the tone flat, bureaucratic, and deadpan throughout — no winking, no asides. A reader versed in the dirty-hands literature should recognize exactly which commitments are being needled in each clause; a reader who isn't should still find it funny as a portrait of an institution that has procedurized its own conscience.
