A colleague walked me through this argument last term, and I agreed to every step before noticing where we had ended up. It concerns the two English conditional forms and the familiar contrast between "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" — plainly true — and "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" — plainly false.

(1) On Monday, planning a Saturday picnic in ignorance of the weather, I truly say: "If it doesn't rain on Saturday, we'll hold the picnic." Take this to be true: we are resolute, and nothing stands in the way.
(2) On Sunday, the picnic rained off, I say: "If it hadn't rained yesterday, we would have held the picnic."
(3) This second remark is simply the first re-expressed from a later vantage point: the same conditional content, with the tenses adjusted for what is now past.
(4) Truth is not time-indexed; what was true on Monday cannot have become false by Sunday merely through the passage of time and the growth of my knowledge, for the underlying facts about our intentions and the weather have not changed.
(5) So an indicative conditional and its later counterfactual counterpart must always agree in truth value.
(6) Hence the Kennedy pair must agree in truth value: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" stands or falls with "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have."
(7) Yet the first is true and the second false; anyone who knows that Kennedy was killed by a single assassin will accept the first while having no reason whatsoever to accept the second.
(8) Therefore one of (1)–(7) must go.

I would most like to keep (4) — the timelessness of truth strikes me as non-negotiable — and the verdicts in (7), which seem to me among the firmest data in this area. I also note that the picnic case is precisely the sort of case in which the two forms appear to coincide, which is what made (3) seem harmless. Which step should I reject, what made it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost?
