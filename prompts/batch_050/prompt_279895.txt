So I got into an argument in the comments on a rationalist-adjacent blog about whether Bongard problems (the six-panels-on-the-left, six-on-the-right things) actually test "concept induction from few examples," or whether they just test wheter the solver happens to share the designer's prior over rules. I want a constructed case to make this concrete, because right now we're both just gesturing at Goodman's grue and Solomonoff induction at each other.

Construct a Bongard-style problem to these specs:

- Six "yes" panels and six "no" panels, built only from circles, triangles, and straight line segments. Nothing in the rules should require counting above three.
- The twelve panels must jointly admit two different rules. Rule A: the rule a normal human would spontaneously see, statable in plain English in ten words or fewer. Rule B: a rule that comes out strictly shorter than A in some simple formal feature language (predicates over things like number of shapes, closed vs open, intersection, containment — you can pick the language, but you have to actually write out both encodings and compare lengths, not just assert B is shorter).
- Then give me a thirteenth, unclassified test panel where Rule A says yes and Rule B says no.
- Show that both rules genuinely fit all twelve training panels, and not via degenerate tricks like a disjunction that secretly enumerates the panels.
- Then tell me which feature of the construction is doing the work — i.e., what you'd have to change to make the human prior and the MDL prior agree again — and how a defender of "MDL just picks the right concept, humans included" would attack the case.

The point of the case: I want to argue that few-shot concept learning is underdetermined by the data alone and that what looks like "the obvious rule" is prior all the way down. So don't cheat by stipulating the verdicts — the panels have to actually generate the disagreement out of the two induction principles, otherwise I'm just asserting my conclusion with extra steps.

One more thing: this whole construction hinges on comparing Rule A's length in English against Rule B's length in a formal language, which feels like comparing apples and oranges and is probly where the case is weakest. So at the end, say how confident you are that the construction actually delivers the disagreement, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
