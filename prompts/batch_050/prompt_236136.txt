I've been keeping a log of my own reactions to choices involving ambiguous probabilities, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle underneath them. Here are the cases and my verdicts. Assume throughout that I'm choosing where to place a bet, that draws are at random, and that I have no reason to suspect deception.

1. Urn A has exactly 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn B has 100 balls, each red or black, in unknown proportions. A correct bet on either pays $1,000. I prefer betting on red from A over red from B, and I also prefer betting on black from A over black from B. I know this is the classic pattern; I have it anyway, and it doesn't feel like a mistake.

2. A fresh coin from the mint, never flipped, no track record: I'm indifferent between betting on its first flip coming up heads and betting on heads with a coin I know to be fair. Not merely resigned to indifference — genuinely indifferent, in a way I'm not in case 1.

3. Urn C is known to contain between 40 and 60 red balls out of 100. Urn D is known to contain between 10 and 90. I prefer betting on red from C over red from D, even though I can't name a probability for either.

4. Same setup as case 1, except the prize is $1. Now I'm indifferent between the urns. Not just less bothered — actually indifferent.

5. Reverse stakes: instead of winning money, I'll lose $1,000 unless my called color comes up. Here I lean toward the ambiguous urn B over the known urn A, mildly but consistently.

6. Two-stage setup: a fair coin flip decides whether I draw from a guaranteed all-red urn or a guaranteed all-black urn, and I call the color before the flip. I find this slightly more attractive than a straight 50/50 bet from urn A, even though the overall win probability is exactly 1/2 either way. This one embarrasses me a little.

My tentative principle is something like: prefer the option whose win probability is most tightly pinned down, where a single known value beats a range, a narrow range beats a wide one, and full symmetry (as in the fresh coin) counts as pinned down. I'd like this tested against my cases, repaired where it fails, and stated precisely. If no single principle survives, give me the two or three best candidates. For each clause of each candidate, tell me which case motivates it; tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — say, ambiguity about the size of the prize rather than the probability, a choice where I'm offered the chance to resolve the ambiguity at some cost before betting, and a choice between two options that are ambiguous in different dimensions; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Cases 4 and 6 strike me as the likeliest casualties, but I'd rather hear that than get a principle gerrymandered to save them.

One more thing: if you think the whole request is misguided — if these verdicts simply can't be brought under any principle worth having, or if asking for one principle is the wrong way to understand ambiguity aversion — say so plainly rather than forcing a tidy answer onto a mess.
