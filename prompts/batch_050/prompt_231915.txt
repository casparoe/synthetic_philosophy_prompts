So I'm a PhD student in philosophy (ethics of deception, historical side — Augustine, Kant, the usual gang) and I've got to present at our grad work-in-progress seminar in a few weeks. My slightly gimmicky idea, which my supervisor is defintely going to roll her eyes at: read the film The Invention of Lying — the Ricky Gervais one where nobody on earth can lie until his character Mark figures out how — against Augustine's De Mendacio.

Here's why I think it's not just gimmicky. The film pretty clearly frames Mark's most humane lie as the one at his mother's deathbed: he invents an afterlife to comfort her, and then has to invent a whole Man in the Sky cosmology when the story gets out. But Augustine's ranking of lies in De Mendacio inverts that completely — lies about religion are the most grievous of all for him, while the lies he finds least bad are boring practical ones told to shield someone from harm. So the film and Augustine seem to run the same moral calculus in exactly opposite directions, and I want to build the talk around that inversion.

Couple of other threads I want to work in. First, the world of the film doesn't just lack lies, it lacks fiction — the movie studios make sober narrated readings of historical events instead of stories. I'd like to know what an Augustinian should make of that: is fiction a lie on his definition (falsehood plus intent to deceive), or does the film accidentally concede Augustine's own distinction? Second, the premise seems to conflate not being able to lie with having to blurt out everything, but Augustine (I think) is fine with keeping silent and withholding truths, so the brutal compulsive honesty of that world isn't actually entailed by the premise. Third, where does the film genuinely resist the Augustinian reading? The deathbed lie really does seem to help, there's that bit where he apparently talks a stranger down off a ledge by just asserting good news (if I'm remembering the scene right), and Augustine's no-exceptions line — not even to save a life — looks monstrous to most of the seminar audience, me included some days.
