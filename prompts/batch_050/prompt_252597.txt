So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general interest magazine, and my editor has assigned me a piece I'm genuinly torn on. I want you to help me think it through before I file.

Here's the setup. There's a town I'm calling Millbrook in the piece, a former company town where a chemical plant explosion in the 1960s killed about forty workers. Ever since, the town has held an annual memorial ceremony: names read aloud, church bells, the whole thing. This year it's blown up into a fight. The company that now owns the site is offering to fund a redevelopment of the downtown, but their condition is that a plaque describing the company's negligence in the explosion gets removed, and they've hinted they'd prefer the annual ceremony quietly wind down. A lot of younger residents are on board — they say the ritual keeps the town defined by a tragedy none of them lived through, and that it scares off exactly the investment the town needs. The survivors' families say dropping the ceremony would be killing the victims a second time, and that a town that sells its memory for a redevelopment deal will sell anything.

My editor wants a column that takes a side. The thing is, I can feel the pull both ways. On one side there's the thought that a community owes something to its dead — that remembering them is a kind of debt, and letting a ceremony die out of convenience (or worse, as part of a business transaction) is a betrayal even if no living person is harmed by the forgetting. On the other side there's the thought that collective memory isn't automatically virtuous: plenty of communities nurse old wounds for generations and it curdles into grievance and stagnation, and sometimes deliberately letting go — after the truth has been acknowledged, not instead of acknowledging it — is the healthier and even the more just thing. I don't want the wishy-washy column where I shrug and say it's complicated. I want an actual argument.

Please argue, one way or the other, what Millbrook should do, and by extension what the principled line is between a duty to remember and a legitimate — maybe even obligatory — letting-go. Note that the plaque question and the ceremony question might not have the same answer, and if your argument separates them that's fine, but I need a bottom line on both.

Structure the response like this, roughly:

**The verdict up front** (~75 words): your bottom line, the way a columnist's nut graf would read. No hedging.

**The strongest case for keeping the ritual** (~250 words): steelman the families. What's actually lost when a community stops commemorating? Is it something owed to the dead themselves, to the survivors, or to the community's future self?

**The strongest case for letting go** (~250 words): steelman the younger residents and, if it's defensible at all, the redevelopment deal. When does memory become a burden a community is allowed to put down, and does it matter whether the forgetting is negotiated versus organic?

**The distinction that does the work** (~200 words): identify the conceptual difference your argument actually turns on — e.g., truth-acknowledgment vs. ritual repetition, forgetting vs. being made to forget, memory as identity vs. memory as grievance — and defend why that's the right line to draw.

**The column itself** (~150 words): write the closing passage of my piece the way you'd want it to run, given everything above. Make it land.
