Next month our town's coal-fired power station closes for good after nearly seventy years, and because I gave a decent leaving speech for my supervisor last summer I've now been landed with the closing address at the decommissioning ceremony. I'm 27, I'm an engineer not a writer, and it needs to be about ten minutes.

The audience is genuinely awkward. Half the room will be current and retired workers, including my own grandad, who moved to this town in the 60s because the plant was hiring — honestly, most of my family only exists because of it. The other half is councillors, local press, and a contingent of climate campaigners who spent years fighting for the closure and will be listening for any whiff of nostalgia-for-coal.

Here's the bind I keep going round in. Every honest sentence cuts someone. If I say closing the plant is right because of what the emissions will do to people alive in 2080, the pensioners hear "your life's work was a harm". If I say the workers did nothing wrong — they followed every rule of their time, they kept the lights on — the campaigners hear me minimising harm that's real and still unfolding.

And there's a wierder wrinkle I want the speech to actually grapple with rather than dodge: if the plant had never been built, the harm wouldn't have happened, but neither would I, nor half the people in the room, because our grandparents would never have met here. So "it would have been better if this place had never existed" seems to imply it would be better if I didn't exist, which I can't bring myself to say — but I also can't see how to reject it without going soft on the harm. Apparantly philosophers have a name for this sort of puzzle, but I don't want to lecture anyone about it, I just want the speech to take it seriously.

So: write the speech. It should honour the workers as people who did honest work, without claiming the plant was harmless; take the future seriously without treating the people in the room as its enemies; and find something true to say about gratitude and harm coexisting, without wrapping it up in a neat both-sides bow at the end. No jokes about coal being "on its last legs" (I've heard them all). British English please, and no quotations from famous writers — someone in that room will know and I will be found out immediately.
