I am a tenured professor of philosophy at a regional university, twenty-three years into a career that stopped meaning much to me some time ago. I stay — so I tell myself, my wife, and my colleagues — because I have no real choice: my pension vests fully in seven more years, my daughter's tuition depends on my salary, the department would lose its only senior hire if I left, and a man of fifty-three does not simply begin again. Last spring the university offered a voluntary separation package; I set the letter aside unanswered, on the grounds that the terms settled the matter by themselves. An old friend who left academia a decade ago listened to all of this over dinner and told me, not unkindly, that my inventory of necessities is a device for ensuring I never have to decide anything — that I have arranged my past choices into a kind of nature, so that the future can arrive without my involvement. I replied that he mistakes prudence for evasion; when he made his leap he had a spouse's income and no children. Which of us is right about what is actually going on in my reasoning? I am less interested in career advice than in whether there is a genuine philosophical distinction between recognizing one's real constraints and hiding behind them — and, if there is, on which side of it my case falls.
