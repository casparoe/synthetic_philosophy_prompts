I'm putting together the laws of nature unit for my grad metaphysics seminar, and before we even crack Armstrong or Lewis I want the standard of adequacy on the table, so students can see what the fight is actually about. Write the desiderata: what does a theory of what laws are have to deliver to count as good enough for serious metaphysics (not just good enough for physicists, who dont care)? Give me the requirements list -- the phenomena it must account for (laws supporting counterfactuals while accidents don't, laws explaining the pattern of events rather than merely summarizing it, the accidental-vs-nomic regularity contrast, genuinely probabilistic laws, the epistemology of how creatures like us could ever come to know them), the distinctions it cannot collapse (law vs. accidental generalization, law vs. what the laws say, necessity-of-a-law vs. necessity-of-necessity), the stock cases it has to get right (empty regularities in worlds lacking the relevant stuff, accidentally true universals like the coins-in-the-pocket case) -- with the consideration behind each requirement, and mark which ones are genuine adequacy conditions and which look to me like prejudices of one camp dressed up as common sense (Humean supervenience is my prime suspect for the latter, but convince me either way).

Two cases I cooked up to stop students from parroting the textbook, which any candidate also has to handle:

1. The rerun world. A universe plays out its entire history, then does the whole thing over again, event for event, and then stops. Is "everything happens exactly twice" itself a law there? The accounts I know give different verdicts depending on details (simplicity, strength, timing) that seem like they shouldn't matter. Say what each of your desiderata implies about this world, and whether a good theory is even obligated to have a verdict.

2. The last particle. A world just like ours except for one extra particle drifting in deep space, never interacting with anything, tracing no discernible pattern. Is there a fact of the matter about whether some law governs it? And is a theory allowed to say "it depends on the whole rest of the world" given the particle never makes a diference to anything?

Then the part I care most about: flag any subset of your desiderata you think is jointly unsatisfiable, since half my students suspect the whole Humean vs. anti-Humean debate is just people talking past each other with mismatched lists. Finally, briefly score the main contenders -- best-systems, governing/universals, dispositional essentialism, and the eliminativist/no-laws option -- against the list. Keep the scorecard short. The criteria are the deliverable; I'd rather the list be right than the table be full.
