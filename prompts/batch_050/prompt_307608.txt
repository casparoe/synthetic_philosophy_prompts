I'm taking a graduate seminar in formal epistemology this term, and we've spent the last few weeks on anthropic reasoning and self-locating belief. My professor has been remarkably consistent in her verdicts across the standard cases, and I've been keeping a record because I suspect there's a single principle underneath them, though I can't pin it down. Her verdicts so far: on the standard Sleeping Beauty case (fair coin tossed Sunday; on Tails she is awakened Monday and Tuesday with memory erasure between them), Beauty should assign probability 1/3 to Heads upon awakening; on a version where Tails produces a hundred awakenings, she says 1/101, so her answer scales rather than reflecting some bare indifference; she rejects the Doomsday argument, denying that her birth rank gives her any evidence about how long humanity will last; and she rejects the Presumptuous Philosopher reasoning, denying that her own existence counts as strong evidence for cosmologies positing vastly more observers rather than few. She is not, however, hostile to self-locating updating in general: in cases where duplicates occupy observably different situations, she accepts that learning which situation one is in licenses ordinary Bayesian updating.

Here is the new case I want to put to her at office hours, and I'd like to predict her answer before I go. An experimenter tosses a fair coin. On Heads, exactly one person is created in a sealed room. On Tails, two people are created, each in a sealed room, the rooms indistinguishable from the inside, with no communication possible and no memory erasure or repeated awakenings involved. Each created person knows the full setup. You find yourself existing in such a room. What probability should you assign to Heads? Predict my professor's verdict, and more importantly tell me what rule she appears to be running. As I see it, her track record sits uncomfortably between two families of views: something like observer-moment counting explains the 1/3 and the 1/101 and would here predict 1/3, but counting views are usually taken to vindicate exactly the presumptuous-reasoner inference she rejects; while a restricted principle that forbids updating on the sheer number or existence of observers and permits updating only on where one is located within a world accommodates her rejections, but has to work to recover her thirder verdicts, and here would seem to predict 1/2. Tell me which of these candidate rules, or what third one, best fits all four verdicts, what it predicts for the incubator case, and where the pressure point in her position lies. Finally, if her verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, tell me which further case -- some variation on duplication, temporal repetition, or observer totals -- would most efficiently discriminate between the candidate rules, and what each rule would say about it.
