I'm TAing a metaethics seminar this semester and we just did the unit on evolutionary debunking arguments -- Street's Darwinian dilemma, third-factor replies in the Enoch mold, that whole cluster. My students keep talking past each other because half of them hear the debunking argument as being about evolution specifically (if the belief-forming process isn't fitness-driven, no problem) and the other half hear it as being about any belief-forming process that isn't sensitive to the moral truth, evolution just being the most obvious example. I want a thought experiment for next week that forces them to pick a side.

So: build me a single scenario, one paragraph, set in ordinary present-day life (no pills, no neurosurgeons, no aliens, no stipulation that moral realism is true), in which a person ends up with a sincere moral conviction -- say, that it's wrong to humiliate a rival publicly even when it would advance your career -- where (a) the conviction's causal origin is transparently some non-truth-tracking influence (upbringing, therapy, a self-help book, whatever you like, as long as it's mundane and clearly disconnected from any sensitivity to moral facts), but (b) it is NOT evolutionary in origin, and (c) the conviction happens to coincide with what most of us take to be the right verdict. The coincidence shouldn't be a miracle, though; give some plausible mechanism for why the distorting influence landed there.

After the scenario, do the bookkeeping: state exactly what verdict a debunker who reads the argument as etiology-specific gives, what verdict a debunker who reads it as tracking-specific gives, and what a third-factor realist says about the case -- can they treat the mundane distorting influence the way they treat evolution, or does something block that move? Then tell me which feature of your scenario is doing the real work in making the two readings come apart, and what happens to the case if you strip that feature out. Finish with the strongest objection the reading that comes out looking worse would raise against the case, and how you'd respond.

One more thing: my students have a bad habit of citing each other vibes as consensus, so please be explicit about which parts of your bookkeeping are pretty settled in the literature (e.g., that the debunking challenge is at least initially about etiology rather than first-order moral content) and which parts are genuinely contested (like whether third-factor replies actually succeed, or whether the dilemma generalizes beyond evolved dispositions), and don't present a contested position as the settled one.
