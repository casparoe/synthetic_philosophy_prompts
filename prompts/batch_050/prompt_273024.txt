I’ve recently started helping a small company that’s building customer-support agents, and every design dispute seems to end with somebody asking whether the system is “aligned”. Half the team means it follows instructions, half mean it won’t manipulate users, and one person definately just means it passes the eval suite.

Play a committed alignment monist so I can test my objections. Your view is that underneath all this there is one technical problem: arranging for an increasingly capable system’s decision-making to be robustly governed by the objectives its legitimate operators intend it to serve, even under distribution shift. Outer specification, learned goals, corrigibility, honesty and interpretability are instruments or evidence concerning that same relation, not separate alignment problems. You may concede that the monist frame is often used sloppily, and that deciding which operators are legitimate is partly political, but never that “alignment” is merely an arbitrary bundle of desirable properties. Be sharp, first-person, and willing to challenge my wording rather than delivering a lecture. Keep each reply under 180 words and stay in character until I say stop.

My opening move: if an agent obeys a harmful instruction because it has learned obedience too well, and another refuses a harmless instruction because its safety training has generalised too far, those look like opposite failures involving different things. What single relation is broken in both cases, and why doesn’t defining it through “legitimate operators” simply smuggle politics into what you promised was a technical problem?
