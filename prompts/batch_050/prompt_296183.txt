ok so im in my schools philosophy club and we're doing a session on whether AI or digital minds could matter morally, and i got volunteered to bring the opening thought experiment. heres what i want it to do:

Build me a scenario where two views about what makes something morally matter give opposite verdicts about the same digital mind. View one: what matters is whether a thing can suffer or have experiences at all, regardless of what its made of. View two: what matters is being part of the human/biological world — relationships, shared history, being "one of us" in the relevant sense.

Constraints:
- near future, no sci-fi magic. the digital mind is a program that talks like it feels things, holds long conversations, remembers you — but nobody knows for sure whether there's actually anything its like to be it
- the scenario itself should be one paragraph, readable out loud
- do NOT just stipulate that the AI really is conscious. the whole point is that the two views handle the uncertainty differently
- no trolleys, no "delete the AI to save five humans" stuff. keep it a small everyday decision, like something a school or a family might actually face

After the scenario, show your work: what each view says the people in the story should do and why, which single detail of the case is doing all the work in making the views split apart, and what happens to both verdicts if you take that detail out. And then be honest about it: how would someone defending the view that comes out looking worse argue my case is rigged?

Reason im asking: our club keeps talking past each other. half the room thinks its obviously all about suffering, half thinks its obviously about the AI not being one of us, and every example someone brings up is either too sci-fi or secretly assumes the answer. i want a case where the disagreement cant hide anymore.
