So I retired from my chemistry department two years ago and, like I keep telling people who ask, I have not been bored for a single afternoon. The latest thing is that I joined a philosophy reading group at the public library, and we've been working through contractualist moral theory, mostly Scanlon. The group leader, a former ethics professor named Diane, has this habit of settling our arguments by just giving her verdict on little rescue cases we invent, and I've started keeping a list in my notebook because I suspect she's more systematic than she admits. Here are the verdicts I have down so far. One: save one person from death or cure ten people of paralysis in one leg -- she says save the one from death, numbers can't make up the difference. Two: save one from death or save two from death -- save the two, obviously. Three: cure one person of total paralysis or relieve a million people of mild headaches -- cure the one, the headaches are too trivial to aggregate no matter how many you pile up. Four: prevent one death or prevent one case of blindness -- prevent the death, the worse individual complaint wins when numbers are tied. Five: cure one person of severe lifelong chronic pain or cure a thousand people of sore throats -- cure the one again.

Now here's the case I want to spring on her at next week's meeting, and I want to predict what she'll say before I ask, partly to win a small wager with another member who thinks Diane is just winging it. New case: you can cure five people of total paralysis, or you can cure fifty people of a permanent moderate chronic pain condition that doesn't threaten their lives but does substantially limit what they can do day to day -- think of someone who can work and get around but can't sleep properly or lift their kids. I've been going back and forth on this. If she's running a strict tiers rule, where the most serious individual complaint always wins regardless of how many lesser complaints pile up, she goes with the five. But if she thinks moderately serious complaints can aggregate once the numbers get big enough, or if she has some threshold where a complaint becomes "relevant" and starts counting toward a sum, she might go with the fifty. I genuinely can't tell wich, and that makes me think the earlier cases underdetermine her rule.

Third, "What each rule predicts" -- a paragraph or two on what each of those candidate rules says about my five-versus-fifty case. Fourth, "The case that would settle it" -- one paragraph describing a further case, different from mine, where the rules you're weighing would come apart, so I can have it ready as a follow-up question.
