For several years I've kept a notebook of moral questions I've changed my mind about, and I've noticed that nearly every change traces back to something another person told me -- a friend, a colleague, an author. That now bothers me, because I've come to think the following argument is sound, and it implies I should not have changed my mind on any of those occasions. I can't find the mistake, but the conclusion seems to prove too much.

(1) Accepting a claim on someone's say-so is reasonable only if you have some reason to think that person is reliable about that kind of claim.

(2) The only way to get reason to think someone reliable about a kind of claim is to check some of their claims of that kind against facts you can establish without trusting them -- the way you'd test a weather forecast against the sky, or a mechanic's diagnosis against whether the car starts.

(3) Moral claims can't be checked that way. When I "check" a moral claim someone has made, all I can do is consult my own moral judgment, and if the whole point of consulting them was that I didn't trust my own judgment on the question, the check is circular. Checking the mechanic's diagnosis does not require me to trust my own diagnosis first; checking a moral advisor's advice apparently does.

(4) So I can never acquire reason to think anyone reliable about moral matters, and by (1) I can never reasonably accept a moral claim on someone's say-so. Moral conviction has to be worked out alone, case by case.

(5) But almost none of my moral convictions were worked out case by case. They were handed to me by parents, teachers, books, and community, and I hold most of them on that basis still. By (4), I should set all of them aside until I've re-derived each one for myself.

(6) Worse, even the convictions I did work out alone I now hold partly on the word of my past self: I remember the conclusion but not the reasoning, and memory delivers conclusions much the way another person would. If other people's uncheckable reports don't confer justification, neither do the reports of the person I was last year.

(7) So I'm entitled to no moral beliefs except those I can re-derive from scratch, right now, without leaning on anything anyone -- including my earlier self -- has told me. That can't be right. It would leave me, and apparently everyone, with almost nothing.

The argument also generalizes in a way I find suspicious, since a parallel version of (3) would seem to sink testimony about history or about events in countries I'll never visit, yet I take myself to know a good deal of both. So presumably something gives. If forced to choose, I'd keep the circularity observation in (3), which still seems to me to mark a real difference between moral and empirical checking, and the symmetry point in (6); I'd be least sorry to surrender (2) or the move from (4) to (5). Tell me which step actually fails, why it seemed not to, and what I give up by abandoning it rather than one of the others.
