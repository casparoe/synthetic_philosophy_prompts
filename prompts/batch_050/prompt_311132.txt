I hold the view that epistemic akrasia is never rational: it cannot be reasonable to believe p while simultaneously believing that the total evidence available to you does not support believing p. On my view, higher-order evidence -- evidence about the reliability of one's own reasoning, such as learning that a trusted peer disagrees, or that one was tired, biased, or operating with a degraded memory when forming a judgment -- is simply evidence, and it must be integrated with first-order evidence into a single unified assessment. The alternative, so-called level-splitting, in which one rationally maintains a belief one judges epistemically unjustified, strikes me as a confusion dressed up as modesty. Tell me what would count against my position.

I want the defeaters mapped, not just listed. Sort them from fatal to merely awkward. For each one, say whether a committed defender of my view would concede it as genuinely damaging or dismiss it -- and on what grounds. In particular, I would like you to consider: cases of disagreement with a peer one knows to be equally informed and equally careful; evidence that one is systematically prone to a specific bias even when one cannot detect the bias operating in the present instance; and the complaint that anti-akrasia views of my kind covertly reintroduce akrasia at a higher level, since believing that my evidence supports p is itself a belief that might come apart from what my evidence supports about that belief. Which of these has real force, and which trade on conflating rationality with idealized self-transparency?

I also want an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is. If the defender retreats to claims about ideal rationality, or about what a fully rational agent would believe, or about normative ideals rather than psychological possibilities, at what point has the view been insulated from any case that could trouble it? Is that a defect for a view of this kind, or is some degree of insulation appropriate for a normative thesis?

Finally, consider a concrete empirical test: suppose a well-run, long-term forecasting study found that subjects who knowingly maintain akratic states -- holding a forecast while explicitly judging that their evidence does not support it -- outperform, over many trials, subjects who always revise their forecasts to match their higher-order self-assessment. Would that count against my view, and by how much? Say what result would move me, what result would not, and why.
