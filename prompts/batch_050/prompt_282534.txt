So a buddy and I have this running argument about whether the world is getting less violent, and I'm going to write up where I actually stand before we record this thing for his podcast, because I suspect I'm contradicting myself somewhere and I want to find it before he does. Here's my position, roughly:

1. There's a fact of the matter about wether the world is getting less violent. It's an empirical question, not just people talking past each other about what the word "violence" should mean.

2. The right metric is per capita rates of violent death, because what morally matters is each individual person's risk of dying violently. Bigger absolute totals over time mostly just track that there are way more people around now.

3. The long-run trend is clearly downward. Life in prehistory and the early states was staggeringly more lethal per capita than even the worst modern societies.

4. That said, the 20th century was the bloodiest century ever in absolute terms — two world wars, the Holocaust, the gulags, the whole thing — and I don't think that touches the decline thesis at all.

5. What counts as violence has rightly broadened over time: bullying, spanking your kids, emotional abuse in a marriage all count now in a way they didn't a few generations back, and that broadening is itself part of the moral progress the decline story is describing.

6. But I don't buy "structural violence" — poverty, unjust institutions, lack of healthcare — as real violence. That's just diluting the concept until it means "anything bad."

7. One nuclear exchange could kill more people than every previous war put together, so the historical trend doesn't tell us much about future risk. That doesn't mean the decline isn't real, though.
