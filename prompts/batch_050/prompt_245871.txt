I'm taking a formal epistemology course, and we're spending a week on the two-envelope paradox (you're given two envelopes, told one contains twice as much money as the other, pick one, and are offered the chance to switch). For a study handout I'm making for my section, please write a taxonomy of the positions on where the switching argument goes wrong — or whether it goes wrong at all — structured as a decision tree.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Each branch should be labeled with the diagnostic question that splits it, phrased so that a classmate could answer it without knowing any position labels. The questions I want doing the work are ones like: whether the player has a genuine, proper probability distribution over the envelope amounts at all; whether that distribution has finite expected value; whether the player has opened their envelope and seen the amount before deciding; and whether the flaw lies in the expected-value calculation itself or in the symmetry reasoning that licenses treating "my envelope" and "the other envelope" interchangeably. Feel free to add one or two further splitting questions if you find a real fork I've missed.

The leaves should include, at minimum: the view that switching is sometimes genuinely correct (for distributions with infinite expected value); the diagnosis that the argument trades on an improper or nonexistent prior over the amounts; the diagnosis that the error is averaging over both envelopes symmetrically when the expected gain must be computed conditional on what you'd do after peeking; and resolutions that accept the expected-value reasoning in the abstract but deny it applies once any concrete, finite distribution is specified. Use established names where they exist and mark a leaf "unnamed" where the position is coherent but nobody standardly defends it. Where two positions the literature treats as distinct end up at the same leaf, flag that explicitly rather than papering over it.

To keep the tree concrete, anchor at least three of the diagnostic questions in specific cases: the standard setup with no stated distribution; the variant where the player is told the amounts are x and 2x with x drawn from a particular specified distribution whose expectation is infinite; and the variant where the player opens their envelope, sees $100, and must decide whether to switch. At the end, briefly note any unoccupied combinations of answers and diagnose whether each is empty because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Aim for about six to eight leaves, pitched at undergraduates who know basic expected-value calculations but haven't read the literature. Please write in American English, and begin directly with the tree itself — no preface, framing remarks, or restatement of my request.
