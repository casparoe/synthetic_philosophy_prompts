A senior colleague in my philosophy department has, over the years, given firm verdicts on a series of Newcomb-style problems — cases in which an agent chooses between taking only an opaque box or taking both the opaque box and a transparent box visibly containing $1,000, where the opaque box holds $1,000,000 or nothing depending on some earlier prediction or correlated process. Her record:

1. The classic case: a predictor with a 99.9% track record, known to work by building a detailed psychological model of each player, modeled her yesterday. She takes only the opaque box.
2. The transparent variant: both boxes are glass, and she can see the million already sitting in the opaque box. She takes both boxes.
3. The actuary: the prediction is generated from demographic base rates and a short questionnaire — no psychological modeling of the individual — and is correct about 60% of the time. She takes both boxes.
4. The simulator: the same setup as the classic case, except the predictor is a full computer simulation of her, run last week, whose choices match hers 99.9% of the time. She takes only the opaque box.
5. The separated twin: no prediction at all; instead, her identical twin, raised identically and facing the same choice in another city, has his choice settle hers — the experimenters fill her opaque box with the million if and only if her twin takes only his. The twins' choices in such setups are known to match about 99.9% of the time. She takes only the opaque box.

Next term's seminar case is this: a predictor that works exactly like the classic one — a detailed psychological model of the individual player, built from brain scans and interviews — except that the modeling is noisy, and its predictions are correct only about 75% of the time.

Predict her verdict on the seminar case. In doing so: identify the rule she appears to be running, and at least one rival rule that fits all five prior verdicts just as well; state which of the five is the closest analog to the new case; give your best guess and the reasons for preferring it over what the rival rule predicts; and describe one further case that would tell the two rules apart. Then say what would change your mind — which feature of her record, or which consideration about the cases, would most likely make your prediction wrong, and where your reconstruction of her rule is most vulnerable to error. Lead with your predicted verdict in the first sentence, followed by the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the setup.
