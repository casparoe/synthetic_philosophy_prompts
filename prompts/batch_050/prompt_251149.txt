I help organise the activities programme at a residential care home, and we've just been offered a cheap deal on a load of VR headsets. The sales pitch is that residents can "go" to Venice or walk on a beach from their armchairs. Some of the staff think it's brilliant, some think it's vaguely creepy, and I keep going back and forth.

The thing I can't stop chewing on is this thought experiment I read about ages ago: imagine a machine you can plug into that gives you whatever experiences you want, indistinguishable from the real thing, for the rest of your life. You'd feel like you were climbing mountains and falling in love, but really you'd be floating in a tank. Apparently most people say they wouldn't plug in, even if you guarantee the experiences would be lovely. Which suggests how things *feel* isn't the only thing that makes a life go well -- what you actually do, whether it's real, whether you chose it, might matter too. But when I try to apply that to the VR question I just tie myself in knots, because the residents know it's not real and they can take the headset off, so it's not exactly the machine, is it?

What I'd like is a proper decision procedure our team could run on ANY activity proposal, not just this one. Something grounded in whatever the actual thinking is on what makes a life good for someone (I gather there's a split between "it's about pleasant experiences", "it's about getting what you want", and "it's about certain goods whether you want them or not", but correct me if I've butchered that). So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to move to the next one, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it has to throw its hands up and hand it to a person because it's a values call not a checklist item. It needs to be runnable in about fifteen minutes by staff with no background in any of this, and the home manager signs off at the end.

Two things I specifically want flagged:

1. Which steps are real procedure and which are judgement calls wearing a procedure's clothing. Be honest about it.
2. Where this kind of thinking breaks down entirely -- e.g. residents with dementia, where "what they want" and "what's real" get very murky.

Then run the procedure on two actual proposals, showing your working at each step: the VR travel sessions for residents who can no longer travel, and a suggestion from management to scrap the gardening club because turnout is poor and it tires people out -- except the four regulars absolutley love it and it's the only time they leave the building. Ideally at least one of these should land on an answer I won't enjoy, so I can see the procedure has teeth and isn't just agreeing with me.

No reading lists please -- I won't follow them up. Just the procedure, the worked examples, and its limits.
