I serve on the acquisitions committee of a small museum, and a major donor has made clear that his continued funding depends on my coming to genuinely admire his late wife's paintings, which strike me as derivative and sentimental. The situation has pushed me into thinking about fitting-attitude accounts of value and the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I have landed on the following commitments. I would like to know whether they hang together.

(1) Buck-passing: for something to be admirable just is for there to be sufficient reason of the right kind to admire it; the reasons explain the value, not the other way around.
(2) The donor's leverage gives me a genuine reason to admire the paintings, though it does not make them any more admirable. I want to insist that it really is a reason for the attitude itself, not merely a reason to want to admire the paintings or to bring it about that I do.
(3) About belief I am a strict evidentialist: incentives to believe something — that it would comfort me, or save my job — are never reasons to believe it, only reasons to induce the belief in myself or to act as if I had it.
(4) The distinction between right- and wrong-kind reasons must be drawn in non-evaluative terms, say by what the reason is about, since an account of admirableness that defines the right kind as reasons bearing on the object's admirableness would be circular.

I take it that (1) and (2) reproduce the textbook problem and are the obvious candidates for tension, but I suspect the real trouble lies elsewhere. Please audit the set: identify the genuine tensions, order them by severity, and for each say which commitment would have to give and what giving it up costs. Note any pair that looks problematic but is in fact fine on closer inspection, and explain why. Then tell me which single revision restores the most consistency at the lowest price. Please say how confident you are in each part of the audit and single out the point on which you are least sure. Please respond in American English.
