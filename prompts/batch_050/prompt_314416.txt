I am preparing a closing exercise for a seminar on early Chinese ethics and want a poem that dramatizes, rather than summarizes, the disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi over human nature. Write a poem of 24 to 32 lines in which the debate is staged as an argument between two unnamed voices, with the form of each voice enacting its metaphysics.

Alternate strictly between the voices for three exchanges, then close with a single braided stanza. The Mencius voice speaks in tercets of long, heavily enjambed lines that keep spilling past their line breaks, built from his signature imagery of spontaneous growth and downward flow: the sprout of compassion stirred in the bystander by the child teetering at the well, water seeking the low ground yet capable of being dammed or splashed uphill, the heart's four beginnings needing only room and care to extend. The Xunzi voice answers in quatrains of short, end-stopped lines, each line a completed stroke, built from his workshop imagery: crooked timber awaiting the press-frame and steam to be made straight, dull metal brightened on the whetstone, clay shaped on the potter's wheel, ritual as the conscious artifice (wei) by which the sage-kings made goodness out of recalcitrant material. Neither voice may name the other, mention the debate, or use the words nature, good, or bad; a reader who knows the tradition should be able to assign every stanza to its author from syntax and imagery alone, and the two vocabularies should never borrow from each other.

The closing stanza of four to six lines must not adjudicate. Have it begin in the Xunzi voice's end-stopped manner and loosen, line by line, into the Mencius voice's enjambment — or run the transformation in reverse — so that the poem ends with the question still formally undecided: whether the work of cultivation extends a sprout that was already there or corrects a grain that never ran that way.

Follow the poem with a single section titled "Maker's Notes," roughly 150 to 200 words, explaining how the prosody of each voice tracks the position it speaks for, which images you drew on, and what you did in the final stanza to keep the transformation from quietly settling the dispute in one voice's favor.
