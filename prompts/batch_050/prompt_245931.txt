So I route most of my giving through one of those charity evaluators that ranks nonprofits by cost per life saved. Their whole method is: look at randomized trials, estimate how much good each dollar does, recommend the top handful. So malaria bednets and deworming pills end up at the top of the list, while the hospice down the street, my friend's literacy program, anything whose value is about dignity or community or art -- none of it even gets scored, because you can't run a trial on it or put a number on the outcome. I've made my peace with that part.

But here's what bugs me. The same donor community, in some cases literally the same people, also pours money into preventing extinction-level catastrophes from future technologies, where there is no data at all, no trials, just arguments and probability estimates over timescales of centuries. The standard of evidence there couldn't be more different. So what view of what matters, and of what it takes to *know* that something matters, does this whole practice presuppose? Does the evaluator's method quietly assume that the only real goods are the countable ones, or could someone fully on board with the method deny that? Is the combination -- hyper-strict measurability for one pot of money, pure speculation for the other -- one coherent position or two different theories being swapped in and out depending on which conclusion someone wants? And if we scrapped the measurable-impact requirement and ranked everything by best-guess expected value, what would concretely have to change about how the evaluator works, and is there anything in the current setup worth keeping that you'd lose?
