I work as a backend engineer on a music-streaming app. Last year we shipped a recommendation model that infers what users want from their behavior — skips, replays, listening time — and optimizes its suggestions to maximize those signals. By every metric it's a success: engagement is way up. But two things bother me. First, users mostly play whatever we put at the top of their feed, so the model "learns" that people love what we were already recommending, and doubles down on it. Second, some users leave the app running in the background for hours and rack up huge listening time, while others carefully curate a handful of albums; the model reads the first group as the satisfied ones. When I raised this, my manager pushed back: the data doesn't lie, people vote with their clicks, and giving them more of what they actually do is the least paternalistic, most honest way to serve them — anything else would mean second-guessing our own users.

I can't cleanly articulate what's wrong with his argument, but I think something is. Is optimizing for what people observably do a sound way of giving them what they want? If not, where exactly does the reasoning break — is the problem that our own system contaminates the behavior we're measuring, that the metrics stand in for something they don't capture, or something more basic about the relationship between choice and wanting? Please take a definite position on where his argument fails and what we should be optimizing for instead, and give your reasons, rather than just surveying the considerations.
