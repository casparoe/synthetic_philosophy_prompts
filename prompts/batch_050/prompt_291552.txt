Okay so here's the situation. I write novels -- literary fiction, nothing fancy, but my last book did better than expected. It's about a man who has his corpus callosum severed to treat epilepsy, and the whole book turns on whether there's one person in there afterwards or two. I did my homework on the actual split-brain research -- the left hand doing one thing while the right does another, the verbal side confabulating explanations for what the other side chose, patients whose two hands fight over getting dressed -- and I made up the rest, as one does.

Now the weird part: there's an annual conference on consciousness research and they've invited me to give the after-dinner speech at their closing banquet. God knows why. The room will be maybe 120 people, and it's a genuinely split audience (ha): half are neuroscientists and philosophers who know the commissurotomy literature cold and will notice instantly if I get something wrong or say something naive about it, and half are spouses, administrators, and a few book-club types who are there because of the novel and who think "split-brain" is just a metaphor for being of two minds about something.

Write me the speech. About twelve minutes spoken. Here's the constraint that makes it hard: I don't want to give a lecture, and I don't want to do the humble "I'm just a storyteller, what do I know" routine either, because I actually think the novel got at something the empirical work has trouble with -- namely what it's like from the inside, for the one consciousness (or two?) that has to go on living as this person, making one life out of it, narrating it afterwards as "my" life even when the narrator might be the confabulating side. I want the speech to take a real position on the unity question: that the interesting thing about these patients isn't whether they're secretly two people, but that they show how much of being one person is an act of narration rather than a fact of anatomy -- while being honest that the scientists in the room could poke holes in this, and pre-empting the two or three most obvious objections they'd raise over dessert.

If it helps, the "split audience in the room" mirroring the split-brain theme feels like an available structural device, but only use it if you can do it without being cute about it. A little humor is fine -- it's a banquet, people will be three glasses in. Don't use any actual quotes from the literature, I'll only mangle them. And definately no slides.
