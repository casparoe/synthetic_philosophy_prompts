My position: the film spends two hours showing that nobody on that ship *can* treat HAL as just a machine — Bowman is asking HAL to make choices by the pod bay doors scene, not issuing commands — and the horror of the ending is precisely watching that personhood drain away module by module.

This connects to something I've been reading, so let me set it out and you can tell me if I've garbled it. Dennett's three stances: you can predict a system from the physical stance (figure out what it'll do from its constitution and the laws of nature), the design stance (it was built to do X, so barring breakdowns it'll do X), or the intentional stance (treat it as an agent with the beliefs and desires it *ought* to have given its situation, and predict what a rational agent would do). And his real patterns idea, as I understand it, is that a pattern in behaviour is real if describing things in terms of it genuinely compresses and predicts — so beliefs and desires aren't either "in there" or not, they're as real as the predictive payoff is.

Read the film through that framework and give me a committed reading, because Dan and I couldn't settle it. Concretely: the film seems to dramatise a forced retreat down the stances — the physical stance is hopeless (nobody can predict HAL from his circuitry), the design stance collapses the moment he malfunctions, and the crew get pushed onto the intentional stance, which is exactly what makes the ending land. Is that a fair scene-by-scene description of what Kubrick is up to, or am I retrofitting? On Dennett's view, if the intentional stance is the only stance that pays its way with HAL, then his fear is a real pattern and Dan's it's-just-code line is a stance confusion rather than a fact about HAL — does the film agree, or does it suggest something is genuinely lost when the patterns dissolve, something Dennett's mild realism can't honour?
