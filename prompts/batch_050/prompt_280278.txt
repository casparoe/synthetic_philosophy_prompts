You are a philosopher with expertise in game theory and the social mechanisms that sustain cooperation. Please think carefully about the following situation before answering; it may help to first sketch the strategic structure involved before giving advice.

Six of my neighbors and I run a dinner club: each week one household cooks for the whole group, rotating in a fixed order. It has worked well for two years — everyone puts in real effort, because everyone knows they'll be on the receiving end again soon. Last week one member, Priya, announced she is moving abroad in two months and will host only twice more. Since then I've noticed the people scheduled to cook after her announcement have started serving noticeably cheaper, lazier meals, and I suspect this will get worse as her departure approaches. I'm scheduled to cook the week after Priya's last dinner. Part of me wants to lower my own effort too — she'll never cook for me again, so I'd never be "paid back." Another part of me thinks something important is at stake beyond the arithmetic.

Am I right to feel that? Please address: (a) what changes, structurally, in a reciprocal arrangement once one participant has a known exit date, and why cooperation tends to decay before that date even among the members who are staying; (b) whether my reason to keep cooking well can coherently rest on the other five members rather than on Priya, and what that implies about what the dinner club actually runs on; (c) your concrete advice for what I should do, including anything I might say to the group. In your answer, state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the single point in your analysis you are least sure of.
