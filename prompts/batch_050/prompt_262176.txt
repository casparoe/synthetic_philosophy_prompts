In a recent seminar on public monuments, a speaker defended retaining statues of Jefferson and Columbus with an analogy the audience found immediately compelling: judging a historical figure by the moral standards of our own century, he argued, is like faulting a medieval physician for failing to prescribe penicillin. The physician could not have known about antibiotics, since the relevant knowledge did not yet exist; likewise, eighteenth-century slaveholders and fifteenth-century colonizers could not have known the moral truths we now take for granted, and blame is appropriate only where the blamed party could have known and done better.

Please audit this analogy. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the unavailable drug, the unavailable science, the physician's benevolent intent, the patient, the malpractice verdict, and so on. Then identify where the mapping strains. Address, at minimum, the following candidate disanalogies: first, whether moral knowledge is ever unavailable in the way empirical knowledge is, given that contemporaries such as Bartolomé de las Casas denounced the Spanish conquest and that Jefferson himself wrote against slavery while enslaving people; second, whether the analogy quietly models moral norms as discoveries on the pattern of germ theory, and what follows for the defence if they are instead conventions, compromises, or deliverances of ordinary sympathy; third, whether the defence is applied symmetrically, since admirers of these figures credit them with moral insight that was ahead of their time while excusing them, as products of their time, for the insight we deplore their lacking; fourth, whether the analogy conflates judging a person with judging a practice, an institution, or a present-day monument, since a verdict about Columbus the man need not settle what a city ought to celebrate.

Conclude with a verdict: once the disanalogies that matter are separated from those that do not, does the 'man of his time' defence — refounded on whatever survives of the analogy — still exculpate Jefferson, Columbus, or Churchill, or was the penicillin comparison carrying the conclusion by itself? If a more faithful analogy for the defence exists, state it and show that it fares better.
