A friend of mine recently admitted that she keeps believing her late business partner never defrauded her, despite evidence she herself calls damning, because the belief lets her sleep. When I said she should follow the evidence, a third friend ended the conversation with the one-liner that belief aims at truth, as though that settled it. I am no longer sure it settles anything, but I cannot articulate why. Please lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing — for instance, a claim about what makes an attitude count as a belief rather than a hope or a wish, a norm about what may properly justify forming or retaining one, a standard of correctness internal to belief itself, or a claim about what epistemic agents ought to do all things considered — and for each, say whether it is near-platitudinous, substantive but defensible, or probably false. Then tell me which readings would actually bear on my friend's case: which of them, if true, would show that her reason for holding on is simply not the right kind of reason, and which leave open that she is doing something permissible or even sensible. If some readings collapse the distinction between epistemic and practical justification in a way that matters here, say so.
