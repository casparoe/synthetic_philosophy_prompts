So I need advice about a mess that blew up at our neighborhood business association meeting last night. Five of us on the board have to decide whether the association signs a lease on a small storefront for a shared pop-up retail space. We agreed beforehand that signing only makes sense if two things are both true: (1) we can actually afford the rent from our reserves, and (2) the location will pull enough foot traffic to make it worthwhile. So the chair had us vote on each question separately. On affordability: three yes, one no, and Cara abstained because she skipped the meeting where we went over the books and didn't feel right voting. On foot traffic: three yes, two no. So both premises passed, and by our own stated rule that means we sign. But then Dan, who voted no on both, demanded we just vote straight up on "do we sign the lease," and when we did that straw poll it went 2-3 against, since only Alice and Erin actually think we should sign. Now half the group says the decision is already made and the other half says the actual vote is the one that counts.

Here's what's bugging me beyond the usual everyone-wants-to-win stuff. The three people who said it's affordable aren't the same three people who said the location is good -- Alice and Erin are the only ones in both camps. So there's no actual majority of us who bought into the whole case for signing, and yet "the board" supposedly believes both things and is committed to signing. Cara is now saying her abstention means the affordability vote doesn't really represent the group either, which feels like a stretch but I can't fully say why. Meanwhile the chair, who I suspect just wants the lease signed, gets to basically pick the outcome by picking which vote counts as official, and our bylaws only say "decisions are made by majority vote" without saying majority vote on what. Alice argues the premise-by-premise route is more rigorous because each claim got scrutinized on its merits; Dan argues that on any big decision the only thing that matters is whether most people actually endorse the bottom line, otherwise a committee could get dragged into conclusions almost nobody wants. Both sound plausible and they can't both be right.

So: which outcome should we treat as the decision, and is there a principled way to answer that that isn't just "whichever procedure gives the answer you wanted"? Does Cara's abstention matter, and should we handle the two questions differently given that one of them passed with different people weighing in? And going forward, should we amend the bylaws to always vote on the final question directly, always vote on the underlying questions, or something else -- because I feel like whatever rule we pick, cases like this are going to keep coming up, and I want to understand what's actually going wrong here rather than just patch it.
