I've fallen into a running arguement with my brother-in-law about national myths. It started with Dunkirk (his view is that the whole 'Dunkirk spirit' business was morale management that got out of hand), wandered into the Confederate Lost Cause, and at some point he ended the discussion by saying history is written by the victors, and pouring himself another drink as if that settled it.

The irritating thing is I think he's half right, but the slogan seems to change shape depending on what's convenient. Sometimes it's just 'winners control the archives', which is obviously true and tells you nothing about Dunkirk. Sometimes it's a much stronger claim, roughly that we can't actually know what happened, full stop. And here's what really bothers me: the Lost Cause was written by the *losers*, pretty much unambiguously, and it dominated how the war was remembered for generations. Masada is similar — the story only became a national myth in the 20th century, and the Romans did no myth-making on its behalf at all. So his slogan seems to fail exactly for the cases we were arguing about.

Can you lay out the different precise claims that one sentence could be making? I'm guessing there's something like: (a) victors control the surviving sources, (b) victors control what gets taught to schoolchildren, (c) the victors' account simply *becomes* the truth, whatever happened, (d) we can never get past the victors' account even in principle, however careful we are. That's probably not exhaustive and the readings probably overlap. For each, say whether it's a platitude, a genuinely contested empirical claim, or just false — and crucially, which reading someone like my brother-in-law is actually leaning on when he deploys it to close an argument down. I have a suspicion the move is: establish the platitude, then quietly bank the strong claim, but I'd like to see it done properly.

One more thing. If the strongest defensible reading turns out to be true, what actually follows for how one should read something like the Masada narrative or Lost Cause historiography? Does it mean distrust the narrative, distrust the narrators, distrust your own schooling, or something more boring? I'd rather have the boring answer if that's where the argument lands.
