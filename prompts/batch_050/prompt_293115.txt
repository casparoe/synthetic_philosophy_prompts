I teach a philosophy of science seminar, grad students plus a few sharp undergrads, about 12 people, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the force of the presumption of independence in heuristic probabilistic arguments -- the move where you estimate P(A) and P(B) seperately, shrug "these have nothing to do with each other," and multiply, ending up wildly confident in some conjunction (like in heuristic arguments for mathematical conjectures, or fine-tuning style reasoning where you stack "unlikely" facts). What I want them to feel in their gut is (a) how seductive the multiplication is, and (b) how catastrophically it breaks when the events are secretly correlated.

So the core mechanic should be something like: participants estimate or are given individual probabilities, compute joint probabilities under the independence assumption, stake points on the outcome, and then get burned because you rigged the setup so the events were dependent -- and then ideally a second phase where independence actually holds so they learn the move isnt always wrong, just unjustified without an argument. Concrete rules, materials, the payoff scheme (no real money, but there should be some real sting to losing), and how you hide the dependence so it doesn't get spotted.

Then the debrief: map what happened in each phase onto how the presumption of independence functions in actual heuristic arguments, including the uncomfortable bit -- in the game you can reveal the hidden correlation afterwards, but in real heuristic arguments nobody ever gets to check, so say explicitly which feature of the epistemic situation the game can't reproduce and what I have to supply by talking. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (someone will accuse me of cheating when the dependence is revealed, for starters) and how to handle it.
