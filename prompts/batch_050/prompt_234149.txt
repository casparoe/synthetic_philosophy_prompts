Next month I'm recording an episode of my podcast on societal "collapse", and my guest is an archaeologist whose new book argues that collapse, as it's usually understood, basically doesn't happen. Her line, roughly: the canonical cases are all misdescribed. The Classic Maya "collapse" was the fall of particular dynasties and the abandonment of certain southern lowland cities, but Maya people, languages, and farming carried on regardless -- and millions of people speak Mayan languages today, so who exactly collapsed? On Rapa Nui, she follows the recent reassessments that push the big population crash to after European contact, with rats and disease doing more damage than any self-inflicted ecocide. And the Late Bronze Age "collapse" around 1200 BCE was, on her telling, the end of an interlinked palace economy in the eastern Mediterranean, after which ordinary village life arguably carried on or even got better without the tax collectors and scribes. Her preferred frame is transformation and adaptation, and she thinks "collapse" is a moralising label later writers stick on societies that were really just changing -- a projection of our own anxieties onto the past.

Draft ten interview questions for this conversation. I don't want her just restating the book. Concretely, I'd like:

- at least one question on the definitional move: the standard working definition of collapse is something like a rapid, significant loss of sociopolitical complexity, and it seems like she might be winning her argument just by insisting on a different definition -- press her on whether the disagreement is empirical or merely terminological
- at least one on perspective and scale: collapsed for whom? The end of a palace bureaucracy or a divine kingship might be a catastrophe for elites and a Tuesday for farmers -- but then is her view just "elites collapse, peasants transform"?
- at least one on Norse Greenland, which strikes me as the hardest case for her: a settlement that really did vanish entirely, people and all. If she concedes that one as a genuine collapse, what distinguishes it from the cases she re-describes?
- at least one on the evidential problem: the archaeological record massively over-represents monumental and elite stuff, so when temples and inscriptions stop, we infer decline -- but does that cut in her favour or against her?
- at least one on whether "resilience" and "transformation" language romanticises real suffering: megadroughts, famine, abandoned cities presumably didn't feel like adaptation to the people living through them, and I'm worried her framework airbrushes that
- and end with a question about what evidence could actually count against her view -- I want to pin down whether there's a possible finding (say, ancient DNA showing genuine regional population crashes coinciding with the "transformations") that would make her concede the word collapse earnt its keep somewhere

For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give -- she's slippery and very good at answering the question she wishes she'd been asked, so the follow-ups matter more than the openers. Please write the questions and follow-ups in British English, and keep them in a register that works spoken aloud, not like exam questions.
