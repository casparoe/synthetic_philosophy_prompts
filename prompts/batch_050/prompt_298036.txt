I am to give a single fifty-minute guest lecture in a second-year undergraduate seminar in philosophical logic. The students are fluent in propositional logic and in elementary probability up through conditional probability and the law of total probability; they have encountered the material conditional and its so-called paradoxes, but they have never seen a formal treatment of indicative conditionals. My assigned topic is Lewis's triviality results: the demonstration that there can be no proposition-forming conditional whose probability, for every antecedent and consequent and across a sufficiently rich class of probability functions, equals the corresponding conditional probability — on pain of collapsing all probability judgments about the consequent to independence from the antecedent.

Plan the lecture rather than delivering it. Specifically:

First, lay out the explanatory arc as an ordered dependency structure. I want to know what must already be in place before the result can even be stated intelligibly: the distinction between the probability of a conditional and a conditional probability; the datum that competent speakers assess indicatives by the latter; the hypothesis that some genuine, truth-evaluable conditional matches this assessment; and the modest-looking premises the proof actually needs. For each item, say why it cannot be introduced before its predecessor, and flag any place where a tempting shortcut in the ordering would produce an illusion of understanding.

Second, design the one carrying example. Specify what it must have (a probability space small enough to compute by hand at the board; an antecedent whose learning visibly changes the probability of the consequent, so that the triviality consequence is shocking rather than abstract) and what it must lack (any feature that invites counterfactual readings, causal intuitions, or disputes about background beliefs). Explain why a generic example fails where this one succeeds.

Third, give me the misconception map: the confusions these students will predictably form, in the order they will form them, and the point in the arc where each should be headed off. At minimum, address the conflation of the two quantities the entire result keeps apart; the reading of the theorem as showing that indicative conditionals are meaningless rather than that one particular hypothesis about them fails; the assumption that the proof smuggles in a strong semantic theory of the connective; and the confusion about whose view the result actually pressures.

Fourth, specify the check for understanding: a single question or task, answerable in five minutes at the end, whose successful completion would show the students have grasped the argument's structure — for instance, by identifying which premise a proposed escape route must give up — rather than merely its conclusion.

Finally, tell me what to refuse to cover at this level, and how to say so honestly without foreclosing the students' further interest.
