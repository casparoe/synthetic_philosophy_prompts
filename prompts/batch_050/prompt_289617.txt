Okay so I'm a PhD student (my dissertation is on discounting and long-term climate policy, so adjacent to population ethics but not squarely in it) and I've been voluntold to run one session of my department's undergrad seminar on intergenerational ethics. The session is on the nonidentity problem. The students are mostly third-years; they've done a bit of intro ethics (utilitarianism vs. deontology, trolley stuff) but nothing on population ethics, and honestly half of them signed up for the climate angle.

I want a teaching plan, not an explanation of the nonidentity problem itself -- I more or less know the material, what I don't trust myself on is the pedagogy, since I've only ever talked about it with people who already get it.

Here's what I specifically want from you. Act as someone who's taught this topic to undergrads many times and has seen every way it goes wrong. Think carefully and brainstorm a bit before settling on the plan.

1. The order of ideas. What has to be in place before the nonidentity problem is even statable? I have a sense that you need the students to first be comfortable with a certain way of individuating people across possible futures, and maybe also with the idea that an act can be wrong without being wrong for anyone, but I'm not sure what order to build those in or whether something else has to come first. Lay out the dependency structure and justify the sequencing -- like, why can't I just open with the problem itself?

2. The carrying example. I need one case that does the whole job. My instinct is to use the two climate/energy policy case (the one where we choose between a cheap dirty policy and an expensive clean one, and after a few centuries the dirty one leaves people worse off, but different people exist under each policy), but I'm worried it's actually a bad first example because the scale is huge and the causal story is diffuse, and students might get lost in the policy details. Alternatively there's the more personal-scale cases. Tell me which example to build the session on, what it has to have, what it must NOT have (e.g. I'm guessing any whiff of "the child could have existed differently" talk is poison at first), and if the climate case is better saved for later in the session, say when to bring it in.

Some I expect: (a) "the future people could just have been born anyway" (a flat-footed modal mistake), (b) "so nothing we do to future generations can be wrong, awesome" (overgeneralizing), (c) quietly sliding into talking about the people who WOULD have existed under the other policy as if they're victims, (d) "isn't this just saying the end justifies the means." 5. What to deliberately leave out at this level -- e.g. I'm assuming I should NOT get into person-affecting vs impersonal views as a taxonomy, or the repugnant conclusion, or attempts to solve it -- and tell me what to say instead when a student pushes in that direction, so I don't just stonewall them.
