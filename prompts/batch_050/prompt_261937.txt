I chair the five-person Continuity Review Panel of a lunar mining consortium whose crews travel by matter-stream teleportation: a scanner reads the traveler's body and destroys it, and a physically indistinguishable successor is assembled at the destination from local stock, psychologically continuous with the traveler down to the last intention and memory. Consortium policy treats the successor as the same person -- contracts, pay, seniority, and family status carry over -- and thousands of trips have proceeded on that basis. Last month a scanner malfunctioned. The successor of a crew member was assembled on the Moon as usual, but the original on Earth was not destroyed and remains fully alive. Both now insist they are the genuine crew member, and his spouse, his employer, and the executor of his estate all need a ruling. Earth-original argues that the malfunction merely exposes what was always true: he never travels, he dies, and the panel has been administratively papering over thousands of quiet deaths. Moon-successor argues that he carries every memory, project, and commitment of the man who stepped into the scanner, that everyone accepted his identity in all prior cases, and that a one-off accident cannot retroactively strip him of a self he fully possesses. A further complication: standing policy directs that a redundant original be retired, which would cost Earth-original his salary, residency, and arguably his life as he understands it. Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the panel's vote. It should rule on who, if either, is the continuing crew member, state what follows for the marriage, the employment contract, and the retirement order, and engage the strongest argument on the side it rejects rather than caricaturing it. Lead with the recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning -- no preamble and no restating of the facts.
