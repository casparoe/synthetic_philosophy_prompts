I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar (14 students, one 75-minute session) and we are about to start the unit on scientific realism. Before anyone reads Laudan or hears the phrase pessimistic meta-induction, I want them to feel its force from the inside. Design a structured in-class simulation that does this.

The core idea I have in mind: the class becomes a succession of scientific communities, each working at a different "era" of inquiry. Each era receives empirical data generated by a hidden process that only I (the facilitator) know, proposes the best theory it can -- including posits about unobservable entities, mechanisms, or structures that explain the data -- and writes that theory on the board under its era number. Then a "new instrument" becomes available (the data stream becomes richer or more precise in some way you specify), the next era gets to work, and the previous era's theory is overturned or drastically revised. By the final era, the board shows a graveyard of discarded theories, each of which was empirically successful in its day. The last group then faces the question: your theory fits everything we've seen so far -- so why should anyone think you've finally latched onto the truth rather than produced era five in the sequence?

Please specify:

1. The actual hidden data-generating process I should use. It needs to be something I can run live with dice, cards, or a prepared sequence, such that (a) each era's restricted data genuinely supports a confident theory with unobservable posits, (b) the enriched data from the "new instrument" undermines something central in the previous theory -- not just adds detail, and (c) there is in fact a true rule, known to me, that no era fully reaches. Explain why your chosen process reliably produces overturn rather than mere refinement, which is the crucial thing for the debrief.

2. Era structure: how many eras fit in the time, how many data points each era gets before it must commit to a theory, what exactly changes with each new instrument, and what counts as a theory being "discarded" versus "revised."

3. Roles and rules: how students are divided into era-teams, whether earlier eras may watch later ones work or must leave the room, who is allowed to speak when, and how you keep each era genuinely confident rather than playing along with what they suspect is coming.

4. A timeline for the full 75 minutes.

5. The debrief plan: how to walk from the board of discarded theories to the meta-inductive argument, and then to the main realist replies -- for instance, the claim that mature theories are not discarded wholesale but retain structure or specific posits that were doing genuine predictive work. Tell me which moments in the simulation to point back at for each reply, and how to run the exercise so that at least one posit visibly survives every era (so students can see what the selective realist is talking about rather than just hearing it).

6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- students guessing there is a trick and staying artificially skeptical, eras converging too fast, the hidden rule being too easy or too noisy -- and how to correct for each.

7. An honest note on where the analogy breaks: real theory change is not driven by a single hidden stochastic rule, discarded theories were often approximately rather than simply false, and the students' situation differs from ours in that I actually know the truth while nature tells no one. Say how I should acknowledge these disanalogies in the debrief without deflating the exercise.
