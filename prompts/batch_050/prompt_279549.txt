The question I set was: "State Russell's theory of descriptions. Show how it handles at least two of the puzzles Russell raises for definite descriptions, and explain how Russell would respond to Strawson's objection."

Here is the student's answer:

"Russell's theory of descriptions says that definite descriptions — phrases of the form 'the F' — are referring expressions whose semantic contribution to a sentence is the unique object satisfying the description. Their real logical form, however, is quantificational: 'The present King of France is bald' means that there is at least one King of France, at most one King of France, and whatever is a King of France is bald. Since there is no King of France, the sentence comes out false rather than meaningless, which solves the puzzle of how sentences about nonexistent things can be meaningful at all.

"The theory also explains informative identities. 'Scott is the author of Waverley' is analyzed as: someone wrote Waverley, at most one person wrote Waverley, and that person is identical with Scott. So the sentence is not the trivial claim that Scott is Scott; it conveys substantial information, which explains how George IV could know the first but wonder about the second.

"Third, the theory handles negation. 'The King of France is not bald' is ambiguous: on one reading it denies that there is a unique bald King of France, which is true; on the other it asserts that the unique King of France is non-bald, which is false. Russell's scope distinction between these readings is part of the theory's power.

"Strawson objected that 'The present King of France is bald' does not assert but presupposes the existence of a King of France, and that when uttered today the sentence is simply false, since its presupposition fails. Russell would reply that our intuitions about what feels odd in such utterances conflate the semantics of the sentence with the pragmatics of asserting something pointless; the sentence is false, and any reluctance to call it so is not a semantic fact."
