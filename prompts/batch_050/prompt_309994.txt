I am developing teaching materials for a graduate seminar on Levinas, and I want a piece I can hand out alongside the primary readings. Write a dialogue between two philosophers on an ethics committee convened by a (fictional) near-future hospital network. The committee must decide whether to deploy a conversational AI "companion" that sits with dying patients who have no visitors, listens to them, and responds to them through a screen that displays a rendered, expressive human face.

One character, Marguerite, is a careful Levinasian. She argues that what is wrong with the arrangement (if anything is) cannot be captured by talking about deception, consent, or outcomes, because what is at stake is whether the patient is ever placed in the presence of a face in Levinas's sense — that which commands and obligates prior to any contract or calculation. The other character, Daniel, is an equally careful critic of Levinas, sympathetic to Kantian and discourse-ethics approaches. He suspects the Levinasian vocabulary either disguises a familiar concern about authenticity and informed consent, or else commits its holder to claims he finds indefensible.

Both characters must be maximally competent and steelmanned. The dialogue must exhibit genuine back-and-forth: each speech should engage the specific point just made, concede where concession is due, and press where the other is weakest. In the course of the exchange, the two should work through at least the following pressure points, though in whatever order arises naturally:

1. Daniel presses that Levinas insists the face is not a phenomenon — not something seen, not a collection of features — so the Levinasian cannot object to the AI merely on the grounds that its face is rendered or simulated; if the face is an imperative rather than an appearance, the objection must lie elsewhere. Marguerite must give her best answer to this.
2. Marguerite presses that the face's command is bound up with vulnerability, mortality, and the possibility of suffering and murder, and asks whether an entity that cannot die, cannot be destitute, and cannot be harmed can face anyone. Daniel must give his best answer, whether by questioning the inference or by offering an account on which the patient's own face, not the machine's, is doing the ethical work.
3. The problem of the third party: Daniel argues that the hospital's situation is precisely one in which many others make claims on scarce resources, so the infinite, asymmetrical responsibility Levinas describes must give way to comparison, justice, and distribution — and that once it does, the Levinasian has no principled objection left to a cheap, tireless, always-available companion. Marguerite must respond without simply retreating to the priority of ethics over justice as a slogan.
4. Marguerite raises substitution and the idea of being a hostage to the other, and Daniel presses on whether this is a dangerous ideal to design institutions around — whether an ethics that glorifies self-emptying responsibility, when operationalized by a hospital, becomes a way of making patients' abandonment by actual humans feel acceptable.
5. Near the end, each character must state, in their own words, what they take to be the strongest point the other has made, and what would have to be true for them to change their mind.

No verbatim quotations from Levinas or anyone else; the characters should paraphrase and argue, not recite. Do not have either character cite page numbers or specific essays. The characters may name concepts (the face, illeity, the third, substitution) as terms of art.

Format the response with the following sections, in this order:

**Setting and Dramatis Personae** — roughly 80–120 words of stage-setting: the committee room, the decision pending, and one sentence of characterization per speaker (not a summary of their views).

**The Dialogue** — roughly 1,000–1,300 words, formatted as Marguerite: / Daniel: exchanges. Speeches should mostly be short-to-medium; avoid either character delivering more than two consecutive long monologues.

**Where It Bottoms Out** — roughly 150–200 words, written by you rather than the characters, identifying the single point of disagreement that proved irreducible in the dialogue and articulating it as precisely as possible, without declaring a winner.
