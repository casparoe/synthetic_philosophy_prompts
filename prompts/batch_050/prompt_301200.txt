So here's the deal. I'm a retired biochemist, spent forty years on protein folding, and only got into philosophy after my wife dragged me to a reading group. Lately I've been obsessed with this idea of "program equilibrium" -- Tennenholtz's thing, where in the prisoners dilemma two computer programs can read each other's source code before deciding, and suddenly cooperation becomes a stable equilibrium because each program can condition on the other's code.

But here's where my own thinking wandered off, and I haven't found anyone discussing this exact wrinkle: suppose the programs don't get to read each other's code perfectly. Suppose each program has a limited "reading budget" -- say it can only inspect some fixed number of lines of the opponent's code -- AND each program also has a "scrambling budget" it can spend to make parts of its own code harder to interpret. So there's now a meta-game: how much do you invest in reading versus scrambling versus just... cooperating or defecting? My intuition as a biologist is that this looks a lot like signal detection vs. camouflage in evolution, and those tend to spiral into arms races rather than settle into honest cooperation.

Write a dialogue between two people arguing about what this modified setup shows. One of them -- call her Margit -- thinks the modified version actually *strengthens* the case that rational agents converge on cooperation, because even partial code-reading is enough to sustain a "cooperate with anyone who provably cooperates with cooperators" strategy, the way you only need to verify a few key lines. The other -- call him Deleuze, no wait, call him Dev -- thinks the scrambling budget ruins everything, because now defectors have an incentive to look like cooperators up to whatever the reading budget can check, and you're back to the original dilemma plus an expensive arms race.

Both of them should be genuinely sharp. Dev shouldn't be a lazy pessimist and Margit shouldn't be a naive optimist. Have them actually engage with each other's arguments -- when one makes a point, the other should concede what's right about it before pushing back. And I'd like it if somewhere in the middle they disagree about whether the whole program equilibrium literature was ever really about *rationality* at all, or just about commitment devices wearing a trenchcoat. Around 600-800 words for the dialogue, maybe end without full agreement but with them having narrowed down exactly where their disagreement lies.
