I'm a security engineer, not an ML person. My working vocabulary is zero trust, assume breach, least privilege, separation of duties, insider threat programs, fail-safe defaults, the occasional canary token. Lately I've been reading about "AI control" — the research agenda (the Redwood Research crowd and others) that asks: can you still extract useful work from a powerful AI system if you assume from the start that it might be actively scheming against you? Their answer is a scaffolding of techniques: have a weaker but "trusted" model monitor the powerful "untrusted" model's proposed actions before they execute, route a sample of flagged actions to human auditors, fall back to the trusted model when things look dicey, plant "honeypot" tasks where misbehavior would be conspicuous, that kind of thing.

Here's my request. Translate the control agenda into my vocabulary, technique by technique, as faithfully as you can. Is untrusted monitoring just separation of duties with a junior reviewer, or is it closer to N-version programming where some of the versions might be colluding? Is defer-to-trusted a fail-safe default? Are honeypots canary tokens? Is the whole program just defense in depth for the case where your compiler might be malicious? For each mapping, tell me where it holds and where it starts to creak, and be honest about it — don't smuggle my security assumptions into the translation and then declare victory.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. What in the control literature has NO real analogue in classic security? Some candidates I've noticed: the "attacker" is also your entire workforce, and unlike an employee you can retrain it between incidents; you can sometimes look at its internals (interpretability), which is nothing like auditing a black-box binary; it can deliberately underperform on your tests so you underestimate it, which seems to break penetration testing as a methodology; and your monitoring tools are themselves neural nets that can be fooled rather than deterministic checkers that either match or don't. For each piece of residue, give me your verdict: is my security vocabulary missing a concept it ought to have, or are the control people rediscovering security 101 with scarier branding? I'm fluent on the security side and know the AI side only at the level of buzzwords (RLHF, evals, fine-tuning), so keep that half concrete.
