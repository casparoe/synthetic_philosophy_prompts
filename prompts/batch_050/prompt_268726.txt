I'm a third-year student taking a course in social epistemology, and I'm writing to get clearer on something that came up in our unit on testimony and recorded evidence.

The unit introduced the idea that photographic and audiovisual recordings historically served as an "epistemic backstop" for public discourse: even when testimony conflicted and trust was low, a recording could in principle settle what happened, and the mere possibility that a recording might exist constrained what people could plausibly deny. We then read about deepfakes, and the argument that once convincing synthetic video can be produced cheaply by anyone, this backstop collapses — any inconvenient footage can be waved away as fabricated, so recordings lose their power to constrain denial (what gets called the liar's dividend).

Here's my question. Given that the backstop is now gone, why do courts, journalists, and institutional fact-checkers continue to treat video footage as substantially stronger evidence than eyewitness testimony? If any recording might be a deepfake, then the rational response would seem to be to discount video evidence across the board — to treat a video of an event as roughly on a par with someone claiming they saw it, since both are now just reports that could have been manufactured. Isn't the continued institutional reliance on video evidence (body cameras, CCTV, bystander footage) just inertia — a failure to update on the new technological reality? And if so, what would a genuinely updated evidence policy look like: should institutions be moving to weight all sources by the credibility of the chain of testimony behind them, rather than by medium?

My assumption is that the epistemic force of recordings always rested on the practical impossibility of faking them convincingly, so once that impossibility disappeared the force should disappear with it. If there's a mistake in that reasoning, I'd like to understand exactly where it is.
