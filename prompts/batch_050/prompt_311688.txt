After forty years as a field biologist — seabirds mostly, with a long secondary acquaintance with farm dogs — I have retired and, to my surprise, become preoccupied with philosophy of mind. I have been reading C. Lloyd Morgan's An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (first published 1894), and I was struck by a remark in his Preface, where he recalls a correspondent's doubts about the so-called philosopher's chick:

"He especially adduced the instinctive knowledge of the cry of a hawk, as such, said to be shown by young turkeys in the absence of individual experience, as in his opinion an unwarrantable assumption. I therefore determined to carry out a series of observations for myself, especially as I wished to study association in young birds."

What interests me is the standard of evidence Morgan models here: a report attributes knowledge to an animal, a sceptic calls the attribution unwarranted, and Morgan's remedy is not argument but a series of observations designed to decide between the rich interpretation and a poorer one. I would like to test whether this standard can be applied consistently, because in my own notebooks I find cases where I honestly cannot tell whether I am observing understanding or merely a well-drilled associative habit. Below are five episodes, drawn from my field notes with identifying details altered. Please rank them from strongest to weakest evidence for genuine comprehension in the animal — as opposed to instinct, associative learning, or trial and error — judging each by the standard you take the passage above to exemplify. For every case, state (a) the strongest lower-level explanation the attribution of understanding must defeat, and (b) the single observation or simple manipulation, in the spirit of Morgan's own series of observations, that would most efficiently discriminate between the two.

1. Pen-reared turkey poults, never exposed to raptors, crouch and fall silent when a hawk-shaped cardboard silhouette is drawn overhead on a wire — but they do the same, at roughly half the rate, for a gull-shaped silhouette and for a drifting balloon.

2. A retired shepherd's border collie reliably retrieves the red item from a pile of miscellany on the command "fetch the red one," but fails when the items are rearranged, and retrieves any red object whatever — including a child's mitten he has never seen — so long as the pile keeps its customary layout.

3. Herring gulls drop whelks onto a single short stretch of tarmac road although equally hard shingle lies a few yards away, and within a season of the road being resurfaced with softer chip-seal, the dropping shifts to an untreated stretch further along.

4. A kea at an alpine refuge opened a spring-loaded feed bin after watching a keeper open it once, though the latch mechanism was concealed from the bird's viewpoint and the bird's first three attempts manipulated entirely different parts of the housing.

5. A pair of rooks refused for over a year to take food from a particular garden table where they had once encountered spoiled meat, while freely taking identical offerings from a second table two metres distant.

Present the ranking as a compact ordered list first, then no more than one short paragraph per case, and close with a sentence or two on whether the standard in the passage, strictly applied, leaves any of my five cases standing as evidence of understanding. Roughly 700 words in total, not counting the quoted passage.
