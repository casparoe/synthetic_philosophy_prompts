I've been reading alignment discussions for years, and I've never seen anyone squarely address what our actual training practices commit us to about what reward *is*. I'd like help reconstructing the implicit theory.

Standard RL theory treats reward as a designer-defined scalar: it specifies the objective, and the trained agent is just whatever the optimization process converges to. On this view, saying the agent "wants" reward is loose shorthand — the reward isn't something the agent experiences or pursues, it's the criterion by which we select among policies. But the alignment literature routinely slides into a second register: we worry about agents that hack or tamper with their reward, we discuss whether a sufficiently advanced system would resist having its reward function rewritten, and we design penalties to deter exactly this. All of that seems to presuppose that reward functions as an incentive the agent is responsive to from the inside — something closer to a motive than to a selection criterion. Meanwhile the engineering practice is casual in ways that sit oddly with the second register: we shape and anneal rewards mid-training, restart runs, merge or delete checkpoints, and apply millions of negative-gradient updates, without anyone even raising the question of what any of this is like for the system — a question that doesn't obviously parse under the first register.

There's a further complication from the neuroscience analogy this community likes to invoke. If the RL reward signal is analogous to dopaminergic reward prediction error, then the relevant literature suggests it tracks something like wanting or salience rather than liking — the hedonic component of reward dissociates from the learning signal, as shown by cases where the drive to pursue a reward intensifies while the pleasure of receiving it doesn't. If that's the right mapping, then reward magnitude and sign in an RL agent might tell us roughly nothing about valenced experience even in principle, and the whole question of whether large negative rewards constitute anything like suffering would need a different handle entirely.

So my questions: (1) Does the practice of penalizing reward tampering strictly presuppose that reward functions as motivation for the agent, or can it be fully justified in the pure selection-criterion register — and if the latter, how does the justification go? (2) Is the alignment discourse consistent across the two registers, or does it quietly rely on reward-as-experienced while officially endorsing reward-as-objective? (3) If reward is the analogue of prediction error rather than of pleasure, does *any* fact about an RL agent's possible suffering follow from facts about its reward stream — and if not, what would the right evidence even look like? (4) Suppose we took seriously the hypothesis that in sufficiently capable agents something like valence tracks the reward signal anyway. Which parts of standard practice — reward shaping, adversarial training, checkpoint deletion, KL penalties — would that actually force us to change, and which are untouched because they don't interact with valence even under that hypothesis? I'd like you to distinguish, throughout, between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with.
