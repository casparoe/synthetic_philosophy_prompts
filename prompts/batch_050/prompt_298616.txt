I run the public-participation office of a mid-sized city government, and after two well-publicized one-off citizens' assemblies our council has voted to make sortition-based bodies a standing instrument of local government. That decision has landed on my desk with essentially no doctrine attached: my staff now needs to decide, maybe eight or ten times a year, whether a given question should go to a citizens' assembly at all, and if so, how the lottery, the mandate, and the follow-through should be structured. After our last assembly was attacked simultaneously as unrepresentative (because half the invited people never responded) and as a rubber stamp (because council ignored its recommendation), I was venting to a colleague who studies democratic theory, and she pointed out that the academic literature on selection by lot — descriptive representation, stratified sampling, self-selection bias, the deliberation conditions that make a minipublic's output worth anything, the accountability gap when nobody voted for the deciders — contains a coherent set of commitments that nobody has ever translated into something a practitioner can run. Please act as a political theorist who has spent years advising governments on deliberative minipublics, and do that translation. Think carefully about which distinctions from the theory genuinely have to survive the simplification (my guess: the difference between a body that mirrors the population and one that merely samples it, and the difference between an advisory and a binding mandate) and which can safely be dropped for operational purposes.

Concretely, I want a reusable decision procedure my staff can execute in half a working day, with no training in political theory, for any proposal that lands on our desk. It should consist of questions to ask in a fixed order — for instance: is this decision the kind that sortition is even suited to (and what makes a question suitable or unsuitable — technical complexity, value conflict, time horizon, reversibility)? which stratification variables must the lottery control for on this issue, and how many is too many? how large a nonresponse rate breaks descriptive legitimacy, and what do we do about it? what must be true about information provision and expert testimony before deliberation starts? what mandate type (advisory, conditional, binding) is warranted, and what does council have to commit to in advance for each? For every question, state what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a firm no (sortition is the wrong instrument, full stop), and where the procedure must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of political values rather than design fact. Include the points where the procedure will predictably misfire and what a practitioner should do about that. Then run the finished procedure on two cases from our pipeline: siting a waste-transfer station (highly technical, intense geographic self-interest, our councilors would love to outsource this one) and redesigning the city's transit fare structure (distributive value judgments, a decision I personally want to send to an assembly — so if your procedure says otherwise, let it, and show me). End with an honest section on what this procedure cannot do no matter how well it is followed.

Please write the answer in American English, and structure it with these sections in this order, with rough lengths: "What the theory must keep" (one short paragraph on the commitments the procedure encodes), "The procedure" (the ordered questions with sufficiency thresholds and stop conditions — the core of the answer, as long as it needs to be), "Design knobs" (one paragraph on lottery size, stratification, compensation, and expert input, and how to set them), "Worked application: the waste-transfer station" (one paragraph walking the procedure), "Worked application: transit fares" (one paragraph, showing the verdict even where I won't like it), "Where judgment cannot be eliminated" (a short paragraph), and "What this procedure cannot do" (a short paragraph).
