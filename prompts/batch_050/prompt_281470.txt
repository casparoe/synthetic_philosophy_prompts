I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I am trying to settle a disagreement before it derails a reading group I run with some microbiology graduate students. The disagreement is about a paragraph from a trade science book one of them assigned. The paragraph runs as follows:

"Biologists used to think of organisms as individuals, but we now know that every plant and animal is really a holobiont: a host plus the trillions of microbes living in and on it. Because natural selection acts on the partnership as a whole, the holobiont -- not the host -- is the true unit of selection, and the human 'individual' has turned out to be a biological fiction. The precedent was there all along: mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria, so your body is simply a colony that has forgotten it is one, no different in principle from a Portuguese man o' war or an ant colony. Nor can host and microbes be pried apart: raise an animal germ-free and it dies, which shows the partnership is obligate. And because the microbiome is transmitted from parent to offspring along with the host's genes, the hologenome -- the combined genetic material of host and microbes -- is inherited as a single package and evolves as such. The upshot is that medicine should stop treating the human body as the patient and start treating the ecosystem."

My suspicion is that this passage is mostly competent popularization with a couple of quietly illicit moves baked in -- in particular, I think it slides between different notions of 'individual' and leans on claims about transmission and obligate dependence that are much weaker than stated -- but the microbiologists in my group largely find it unobjectionable, which makes me doubt myself. Here is what I would like from you:

1. Go through the passage claim by claim. For each, tell me whether a philosopher of biology working on biological individuality would accept it, accept it only under a substantial qualification, or reject it -- and flag anywhere the passage trades on an ambiguity (for instance, between an organism as a physiological/metabolic unit and an organism as an evolutionary individual, or between a community of interacting lineages and a genuine unit of selection).
2. Identify which parts a careful microbiologist (rather than a philosopher) would object to on empirical grounds, if any, and which are merely contested.
3. Say whether the passage's analogies do the work assigned to them. Does the endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria actually support the claim about bodies being colonies? Is a man o' war or an ant colony the right comparison class for a host-microbiome assemblage, and what disanalogies matter?
4. Finally, assess the conclusion. Suppose the accurate parts of the passage are all granted: does anything follow about what medicine should treat as 'the patient,' or does the practical upshot require additional premises the passage never supplies?

Please be explicit about where I am being too suspicious and where my reading group's complacency is the real problem. I would rather have the passage's strongest possible reading on the table than a demolition of a strawman.
