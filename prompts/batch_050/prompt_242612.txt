I am a novelist, and twice a year I lead a craft seminar for a small group of fiction writers -- mostly published, all competent, none trained in psychology or economics. Next month's session is on what I privately call the implausibly decisive character: the protagonist who weighs every option, correctly identifies the best one, and acts, because the author needed the plot to move. I want to use the session to teach the idea of bounded rationality and satisficing as a craft tool -- the notion that real agents decide under limits of time, information, and attention, and mostly search until something is good enough rather than optimal -- so that my students can write decisions that feel lived rather than engineered.

Do not give me the lecture; give me the plan for it. I want the explanatory arc: which idea has to be in place before boundedness can even register as a claim rather than a truism, and why (I suspect the ideal of the maximizing agent must be built up carefully first, because bounded rationality only has content against that background -- but tell me if I have the dependency wrong). Then the carrying example: one concrete choosing situation, taken from ordinary life rather than from a laboratory, that the whole session can return to. Tell me what it must contain (an aspiration level the audience can feel, a stopping point, real time pressure) and what it must lack, and say why a high-stakes dilemma like leaving a marriage would be a worse choice than something smaller, if you agree that it would be.

Then the misconception map. I expect, roughly in this order: that satisficing means settling for less and is therefore a character flaw; that bounded rationality is just a polite word for irrationality; that heuristics are merely biases, i.e., defects; and, from the sharper students, that a satisficing character is an inconsistent character and thus bad craft. For each, tell me where in the sequence it will naturally surface, and where I should head it off -- before it hardens, not after. If any of these is actually half-right, say so rather than designing me a rhetorical trap.

Finally: the check for understanding. I want one end-of-session exercise -- I am imagining each student rewriting a decision scene from their own work so that the character's choice exhibits a stopping rule without any narrator's commentary -- and a way to tell, from what they produce, whether the idea has landed or whether they have merely learned to make characters dither. And tell me what adjacent material (prospect theory, nudges, the full heuristics-and-biases literature) I should explicitly refuse to open, and how to say so without making the refusal feel like a gap.
