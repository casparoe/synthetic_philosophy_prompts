So I need some help and I'll try to explain this properly. I spent 35 years as an analytical chemist (mass spec, mostly polymers) and since retiring I've gotten into philosophy the way some guys get into golf -- slowly and with a lot of muttering. I also joined the collections committe of our little regional art museum because I missed arguing with people, and now I'm in over my head.

The situation: our star painting, on display since 1963, attributed to a Dutch Golden Age painter (I'll just call him V. in the memo). Last year we finally got samples tested and the lab found titanium dioxide in the white pigment. As I understand it that stuff didn't exist in artists' paint until the 1920s, so chemically speaking it's a twentieth-century forgery, case closed. Except the case isn't closed, because:

1. The donor's gift agreement says the painting stays on permanent display, and her grandson is on our board and takes this VERY seriously.
2. The brushwork apparently matches a fairly notorious forger whose works now have a market of their own -- we've had an informal offer in the low six figures, which would fund a genuine acquisition.
3. Half our docents want to keep it hanging with a big honest label that says FORGERY and turn it into an educational thing about fakes.

The chair asked me to write the memo recommending what we actually do, since I'm "the science guy" (their words -- the lab did the work, I just read the report, but fine). The options are basically: keep displaying it with an honest label, move it to storage and fight the donor clause, or sell it.

Here's where I want the memo to do real work. Our head docent made an argument I find annoyingly strong: the painting hasn't changed. Same canvas, same brushstrokes, visitors loved it in 2022 and they'd love it now if we'd never tested it. The lab report didn't chip any paint off. So what exactly was lost? I have a stubborn gut feeling that something WAS lost -- that a room full of people admiring a forgery they think is real are in a different situation than people admiring the same object knowing what it is -- but I'd like the memo to actually make that case rather than just assert it, and to say what it implies about why anyone should care about originals at all. (If it helps: I've been reading around and I gather philosophers disagree about whether authenticity has aesthetic value or just snob/historical value. I don't want a literature review, I want a decision.)

So: write the memo. Concrete recommendation the committee can vote on next month, including what to do about the donor clause, honest engagement with the strongest argument for whatever it recommends against, and under 600 words. And please don't make it sound like a chemistry paper -- I've written ten thousand of those and I'm trying to branch out.
