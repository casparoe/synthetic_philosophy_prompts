My reading group has been stuck on the same argument for two sessions now, and I want an outside verdict before we meet again. The topic is money pumps. The standard thought, as we understand it, is that someone with cyclic preferences — say, someone who prefers a mountain holiday to a seaside one, the seaside to the city, and the city to the mountains, because a different consideration (rest, novelty, cost) dominates each pairwise comparison — can be walked around a circle of trades, paying a small fee at each step, until they hold precisely what they started with, minus the fees. This is supposed to show that such preferences are irrational. My friend Marta thinks this argument proves too much, and her rebuttal runs as follows. (1) A pattern of preferences counts as irrational only if it renders you exploitable: there must exist a series of deals, each acceptable to you given those preferences, that returns you to your starting position minus resources — worse off by your own lights. (2) "Acceptable to you" must mean acceptable to you at the moment you face the deal; a person cannot be held answerable to preferences they no longer have, any more than to preferences they never had. (3) But then exploitability condemns anyone whose preferences change in a predictable direction, since a counterparty need only schedule the deals so that each successive version of you is met with a trade that version welcomes. Her example: her uncle's taste in tea rotates with the seasons — each spring he prefers sencha to the jasmine he owns and pays £5 to swap, each summer he prefers oolong to the sencha and pays £5 again, each autumn he prefers jasmine to the oolong and pays £5 once more — and by the following spring he holds his original tin, £15 poorer, with a merchant who can plan the whole itinerary a year in advance. (4) Yet predictable preference change of this sort is ubiquitous and frequently beyond reproach: tastes mature, and nearly everyone weights the near future in a way their later selves predictably reverse. (5) A criterion of rationality that condemns rational patterns wholesale indicts itself rather than them, so (1) fails: being pumpable is no mark of irrationality. (6) But the money pump was the only non-question-begging ground for demanding transitivity in the first place — everything else, she says, just asserts that cyclic choice "looks wrong." (7) So the holiday planner stands vindicated, and the claim that rationality requires transitive preferences is superstition dressed up as logic.

Nobody in the group will sign the conclusion, but nobody has pinned down the misstep either, and I should say that (4) and (5) are the claims I would most like to keep — I share Marta's suspicion of any standard of rationality that no actual person meets. So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent in context, and what does rejecting it cost? I would especially like to know whether your diagnosis turns on what (2) does to the phrase "by your own lights" in (1) — several of us feel the meaning of that phrase drifts somewhere in the first three steps, but we cannot make the drift precise — and whether your answer leaves any money-pump-style argument standing against the holiday planner, or whether that argument must fall together with Marta's rebuttal.
