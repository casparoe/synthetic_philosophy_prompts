As a novelist at work on a novella, I would like your help with a piece of philosophical fiction. Please take on the role of a literary fiction writer who also happens to be a professional political theorist, and think carefully before you draft. The concept I want the story to engage is the democratic boundary problem: every democratic decision presupposes a demos, a settled answer to the question of who counts as a member, yet the membership question itself cannot be settled democratically without either circularity or infinite regress, since one must first decide who gets to vote on who gets to vote. Competing principles pull in different directions here — the all-affected-interests principle enfranchises everyone whose interests are touched by a decision, while the all-subjected (or all-coerced) principle enfranchises only those bound by the polity's coercive authority — and in hard cases they deliver sharply different electorates.

The setting I have in mind is Sennevåg, a storm-battered tidal island commune reachable by causeway only at low tide, governed under an old charter requiring that any measure binding residents be ratified by "all who dwell within the seawall." The assembly is now debating whether to extend the seawall and divert the river that feeds it, and to do so it must first conduct a founding referendum on a new charter. But candidacy for that referendum is bitterly contested: the seasonal fishers who winter on the island and their children born mid-storm; the comatose founding member whose sister holds her proxy; the mainland farmers downstream whose water the diversion would take; and the caretaker who has lived there thirty years without ever being inscribed on the rolls. Write a short story of roughly 900 to 1,200 words set during the assembly meeting that must fix the referendum's electorate. Dramatize the regress concretely — the assembly cannot vote on the electorate without already having one — and let at least two characters embody, through their interests and arguments rather than through lecture, the affected-interests and subjected-to-coercion views. Avoid tidy resolution; the story should end with a decision taken and a quiet sense of its arbitrariness, or with the regress visibly unclosed. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm two or three distinct ways the regress could be enacted in scene, and state in one or two sentences why you chose the one you used.

After the story, append a short afterword (around 150 words) addressed to me as the commissioning novelist: indicate where the story takes liberties with the philosophical debate or leans on a contested reading of it, state how confident you are that the story faithfully represents the boundary problem rather than a simpler problem of immigration or inclusion, and single out the single point in your own treatment you are least sure of, with a candid explanation of why. Finally, because I am cataloguing these stories for a collection, close your entire response with one last line beginning "Summary:" followed by a single sentence capturing what your story ultimately suggests about who belongs to the demos.
