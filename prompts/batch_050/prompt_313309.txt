I sit on my university's academic integrity committee, and we've got a case that's split us right down the middle. A senior turned in a history paper with two citations to journal articles that don't exist and a very confident statistic (something like "French railroad mileage tripled between 1850 and 1870") that is just flat-out false. When she was called in, she admitted she drafted the paper with an AI chatbot, said the citations "looked real," and swears she never knowingly invented anything. Our code has three relevant bins: fabrication (deliberately inventing sources or data, usually means suspension), reckless disregard for accuracy (sanction at the instructor's discretion), and honest error (no violation, just a lousy grade). Half the committee says the chatbot did the fabricating and she merely failed to catch it, so its negligence at worst. The other half says that submitting text you never checked is worse than negligence -- you're passing on claims with total indifference to whether they're true, and that's arguably what the fabrication category exists to punish.

Draft the written opinion I want to circulate before Thursday's vote. It needs an actual bottom line up front: recommend one of the three categories, or argue the code needs a fourth one and say what it should be called and how it differs. To get there, I think the opinion has to classify what the chatbot's output even is -- a lie, bullshit in Frankfurt's sense (indifference to truth as opposed to an intent to deceive), or plain error like a miscalibrated scale spitting out bad numbers -- because how we grade her culpability seems to depend on what, exactly, she relayed. But one of my colleagues insists the machine's status is irrelevant and only her state of mind matters, so a fabrication charge can't be built out of what the software did. He's not obviously wrong, and the opinion has to take his objection apart seriously rather than waving at it.

You're basically playing the committee member everyone stares at when things get philosophical, so take your time with this -- before committing to a recommendation, work through the strongest version of the case for each of the three categories (and for the new-category option), then write the opinion as a real committee document, a page or so, that a split group could actually vote on. One thing I care a lot about: be honest about which parts of this are settled among people who work on lying and bullshit and which parts are still genuinely contested -- don't write as if Frankfurt's account, or any rival account of hallucination, is received wisdom if it isn't. And keep it philosophy, not tech commentary: I don't care whether "AI" can "lie" in the sci-fi sense, I care which category of speech act a confident hallucinated citation is, and what follows for the person who signs their name to it.
