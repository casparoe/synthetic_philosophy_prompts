So I chair the terminology committee for a mid-size industry standards body -- we maintain the controlled glossary for a data-interchange standard used in health records, and every term in it is referenced by hundreds of downstream systems, court filings, and vendor contracts. Here's our recurring nightmare: somebody proposes revising a definition (recent fights: whether "residence" requires intent to remain, whether "caregiver" covers unpaid family members, whether a diagnosis timestamp means when the clinician decided or when it got entered). Half the committee says the revision just updates our beliefs about what the term covers, so it's a minor editorial patch. The other half says it changes what the term *means*, which under our charter triggers a full re-review of every document that cites it -- months of work and legal sign-off. And I keep realizing this is exactly the analytic/synthetic mess from Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," which I read in college: if there's no principled line between meaning-claims and belief-claims, then my committee's whole patch-vs-re-review distinction might be built on sand. Except I can't just dissolve the distinction, because our charter requires it.

So: write me a procedure my committee can actually run, in a single two-hour meeting, to classify a proposed definitional revision as meaning-change or belief-change. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of language genuinely has to offer here -- synonymy, analyticity, Quine's objections to the whole distinction, whatever survives scrutiny -- but translated into ordered tests a smart non-philosopher can apply (e.g., something like: would the old sentence come out contradictory, would a competent speaker be confused or just wrong, that kind of thing -- you tell me which tests are respectable and which are vibes). Be explicit about which parts of the procedure are real distinctions and which are judgment calls we've dressed up as procedure, because Quine's whole point is that the line may not exist, and I'd rather know where the procedure is going to misfire. Then run it on the three disputes above -- and I want at least one case where the procedure gives an answer that's politically painful for us, i.e. classifies a popular small tweak as a full meaning-change or vice versa. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't settle and what I should put in the charter to handle those cases.

Answer in American English, and keep it usable -- this gets stapled into our committee handbook, not submitted to a journal.
