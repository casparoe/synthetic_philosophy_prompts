Here are my intuitions about rescue choices, and I would like to know what principle about aggregation, if any, lies behind them. In each case I am the only rescuer, everyone at risk is a stranger to me, and I cannot reach both groups.

First case: I can save one person from drowning or, at equal effort, save five people from drowning elsewhere. I should save the five, and I don't think I owe anyone a coin flip. Second case: I can save one person or one other person. Here I do think I should flip a coin rather than pick on any basis of my own; each is owed an equal chance. Third case: I can save five or save six, all facing the same harm. My honest verdict is that flipping a coin between the groups is perfectly acceptable — the extra person doesn't seem to impose the kind of decisive reason the five had against the one. Fourth case: I can save one person from death or save a thousand people from losing a finger. I should save the one from death; no number of fingers seems to outweigh a life. Fifth case: I can certainly save one person, or attempt a rescue with a 60% chance of saving five and a 40% chance of saving no one. I should attempt the rescue of the five. Sixth case: a trapped miner, identified and on the news, can be rescued at enormous cost; the same money spent on mine safety would, over a decade, prevent the statistical deaths of several anonymous miners. I feel the community is permitted — maybe even required — to save the trapped miner, even knowing the numbers favor the safety program.

Some things I suspect but am not sure of: my second and third cases together suggest that what I owe people is chances rather than lives saved, yet my first case denies a coin flip exactly where equal chances would give the lone person a one-in-six shot at survival, which seems to treat his claim as counting for nothing. And my sixth case seems to reward identifiability, which I suspect is morally arbitrary.

Is there one coherent principle about whether and how the numbers count that delivers all six verdicts — for instance, one that weights claims by the seriousness of what each person stands to lose, gives each person a chance proportional to that claim, and yet still makes saving the greater number mandatory when the conflict is between equally serious claims? If a single principle can't do all this, tell me which verdict you would overturn first and why. Please also state what your proposed principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a rescue where I can save one person or give a 50% chance of survival to each of two (who otherwise both die for certain), and a case where the one person and the five people face different harms — death for the one, paralysis for the five.
