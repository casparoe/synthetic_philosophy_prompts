I work as a software engineer, with no formal training in philosophy, and I recently spent two days debugging a nondeterministic crash that turned out to be caused by a custom comparator that wasn't transitive: it ranked A above B, B above C, and C above A depending on which elements it happened to see first. Fixing it got me thinking about whether something analogous could afflict evaluative comparisons rather than code. From my limited reading I gather that so-called spectrum arguments press exactly this point: you construct a long sequence of options where each neighbouring pair differs only minutely — a slightly worse headache, a marginally shorter life, a trivially smaller population at higher welfare — and at each step it seems obvious which of the pair is better, yet the endpoints of the sequence seem clearly ranked the opposite way. Some philosophers apparently conclude that 'better than, all things considered' can be non-transitive; others insist one of the premises must give.

Please act as a professional philosopher specialising in value theory and the formal structure of preference, and think carefully before answering. Begin with a brief brainstorm of the main escape routes available to someone who wants to keep transitivity, then settle on your analysis.

My own credence that all-things-considered 'better than' is genuinely transitive is about 0.9, though I admit this is partly motivated reasoning — I want comparators to be well-behaved. Give me your credence, but more importantly decompose it: the probability that at least one well-constructed spectrum argument has all its pairwise premises true; the probability that adjacent pairs are genuinely comparable at all, rather than merely indistinguishable or roughly equal; the probability that 'roughly equal' or 'on a par' relations rescue consistency without collapsing into indifference; and whatever other terms you think belong. Assign a rough probability to each with one line of justification, show how they combine, then tell me which term my 0.9 and your number most likely diverge on, and what argument or result would move it most.
