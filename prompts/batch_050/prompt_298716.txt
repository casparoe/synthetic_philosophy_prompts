I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized tech company, and I recently got into a drawn-out dispute with a teammate that left me genuinely puzzled. During a discussion about why so few women apply for our infrastructure and embedded systems roles, he argued that the explanation is simply natural: men and women, on average, just have different interests, and no amount of outreach or pipeline effort will change the numbers much. I pushed back with what I thought were strong counterarguments — studies about how interests are shaped by expectations, evidence that the gender ratio in our field has shifted dramatically over the decades, examples of women leaving because of workplace culture rather than lack of aptitude. Here is the strange part: every argument I dismantled seemed to make him more confident, not less. Each time I refuted one of his points, he produced a new one, and by the end he told me the whole exchange had only confirmed that he must be onto something deeper that my arguments couldn't touch.

Around the same time, I happened to be reading John Stuart Mill's essay The Subjection of Women, and one passage in the first chapter stopped me cold:

"So long as an opinion is strongly rooted in the feelings, it gains rather than loses in stability by having a preponderating weight of argument against it. For if it were accepted as a result of argument, the refutation of the argument might shake the solidity of the conviction; but when it rests solely on feeling, the worse it fares in argumentative contest, the more persuaded its adherents are that their feeling must have some deeper ground, which the arguments do not reach; and while the feeling remains, it is always throwing up fresh intrenchments of argument to repair any breach made in the old."

I'd like help thinking through three things, in American English please. First, interpret the passage: what exactly is Mill's diagnosis of why refuting someone's arguments can entrench their belief, and what does he mean by a conviction resting on feeling rather than argument? Second, apply it: does my teammate's appeal to what is "natural" for men and women fit Mill's pattern — and is it significant that Mill develops this diagnosis specifically in an essay about the legal and social subordination of women, where appeals to nature were the standard defense of the status quo? Third, advise me practically: if Mill is right, what should someone in my position actually do? Does winning the argument on the merits matter at all in a case like this, and what are the limits of Mill's diagnosis — in particular, how could I check whether my own conviction on this topic is rooted in argument rather than in the kind of feeling Mill describes?
