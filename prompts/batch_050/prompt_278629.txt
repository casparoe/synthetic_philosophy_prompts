Next week I'm taping an episode of my little classics-and-philosophy podcast with a scholar whose new book makes a pretty bold claim: the long, famously weird discussion of wine-drinking in Books 1 and 2 of Plato's Laws isn't an embarassing digression or just local Athenian color, but a serious piece of moral psychology — she calls it "assisted training in shame." Her pitch, roughly: the Athenian stranger thinks courage gets trained by controlled exposure to danger, and self-control (sophrosyne) deserves the same treatment. Wine is the controlled danger. It lowers inhibitions in a supervised setting, under a sober symposiarch, so people practice resisting their impulses at exactly the moment resistance is hardest; and because drink loosens the tongue, it doubles as a diagnostic — you learn who someone is over wine faster than over years of ordinary company. She contrasts all this with the Spartan-style total prohibition, which on her reading Plato treats as leaving citizens untested against pleasure, and she leans hard on the puppet image from Laws I — the soul yanked by iron cords of appetite and one golden cord of reason, which wine slackens on purpose.

I need eight to ten interview questions that go beyond letting her summarize the book. Some things I want to hit:

- At least one question pressing the analogy between courage-training and shame-training: is resisting temptation while impaired actually practice for resisting it sober, or does impairment change the thing you're resisting? She'll almost certainly say that's the whole point — you train the disposition under load, like a resistance band — so give me a follow-up keyed to that answer.
- One question on the diagnostic claim: does wine reveal character or create it? If a basically decent man turns cruel after three kraters, what exactly did we learn about the sober one?
- One on the politics: the Laws symposium is compulsory-ish, tightly policed, and chaired by an elder. What's the difference, for her Plato, between this and just having a well-run drinking culture? Does the argument quietly depend on trusting whoever's holding the golden cord?
- One on the Republic: elsewhere Plato seems pretty down on the appetitive soul and doesn't propose wine-drills for the guardians. Is she saying the Laws corrects the Republic, or that the two works answer different questions? Anticipate her likely answer and write the follow-up.
- And one about the weirdest detail: in Laws II the drinking chorus is for the old, while the young are kept more or less sober. If wine is moral training equipment, why do the people with the least formation left to do get the most wine? That seems backwards, so I want her actual story for it.

No softballs about what drew her to Plato. Assume she's read the skeptical secondary literature and has polished answers ready — I want the questions that make the polished answers do real work.
