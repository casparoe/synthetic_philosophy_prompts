Last weekend I got into a genuinely annoying three-hour argument with my friend Dani (we've had a running epistemology dispute since undergrad, this is not the first time) about the regress problem, and it ended the way it always ends: both of us restating our opening positions louder. She's a coherentist, I keep flirting with foundationalism, and at one point we both agreed infinitism at least deserves a seat at the table even if neither of us buys it. What got me is that none of the moves were new. Regress argument, myth of the given, the circle-is-too-small objection, the circle-is-too-big objection, Peter Klein's thing about how an infinite chain isn't vicious -- we've all heard them a hundred times, nobody in the literature seems to have changed sides in decades, and neither did we. So my actual question isn't who's right, it's: what kind of disagreement is this, that it can run for a century with every argument already on the table?

Here's my working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed: the three camps aren't answering the same question. Foundationalists are asking what the structure of justification has to be if anything is justified at all, coherentists are really describing how belief systems hold together and get revised, and infinitists are talking about what an ideal reasoner could always keep doing -- and the debate stalls because "justification" is doing three jobs under one name. Dani says no, it's a real first-order disagreement about whether a circle of reasons can justify, full stop. Which of us is diagnosing this right?

The reason I keep coming back to framing is Aristotle. In the Posterior Analytics (Book I, in the old Mure translation I found online) he lays down that "The premisses must be primary and indemonstrable; otherwise they will require demonstration in order to be known, since to have knowledge, if it be not accidental knowledge, of things which are demonstrable, means precisely to have a demonstration of them." Read that closely and it seems like the whole modern trilemma -- terminate, loop, or run forever -- is already baked into one sentence: if it's demonstrable, you only know it by demonstrating it, so knowledge needs indemonstrable starting points. Is the modern debate just living inside Aristotle's assumption that justification is a chain that has to do *something* at the end, and is the real disagreement actually about that sentence rather than about chains vs circles? Or is he just baldly stipulating foundationalism there and everyone since has (maybe without noticing) accepted the stipulation as the problem set-up? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress -- a distinction everyone would have to accept, a formal result, an agreed test case -- or argue honestly that nothing would. And please say how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure about, because I fully intend to quote you at Dani and I'd rather know in advance where you're shaky.
