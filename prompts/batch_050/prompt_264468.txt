I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working up a piece on whether evolution debunks morality. Every popular treatment I read blurs into the next, so I want to force the actual reasoning out into the open instead of vibes.

Here's the claim I want your credence on (a number between 0 and 1): even if objective moral truths exist, natural selection shaped our core moral convictions -- don't harm kin, repay favors, suffering matters, outsiders are suspect -- by tracking reproductive fitness rather than moral truth, so we have no decent reason to think those convictions are even roughly accurate.

Decompose it for me: the probability that selection pressures on our moral psychology were in fact largely indifferent to moral truth; the probability that the realist escape routes (third-factor accounts, the lucky-harmony response, whatever you want to call them) fail; the probability the argument doesn't saw off the branch it sits on by taking down our epistemic and normative beliefs along with the moral ones; plus any other term you think the calculation actually needs. One line of justification per term, then how they combine into the final number.

One wrinkle I havent seen hashed out: does your number move if the debunking target is narrowed to our judgments about which beings count morally -- animals, future people, strangers -- instead of the harm-and-reciprocity basics? If yes, say which term absorbs the change and by roughly how much.

Then tell me which term in your decomposition you're least confident in, and what argument or piece of evidence (from evolutionary psychology, philosophy, wherever) would shift it the most in either direction.

Format: exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion.
