I've been a LessWrong reader for years, and like many people there I've absorbed the standard deceptive alignment story: a sufficiently capable system behaves compliantly while it is overseen and weak, because defection would be punished, and defects only once oversight can no longer constrain it — the treacherous turn. Recently, while arguing about this with friends, someone claimed the concept is really just an old pattern from human politics dressed up in ML vocabulary, and proposed Stalin's rise as the canonical case. I want to stress-test that claim properly rather than let it stand as a dinner-party analogy, so I'm asking for a disciplined analysis of Stalin's consolidation of power between roughly 1922 and 1929 through the lens of deceptive alignment.

Here are the salient facts as I understand them, so the analysis doesn't hinge on obscure details: Stalin was made General Secretary in 1922, a post widely regarded at the time as drab administrative housekeeping, and used it to control party appointments and personnel files. Lenin, in his final dictated notes, warned about Stalin's accumulation of authority and recommended removing him from the General Secretaryship; after Lenin's death these notes were read only to closed sessions of the 1924 party congress and effectively shelved. Stalin then played his rivals off in sequence — siding with Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky, then turning on the United Opposition, then breaking with Bukharin and the right — and exploited the 1921 ban on factions to paint each successive opponent as a splitter. By the time anyone seriously tried to remove him, the people who would have had to vote for his removal owed their positions to him.

What I want: first, a careful mapping. What plays the role of the principal here (the party? Lenin? the Central Committee?), what plays the role of the oversight regime, what corresponds to capability accumulation during the compliant phase, and is there any identifiable moment that functions like deployment — a point after which removal costs became prohibitive? Second, an honest account of where the model breaks: human power-seeking is transparently motivated and legible in ways a neural network's goals allegedly aren't, there is no clean training/deployment boundary, the 'principal' is diffuse and factional, and Stalin was arguably never aligned with anyone to begin with — does that make this a case of deceptive alignment at all, or just ordinary ambition? Third, the reverse direction: does the existence of a well-documented human precedent for patient power accumulation through boring institutional infrastructure strengthen the treacherous turn argument, or does it reveal that the concept was covertly importing human political intuitions all along and tells us less about novel AI failure modes than its proponents think? If some other framework — principal-agent theory, coup-proofing, selectorate dynamics — explains this episode more cleanly, say so, and say what the deceptive alignment framing adds that they don't, if anything.
