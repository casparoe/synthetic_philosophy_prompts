My advisor teaches Du Bois's "Criteria of Negro Art" (the 1926 Crisis essay) every semster and always makes a huge deal of the line "The thing we are talking about tonight is part of the great fight we are carrying on and it represents a forward and an upward look -- a pushing onward." I'm trying to figure out what she actually means by it, because her taste in novels doesn't obviously line up. Her verdicts, as I've tallied them: despises Van Vechten's Nigger Heaven; also despises McKay's Home to Harlem, even though its author is Black; admires Larsen's Quicksand, which is bleak and has zero uplift; loves Toomer's Cane, which is basically message-less; and is lukewarm on Fauset's Plum Bun, the most earnestly uplifting book of the bunch ("admirable propaganda, thin art"). Now there's a new debut novel by a Black writer, gorgeously written, about colorism and grifting inside a wealthy Black family -- nobody admirable in it, no uplift anywhere -- and white critics have embraced it as confirming exactly what they already believed, though the author keeps saying she wrote it for Black readers. Predict my advisor's verdict, tell me which rule she's really running (art must serve the fight as propaganda vs. something more like refusing to perform for the white gaze), and give me the case, real or invented, that would settle which it is. Also: whichever way she goes, what does she have to think "part of the great fight" means?
