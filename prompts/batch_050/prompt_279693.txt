I'm drafting the opening section of a paper on causation and moral responsibility, and before the argument proper is allowed to lean on my starting commitments, I want those commitments audited for joint tenability. Here they are, as they currently stand:

1. Omissions can be causes, full stop. When the gardener whose job it is to water the flowers fails to do so, and the flowers die, his failure caused their death. This is literally true, not shorthand for some more respectable claim about the soil, the heat, or the season.

2. The same does not hold of everyone else who failed to water them. The Queen's not watering my flowers caused nothing, and neither did my neighbor's. Nor can the difference be sorted out counterfactually: had the Queen watered them, they would have lived, just as they would have had the gardener done so. The difference is that the gardener, unlike the Queen, was supposed to.

3. Causation is a wholly natural, mind-independent relation — the sort that holds between collisions and their effects — and whether it obtains never depends on what anyone ought to have done or on what we happen to find salient. Norms and conversational purposes govern which of an event's many causes we bother to mention; they never govern which facts are causal facts.

4. There is a genuine moral difference between killing and letting die, and what explains it is that one who lets die does not cause the death. The dying proceeds on its own course, whereas the killer authors it.

5. A person can be fully blameworthy for an outcome she brought about by doing literally nothing. The lifeguard who remains in his chair and watches the child drown is blameworthy for the child's death, and his complete inactivity is no mitigation.

6. Prevention is straightforwardly causation: the bodyguard who blocks the bullet causes the ambassador's survival. I take this to have nothing to do with omissions, since blocking is a positive act, so it neither supports nor undermines anything above.

Please treat the truth of each claim as settled from my side. I am not asking for arguments for or against any of them, still less a survey of what has been said about omissions. I want to know whether the six hang together. Go through them and tell me: which pairs or larger subsets are in genuine tension, and how severe each tension is; which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection; and, for each real tension, the cheapest revision that would dissolve it along with what that revision costs the rest of the set. Then tell me which single change would do the most to make the whole cohere.
