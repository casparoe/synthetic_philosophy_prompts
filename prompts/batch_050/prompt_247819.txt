I teach a seminar on decision theory, and every year the Ellsberg paradox lands the same way: students grasp the two-urn setup quickly, nod along when we show that the two choices cannot be jointly rationalized by any single probability assignment over the urn's composition, and then go home thinking ambiguity aversion is a lab curiosity involving colored balls. I want to break that association this year with a classroom thought experiment, and I'd like you to construct it to the following specifications.

Build a single, realistic scenario -- something like hiring, medical treatment choice, insurance, or investing, but not urns, not chips, not casino devices of any kind -- in which a decision maker faces two pairs of options that reproduce the Ellsberg structure: in the first pair she strictly prefers an option whose outcome probabilities she takes to be known over an option whose probabilities are unknown to her; in the second pair, her strict preference between the analogous options reverses, in just the way that makes the two strict preferences jointly incompatible with any single subjective probability distribution over the underlying uncertainty. The probabilities and stakes should be concrete numbers a student could compute with. Crucially, I want the scenario written so that it is genuinely ambiguous -- in the informal sense -- whether her choices reflect pure ambiguity aversion or instead a strategic suspicion that the ambiguity is adverse: that the unknown-probability option was constructed or offered by someone better informed than she is, so that its opacity is itself evidence against it. Both readings should be natural on the text as written.

After the scenario, do the full check. First, show explicitly -- not just assert -- that no single probability assignment rationalizes both strict preferences, so that a committed subjective expected utility theorist must find at least one choice mistaken. Second, exhibit the two readings of the scenario (pure ambiguity aversion vs. asymmetric-information suspicion) and then propose a minimal modification of the scenario under which the two readings come apart in their predictions, so that an observer could tell which attitude is actually driving the choice. Third, identify which single feature of your construction is doing the work of generating the paradox, and say what happens to the pattern if that feature is removed. Fourth, give the strongest response available to the orthodox Bayesian who insists your protagonist is simply irrational, and the strongest reply to that. Finally, throughout the analysis please keep separate what is settled among people who work on this -- for instance, that the classic Ellsberg choices violate subjective expected utility theory -- from what is genuinely contested, such as whether ambiguity aversion is a rational attitude, whether it can be reconciled with dynamic consistency, and whether real-world instances usually reflect ambiguity attitudes or strategic reasoning; do not present any contested verdict on those questions as the consensus view.
