I run data governance for a small hospital network (three sites, one inherited from a takeover) and I've somehow become the person who has to deal with contradictory records. After the merger we've got thousands of them: the same patient listed with two different blood types, an allergy recorded at one site and explicitly ruled out at another, a drug marked current in one system and discontinued for an adverse reaction in the other. Right now our policy is blunt: when two sources disagree, the record gets locked and every query touching it bounces until a human resolves it. Resolution takes weeks, clinicians route around us with phone calls and post-it notes, and I suspect we're now causing more harm than we're preventing.

A colleague who studied philosophy told me logicians have spent decades on exactly this — how to reason from inconsistent information without concluding everything (apparently in ordinary logic a single contradiction lets you derive anything at all, which seems mad), that there are paraconsistent systems built to keep contradictions quarantined rather than infectious, and that a few philosophers even hold that some contradictions are simply true, which I'm not sure I believe but seems relevant to legal edge cases. I've read a bit and I'm convinced the ideas should be usable, but the literature is all about proof systems and I need something a records officer can do at a desk in twenty minutes.

So: turn whatever paraconsistent logic and the dialetheism debate actually teach into a triage procedure for contradictory records. Specifically:

1. A small set of tests, run in order, that sort a contradiction into at least three buckets: must fix before any further use; can be carried as a flagged dispute, with restrictions on what may be inferred from it; can be tolerated indefinitely. Spell out the principle behind each test in terms a smart non-logician can defend in front of a clinical director.
2. The tests should exploit the distinctions the logicians insist on — I'd guess these include the difference between having no information and having too much, between a contradiction that stays local and one that spreads through your inference rules, and whatever explains why ordinary logic explodes and how that maps onto what our query system actually does when it hits a conflict.
3. Say explicitly which parts of the theory must survive the translation into a desk procedure and which can be dropped without real loss.
4. Work it through three of my cases: the two-blood-types patient (transfusions happen here), the allergy recorded/ruled-out case (prescribing happens here), and a contradiction about a patient's registered GP which affects almost nothing clinical but matters a great deal to auditors. I suspect the procedure will give me at least one answer I won't like — probably the GP one or the blood-type one — and I'd rather watch that happen than have it smoothed over.
5. Be honest about what the procedure cannot do. My nagging worry is that "keep both values and restrict inference" is just ignoring the problem in a tweed jacket. If there's a principled line between responsible tolerance of inconsistency and negligence, I want it drawn; if there isn't, I want to be told that too.

Context: I process maybe forty of these a week, flag-and-carry decisions are signed off by me alone, anything in the must-fix bucket goes to a clinician, and I get about twenty minutes per case, occasionally less. Please answer in British English.
