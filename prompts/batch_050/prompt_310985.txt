I'm a novelist -- I write literary fiction, mostly close third person, and I spend my days thinking about point of view, interiority, free indirect discourse, that sort of thing. I've come across Hofstadter's idea that a person's self, their "I", can persist in a distributed way in the minds of people who knew them -- that when his wife died, some low-resolution copy of her strange loop kept running in his head and in others' heads. I find this genuinely moving but I can't tell if it's a literal claim or just a nice metaphor for grief.

So: restate the position in the vocabulary I actually work in. Treat each person who knew the deceased as carrying something like a character -- a model with a voice, habits, typical reactions, an implied interiority -- and talk about what it would mean for that character to be well- or badly-written, how much "screen time" it gets, whose narration it shows up in, whether it can do anything novel or just recur. Then give me the honest residue report: which parts of Hofstadter's claim translate cleanly into character-and-narration talk, which parts you had to distort, and which parts have no counterpart at all in how characters exist in a novelist's or reader's head. And tell me whether the residue is a limitation of my framework or a sign that Hofstadter's claim was more metaphor than thesis in the first place.
