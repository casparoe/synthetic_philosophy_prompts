I'm a graduate student running an informal reading group on bargaining between artificial agents -- systems that negotiate on behalf of principals with genuinely different values, where there is no shared currency in which to compare gains. Our group has split into two camps over a proposal for how such agents should generalize the idea of a Pareto improvement. The proposal, which I'll call the safe-Pareto criterion, runs roughly as follows: when two agents bargain, any deal they reach will depend on how each agent's utility function is normalized (e.g., on affine rescalings), and since neither normalization is privileged, a compromise outcome should only count as a genuine improvement over the status quo if both agents prefer it to the status quo under every admissible normalization of their utility functions -- that is, the improvement must be robust, or "safe," across all the ways of putting the two utilities on a common scale. A compromise that improves things for both agents only under some weightings but not others is, on this view, not a safe Pareto improvement and should be rejected in favor of the status quo, even if both agents would in fact accept it. One camp in my group thinks this is the principled foundation for cooperation between agents with different values: it needs no interpersonal comparison of utility, it is more robust than picking a single bargaining solution like Nash's or Kalai-Smorodinsky's, and it explains why some mutually accepted deals still feel illegitimate. The other camp thinks the criterion is confused: they argue that requiring robustness across all normalizations conflates two different things -- uncertainty about which deal to pick and arbitrariness in the representation of the agents' preferences -- that the criterion is either vacuous (because almost any interior compromise survives all reweightings once both parties genuinely gain) or paralyzing (because it vetoes deals both agents would endorse), and that the real work in bargaining is done by threat points and fairness constraints, not by scale-invariance.

Please write the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides accept -- about what a Pareto improvement is and isn't when agents lack a common utility scale, about what standard bargaining solutions presuppose, and about which phenomena any adequate criterion must handle (e.g., deals one side would regret under a defensible reweighting, the difference between compromising on outcomes and compromising on how strongly each side cares); (2) a disagreements section stating each residual dispute in wording each side would endorse for its own position -- no strawmen, and if part of the quarrel turns out to be verbal (say, over what "improvement" is even supposed to mean absent a shared scale), identify that part explicitly; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp, which I suspect include whether invariance across renormalizations is the right response to the arbitrariness of interpersonal weighting, and whether vetoing mutually accepted deals has acceptable costs when the status quo is itself bad; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or formal result that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements, then a crux table.
