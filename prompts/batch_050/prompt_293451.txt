I facilitate a monthly philosophy-and-rationality reading group (about 12 adults, mixed backgrounds, one 90-minute session). Most of them are fluent in consequentialist jargon and will happily say "shut up and multiply" — but I suspect this fluency is exactly what lets them treat the problem of dirty hands as solved. I want an activity that makes them feel that a choice can be both the right choice and a genuine moral stain, something that stays on you rather than canceling out in the calculus.

Design a runnable simulation or staged role-play in which participants face an escalating sequence of decisions where preventing a clearly worse outcome requires personally doing something they consider wrong (betraying a confidence, framing an innocent party, breaking a promise made in the game itself). The escalation and incentives should be arranged so that nearly everyone crosses at least one line, without any lecture up front and without naming the concept. Crucially, I want a mechanic that captures the *residue* — some structured moment afterward where participants must choose whether they want to be thanked, forgiven, or punished, and get to notice which they actually reach for.

Include: the setup and roles, the decision sequence with its payoff structure (no real money), a timeline, a debrief mapping game moments onto the philosophical structure, what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., participants gaming it ironically to avoid real discomfort), and a frank note on which feature of dirty hands the activity cannot reproduce.
