I’m a novelist working on a linked story cycle in which psychiatric labels keep changing the course of people’s lives. I want a practical plotting procedure based on Ian Hacking’s idea of looping effects: once a kind of person gets named, people may understand themselves through that name, institutions may treat them differently because of it, and the resulting behavior can become evidence that keeps the classification in place. So a diagnosis is never just a neutral description sitting on top of the plot.

Please turn that idea into something I can actually use. Call it a loop-pressure audit if that helps. I want an ordered checklist I can run in about 45 minutes whenever I’m tempted to give a major character a psychiatric classification, especially when the label will affect treatment, family dynamics, school, work, policing, or the character’s own self-image. I’m not a clinician, and I don’t want a bibliography or a mini-history of psychiatry. I want a decision tool that captures the philosophical point without pretending to settle clinical questions.

The procedure should distinguish at least these possibilities:

- label-heavy but not loop-rich: the diagnosis is mentioned a lot, but nobody’s options, interpretations, or incentives really change
- self-looping: the character starts interpreting their memories, emotions, relationships, or future through the category
- institutional looping: teachers, relatives, courts, clinicians, employers, or police respond differently, thereby producing some of the behavior they take the label to explain
- adversarial looping: the character resists the label, but that resistance is interpreted as further evidence for it
- category-return looping: enough classified people behave differently that the surrounding concept of the condition starts shifting too
- counterlooping: the character deliberately borrows, performs, refuses, or weaponizes the category for their own purposes

For each step, tell me what question to ask, what counts as a sufficiently concrete answer, and what should happen if I can’t answer. I’d like a simple scoring system, but please be explicit about which parts are genuine checks and which parts are just judgment calls with boxes around them. Also say where the procedure should stop me from using the diagnosis at all, where it should only require rewriting, and where the moral or aesthetic issue is too case-specific for any checklist to decide.

Build in an agency check so I don’t accidentally write the character as nothing but a product of classification. I also want a distinction between a character being harmed by a label and a character opportunistically using one, since both can happen at once. And flag the difference between depicting a harmful institutional loop and endorsing the institution’s view of the person. That distinction matters alot to me.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical story ideas:

1. A fourteen-year-old is classified as oppositional after conflicts with one teacher. Once the label enters his school record, ordinary argumentativeness gets documented as a symptom, and he begins deliberately acting up because he thinks he has already been cast as the problem.

2. A woman in her twenties develops panic attacks. After receiving a diagnosis, she organizes her life around avoiding panic, becomes highly knowledgeable about the category, and eventually cannot tell whether she is managing an illness, obeying an identity, or both.

3. A manipulative political candidate starts publicly describing himself as traumatized whenever he is criticized. Some of his suffering is real, but he also uses the vocabulary to make every objection sound like cruelty.

For the third case, let the procedure give me an answer I probably won’t like if that’s where it leads: for instance, maybe I should remove the explicit psychiatric label rather than using it as a shortcut for moral corruption. Explain what would have to change for that verdict to flip.

End with a short section called what this procedure cannot do. Be blunt about its failure modes, especially stereotyping, making diagnosis too narratively powerful, treating every self-description as fake, and turning a fictional checklist into armchair clinical judgment.
