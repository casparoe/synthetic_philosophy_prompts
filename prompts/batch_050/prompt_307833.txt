A friend who did a philosophy PhD tried to explain Sellars's myth of the given to me over coffee, and wrote me this summary afterwards:

"Sellars's target is the idea of the given: the classical empiricist picture where sense data are simply present to the mind and can ground knowledge without presupposing anything else. His argument is that nothing can justify a belief unless it has conceptual content, because only conceptually contentful states stand in the logical relations (entailment, consistency) that justification requires. So the sense datum can't serve as a foundation for knowledge. More than that, though: since a non-conceptual state can't do epistemic work, Sellars concludes there's no such thing as raw, non-conceptual experience at all — experience is conceptual all the way down. It follows that experience never really justifies our beliefs; only other beliefs do, which makes Sellars basically a coherentist about justification. He aimed the argument mainly at Russell-style sense-data theory, so it doesn't have much bite against other epistemologies."

How much of this is fair? Go through it and tell me what a Sellars scholar would sign off on, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong. I'm especially unsure about the move from "the given can't justify anything" to "there's no non-conceptual experience" and then on to coherentism — my friend ran those together as one smooth argument, but I have a hunch Sellars's actual view is more awkward than that, since he clearly thinks perception gives us non-inferential knowledge somehow.
