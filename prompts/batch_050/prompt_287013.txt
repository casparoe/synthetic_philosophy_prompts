Design a self-amendment paradox in a minimal rule-governed system -- a club, committee, or simple game of the Nomic type -- whose entire constitution you state upfront in no more than eight or ten rules, one of which permits amendments. The system must, by a sequence of steps each valid under the rules as written, generate two incompatible rules that both count as validly enacted, or a single rule that simultaneously requires and forbids the same act. Constraints: no infinite regress of rules about rules, no vagueness or ambiguity in the rule text (the paradox must come from self-application, not from interpretation), no appeal to an outside court or legislature to break the tie, and the amendment rule may not simply be written to immunize itself in advance -- the problem should arise precisely because the amendment power is self-applicable. After stating the rules, give the step-by-step derivation of each contradictory output, identify which structural feature is doing the work (self-application itself, the absence of a hierarchy between constitutional and ordinary rules, or whatever it turns out to be), and say what single change would dissolve the paradox -- along with whether that fix genuinely solves the problem or merely pushes it up to the next level of rules, as critics of hierarchical solutions contend.
