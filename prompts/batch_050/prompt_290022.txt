I am writing an essay for a graduate seminar on population ethics, and I want to read Alfonso Cuarón's film Children of Men (2006) through the procreation asymmetry: the claim, defended in different forms by McMahan, Benatar, and others, that while we have strong moral reason not to bring into existence people whose lives would go badly, we have no comparably strong reason to bring into existence people whose lives would go well, since the never-created happy person is not thereby worse off or wronged. The film's premise -- roughly two decades of total human infertility, with the death of the youngest person alive treated as a world-historical event -- seems at first glance to be a cinematic reductio of that asymmetry: everyone in the film grieves the absence of future people as a catastrophe, yet on the asymmetry no one has been harmed by the non-creation of those people, because there is no one for whom non-existence is worse.

Please give me a reading that is careful in both directions. First, the strongest pro-asymmetry reading: the film's catastrophe is entirely a harm to existing people -- the despair, the suicides, the collapse of public order and of the point of ordinary projects -- and the never-born are not among the victims. On this reading the film is best understood as an illustration of the idea, associated with Samuel Scheffler, that the value and intelligibility of much of what we do depends on believing that humanity will outlast us. I'd like you to test this against the scene in which Theo's cousin preserves masterpieces in a state-run art collection: what exactly is the project of saving Michelangelo's David for a species with no future viewers, and does the scene support or undercut the meaning-collapse diagnosis? Second, the anti-asymmetry reading: the film's most famous sequence -- the birth, and the combatants lowering their weapons as the newborn is carried through the fighting -- seems to present the child as a good that outruns anyone's welfare, hers or her mother's or the witnesses', which is precisely the impersonal value the asymmetry denies. Is that a defensible gloss on the scene, or is it sentimentality the film itself hasn't earned? Third, tell me where the reading fails altogether: the film is also about refugee camps, state violence, and the treatment of the already living, and I suspect that forcing the whole film into the population-ethics frame distorts it. Finally, say what, if anything, the film contributes to this debate that the journal literature does not -- whether dramatizing the infertility scenario shows us something about our own practices of valuing that the arguments, conducted in the abstract, cannot.
