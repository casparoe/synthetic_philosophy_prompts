I run a small ethics reading group, and for the past semester two of our members have been circling the same fight. One defends a position she calls tranquilism: roughly, the view that nothing is good or bad for a person except insofar as that person experiences cravings (unfulfilled desires, felt needs, dissatisfactions) or the absence thereof. On her view, a moment of experience entirely free of craving — including dreamless sleep and, she insists, non-existence itself — is fully optimal for the person, and the presence of pleasures or fulfilled desires adds no further value once craving is absent. The other member is a classical hedonistic utilitarian who thinks positive pleasure is good in itself and that a world of intensely happy people can be vastly better than an empty one. They keep talking past each other: he accuses her of implying that a world with no one in it is the best possible world; she accuses him of smuggling in an obligation to manufacture craving in people just so it can be relieved.

Act as a professional moral philosopher with expertise in axiology and population ethics, and think carefully about what each side is actually committed to before you draft anything. Begin with a short brainstorming stage (brief notes are fine) identifying candidate points of agreement and candidate cruxes. Then produce the deliverable: the joint statement these two people could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. Format it as (1) a numbered section of agreed claims — including claims about the structure of their dispute, about what each view does and does not entail, and about cases they judge the same way — followed by (2) a disagreements table in which each row states a residual disagreement in wording that each side would accept as a fair description of its own position, and (3) a cruxes section. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument that both parties would agree bears on it (candidates you might consider, or replace with better ones: whether non-existence can be good or bad for a person at all; whether the relief of a craving that was created solely in order to be relieved adds value; torture versus dust-specks-style tradeoffs; whether tranquilism must aggregate across persons or only within a life) and record in advance what each side would predict or conclude about that case. If some part of their dispute turns out to be merely verbal — for instance, if they are using "good for" in different senses — identify that part explicitly and explain why. Write the response in American English.
