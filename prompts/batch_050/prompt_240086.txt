I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has spent the past two years reading philosophy of mind, and next month I am presenting a talk to a university philosophy-of-cognitive-science seminar. My thesis is that Daniel Dennett's tower of generate-and-test — the progression from Darwinian creatures (hardwired variation selected over generations), to Skinnerian creatures (that learn by reinforcement within a lifetime), to Popperian creatures (that can test hypotheses in an inner model before acting), to Gregorian creatures (that import thinking tools from a shared culture) — gets the ordering wrong in a specific way. Drawing on what I know from the animal-learning literature, I want to argue that the Skinnerian and Popperian stages are not cleanly separable in practice: reinforcement learning and model-based hypothesis testing are intertwined even in corvids and cephalopods, so the tower is better understood as a set of capacities that can be recruited in different combinations than as a ladder each mind must climb rung by rung.

The audience will be mostly philosophers of mind and a few cognitive scientists: people who know Dennett's framework well, some sympathetic to it, some committed to a sharp distinction between model-free and model-based cognition, and at least one person who works on animal cognition and will know the empirical literature better than I now do. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I am actually likely to get from this particular audience, ordered by how damaging they would be, each phrased the way such a person would phrase it, with a reply I can give in a couple of minutes and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections I should simply concede and how to concede them without losing the thesis, and identify the question I am hoping nobody asks. Where you are uncertain about any of this — for instance, about what this audience will actually press on — say how confident you are, and single out the one point in your prep sheet you are least sure of.
