Okay, novelist here. I write literary fiction, and I’m drafting a craft essay about why readers emotionally invest in people they know perfectly well I made up. Before I commit to this philosphical picture, can you audit whether my commitments actually hang together?

Roughly, I think:

1. A competent reader does not literally believe that the events in my novel happened. They may be immersed, but they are not deceived.
2. Readers can nevertheless have real emotions toward fictional people and events: grief, pity, dread, relief, and so on. These are not merely pretend emotions.
3. Emotions toward fiction can be fitting or unfitting. For example, pity for a genuinely sympathetic character can be fitting, while panic at a scene written only as a joke would be unfitting.
4. In general, an emotion is fitting only if its object actually obtains: pity is fitting only if someone is really suffering, fear only if there is real danger, guilt only if one actually did something wrong.
5. Part of a novel’s value is that a fitting emotional response to an imagined situation can reveal or confirm some truth about actual human life.
6. Fiction can therefore provide defeasible evidence about actual moral psychology, though not because readers confuse fictional people with real ones.
7. Someone rereading a novel can rationally feel suspense even when they remember how it ends. Yet suspense always involves some kind of uncertainty.
8. A reader might rationally be angry at me, the author, for killing off a character while also grieving for the character as if the death were something that happened to her. Those are two emotions with two different targets.
9. Immersion does not require false belief. Forgetting that one is reading fiction is a matter of attention and imaginative absorption, not believing that the fictional world exists.

Please check which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each problem is. For every real tension, tell me what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost. I’d also like to know which one revision would make the whole package most coherent. Don’t give me a history of the paradox of fiction or a reading list; just assess my position as stated. At the very end, give the overall result in exactly one of these forms: \answer{consistent as stated}, \answer{consistent with one revision}, or \answer{inconsistent as stated}.
