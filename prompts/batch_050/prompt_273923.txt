A colleague at a department lunch last week called the deinstitutionalization of the American mentally ill "the purest natural experiment in applied Burke we have," and I've been turning that over while prepping my political philosophy lecture on conservatism and rationalism. I want to use the emptying of the state mental hospitals between the mid-50s and the early 80s as the central case, and I'd like you to pressure-test the framing before I teach it. The standard conservative reading is clean: the asylums were a fence, ugly but load-bearing. Reformers -- civil liberties lawyers, Goffman-inspired sociologists, the whole antipsychiatry moment, helped along by the new antipsychotics and by Cuckoo's Nest on the cultural side -- saw abuse and concluded the institution was the abuse, tore the fence down through litigation and the community mental health legislation, and then found out what the fence had been doing. The promised community centers were never funded at anything like the planned scale, and the functions the hospitals performed (feeding, sheltering, medicating, containing people who couldn't manage on their own) didn't disappear; they got reallocated to sidewalks, jails, and emergency rooms. Burke predicts this outcome and Chesterton's fence diagnoses the reformers' mistake.

But I think the clean reading papers over three things, and those are what I actually want analyzed. First: the reformers mostly did know why the fence was there. Goffman's point wasn't that the hospitals served no function, it was that the function was warehousing dressed up as treatment, and the plan all along was to replace custody with community care -- the money is what failed to materialize. So is this a Chesterton's-fence violation at all, or just a bet that lost? Does the fence principle require you to understand the fence's purpose, or to have a working funded replacement before you tear down, and can Burke even tell those apart? Second, what you might call the reverse-fence problem: the absence of asylums is now itself a decades-old status quo, and the institutional knowledge to run the old system humanely is gone. If Burke counsels conserving what exists and rebuilding only incrementally, does he counsel against reinstitutionalization too -- i.e., is the theory just "whatever exists now is wise" with a lag, and is that a feature or a reductio? Third: the asylums genuinely were horrendous, and the theory seems to have nothing to say about when a fence's purpose itself is rotten (compare segregation). Does the case show that Burkean prudence needs to import an external moral theory to tell it which fences to inspect first -- and if so, whats left of conservatism as a freestanding position rather than a heuristic bolted onto liberalism?

Run the analysis in both directions: what the theory says the actors should concretely have done in 1955 and in 1963 (not "be prudent" -- what would prudence actually have prescribed, given what was knowable then), which features of the episode the theory nails and which it misses or misdescribes, and then what the episode shows about the theory -- confirmation, anomaly, or a case it was never built for. If your verdict is that the theory handles all of this fine, say so, but I suspect it doesnt.
