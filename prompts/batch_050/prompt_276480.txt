Last night I spent nearly three hours in a pub argument with a friend — a lawyer, annoyingly disciplined about premises — over whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible: situations in which an agent ought to do each of two things and cannot do both. She says they are not, and she ended the evening with an argument I have been turning over since. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I cannot find the faulty step either. Here it is, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Ought implies can: no one is obligated to do what it is impossible for them to do.
(2) Obligations agglomerate: if S ought to do A and S ought to do B, then S ought to do both A and B.
(3) In a putative genuine dilemma, S ought to do A and S ought to do B, while doing both is impossible.
(4) From (2) and (3), S ought to do both A and B.
(5) From (1) and (4), S can do both — contradicting (3).
(6) Therefore no situation is one in which an agent genuinely ought to do each of two incompatible things; in every apparent dilemma, at most one of the two oughts was ever real, and sufficiently thorough deliberation would in principle reveal which.

Then she added what she called the coroner's step, since it examines what is left after the body is buried:

(7) If (6) is true, then an agent who faces an apparent dilemma and chooses correctly — does the thing she actually ought to do — has discharged every obligation in play; nothing remains outstanding.
(8) But nothing remains outstanding only if no apology, compensation, or regret is owed afterward, and in the hardest cases that seems plainly false. A doctor who sends the one rescue helicopter to the larger group does not merely feel bad about the man she left on the mountainside; she owes something to his family.
(9) So either (6) is wrong, or our practice of treating some obligations as surviving their non-fulfillment is a superstition to be discarded.

She presented the whole thing as a reductio of dilemmas via (6), followed by a fork via (9), and dared me to find the exit. If I am forced to choose, I would rather surrender (1) than (2) — agglomeration strikes me as close to a meaning-postulate of "ought" — though I am not confident that is where the pressure belongs. I also have a vague suspicion that something goes wrong between the description of the hard cases in (8) and what (3) needs to be true, but I cannot make the suspicion precise.

Please tell me which step or steps you reject, why the rejected step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs — including which nearby claims I must then give up or reinterpret. If you think the argument is in fact sound, say which of (1), (2), or (8) you would advise me to abandon and what I lose by abandoning it. Finally, tell me what would change your verdict: which consideration, if I produced it, would show the soundness of the argument, or would show that the flaw lies somewhere other than where you locate it — and where is your own diagnosis most likely to fail?

Please answer in American English.
