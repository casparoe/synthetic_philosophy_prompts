I'm a software engineer (backend stuff, ~8 years, zero philosophy training) and this came out of a dumb argument at work that I keep thinking about.

So: we had a flaky integration test for like six weeks. I was convinced it was a race condition in our code, my coworker was convinced it was the CI runners being overloaded. We both saw the exact same logs, the exact same dashboards, everything. And after six weeks of data we were... still disagreeing? He'd look at a failure and say "see, happens at peak load" and I'd look at the same failure and say "peak load is exactly when races show up, that's my point." We weren't even disagreeing about the facts, we were disagreeing about what the facts were evidence FOR.

Someone at a party later told me there's a whole thing about this: if two Bayesian reasoners with different priors see enough of the same evidence, their beliefs supposedly converge, priors "wash out." OK. I want to know how much to believe that.

Give me your actual credence -- a number, not "it depends" -- for two separate propositions:

P1 (the math version): two rational agents with different priors over a fixed hypothesis space, both assigning nonzero probability to the true hypothesis, both agreeing on the likelihood of any observation, updating on the same infinite stream of evidence, will end up arbitrarily close in their beliefs.

P2 (the version I actually care about): for real human disagreements like my flaky test one, accumulating shared evidence tends to dissolve the disagreement within a practically relevant timeframe (say, before the project ends).

For P2 I want the decomposition, with a rough number and one line of justification for each term. Some sub-questions I suspect matter, but use your own breakdown if mine's wrong: (a) how likely is it that the two sides even agree on likelihoods -- my suspicion is most real fights are secretly fights about what the data means, i.e. the likelihood function itself, not the prior; (b) how likely is it that the true hypothesis is even IN both people's hypothesis spaces (neither of us had "the test harness itself has a bug" on the table, which is what it turned out to be, annyway); (c) how likely is it that enough informative evidence arrives before the decision becomes moot. Then show how the terms combine into your final number for P2.

Finally: which term in your decomposition are you least confident in, and what observation or argument would move it most? And one bonus thing I can't figure out: does convergence theory assume you keep asking the same question the whole time? Because in debugging, the question itself keeps mutating as you go. If the hypothesis space is a moving target, is the whole "washing out" story even applicable?
