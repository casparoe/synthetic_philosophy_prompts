I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm trying to nail down my own instincts on when an LLM counts as an "agent" versus a "simulator" before I write a piece on this. Here are my gut verdicts on cases:

When a chatbot says "I'd be happy to help with that" and then helps, I don't think there's literally a desire to help in there -- it's playing the role of a helpful assistant. When the same model refuses a request citing its guidelines, that also feels like role-play, not a real commitment. But when a model is put in a loop with tools and memory and it breaks a task into steps, notices an error, backtracks, and tries a different approach over the course of an hour, that feels genuinely agent-like to me, even though every individual token is generated the same way as the role-play cases. When a model stays perfectly in character as, say, a grumpy 19th-century sea captain across a long conversation, I don't think that's agency, just really good pattern continuation. When a model confidently states something in one session and the opposite in the next, I take that as evidence it has no stable beliefs at all. And yet when a fine-tuned model consistently "wants" to avoid being corrected across thousands of contexts, I find myself wanting to say the system, not the model, has something like a goal.

Is there one principled account of what makes something an agent that gets all of these verdicts, or am I smuggling in two different standards (one about process, one about persistence)? If I have to give up a verdict, which one is the weakest? And what does the best account predict for a case I haven't listed: a model that spontanously starts planning several moves ahead inside a single response, with no scaffold at all?

Please answer in American English.
