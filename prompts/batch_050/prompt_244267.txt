Last night I had a long argument with a friend about Nietzsche, and I came away unsure whether his account of the Genealogy of Morality is fair. His summary ran roughly as follows:

"Nietzsche's argument is that what we call morality — pity, humility, self-denial, the equality of persons — was invented by the weak as a cunning act of revenge against the strong. The nobles were originally the good in every sense: healthy, happy, and admirable, calling themselves good and the common herd bad. The priests and the slave masses, seething with a resentment they could not discharge in action, revalued all this, rebranding the nobles' excellences as evil and their own impotence as goodness. Once you see that morality's pedigree is resentment rather than truth or divine command, the thing stands refuted: tracing an ugly origin is enough to discredit a value. Nietzsche's positive message is accordingly that we should shake off slave morality and return to the master morality of the nobles, who at least affirmed life instead of denying it."

Something in this smells off to me, but I lost the argument on points, so I may be the one who is wrong. Which parts would a careful reader of Nietzsche accept as accurate, which are common simplifications, and which are misrepresentations such a reader would push back on? In particular: does Nietzsche actually argue that an origin in resentment by itself refutes a morality, or is the relation between genealogy and evaluation more complicated than my friend makes out? Is it right that Nietzsche straightforwardly endorses master morality and counsels a return to it? And does my friend's conclusion — that the Genealogy discredits our values and licenses discarding them — follow from the parts of his summary that are accurate? Please separate what he got right from what he got wrong before telling me who won.
