I am a graduate student in philosophy, and next month I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary colloquium attended by epistemic game theorists, decision theorists, and a handful of experimental economists who study strategic behavior in the lab. My thesis is that the backward induction argument in the centipede game does not show what it is usually taken to show. My claim, roughly, is this: backward induction requires common knowledge of rationality, but in a long centipede game that condition is self-undermining, because any player who observes the game continuing past the first few nodes has received evidence that the condition fails, and once it fails, continuing to cooperate can be instrumentally rational. So I argue that the "paradox" is not a puzzle about whether players should take at the first node but a demonstration that the epistemic prerequisites of backward induction cannot coherently survive actual play of the game.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this particular audience will raise, phrased the way each faction would actually phrase them: the game theorists who think I have simply rediscovered a well-known technical result and dressed it up as philosophy, the decision theorists who will press me on whether my argument covertly abandons standard rational choice, and the experimentalists who will ask what my view predicts about observed play and whether it is falsifiable at all. For each objection, tell me how likely it is to come up, how damaging it is to the paper if I answer it badly, what my best short reply is, and where that reply is weakest. Also tell me: what I should concede immediately and gracefully, because conceding it costs my thesis nothing and buys credibility; what I must not concede under any framing, because conceding it collapses the paper; and which single objection I should be most afraid of, given that the room contains people who work on epistemic foundations of game theory for a living. If there is a natural way to distinguish my position from nearby, more familiar positions — for example, from merely claiming that real humans are boundedly rational, or from the reputation-based models where players act as if opponents might be a cooperative type — tell me how to draw that line crisply in one or two sentences, because I suspect several questions will amount to asking whether my thesis is anything more than one of those. Finally, give me the one reply that has to land for the paper to survive, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
