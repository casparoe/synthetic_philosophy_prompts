Roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm an objective Bayesian. Given the same total evidence, there is (at least often) a rationally *required* credence function, not just a permissibility constraint. Subjective Bayesians who say "any coherent prior is fine" are giving up too much.

(2) I'm sold on the maximum entropy principle: if your evidence only fixes some constraints (say, an expectation value), the rational prior is the distribution with maximum Shannon entropy subject to those constraints. Jaynes basically had this right.

(3) An agent's rational credences can't depend on arbitrary choices of description. If I reparameterize the quantity — work in log x instead of x, or in volume instead of side length — my beliefs about the underlying physical facts should come out the same.

(4) In genuinely symmetric finite cases, indifference is the rational response. A fair-seeming die I know nothing about: uniform over six faces. Three identical doors, one prize: a third each. This is the clean case where the principle of indifference just works.

(5) Total ignorance about a bounded continuous quantity is representable, namely by the uniform density over the interval. If I know only that the proportion of water in a mixture is between 0 and 1, uniform is my rational state.

(6) Standard conditionalization is the right update rule when you learn a proposition outright; max entropy is about setting priors, not about revising them, so the two operate at different stages and don't compete.

Which of these actually hang together and which don't?
