I serve as the board secretary of a forty-unit housing cooperative, and I need advice on how to rule on a dispute that has paralyzed us. Our bylaws, drafted in the 1970s, contain a single rule about change: Rule 12 provides that these bylaws may be amended or repealed by a two-thirds vote of members present at any duly noticed meeting, full stop. Last year a faction of members began pushing to sell the building to a developer, which would have dissolved the co-op's affordability covenant -- the clause capping resale prices so units stay below market. In response, the majority proposed and passed, with 78% approval, an amendment stating that the affordability covenant shall not be amended or repealed, and that the entrenching sentence itself shall not be amended or repealed. The losing faction now insists the amendment is void, however lopsided the vote: Rule 12, they argue, presupposes a rulebook that always remains changeable by two-thirds, so an amendment invoked under Rule 12 cannot coherently produce a provision that Rule 12 can no longer reach -- you cannot use a power whose whole content is 'everything stays revisable' to make something unrevisable. The majority replies that Rule 12 authorizes any amendment whatsoever, and a power limited only by a vote threshold is not exceeded when the thing it creates happens to restrict the power's future exercise. I have to advise the board, and a bank deciding whether to refinance us wants to know whether the covenant is genuinely permanent.

While researching, I found a passage that seems to bear on this, from Peter Suber's The Paradox of Self-Amendment, in the appendix where he introduces his game Nomic: "One purpose of making some rules more difficult to change than others is to prevent a brief wave of fanaticism from undoing decades or centuries of refined structure. It is self-paternalism, our chosen insurance against our anticipated weak moments. But that purpose is not met unless the two-tier (or multi-tier) system also creates a logical hierarchy in which the less mutable rules take logical priority over the more mutable rules." I take the spirit of this -- our members plainly wanted exactly the insurance he describes against a future wave of enthusiasm for selling -- but our rulebook has no tiers at all. It's completely flat: one amendment rule, applying to everything, including, apparently, itself. Does Suber's point about logical hierarchy mean the 78% vote failed to accomplish what it aimed at, because a flat rulebook can't bootstrap a protected tier into existence? Or does the vote itself show that an unlimited power to amend includes the power to create hierarchy where none existed?

I'd like advice on both the principle and the practice. On the principle: who is right about whether the entrenchment is valid, and does the answer turn on how we understand what kind of power Rule 12 confers in the first place -- and if so, what are the live options for understanding it? On the practice: if the entrenchment is defective, is there a repair, such as first amending Rule 12 itself by two-thirds to expressly permit entrenched clauses, and only then entrenching the covenant -- or would that sequence run into the same objection one level up? Please reason from the structure of the situation rather than from generalities about democracy or majority rule, since both factions here agree the majority should prevail; they disagree about what the majority is capable of doing to itself.
