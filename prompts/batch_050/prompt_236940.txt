I am a novelist at work on a political novel, and I need your help with a document that appears inside it. Before anything else, please take on the role of a political philosopher with expertise in social trust, institutional design, and comparative political culture — someone who has thought carefully about why the Nordic countries and the United States seem to reason so differently about government.

The situation in the novel: my two central characters are Sigrid, a Norwegian political theorist who defends high-trust welfare-state institutions, and Marcus, an American essayist who regards habitual suspicion of government as a citizen's first duty. Late in the book, a scandal forces them into the open: the national welfare agency in Sigrid's country is revealed to have auto-approved benefit claims for a decade with almost no human review, and while overpayment was trivial, thousands of claims from immigrants and the homeless were wrongly rejected and never appealed, precisely because everyone assumed the system worked. A newspaper commissions the two of them to co-write a joint statement — an adversarial collaboration rather than a debate — and the draft they produce is a set piece in the novel. I need you to write that draft for me, in full, as it would appear on the page.

Work in stages. First, in a short planning section labeled "Notes to myself," brainstorm the strongest version of each character's position and the two or three places where their disagreement is most likely to be genuine rather than rhetorical. Then write the statement itself, with these numbered sections:

1. Agreed claims. Six to eight claims both can honestly sign — for instance, about what the welfare-agency scandal does and does not show, about the difference between trusting institutions and merely relying on them when one has no alternative, and about the fact that both high trust and high suspicion can become self-confirming: trust by funding the competence that rewards it, suspicion by generating the adversarial bureaucracy and talent flight that justify it.

2. Residual disagreements. Four disagreements, each stated in one or two sentences phrased so that each character would accept the wording of their own side. I want precision here, not caricature — Sigrid is not naive and Marcus is not paranoid.

3. Cruxes. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one of them. I suspect one crux is whether the citizens of high-trust states genuinely trust or merely have well-founded assurance — and whether anything moral turns on the difference. Another may be the line of thought, associated with Onora O'Neill, that ever-heavier transparency and auditing demands can themselves corrode trustworthiness; I want to know whether Marcus can accept that point without abandoning his position, and whether Sigrid can accept the scandal's lesson about "quiescence" — that unmonitored trust decays — without becoming a skeptic.

4. Tests. For each crux, a concrete case, observation, or finding that both characters agree in advance would bear on it, with each one's prediction recorded beforehand — the way real adversarial collaborators register forecasts.

Finally, a closing paragraph in my voice as the novelist, not the characters': state whether any part of their dispute dissolves once "trust" is disambiguated, and which part survives.

Think carefully at each stage; the document fails if either character is a straw man, since my readers will include people who hold each view. Roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words in total, and please keep the tone of the joint statement itself dignified and public-facing, as befits two people who dislike each other but respect the commission.
