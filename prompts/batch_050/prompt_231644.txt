Analyze the International Astronomical Union's 2006 redefinition of "planet" -- the decision that demoted Pluto by introducing the requirement that a planet must have cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit -- as a case study in conceptual engineering. I am not interested in the astronomy or in the public outcry over Pluto; I am interested in what the episode reveals about the philosophical project of fixing concepts rather than analyzing them.

Here is the theoretical frame I want applied. On one side stands traditional conceptual analysis: the project of uncovering the conditions under which our existing concept, as revealed by our considered judgments and usage, actually applies -- so that a good definition of "planet" would be answerable to how competent speakers and scientists have in fact used the term. On the other side stands the ameliorative or engineering project, associated in contemporary philosophy with work on concepts like "woman," "race," and "knowledge": the view that the right question is not what our concept is but what concept we should have, given the purposes the concept is meant to serve -- and that when a concept serves those purposes badly, we are entitled to revise it and stipulate a replacement. A further, more radical position in this literature holds that there is often no fact of the matter about what our shared concept "really is" -- that talk of the meaning of a term is just a diplomatic fiction -- so that analysis in the traditional sense is chasing something that does not exist, and engineering is the only game in town.

With that frame in place, work through the following, in order. First, the mapping: what was the IAU actually doing -- discovering the true extension of an existing concept, revising a concept to better serve a function, or stipulating a new technical term while leaving the old one alone? Note the wrinkle that the IAU defined a term of art for professional astronomy while the public and much of the culture kept using the old word, so be careful about whose concept was being engineered. Second, the function: a conceptual engineer wants to know what work a concept does before fixing it. What work was "planet" doing -- classification for research, museum pedagogy, public wonder, demarcating a natural kind -- and did the "cleared the neighbourhood" criterion serve those functions or only some of them? If the functions conflict, does the episode show that engineering presupposes a settled answer about a concept's purpose that is rarely available? Third, the resistance: many astronomers and members of the public treated the vote as an illegitimate stipulation rather than a discovery or an improvement -- as if the IAU lacked the standing to change what "planet" means. Is that reaction confused on the engineering picture, or does it reveal something the engineering picture underweights, such as the value of continuity with usage or the way concepts coordinate people who never agreed to the revision? Fourth, the verdict in reverse: does the episode confirm the radical view that there was never a determinate concept of planet to analyze (the pre-2006 usage was arguably just a historical list with no unifying criterion), or does the smooth functioning of the post-2006 category show that concepts really can be legislated -- and would either conclusion generalize to the concepts, like "woman" or "knowledge," that the ameliorative literature is actually motivated by, where the stakes are not taxonomic?

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested: the historical facts of the episode and the broad shape of the engineering-versus-analysis distinction are common ground, but whether concepts have determinate contents to be discovered, whether revision requires uptake to count as changing a concept rather than merely coining a homonym, and whether the Pluto case is a good model for ethically loaded cases are all live disputes. Do not present any of those contested positions as the consensus, and where the episode underdetermines the philosophy, say so rather than forcing a verdict.
