I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in early modern philosophy, and I am writing the final exam. The students have read Meditations I–II closely, and in class we reconstructed the cogito in its standard inferential form (I am thinking; whatever is thinking exists; therefore I exist), discussed the rival performance and self-verifying-intuition readings, and considered the Lichtenberg-style objection that the most Descartes is entitled to is an impersonal claim that thinking is occurring, just as one says it is raining. In short, they know the textbook map. I want a question that cannot be answered by reproducing it.

Draft one essay question, answerable in 75 minutes (roughly 900 words), on what exactly the cogito proves. The question should force candidates to confront two specific cruxes rather than summarize the literature. First, the question should present candidates with the impersonal reformulation — thinking is occurring now — and require them to say precisely what, if anything, is lost relative to the first-personal version: does the cogito establish the existence of a subject of thought, or only the occurrence of a thought, and what turns on the difference. Second, the question should introduce a case of my devising and ask candidates to reason about it: a being is created fully formed, endowed with vivid apparent memories of a long past, thinks a single thought, and is annihilated one second later. Can this being run the cogito successfully? Can it run a retrospective cogito about its own apparent past (I was thinking yesterday, so I existed yesterday)? The point of the case is to test whether candidates understand that the cogito, however interpreted, secures at most existence at a time — and to see whether they can say why the synchronic version survives the case while the diachronic version does not, and what that shows about the gap between the cogito and the conclusion Descartes actually needs further into Meditation II, namely a persisting thinking thing with a nature he can investigate.

Please provide: (1) the question text itself, worded so that a candidate who merely recites the three-step reconstruction and the circularity objection will clearly fail; (2) a model answer of about 600 words that takes a definite stand on the inference-versus-performance issue and on whether the first-personal formulation carries weight the impersonal one does not; (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks for each (total 25) and, for each point, a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker candidates get it wrong (for instance, conflating certainty that a thought is occurring with certainty about a persisting self, or assuming the cogito delivers knowledge of one's nature rather than mere existence); and (4) one paragraph stating what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer — name the moves an A must contain that a competent B will typically omit.
