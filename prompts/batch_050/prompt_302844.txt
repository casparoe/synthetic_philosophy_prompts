I am designing a comparative cognition assessment around the Dennett-style distinction among Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. My team treats these as successive levels of intelligence: Darwinian creatures act on inherited dispositions, Skinnerian creatures improve through reinforcement, Popperian creatures test candidate actions in an internal model, and Gregorian creatures improve that internal testing by using culturally supplied tools.

Our pilot battery includes three agents. An octopus begins avoiding a compartment after observing another octopus receive an aversive stimulus there, although it has never been punished itself. A robot evaluates several possible routes in a learned simulator before moving, but none of its representations or procedures came from another agent. A child reliably uses a publicly taught mnemonic to solve arithmetic problems, yet cannot explain why the mnemonic works and does not represent alternative methods before applying it. We provisionally rank the child above the robot and the octopus because cultural transmission places the child at the Gregorian level.

Given that Gregorian creatures can benefit from other minds’ accumulated trial and error, should every case of social learning count as Gregorian? And should a Popperian agent that internally tests every strategy nevertheless be considered less advanced than a Gregorian agent that follows an inherited heuristic without understanding it? I suspect our scoring rule is too crude, but I would like the mistake identified precisely rather than merely told that intelligence is multidimensional.
