Teams that build and evaluate large language models describe them in resolutely mentalistic terms: a model is said to hallucinate, to be sycophantic, to know its training cutoff, to refuse a request; alignment fine-tuning is said to make the model honest and helpful, and interpretability researchers speak of finding where a belief is stored. The same organizations' documentation often adds that these are mere metaphors for next-token prediction. Take this practice as the object. What does it presuppose about what it takes for a system to have a belief at all -- something like Dennett's intentional stance, on which belief attribution needs no more than predictive fruitfulness, a functionalist requirement that the system harbor states playing the right causal role, or nothing at all, because the talk is eliminable shorthand? Work through the harder cases: does calling an output a hallucination rather than a lie or a mistake already smuggle in commitments about perception and intent, and can an evaluation that scores a model's honesty be made sense of on a purely deflationary reading? Say which parts of the practice strictly require the attributed attitudes and which would survive unchanged if we concluded the models have none, and identify where the practice is genuinely underdetermined -- compatible with several theories of belief -- versus where it quietly rules one out.
