Last fall I nearly missed the only train that would have gotten me to a job interview on time, because I trusted my memory that the 7:40 stops in my town instead of double-checking the schedule. It did stop there, and I made the interview, but a friend with philosophy training insisted afterward that, given what was riding on it, I hadn't really known the train stopped — and that my not checking was proof of it. That conversation led me into the literature on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and I'm now designing a discussion section for an undergraduate epistemology course. My problem is that students learn the labels in a week and start self-identifying ("I think I'm a contextualist") before they've examined what they actually believe about any concrete case. I want an instrument that sorts them by their judgments rather than by their self-descriptions.

Please write me a decision tree of seven to nine questions that begins from a shared starting commitment — radical skepticism is off the table, and the respondent grants that low-stakes and high-stakes versions of bank-style cases at least seem to invite different verdicts about knowledge — and ends at a named position. Every branch node must be phrased as a concrete scenario the respondent can react to without knowing any terminology, and the set of scenarios must include: one where the stakes are high for the subject but trivial for the person judging whether the subject knows; one where the stakes are high for the judge but trivial for the subject; one where an error possibility is merely mentioned and made salient without anything practical depending on it; one where two speakers in different situations make apparently conflicting claims about whether a third party knows something, so the respondent must say whether it feels like a genuine disagreement; and one where a person sincerely asserts a proposition while denying they know it, so the respondent must say whether anything has gone wrong and if so, what. The leaves must include strict intellectualist invariantism, interest-relative invariantism, attributor contextualism, relativism about knowledge attributions, and contrastivism; you may add further leaves (skeptical invariantism, pragmatic encroachment about justified belief rather than about knowledge, or others) if your question set can actually reach them. Each leaf should get its name plus a two-to-three-sentence plain-English description of the view.

Two further demands. First, your questions will generate answer combinations that no defended position occupies; pick at least two such empty nodes and diagnose each briefly — is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, and why? Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate, and state what additional question would pry them apart if one exists. I suspect contextualism and relativism will collapse into each other unless the disagreement scenario is doing real work, and I'd like to know whether strict invariantism can be distinguished by scenarios alone from the view that stakes govern the propriety of acting on a proposition while leaving knowledge itself untouched. Close with one worked example: trace the path a paradigmatic attributor contextualist would take through your tree, question by question.
