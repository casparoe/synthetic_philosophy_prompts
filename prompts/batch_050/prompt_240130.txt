I'm putting together the decision theory unit for my philosophy of economics seminar and I've been informally polling a colleague of mine — she's in the psych department, works on judgment and choice — with little hypothetical bets, mostly to generate classroom examples. Now I want to see if I can predict her next move, and I'd like your help reasoning it through. Here are her verdicts so far. Case 1, the classic setup: an urn with 90 balls, 30 red, the other 60 black or yellow in unknown proportion; offered a prize for drawing black (unknown odds) versus a prize for drawing red (a clean 1/3), she took red. Textbook. Case 2: offered a bet that a card drawn from a shuffled standard deck is red (clean 1/2) versus a bet that her hometown team, which she follows obsessively, wins its next match — she took the sports bet instantly, saying she'd rather bet on something she actually understands. Case 3: a bet that a fair die comes up even (clean 1/2) versus a bet that it rains in her city next Tuesday, where she's lived twenty years and reads the forecast religously but was given no stated probability — she took the die. Case 4: I showed her a little gadget whose workings I refused to explain, but which she'd watched output "win" with frequency indistinguishable from 1/2 over thousands of trials, and I told her the probability is exactly 1/2 by construction (she trusts me on this) — offered that versus an ordinary coin flip, she took the coin. Both probabilities were precisely specified; she just didn't like not knowing the mechanism.

Now the new case. Option X: an urn whose exact composition I tell her — say exactly 40 black out of 100 — but the ball is drawn by an enclosed machine she can't inspect; she just sees the result. Option Y: an urn whose composition I won't specify beyond "contains black and white balls," but she watches me dump the balls in, mix them by hand, and draw blindfolded. So: precise probabilities delivered through an opaque mechanism, versus vague probabilities delivered through a fully transparent one. Which does she take? Give me your prediction, and make the reasoning explicit: what's the best candidate rule she's been running all along — textbook ambiguity aversion (she dislikes unspecified probabilities, full stop), a familiarity-or-competence effect, or an aversion to mechanisms she can't understand regardless of how precise the stated odds are? Cases 2 and 3 seem to cut against each other on the familiarity reading, and case 4 looks like trouble for pure ambiguity aversion unless you get creative about what counts as ambiguity. Walk me through which rule each verdict supports, which candidate rules survive all four cases, and which of those the new case splits.

Then commit: your best guess at her actual choice, with the reason you'd bet on it, and one further test case that would settle the matter between whichever rules you're still torn between. One more thing, since I'm going to teach from this: when you describe the background — what Ellsberg-style choices are standardly taken to show, whether ambiguity aversion is usually classed as rational or as a bias, the competence hypothesis, and so on — please flag clearly what is settled and what is genuinely contested among decision theorists and psychologists. I don't want to present a contested reading as consensus to the students.
