I am a novelist, and for some years I have been subjecting my drafts to the same small panel of trusted readers, putting to them a question that recurs in my work: may the narrative treat this character as genuinely answerable for what he does — a fit object of the reader's condemnation — or must it withhold judgement and offer him instead as a figure of pity, or of horror, or of plain bad luck? Their verdicts to date:

- A smuggler who knows the mores of his port town, avows them sincerely, feels a genuine pang of guilt, and smuggles all the same: answerable.
- A foundling raised beyond the reach of any community, who has never learnt that promises bind and betrays his benefactor in calamitous ignorance: not answerable.
- A man of profoundly limited understanding, incapable of following rules of any kind, however patiently they are explained to him: not answerable, whatever the harm.
- An executioner whose office is sanctioned by his own order but condemned by the wider society in which he moves; he avows the righteousness of that office with complete sincerity, can state the outside verdict as accurately as any of its critics, and is inwardly sickened by every hanging: answerable. My readers were unmoved by the avowal; the nausea, they said, showed that on some level he knew.

I am now plotting a novel around an antagonist drawn from the clinical profile of the psychopath: a man who can articulate his society's moral code more precisely than most of its upstanding members, who predicts infallibly which of his acts others will condemn, and who will concede, accurately, that what he does is 'wrong' — in much the same tone in which he might concede that it is unlawful or unfashionable — but in whom no wrongness has the slightest grip: no guilt, no aversion, no surprise at the revulsion of others.

Before I write the final act, I should like to know how my panel will rule, since the architecture of the ending depends upon it. Please predict their verdict and give your reasons; set out the rule their four verdicts appear to embody, together with the rival rules that fit the same record equally well, and state what each rule predicts in the present case — whether moral knowledge consists in command of the relevant norms, in some felt uptake of them, in the mere capacity to have known, or in something else again. Where the rules diverge, describe the further character whose case would force the panel to disclose which rule it is really running. Finally, tell me what follows for the telling: if my antagonist is answerable, whether my narrator may condemn him outright, and if he is not, whether the story owes him something closer to the manner in which one writes about a storm.
