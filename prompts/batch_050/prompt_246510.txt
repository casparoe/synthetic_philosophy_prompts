So last week I told two coworkers that our department head was probably interviewing at a competitor. I want to be clear I didn't just blurt it out as fact -- I said it hedged, like "don't repeat this, but I'm fairly sure Dana's been talking to people at Meridian." And I had what felt like decent reasons at the time: she'd taken two unexplained half-days off, and a guy I know over there mentioned she'd been "in for conversations" or something. Anyway, turns out it's completely false, she was dealing with a family medical thing, and obviously it got back to her and she's furious.

Now my friends are split and I genuinely don't know who's right. One of them says I did nothing wrong because I hedged -- I never claimed to *know*, I calibrated my language to my confidence, and everything I literally said ("I'm fairly sure that...", "I heard that...") was technically true. I was fairly sure. I had heard it. My other friend says the hedging is a total dodge, because everyone who heard me walked away believing Dana was leaving, which is exactly what I wanted them to walk away with, so I own that belief regardless of how I dressed it up.

And there's a second thing bugging me: Dana's actual complaint isn't really about the hedge at all. She said something like it would've been more forgivable if I'd known it was true -- like the problem is that I passed it on without being *in a position* to, not that it turned out false. Which seems almost backwards to me? If it had been true, surely she'd be just as mad?

So give me an actual answer, not "it's complicated": when you pass something on with a hedge, what exactly are you on the hook for -- whether it's true, whether you believed it, or whether your evidence was good enough? Does the hedge change what you said, or just the vibe? And practically: do I owe Dana an apology, and if so, what precisely am I apologizing *for*? Because "sorry it turned out to be false" feels off if the real wrong happened the moment I opened my mouth.
