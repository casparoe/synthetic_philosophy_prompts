I'm preparing to interview a guest for a university philosophy podcast that I help produce, and I'd like your help designing the questions. The guest has written a recent book defending a broadly Harsanyian position: that rational choice under genuine ignorance is simply expected utility maximization with equal probabilities assigned to the possible states, and that maximin-style reasoning of the sort Rawls invoked is only ever rational as expected-utility reasoning in disguise, once utilities are allowed to properly reflect how much the agent cares about avoiding the worst outcomes.

This has recently stopped being an academic exercise for me. A few months ago I had to decide whether to leave a stable position for a one-shot opportunity whose probabilities I honestly could not estimate — no relevant frequencies, no repetition, no insurance available, and a worst case I could not fully recover from. Working through the Rawls–Harsanyi disagreement in that frame left me feeling that both sides move faster than they should, and I'd like the interview to slow them down.

Please write a sequence of about eight questions for the guest. Each question should state the main question, then name the reply a sophisticated defender of the book's position would most likely give, then give the follow-up that makes that reply cost something. I'd like the sequence to be cumulative rather than a list, and to cover at least the following pressure points, in whatever order you think builds best:

First, the equiprobability step. If ignorance is really complete, the principle of indifference is doing all the work, and its recommendations are notoriously sensitive to how the state space is carved up. The standard reply is that one should use the most natural or finest partition; the follow-up should press on what makes a partition natural without smuggling back in information the agent by hypothesis lacks.

Second, the absorption move. If maximin choices can always be redescribed as expected-utility maximization under a sufficiently concave or lexically structured utility function, then the disagreement seems to collapse into a dispute about which utility functions are permissible — and the book's central claim starts to look unfalsifiable, since no choice pattern could refute it. I want a question that forces the guest to say what the view actually rules out.

Third, the analogy between states and persons. Harsanyi's aggregation reasoning treats uncertainty over which state will obtain on a par with uncertainty over which person one will turn out to be, and Rawls's objection rests on the separateness of persons: gains to one cannot simply compensate losses to another. I'd like a question about whether the book's defense of averaging under ignorance quietly depends on that analogy, and whether it survives if the analogy is denied.

Fourth, the scope of Rawls's actual claim. Rawls never proposed maximin as a general decision rule; he invoked it only under specific conditions, including genuinely grave stakes and the absence of grounds for assigning probabilities. I want a question about whether the book refutes a general maximin nobody held, and whether the guest concedes maximin within Rawls's restricted domain — and if so, what that does to the book's thesis.

Fifth, one-shot decisions. The intuitive case for averaging is strongest where choices repeat and losses average out; my situation had no such structure. The likely reply appeals to coherence considerations, and I'd like the follow-up to press on whether those considerations assume the separability across states that is precisely what a maximin reasoner denies.

Finally, I'd like the sequence to end with a question about what could count against the guest's view — what result, example, or body of considered judgment would lead the guest to concede that maximin reasoning is sometimes rational in its own right rather than as disguised expected-utility reasoning.

No biographical questions, no questions about the writing process, and nothing the guest could answer with a summary of the book's introduction.
