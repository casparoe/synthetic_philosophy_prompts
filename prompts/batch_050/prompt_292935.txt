You are a moral philosopher specializing in the theory of normative powers. Think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the candidate diagnoses before committing to one.

The debate over why promises bind has stalled. Two broad camps have been exchanging the same stock of test cases for decades. The first camp grounds promissory obligation in what promising does to other people: it invites expectations and reliance, or constitutes an assurance, and breaking it wrongs the promisee by abusing that invitation (the practice and expectation views). The second camp grounds it in what promising does to the promisor: it is an exercise of a normative power that transfers authority over whether the promisor acts, so the obligation survives even when no one relies on or even knows about the promise (the will or rights-transfer view). Both camps reach for the same cases — coerced promises, promises with immoral content, promises the promisee never learns of, secret promises broken with no harm done — and both claim the cases support them.

My hypothesis is that the dispute is not really about promises but about which cases count as the data: the expectation camp treats ordinary reliance cases as central and waiver as peripheral; the will camp treats the promisee's power to release the promisor as central and reliance as a side effect. Diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is it a substantive disagreement about normative powers, a verbal disagreement where each side answers a different question under the word "binding," a standoff of starting intuitions, or a concealed empirical question about what promising actually does to people? Support the diagnosis from what each side actually says about the stock cases, and say what would count as progress given it — a distinction, a better taxonomy of cases, a changed question — or argue that none should be expected.
