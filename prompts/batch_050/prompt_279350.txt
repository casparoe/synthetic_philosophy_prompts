I am taking an undergraduate course in metaphysics, and we have just finished the unit on presentism and eternalism. In our discussion section a classmate defended the following argument. She advertises it as a refutation of presentism, but by the end it appears to refute something much stronger, and neither of us can locate where it goes wrong.

(1) If presentism is true, then only present events exist.
(2) According to special relativity, which events are simultaneous with a given event is relative to an inertial frame; there is no frame-independent fact of the matter.
(3) So if presentism is true, which events exist is likewise relative to an inertial frame.
(4) But existence cannot be frame-relative. Inertial frames are coordinate systems we adopt for convenience, and what exists cannot depend on our choice of coordinates.
(5) Therefore presentism is false: past and future events exist just as present ones do.
(6) If future events exist, then the outcomes of my current deliberations exist already, and nothing I now do can make it the case that those events fail to obtain.
(7) So deliberation is pointless: whatever event results from my deciding is part of reality regardless of how I decide.
(8) Deliberation is not pointless.
(9) Therefore something between (1) and (7) must give.

My own inclination is to hold on to (2) and (8) at almost any price, since I am not prepared to revise either the physics or my ordinary practice of deliberating. That seems to leave (4) and (6) as the candidates. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if the fault lies in (6), I would like to know exactly what I am thereby denying: does rejecting it commit me to saying that eternalism settles nothing about the future, or does it commit me to some specific account of what deliberation is? And if instead the fault lies earlier, I would like to know why (4) looked so compelling in the first place.
