(1) The assigned question.

Let H be the hypothesis that all ravens are black.

(a) State the raven paradox precisely: identify the two principles about confirmation which, together with an elementary logical equivalence, entail that observing a white shoe confirms H.

(b) Give the standard Bayesian resolution: explain, in terms of prior probabilities and the relative sizes of the relevant classes of objects, why a white shoe confirms H only negligibly, why a black raven confirms it more, and under what sampling circumstances a white shoe does not confirm H at all.

(c) Consider the following case. An ecologist knows that an island holds exactly 1,000 birds and that exactly one of the following is true:

H1: 200 ravens, every one of them black, and 800 other birds.
H2: 900 ravens, of which 890 are black and 10 white, and 100 other birds.

She nets one bird at random from the island's bird population, and it is a black raven. Does this observation confirm or disconfirm the proposition that all of the island's ravens are black, relative to these two alternatives? What does the case show about the adequacy of the simple instance model of confirmation relied on in parts (a) and (b)?

(2) The student's answer.

(a) The paradox is generated by two principles. The first is Nicod's criterion: a generalization of the form "all Fs are Gs" is confirmed by its instances, that is, by objects that are both F and G. The second is the equivalence condition: evidence that confirms a hypothesis confirms any hypothesis logically equivalent to it. Now "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, "all non-black things are non-ravens." A white shoe is a non-black thing that is not a raven, hence an instance of the contrapositive; by Nicod's criterion it confirms the contrapositive, and by the equivalence condition it confirms H. This is absurd, since ornithologists do not study ravens by examining footwear.

(b) The Bayesian response is to accept the conclusion but drain it of content. Confirmation is a matter of degree, fixed by Bayes's theorem, and the shoe's confirmation is negligible. Roughly: when a randomly sampled object turns out to be a black raven, one of the relatively few ravens that might have falsified H has failed to do so; when a randomly sampled object turns out to be a non-black non-raven, one of the astronomically many non-black objects that might have been a raven has failed to be one. The degrees of confirmation stand in inverse ratio to the sizes of these classes: if non-black objects outnumber ravens by a factor of a million, the raven confirms exactly a million times as much as the shoe. As for when the shoe confirms not at all: if the object is selected in a way that already settles its status as a non-raven — I pull a shoe from the closet, already knowing it is a shoe, and only then observe its color — then its turning out to be white teaches me nothing about H, since a shoe was never a candidate counterexample. So in the usual case the confirmation is zero; it is only when I draw an object at random and learn its color and its kind at once that the shoe confirms, albeit negligibly.

(c) The observation confirms the generalization, though only slightly. Given that the netted bird is a raven, the probability that it is black is 1 under H1 and 890/900, about 0.989, under H2. The likelihood ratio therefore favors H1 over H2 by roughly 1.011 to 1, so the black raven confirms H1, and with it the proposition that all the island's ravens are black. This is exactly the moral of part (b): positive instances confirm, but weakly. Nor does the case embarrass the instance model. The black raven is a genuine positive instance of the generalization, so Nicod's criterion correctly predicts confirmation here; the paradoxical flavor of part (a) never arises, because nothing like a white shoe is involved. If anything, the case vindicates the instance model once confirmation is treated quantitatively.
