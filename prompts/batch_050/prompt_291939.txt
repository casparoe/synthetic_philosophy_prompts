I am a novelist at work on a book in which two of the central characters — Ingrid, a career official in the Norwegian tax administration, and Daniel, her American brother-in-law, a building contractor from rural Ohio — spend a long evening arguing about whose country's default posture toward government is the more reasonable one: Scandinavian confidence or American wariness. Write their dialogue. Both must be equally intelligent and informed; neither is to be a caricature, and each must answer the other's actual arguments rather than trade stock lines. The exchange should get at the hard questions underneath the debate: whether Ingrid's trust is warranted by a track record or is partly self-fulfilling — institutions working well because people treat them as trustworthy — and whether an attitude can be rational only because it is widespread; whether Daniel's suspicion is a proportionate reading of American history or an inherited reflex that itself degrades the government it distrusts, through whom it deters from public service and what it licenses citizens to believe; whether the two are even disagreeing about the same object, since trust in fellow citizens, in the administrative state, and in elected politicians are different things; and what "reasonable" should mean here — epistemically justified, instrumentally sensible for the person holding it, or a civic obligation. Give the argument real movement, with concessions and at least one point where each lands a blow the other cannot fully parry; it need not end in agreement. Keep the empirics to well-established general findings rather than invented statistics. After the dialogue, append a short note to me flagging which of the characters' claims rest on genuinely contested ground — the causal direction between trust and good institutions, whether high trust can be deliberately built or only grown — so that I do not unknowingly put a disputed view in a character's mouth as though it were settled.
