I'm a staff software engineer at a company running a multi-tenant SaaS platform (scheduling and dispatch software for mid-sized logistics firms). A few times a year, two serious incidents coincide and our response capacity only covers one of them — say, the one engineer who understands the failing subsystem can only fix one before the other tenant has been down for half a day. Our current runbook is simple: whichever incident affects more user accounts gets the resource, every time, no matter the margin.

I recently went down a rabbit hole on a debate in moral philosophy about whether "the numbers should count." As I understand it, a philosopher named John Taurek argued in a well-known paper from the 1970s that if you can save either one stranger or five strangers but not all six, you are not required to save the five: losses don't aggregate into one giant loss, because each loss is suffered by someone individually, and a fair coin flip between the two groups respects everyone as an equal. Critics answer that each of the five has the same claim to be saved as the one, and refusing to let the extra four make any difference fails to take them seriously as persons. Others stake out middle ground, like giving each group a chance proportional to its size, so numbers act as weights rather than as a sum that automatically wins. I've never studied philosophy, and I'm not asking you to settle the debate. I want it translated into a decision procedure my on-call team can run.

Concretely, give me:

1. An ordered sequence of questions the on-call engineer asks about two competing incidents, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where the procedure terminates in a decision. It must be executable in under ten minutes by someone with no philosophy background, and it must survive a postmortem review by executives and by the customer who lost.
2. A handling of harm severity. Taurek's argument is about deaths; my incidents range from mild inconvenience to data loss to one customer whose platform feeds a hospital's courier routing. Decide whether and where the anti-aggregation reasoning transfers to harms that aren't lives, and build in at least one severity tier where plain aggregation is obviously correct.
3. A defensible default rule for same-tier conflicts (strict numbers, coin flip, or weighted lottery), justified as a translation of the philosophical debate rather than an engineering convenience. State explicitly which philosophical commitments the chosen rule encodes and what it sacrifices.
4. The points where judgment can't be eliminated, marked as such, with who gets escalated to and who signs off.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a tie (two incidents, roughly equal user counts); a lopsided one (one incident affecting about ten users, another affecting about ten thousand, same severity); and one where the harms differ in kind. Include at least one case where the procedure hands back an answer I will hate, and don't soften it. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do.
