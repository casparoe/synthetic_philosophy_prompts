QUESTION: State the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. Why is it a problem specifically for views that explain the meaning of moral sentences in terms of the attitudes those sentences conventionally express? Then describe one expressivist response and evaluate whether it succeeds.

STUDENT'S ANSWER:

The Frege–Geach problem was first raised by Frege as an objection to the emotivists of his day, who held that moral sentences merely vent the speaker's attitudes rather than stating facts, and it was later sharpened by Geach against prescriptivists like Hare. The problem is easiest to see with an example. Consider the argument: (1) If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong. (2) Lying is wrong. (3) So, getting your little brother to lie is wrong. This is an instance of modus ponens, and it is clearly valid. But the expressivist says that uttering "lying is wrong" expresses disapproval of lying. The trouble is that in premise (1) nobody asserts that lying is wrong — the sentence occurs only as the antecedent of a conditional, so no disapproval is expressed by it there. In premise (2), by contrast, the same sentence is asserted and does express disapproval. So "lying is wrong" means something different in its two occurrences, and the argument trades on an equivocation — no more valid than inferring that the ham is ready from the fact that I am ready. For the argument to be genuinely valid, the embedded sentence must make the same semantic contribution in both positions, and that requires a compositional account of what moral vocabulary contributes under embeddings, which is precisely what an attitude-expression semantics cannot supply. The same difficulty resurfaces for other embeddings: "stealing is not wrong," for instance, cannot be glossed as failing to disapprove of stealing.

The standard expressivist reply is Blackburn's quasi-realism. On this view, accepting the conditional premise expresses a higher-order attitude — roughly, approving of the pairing of disapproving of lying with disapproving of getting one's brother to lie. Someone who accepts both premises but rejects the conclusion thereby falls into a straightforward logical inconsistency, since their attitudes contradict one another just as incompatible beliefs do. Moral arguments are therefore valid in exactly the logician's sense, and the problem dissolves without any appeal to truth-conditions. And since a minimal or deflationary notion of truth is available anyway, the expressivist can even grant that moral sentences are true or false, so nothing is lost. I conclude that the response succeeds.
