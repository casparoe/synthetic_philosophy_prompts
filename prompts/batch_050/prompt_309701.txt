I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in a monastery, and I need help drafting a document that appears in the story. Two of my characters, Brother Anselm and Brother Casimir, have spent years arguing about the morality of lying. Anselm is a strict Augustinian: he holds that every lie — any utterance made with the intent to deceive — is sinful, no matter how good the consequences, and he grounds this in the idea that speech exists to reveal the mind, so a lie perverts the instrument at its root. Casimir defends what the tradition calls the officious lie: he holds that a falsehood told to protect someone from grave, undeserved harm, injuring no innocent party, is at worst a venial fault and sometimes a duty. Their abbot, tired of the quarrel, has ordered them to produce a single joint statement they can both sign — agreed ground, exact disagreements, and the questions on which their difference actually turns. I need you to write that document.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Preamble and Agreed Claims** (about 150 words): a short in-world framing sentence, then a numbered list of claims both monks accept — these should be substantive rather than vacuous, e.g. that a lie differs from a joke, from a falsehood told in error, and from keeping silent.

2. **Verbal Disputes Set Aside** (about 100 words): points where the two had been talking past each other because of words rather than convictions — for instance, whether deceiving by gesture, omission, or technically-true-but-misleading speech counts as a "lie" at all — with the agreed terminology for what remains.

3. **The Genuine Disagreements, Stated Neutrally** (about 200 words): each residual disagreement formulated so that each monk would sign the description of his own position without the wording smuggling in the other's verdict.

4. **Cruxes and Test Cases** (about 250 words): the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one monk. For each crux, give a concrete case both agree bears on it — candidates include the persecutor at the gate asking whether a fugitive hides inside, a dying man who asks after a son already dead, and a false promise made to a tyrant — and record in advance what verdict each monk gives on that case and why.

5. **Author's Note on Liberties** (about 100 words, addressed to me, not in-world): flag where the document flattens Augustine's actual position, where Casimir's stance imports later casuistic developments a medieval monk would not yet have had in systematic form, and any other anachronism or contested reading you leaned on.

One request before you comply: if this task as posed rests on a confused premise — say, if the two positions as I've sketched them turn out not to be genuinely opposed, or if the joint-statement format cannot do justice to this dispute — tell me so plainly in place of the document, rather than papering over the problem politely.
