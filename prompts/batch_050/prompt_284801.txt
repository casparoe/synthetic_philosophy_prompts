So I'm a retired organic chemist, spent 40 years in drug discovery, and in my retirement I've gotten sucked into the philosophy and history of science and now, apparently, into sociology. I just finished reading Weber's Protestant Ethic (the Talcott Parsons translation, which I gather people grumble about) and then fell down a rabbit hole reading about the fight that's been going on for over a century over whether he's right that Calvinist values helped cause modern capitalism. What baffles me is that the debate seems completely stuck. The materialists point at the same cities Weber pointed at, the Weberians point at the same documents the materialists point at, everyone seems to agree on roughly what the historical record contains, and yet nobody's position has budged. In chemistry, when two groups interpret the same data differently, you design an experiment that discriminates between the mechanisms, and then one side has to cave. That clearly hasn't happened here.

Here's my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis since I'm a beginner at this: the disagreement isn't really about the facts at all but about what has to be true for something like an ethic or a value system to count as a *cause*. I keep thinking of it like the question of whether the spark or the oxygen "caused" the fire -- the answer seems to depend entirely on what you're holding fixed and what contrast you have in mind. The Marxist-flavored critics seem to treat ideas as basically downstream of economic arrangements, so for them Weber is just getting the direction wrong; Weber himself seems to only need values to be one independent factor among several, not the sole engine; and the modern economic historians with their regressions seem to be answering a third question entirely, something like "does literacy or denomination predict growth," which neither of the other two would even recognize as the thesis. Am I right that this is the structure of the thing?

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on Weber. Is this a substantive disagreement about history, a verbal one about the word "cause," three different questions wearing the same costume, or a clash of starting intuitions (about whether humans are driven by meaning or by material interest) that neither side can really argue for from neutral ground? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress here -- is there a distinction, a kind of evidence, or a reframing that could actually move it, or is this the kind of dispute that only ends when people get bored and the field renames the question? If it helps, imagine you're explaining to a scientist why his instinct -- go find the discriminating experiment -- does or doesn't apply.
