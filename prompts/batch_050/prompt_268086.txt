Examination question:

Do mental-rotation and image-scanning results support the claim that some cognition uses depictive, spatially organised representations rather than only propositional representations? Your answer should explain the difference between depictive and propositional formats, state Kosslyn’s and Pylyshyn’s broad positions, explain how chronometric evidence bears on the dispute, and assess the objection that subjects may reproduce the expected patterns through their understanding of the task or through tacit knowledge. Distinguish the phenomenology of having an image from the format of the underlying representation.

Student answer:

Kosslyn holds that at least some mental imagery is depictive. An image is depictive not because it is literally a photograph in the head, but because its parts and their spatial relations correspond functionally to the parts and relations of the represented object. Rotating or scanning such a representation is therefore an operation over a spatially organised structure. Pylyshyn instead holds that cognition operates over propositional representations. The vivid picture of which we are consciously aware is a surface accompaniment of cognition and therefore has no genuine causal role in the processing.

The mental-rotation evidence strongly favours Kosslyn. Subjects generally take longer to decide whether two figures match as the angular difference between them increases. If the figures were represented propositionally, there would be no reason for response time to vary continuously with angle. The data are naturally explained by subjects transforming a spatial representation through intermediate orientations, much as a physical object would have to pass through intermediate positions when rotated.

Image-scanning results provide further support. When subjects report scanning between landmarks on a memorised map, response times tend to increase with the represented distance. A depictive representation explains this because moving attention across a spatial medium takes longer when more spatial structure must be traversed. A propositional system could contain one proposition stating that one landmark is farther from a second landmark than a third is. However, merely adding such propositions would describe the result without explaining the graded pattern of processing times, unless the propositional theory introduced additional assumptions that made it resemble the depictive account.

Pylyshyn’s tacit-knowledge objection is that subjects consciously work out what pattern the experimenter expects and then deliberately reproduce it. That explanation is implausible because participants often say that the rotation or scanning feels automatic rather than calculated, and they may not know the relevant experimental hypothesis. Tacit knowledge therefore cannot explain the findings.

The evidence does not logically prove the depictive theory, since a sufficiently elaborate propositional model could predict the same response times. Nevertheless, mental rotation and image scanning provide good abductive support for Kosslyn’s position because the spatial account connects the format of the representation to the observed chronometric pattern in a simpler and more explanatory way.
