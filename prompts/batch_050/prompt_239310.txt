I am designing the marking scheme for an interdisciplinary prize essay on the evaluation of long-range social-scientific predictions, and I want the scheme to rest on a proper specification rather than on my intuitions. The target class of predictions is the Malthusian one: sweeping, multi-decade forecasts of scarcity or collapse derived from an identified mechanism, of which Malthus's 1798 Essay, Ehrlich's late-1960s population forecasts, and the 1972 Limits to Growth modelling are the canonical instances. All three failed on their stated timelines, yet defenders of each insist the underlying mechanism was sound and the forecast merely premature, misread, or conditional. Your task is to write the desiderata that any general criterion for judging such predictions must satisfy — a requirements list for a theory of when a long-range forecast has been falsified, when it has been legitimately rescued, and when 'right but early' is a defensible verdict rather than an immunising manoeuvre.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should cover, at minimum, the following problems, but please add any I have missed:

1. The horizon problem: a prediction without a stated expiry can never lose, so any adequate criterion must say something about open-ended timelines; but a hard rule that unfalsifiable-by-construction forecasts are worthless seems to exclude legitimate claims about eventual physical limits.
2. The auxiliary problem: long-range forecasts ride on ceteris paribus clauses about technology, institutions, and preferences. When the forecast fails, how should a criterion apportion blame between the core mechanism and the auxiliaries, given that every failed prediction can be defended by sacrificing an auxiliary?
3. The rescue asymmetry: defenders may revise a failed forecast and claim continuity with the original theory, but a criterion needs to distinguish a revision that was available in advance from one contrived after the fact, without requiring a time machine to tell the difference.
4. The scorekeeping problem: 'early' implies the prediction will come good later. What, if anything, must a theorist stake in advance for the 'early' verdict to be checkable rather than a promissory note that is never called in?
5. The partial-credit problem: Malthus arguably described something true of the pre-industrial world even where his forecasts failed prospectively. A criterion should say whether retrodictive fit earns anything toward the standing of the failed predictions, or whether these are simply different claims.

Please indicate which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are negotiable preferences of particular methodological camps (Popperian, Lakatosian, Bayesian, or otherwise), and flag any subset of the desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable — if no criterion can meet them all, that is itself the result I most need to know. Then identify the single requirement that excludes the largest number of intuitive verdicts, including any of my own framings above that you think smuggle in a contested assumption. Finally, as a brief demonstration only, apply the finished list to the Malthus and Ehrlich cases in two or three sentences each; the criteria and their justification are the deliverable, not the case studies. No bibliography is needed, and please do not recommend readings.
