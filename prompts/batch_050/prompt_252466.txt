I am a PhD student in cognitive science working on introspection and metacognition, and I keep running into a phrase that does an enormous amount of argumentative work in my department: "consciousness is an illusion." It surfaced most recently in a journal-club exchange about whether behavioural report is a good guide to phenomenal experience — one participant ended the discussion by saying that since consciousness is an illusion anyway, there was nothing for our measures to miss. I have also seen the same sentence used to dismiss the hard problem outright, to argue that the visual world only seems detailed and continuous, and, drawing on the "user interface" metaphor, to claim that our introspective self-model bears no more resemblance to the underlying neural processes than a desktop icon does to a file. These strike me as very different claims wearing one slogan, and I suspect some are near-platitudes while others are radical.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that "consciousness is an illusion" could be expressing — for instance, eliminativism about phenomenal properties; the weaker illusionist claim that phenomenal properties exist but introspection systematically misrepresents their nature; claims specifically about the apparent richness or unity of experience; and the interface-style claim about misrepresentation of underlying mechanisms — and for each thesis say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false or incoherent? For each reading, please also assess whether it would actually support the dialectical uses I have described: dissolving the hard problem, and licensing scepticism about report-based measures of experience.

Finally, I would like your view on the standard retort that the slogan is self-undermining, since an illusion is itself a way things seem, and seeming requires consciousness. Is that a genuine refutation of any of the readings, or does it equivocate on "seems" in a way the illusionist can absorb?
