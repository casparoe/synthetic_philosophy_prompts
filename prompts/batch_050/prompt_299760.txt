I'm trying to work out whether "generations" — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and the rest — are real social kinds or just labels imposed on arbitrary birth-year ranges, and I want to settle it by reflective equilibrium. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases. Tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and be honest about where it breaks.

My cases and verdicts:

1. The American Baby Boom (roughly 1946–1964). Real. There is an actual discontinuity in birth rates at both edges, and the cohort shared television, postwar prosperity, and — for its older half — the Vietnam draft. My paradigm case of a genuine generation.

2. The European cohort that came of age during the First World War. Real. A single catastrophic event, sharply bounded in time, that marked nearly everyone in the age range. If anything counts, this does.

3. Millennials (roughly 1981–1996). Real, but uneasily. The boundaries look arbitrary — nothing distinguishes a child of 1981 from one of 1980 — yet the cohort genuinely shares a childhood straddling analog and digital life, a formative-age experience of 9/11, and entry into the labor market around the 2008 crash. My verdict: a real kind with conventional edges, like a species with fuzzy borders.

4. "Xennials," the claimed micro-generation born 1977–1983, defined by an analog childhood and a digital adulthood. Not real. This strikes me as a marketing construct — a nostalgic identity sold to people who don't fit the neighboring labels.

5. China's post-1980 cohort, whose boundary is conventionally drawn at the introduction of the one-child policy. Real. The boundary is set not by an event but by a policy that changed family structure for everyone born after it. A discontinuity without a shared experience.

6. "Generation Alpha" (births from about 2010, boundary drawn at the arrival of the iPad). Not real, or at least not yet — the label precedes any evidence of a shared formative condition. A name in search of a thing.

7. Children who were in early primary school during the COVID-19 lockdowns. Undecided. Plausibly a real one — a shared jolt at a sensitive age — but I can't tell whether two disrupted school years are the kind of thing that makes a generation.

8. "90s kids" as an identity. Not real. Decade nostalgia; a calendar artifact.

The pair I cannot reconcile is 3 and 4. Whatever I say to save the Millennials — shared technological transition, formative years straddling a discontinuity — seems to save the Xennials, whose defining story is exactly a technological transition, and arguably a cleaner one than anything the Millennials have. Conversely, whatever excludes the Xennials (too few years, thin shared experience, label invented retrospectively) threatens the Millennials, whose boundaries are equally contestable and whose shared experience is a collage assembled after the fact. A milder tension: 2 and 5 commit me to accepting both event-boundaries and policy-boundaries, while 6 rejects a technology-boundary — and I can't say why the one-child policy is a legitimate boundary-maker and the iPad isn't, beyond "demography counts, gadgets don't," which feels question-begging.

What I want:

(a) A principle — or, if no single principle works, two or three candidates — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case mechanically. Something like "a generation is real iff a birth cohort shares a causal jolt during a formative window," repaired and tightened, with each clause justified by the case that motivates it. The principle needs a view on at least: what counts as a formative window; whether the shared condition must be experienced as shared by the cohort itself or can be visible only to demographers afterward; and whether boundaries must be sharp discontinuities or can be graded.

(b) A statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would overturn those verdicts or complicate the principle. I am genuinely unsure whether the right move is a defense of 3 that swallows 4 (Xennials are real — micro, but real) or a stricter principle that demotes Millennials to a demographic cluster plus a media narrative.

(c) Your principle's verdicts on cases I haven't listed: the Soviet cohort that came of age in 1991; a future cohort defined by growing up with AI tutors from age five; and a cohort defined only by a birth-rate anomaly with no shared event (say, a two-year baby bump caused by a tax incentive).

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether a principle capturing most of my verdicts exists, and its one-sentence form — then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my cases back to me.
