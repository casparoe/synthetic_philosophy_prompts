I'm in a reading group on decision theory and the rationality of advanced AI systems, and this week two other members, Zoe and Marcus, had a running argument I couldn't settle. It started when Zoe defended a proposal she'd read about and now endorses: when we eventually build highly capable AI agents, we should consider giving them a so-called surrogate goal — a goal we don't intrinsically care about, but one chosen so that other advanced agents anywhere in the universe can figure out, from first principles, that it's the goal we would have chosen. Her reasoning: two agents that can predict each other can trade without ever meeting or communicating, each benefiting the other's simulation or abstract reasoning about it in exchange for the same in return, and a legible surrogate goal is what makes you a viable trading partner. Marcus replied that this is simply abandoning your values: an agent pursuing a goal you don't care about is an agent that doesn't serve you, full stop. Zoe answered that the surrogate goal is chosen precisely so that pursuing it, together with the acausal trades your predictability attracts, delivers more of what you actually want than pursuing your real goals directly would — it's like adopting a convention, and conventions aren't betrayals of anyone's purposes. Marcus countered that conventions work because parties can observe and enforce; here nothing verifies anything, so we'd be spending real resources on a story about distant computers running simulations of us, which he called paying cash for a rumor.

Zoe then argued that verification is beside the point, invoking Newcomb's problem: the predictor's choice there is also fixed before you act and can never be checked, yet the correlation between your decision and hers still justifies one-boxing, and acausal trade has the same structure. Marcus said that even if he granted one-boxing, her proposal cuts the other way, because predictability buys extortion as readily as cooperation: any distant agent clever enough to reason about us could commit to rewarding only those civilizations whose surrogate goals happen to favor it, so making yourself legible mostly makes you mug-able across the cosmos, and the rational policy is to stay opaque. Zoe's last move was that you can adopt a legible policy of trading fairly while refusing extortion, so that would-be extortionists, predicting your refusal, never bother threatening you — being legible about your stubbornness is exactly what protects you. Marcus said this gets the incentives backwards: a refusal policy only deters a threatener who can react to it, but in her acausal setup the threatener commits in advance and gains nothing by sparing refusers, so her legibility accomplishes nothing except advertising which threats will work on her descendants' agents. They both turned to me and I genuinely couldn't say who had the better of it. Please go through the exchange move by move — Zoe's initial proposal, Marcus's abandonment objection, her convention reply, his verification objection, her Newcomb analogy, his extortion point, and her stubbornness defense — saying who is right at each step and why, whether any move rests on a confusion the other side failed to exploit, and who you think is more right overall.
