Civil marriage in most Western countries is a strange bundle when you actually look at it closely. The state hands out licenses to any two consenting unrelated adults, including couples who are infertile, past childbearing age, or openly intend to stay childless -- but it won't give one to two siblings who've shared a home and cared for each other their whole lives. It lets either spouse end the marriage unilaterally through no-fault divorce, yet in many places it still reserves annulment, the idea that the marriage never really existed, for things like non-consummation or fraud about something essential. And it attaches all of this to a package of tax breaks, inheritance rights, and hospital visitation that has nothing obvious to do with romance. So what conception of what marriage is *for* does this bundle presuppose, if any? Does it presuppose a procreative or conjugal view that it's own rules about infertile couples seem to contradict, a companionship view that can't explain the sibling exclusion, or just a convenient administrative shortcut? Reconstruct the implicit theory: say which features fit which view, which parts of the practice are underdetermined and would be licensed by several theories, and what would have to change in the law if we swapped the presupposed view for a rival one. I'm also curious whether folding same-sex marriage into this same institution settled anything about which conception was really doing the work, or whether the institution stays philosophically ambiguous even after that.
