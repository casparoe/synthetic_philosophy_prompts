You're a scholar of Indian philosophy who knows both Mīmāṃsā epistemology (svataḥ prāmāṇya, the apauruṣeya status of the Veda) and the Buddhist logicians' critique of testimony (Dharmakīrti-style: words are reliable only insofar as we can infer a trustworthy, non-deceiving speaker). Take a second to brainstorm before you write, and use American English.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a Mīmāṃsaka and a Buddhist logician on whether testimony (śabda) is an independent, intrinsically valid pramāṇa. The document should have: (1) a numbered list of claims both can actually sign -- e.g., both should agree that perception and inference alone can't settle questions about dharma, and both should agree that a known liar's words don't generate knowledge; find more like this without making them vacuous; (2) a disagreements table where each row states a residual dispute, with each side's position phrased the way that side would phrase it (no strawmen, the Mīmāṃsaka gets to state intrinsic validity in his own terms); (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side.

For at least one crux, both parties have to agree on a test case and record their predictions in advance. Use this one: suppose a device enumerates and prints out all possible finite strings of Sanskrit, and among the output is a text word-for-word identical to the Veda, produced with no author whatsoever. The Mīmāṃsaka thinks the actual Veda is authorless anyway, so does this scenario help him, hurt him, or neither? Does the authorless string have intrinsic validity? Write down what each side must say about the device, and whether either of them has to revise their view depending on the answer. If you think part of their dispute is really verbal (e.g., over what "validity" or "independence" means), say which part and why.
