A colleague in machine learning defends large-scale machine translation with a chess analogy: a chess engine does not understand chess either -- it searches and evaluates, nothing more -- yet it outplays every human grandmaster, so a translation model's lack of understanding is no defect, and complaints, in the spirit of Douglas Hofstadter's, that statistical translation does not grasp what it translates are nostalgia for a pre-computational age. Audit the analogy. Give the correspondence table -- search versus next-word prediction, the evaluation function versus the training objective, checkmate versus a felicitous rendering -- then identify where the mapping holds and where it breaks: whether success in each domain is decidable from within the domain's own formal rules, whether failures announce themselves, what a translation is answerable to that a chess move is not, and what each system could in principle learn from a single example. Finally, say whether my colleague's conclusion -- that understanding is irrelevant to translation quality -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if a better analogy for machine translation exists, propose it and say what it preserves that the chess analogy loses.
