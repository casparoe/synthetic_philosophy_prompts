So I'm in a research methods course (psych major, minor in stats) and we just did the unit on the replication crisis, and my friend in the class hit me with this argument during our study group that I can't find the hole in, even though the conclusion has to be wrong. His claim is basically that the replication crisis shows that the whole p-value framework is incoherent, not just misused. Here's his argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) A p-value of 0.05 means that if the null hypothesis were true, you'd only see data this extreme 5% of the time. (2) So when a study reports p < 0.05, there's only a 5% chance you'd get data like that if there were really no effect. (3) So there's a 95% chance the effect is real -- that is, the probability that the null is false, given the data, is 0.95. (4) But when big replication projects redo published psychology studies that were almost all p < 0.05 originally, fewer than half of them replicate. (5) If (3) were right, about 95% of those should have replicated, not less than 50%. (6) The replications were done with bigger samples and more care than the originals, so the problem isn't with the replications. (7) Therefore the problem is with the statistical framework itself: the inference from p < 0.05 to "the effect is probably real" is just invalid, and most published findings built on it are noise.

He says this isn't the usual boring "p-hacking and publication bias" story, it's deeper -- the math itself doesn't license what everyone thinks it licenses. I feel like something is off between (2) and (3), or maybe (5), but when I push back he just restates the definitions at me. Which step should I actually reject? And here's the thing -- I'd really like to keep (1) and (2), since those are basically the definitions we were taught, and I'd also like to not have to conclude that all of experimental psychology is garbage. But if rejecting the bad step means accepting something weird about what probabilities can even attach to hypotheses (my friend keeps mumbling about Bayesians and priors when I bring this up), I want to know what that costs and whether the replication crisis ends up looking better or worse on the fixed version of the argument.
