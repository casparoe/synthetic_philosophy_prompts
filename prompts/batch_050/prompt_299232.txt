I'm drafting a proposal for a nine-month postdoctoral fellowship in philosophy of mind and need a two-page document. My topic is Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self: the idea, roughly, that the 'I' is not an inner entity but a self-referential symbol -- a pattern that arises when a system's model of the world comes to include a model of itself, and that the felt solidity of the self is what such a loop looks like from the inside. My half-formed thought is that the usual objections to this view all target it as a single thesis, when it is really at least three separable claims: (i) a cognitive architecture claim, that minds implement self-models and that this architecture explains first-person content; (ii) a metaphysical claim, that the self just is the self-model rather than something the model represents; and (iii) a phenomenal claim, that consciousness or 'what-it-is-like' character somehow falls out of the self-referential structure itself. My suspicion is that (i) is fairly defensible, (ii) is where the interesting and contested work happens -- and where critics conflate it with (iii) -- and (iii) is the claim the account needs for its famous boldness but can least afford to defend in its strong form. I want the proposal to test whether this carving is right and what survives of it.

Please turn this into a proper proposal with the following components. First, a precise statement of the central question: something like 'does separating the strange-loop account into its architectural, metaphysical, and phenomenal components dissolve the standard objections, relocate them, or reveal that the three claims stand or fall together?' -- sharpen this so it could in principle be answered. Second, a section explaining why existing treatments leave the question open: both defenders and critics of self-model theories tend to argue about the package as a whole, and I want to argue that the package-deal framing is itself the obstacle. Third, the method: say concretely what I would do across the nine months -- which parts are conceptual analysis, which involve engaging with empirical work on self-modeling in cognitive science and predictive processing (described at the level of positions and findings, not citations), and how the three sub-claims would each be assessed against the objections that standardly get raised against the undifferentiated view. Fourth, the possible outcomes: what it would look like if the carving dissolves the objections, what it would look like if the phenomenal claim turns out to be load-bearing for the other two, and which considerations would decide between these. Fifth, an honest statement of what would count as the project failing -- for instance, if the three claims cannot be pried apart without distorting the view beyond recognition, I should say so up front and explain why even that result would be worth having. Finally, scope: flag anything in this plan that risks overflowing nine months and suggest what to cut first.
