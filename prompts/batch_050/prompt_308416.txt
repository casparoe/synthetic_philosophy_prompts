Having spent my career in experimental physics and only lately turned, in retirement, to the philosophy of the discipline I practiced for forty years, I find myself repeatedly drawn back to Newton's rotating bucket. I should like a decision tree that would let me sort the positions on this case for a reading group of fellow scientists who are, like me, philosophically interested but philosophically untrained.

Please construct the tree as follows. The root is a shared starting commitment that everyone in the group accepts: when the bucket and the water rotate together, the water's surface becomes concave, and this deformation therefore cannot be explained by any motion of the water relative to the vessel or the observer. From there, each branch node should be a single question, answerable yes or no, that a working physicist could decide on the merits without knowing which named position it leads toward. Where possible, phrase the questions as concrete counterfactual or thought-experimental cases rather than as abstract theses: for instance, whether the effect could still occur in a universe containing nothing but the bucket and its water, or whether an isolated pair of fluid globes in otherwise empty space could exhibit an analogous deformation.

The leaves of the tree must include, at minimum, the following five positions, and you may add others if you think the logical space demands them: a substantivalism on which rotation is motion relative to space itself, as Newton held; a strict relationism on which all motion, including rotation, is nothing over and above relations among bodies, in the Leibnizian spirit; a Machian view on which inertial effects are grounded in the distribution of distant matter; a view on which acceleration is simply primitive and admits no further explanation, relational or otherwise; and a view on which spacetime's geometric structure does the explanatory work without spacetime itself being a substance or thing.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first identify the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the five positions within that space. No path from root to leaf should require more than seven questions. For any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, say so explicitly and explain what further question would be needed. Finally, identify at least one combination of answers that corresponds to no position on your tree, and diagnose the vacancy: is that region of the space empty because a view located there would be incoherent, because it would be coherent but unattractive, or because it simply has not been developed?
