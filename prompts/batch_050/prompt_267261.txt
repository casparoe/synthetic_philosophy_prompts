I am leading a seventy-five-minute session for a reading group of longtime rationalist-blog readers. The audience is comfortable with functionalism, Bayesian reasoning, unreliable introspection, and the idea that verbal reports are evidence rather than transparent records, but has little background in philosophy of mind. The topic is the dispute between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are experiences.

Plan how I should teach the problem rather than explaining the whole problem to me. Use the following working contrast: the naive view treats a dream as a sequence of conscious episodes occurring during sleep and later remembered and reported. Malcolm challenges that inference from a waking report to concurrent experience, questioning whether our ordinary concept of experience has the right application conditions during sleep. Dennett takes the challenge seriously while offering a broadly functionalist treatment on which a dream’s apparent continuity and duration may arise through memory formation or retrieval, including the possibility that what seems to have unfolded over time was assembled at waking. Present this contrast as a productive philosophical problem, not as Malcolm absurdly denying that people dream or Dennett accusing dreamers of fabrication.

Specify the explanatory sequence and justify the dependencies. What must be settled first: the difference between having an experience, remembering one, reporting one, and constructing a narrative about one? When should the temporal question be introduced? At what point can the group distinguish the empirical question of what happens in the brain during sleep from the conceptual question of what would count as a dream experience? Identify one carrying example, preferably a carefully described sleep-laboratory awakening, that can sustain both positions. Explain why that example is better than a vivid personal dream, a lucid dream, or a case in which an external noise is incorporated into the dream. State exactly which features the example must contain and which it must omit.

Include a misconception map in the order the misconceptions are likely to arise. Anticipate at least the following: taking Malcolm to claim that dream reports are meaningless; taking Dennett to claim that all experience is retrospective confabulation; assuming that REM physiology can settle the conceptual dispute; treating incorrigible first-person authority as the issue; declaring the disagreement merely semantic before identifying what survives each proposed definition; and concluding that if a report is unreliable, there must have been no experience. For each misconception, say where in the teaching sequence to head it off, what question or distinction should do the work, and how to avoid making either philosopher look naively anti-scientific.

Pay particular attention to rationalist failure modes. These participants may move too quickly from unreliable self-report to eliminativism, treat a clean functionalist model as an answer to the phenomenological question, or dismiss the dispute with some version of taboo your terms. Build into the plan a point at which they must articulate what would count as evidence for a dream’s having occurred as an experience, what would count as evidence for retrospective construction, and whether the two views make any genuinely different predictions. If they do not, require the group to say precisely where the remaining disagreement lies.

End with two checks for understanding: one in which participants analyze a newly described dream-report case from both positions without being told which is correct, and one in which each participant states the strongest consideration against their own initial view. Also tell me what to omit at this level—detailed Wittgensteinian exegesis, Dennett’s wider theory of consciousness, lucid-dream research, and dream interpretation are candidates—and what I should explicitly defer rather than pretend to resolve. Do not provide readings; produce a teaching plan with timings, transitions, and decision points.
