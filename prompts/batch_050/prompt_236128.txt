I'm teaching an intermediate undergraduate course in metaphysics (14 students, one 75-minute session) and we've just spent a week on the problem of material constitution: the statue and the lump of clay, coincidence, Leibniz's Law, and the standard menu of solutions (constitution without identity, coincident objects, eliminativism about one of the two, four-dimensionalist accounts). My students can recite the moves, but they clearly experience the puzzle as a word game -- nobody feels any real pressure when told the statue and the lump are one thing, or two things. I'd like you to design a structured in-class simulation that makes the pressure land. Here is the framing I have in mind, which you should build on rather than replace: the class collectively runs a fictional municipal "registry of objects" for a workshop district. Sculptors bring in matter, commission works, reshape or destroy them, and insure, tax, and transfer ownership of things; the registry must issue and revoke permits and levy fees under a written rulebook the students draft at the start. The catch is that every rulebook decision the office makes has to be *consistent across the whole simulation*: if they treat the statue and the lump as a single registered object, they must live with consequences like the disc that remains after the statue is squashed inheriting the statue's insurance payout, or being unable to say when anything was created or destroyed; if they register two coinciding objects, they must live with double taxation of what is visibly one hunk of clay and with explaining which object the workshop's weight limit applies to. The rules, roles, and event cards (a casting, a squashing, a partial repair, a sale) should force the group to run into the puzzle themselves before anyone names it.

Please give me a fully runnable design: the opening rulebook-drafting phase and what constraints to put on it so it reliably produces the collision later; the event cards in the order they should be played; roles or a rotating-decision mechanism so no single student can dominate; a simple scoring or penalty scheme that doesn't involve real money but makes inconsistent rulings costly in-game; and a rough minute-by-minute timeline. Then give me the debrief: which moments in the simulation map onto which positions from the literature (so a ruling that felt natural in-game can be identified as, say, the constitution view or a disguised four-dimensionalism), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover, and an honest note about which feature of the actual philosophical problem a game like this cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in discussion.
