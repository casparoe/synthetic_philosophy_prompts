"Philosophers often distinguish lying from merely misleading. Explain what the distinction consists in, and then discuss whether it makes any moral difference -- is lying worse than misleading, and why or why not?"

Here's the student's answer (they're generally one of the stronger students in the section):

"Lying is standardly defined as asserting something you believe to be false with the intention to deceive your audience. Misleading, by contrast, is saying something you actually believe is true, but in a way that leads your audience to a false conclusion -- typically by exploiting conversational implicature rather than the literal content of what you said. The classic example: asked where the car is, I say 'it's not in the driveway,' believing it's in the garage, knowing you'll conclude I don't know where it is.

Grice's framework explains how this works: implicatures are generated when a speaker flouts one of the conversational maxims. In the case above, the implicature arises from flouting the maxim of quality, since I'm inviting you to draw an inference my utterance doesn't literally support.

The moral question is whether this difference matters. One might think lying is worse because the liar abuses the very convention of truth-telling that makes assertion possible, whereas the misleader stays within the rules and merely lets the hearer jump to conclusions. Jennifer Saul, however, defends the view that lying is morally worse than misleading in just this way, arguing that asserting falsehoods is a distinctive wrong against the practice of communication. I'm inclined to agree with her: the misleader at least respects the letter of cooperative conversation, and the hearer who is misled has arguably contributed to their own deception by drawing an inference they weren't strictly entitled to.

So: the distinction is real, and it does moral work. Lying is a breach of the norms governing assertion; misleading merely exploits them."
