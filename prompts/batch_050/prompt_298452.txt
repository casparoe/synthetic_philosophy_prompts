I'm a second-year PhD student putting together an application for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and I need a two-page proposal. The background: I'm working on inferentialist semantics in the tradition associated with Robert Brandom, where the meaning of an expression is identified with its role in material inferences — what you commit yourself to by uttering it, and what would entitle you to do so. The problem I keep circling is one my committee raised in a prospectus meeting and I didn't have a good answer to. If meaning is inferential role all the way down, then whenever someone revises their inferential commitments — stops inferring from "whale" to "fish," or revises what follows from calling an action "courageous" — it looks like they've changed the meanings of their words rather than changed their mind about the subject matter. That seems to make rational conceptual revision impossible by definition: every apparent improvement in our concepts collapses into a change of topic, and two people with different inferential habits can never genuinely disagree, only talk past each other. The obvious escape routes all look closed to the inferentialist: you can't appeal to a privileged set of meaning-constituting inferences without reinstating something like the analytic/synthetic distinction the view was built to reject, and you can't appeal to shared reference as the stable element without giving the game away to the referentialist.

My half-formed idea, which I'd like the proposal to build out, is that the resources for an answer already exist inside the inferentialist's own framework rather than outside it. The thought is that the distinction between revising a belief and changing a meaning isn't a distinction that has to be drawn in advance, from nowhere, before conversation begins; it's something that gets drawn retrospectively from within the practice of interpreting one another — the same practice by which we keep track of who is committed to what, and by which we attribute beliefs to others in ways that can be more or less faithful to what they'd accept. On this picture, "same concept, different view" versus "different concept" is itself a status negotiated in interpretation, and the question becomes what norms govern that negotiation well. I want the proposal to state the question precisely, explain why the existing replies to the change-of-subject objection leave it open, and lay out what I'd actually do across twelve months: which parts of the framework I'd need to reconstruct, which historical versions of the objection I'd use as test cases (ordinary scientific recarving of a term, moral disagreement across large differences in outlook, and engineered revisions where we deliberately change how a term works), and what a success would look like versus what would show the project had failed — for instance, if it turns out the interpretive practice itself presupposes the very meaning/belief distinction it was supposed to generate, which would be a genuinely interesting negative result rather than an embarrassment.

Two requests about how the proposal is written. First, don't include citations or a bibliography; I'll add those myself, but you may refer to positions and arguments by description. Second, be careful throughout to distinguish what is common ground among people who work on inferentialism from what is genuinely contested — for instance, don't present my half-formed idea as a settled reading of Brandom's own view, since whether his framework already contains this answer or needs it bolted on is exactly the sort of thing interpreters disagree about. Where the proposal leans on a contested interpretation, flag it as such rather than stating it flatly.
