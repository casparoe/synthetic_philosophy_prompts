So I run a small decision theory reading group (mostly grad students and a few people from the local rationality scene) and we've been stuck for months on what feels like the core fight between causal decision theory on one side and evidential / functional / "updateless" style theories on the other. And I honestly can't tell anymore what we're even disagreeing about, so I want a diagnosis rather than another round of arguments.

Here's what the stall looks like concretely. The CDT people keep producing problems where EDT and its descendants give what looks like the wrong answer -- one-boxing in Newcomb, paying in counterfactual mugging, giving in to blackmail even when the blackmailer only sends the letter because they predicted you'd pay. The UDT-ish people respond that these problems are rigged: the environment is rewarding you for being the kind of agent who one-boxes and punishing you for two-boxing regardless of what you actually choose, so of course CDT "wins" on its own turf -- the problems are measuring which agent type gets rich, not which decision is correct, and CDT can only ever endorse decisions ex post while the agents running it get predictably exploited. Then the CDT people say "being the kind of agent who gets rich" is just a smuggled-in rebranding of outcomes, and if you evaluate theories by which agent-type wins you've stopped doing decision theory and started doing something else (character ethics? agent selection?). Then the UDT people say there's no principled line between "the decision" and "the agent's policy" anyway once you allow predictors and copies into the problem set. Then we go around again.

What makes me suspect this is a weird kind of disagreement is that both sides seem to agree on all the math and on which action each theory outputs in every problem. Nobody's disputing any calculations. The fight is entirely over which outputs count as correct, and more specifically over what makes a problem a fair test of a decision theory rather than a test of what the universe happens to reward. My tentative hypothesis is that this isn't a factual or formal disagreement at all -- it's a disagreement about what a normative theory of rational choice is even supposed to be evaluated on (rightness of individual decisions vs. success of the agent over a lifetime vs. something else), and that this question might not have an answer the way "what does EDT prescribe here" has an answer. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a real hidden question doing the work -- like a disagreement about what "available options" means when the problem involves predictions of your own behavior, or about whether counterfactuals over your own decisions are even well-defined.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Break down where the two sides genuinely diverge -- is it verbal, is it a hidden formal question, is it rival intuitions about a paradigm case neither side can argue for, or are they answering different questions under one name? Engage with what each side actually says (including the "rigged problem" move and the response to it), not with caricatures. And then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a theorem, a new class of problem that cleanly separates the theories without the contested features, an agreement on evaluation criteria, or should I just accept that the debate bottoms out in bedrock and stop spending group meetings on it?

Please write the answer in American English.
