I am a novelist. My most recent book was set largely inside a prison loosely modeled on Halden in Norway, and a magazine has now asked me for a nonfiction essay on punishment. Below is my working draft, which hangs on a passage from Cesare Beccaria's An Essay on Crimes and Punishments (1764, ch. XII):

"The end of punishment, therefore, is no other, than to prevent the criminal from doing further injury to society, and to prevent others from committing the like offence. Such punishments, therefore, and such a mode of inflicting them, ought to be chosen, as will make the strongest and most lasting impressions on the minds of others, with the least torment to the body of the criminal."

Draft:

Beccaria's sentence is usually read as one thought, but it contains two audiences. Punishment is aimed at the criminal, who must be stopped from doing further injury, and at everyone else, on whose minds it must leave an impression. The modern quarrel between American mass incarceration and the Norwegian model assumes these audiences rise and fall together: harsher prisons deter more, gentler prisons reform more, pick your audience. I want to argue they come apart, and that the seam is where my own trade operates.

Deterrence does not require that punishment be experienced as terrible. It requires that punishment be imagined as terrible, by people who will never see the inside of a cell. What forms that imagination? Not sentencing statistics, which almost nobody reads, but depictions: films, journalism, and novels. The deterrent arm of the state runs, to a degree no criminologist will put a number on, on representations. An American prison warehouse inflicts torment that mostly occurs out of the public imagination's sight, and is in that respect wasted cruelty by Beccaria's own standard. Halden, with its single rooms, unarmed guards, and normal furniture, does the opposite: it is nearly impossible to picture as suffering, and so it is deterrent-inert, whatever its excellent record of returning people to society less broken than they entered. Beccaria could not have foreseen rehabilitation as the mechanism of his first clause; he assumed prevention worked on the will, through fear, not on the person, through repair.

Then the uncomfortable confession. My novel meant to indict the prison, but any vivid rendering of incarceration, however humane my intentions, deposits one more image into the stock of impressions on which deterrence draws. If I am right, the novelist who writes about prison is an unwitting, unpaid employee of the deterrent state — and the more truthful the depiction, the better the work.

This suggests a cynical endpoint I do not know whether to endorse or flee: a rational Beccarian state might run humane prisons while licensing, even quietly encouraging, terrifying fictions about them. Call it the deterrence gap. Is that policy, hypocrisy, or just a description of what every society already does?

Please give me comments, prioritized, on the following. First, is my split of Beccaria's sentence into two audiences a fair reading or a rhetorical convenience, and does anything in the quoted passage resist it? Second, the inference from "impressions on the minds of others" to "deterrence runs on depictions" is the load-bearing step; attack it. Third, which paragraph is weakest as argument, whatever its merits as prose? Fourth, is the "deterrence gap" ending a genuine dilemma or a trick I should cut? Finally, flag any claim about Halden or American incarceration that strikes you as factually shaky or in need of a hedge.
