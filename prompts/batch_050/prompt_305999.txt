I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I'm stuck on a scene: a character sees an old photograph and feels she's meeting her dead grandmother face-to-face, while a painted portrait leaves her cold. In The Pencil of Nature, William Henry Fox Talbot describes photography as "impressed by the agency of Light alone, without any aid whatever from the artist's pencil," and I'm wondering what that actually establishes. Here are my instincts:

- An 1890s studio portrait of my grandmother: I see her through it, even if the pose is staged.
- A skilled portrait painted from life: I see a picture of her, not her.
- A live CCTV feed of a room: I see the room, just mediately.
- A photograph developed from a lens that was accidentally left capped: no seeing anything, however lifelike the resulting pattern.
- An AI image that happens to look exactly like my grandmother: not her, even if it gets every wrinkle right.
- A heavily retouched photograph that changes her nose and removes a scar: I still see her, just under false aspects.
- A false-color space telescope image: I genuinely see the nebula, even though no human eye could see it that way.
- A photorealistic image generated by combining fifty real women's faces: I don't see any particular woman, though all fifty caused the result.

Is there one principle behind these verdicts? I keep wanting to say photographs are transparent when their appearance counterfactually depends on the scene via the right kind of causal chain, but the CCTV, retouching, false-color, and fifty-faces cases are making that formula creak. State the best principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and apply it to two cases I haven't given. Also tell me how confident you are and which part of your answer you're least sure about.
