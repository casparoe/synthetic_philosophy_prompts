So I work as a software engineer (mostly ML infrastructure) and have been reading about machine ethics on the side, and I wrote down my current position for a reading group some coworkers are starting. Can you sanity-check whether it actually hangs together before I embarrass myself? Here's where I've landed:

(1) You can't hard-code ethics into a machine by hand. Moral rules always have exceptions, and any fixed rulebook will fail in situations the designer didn't forsee.

(2) So the system's ethics has to be learned bottom-up, from human feedback and human moral judgments, the same way we train everything else.

(3) But human moral judgments are demonstrably inconsistent and biased -- people flip their verdicts on the same dilemma depending on framing, order effects, whatever. So a system trained to imitate us just inherits the mess.

(4) Despite all that, moral behavior in machines is achievable, and it's fundamentally an engineering problem, not a philosophical one.

(5) The machine doesn't need to understand *why* anything is right or wrong. It just needs to behave correctly. A good enough behavioral clone is indistinguishable from a genuinely ethical agent for every purpose that matters.

I don't want a lecture on machine ethics generally, I want an audit: which pairs of these are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are, ranked by how bad it is. For each real tension, tell me what the cheapest fix is and what I'd have to give up. If the whole set is fine, tell me that too, but I suspect it isn't.
