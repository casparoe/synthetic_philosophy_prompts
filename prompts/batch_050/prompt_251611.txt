So this happened in a comment thread last week (the usual place, you know the one). Somebody was making the case that a bunch of human prosocial instincts -- fairness norms, shame, loyalty that extends way past family to whole tribes and nations -- are best explained by selection acting on groups of early humans, the whole "groups with better norms outcompeted groups with worse norms" story. And another commenter shut them down with basically one line: "genes are selfish. selection acts on genes, not groups. this is settled." Thread kind of died after that, but it kept bugging me.

I know the original book is more careful than this -- Dawkins explains altruism-looking behavior through kin selection and reciprocity rather than denying it exists, and im pretty sure he even concedes group selection could happen under the right conditions -- but the slogan has clearly escaped the book and lives its own life now. So what I want is a proper disambiguation of "genes are selfish" / "the gene is the unit of selection": the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. I can already see a few candidate readings floating around, like:

- the literal-psychology reading: genes have motives and wants (surely not the claim, but people slide into it)
- the replicator/vehicle reading: genes are what actually gets copied across generations, organisms are temporary carriers
- the bookkeeping reading: anything that evolves can be redescribed as gene frequencies changing, so gene-level accounting always works
- the exclusionary reading: selection NEVER acts at the group level, full stop (this looks like what my commenter needed)
- the cynical-upshot reading: therefore organisms must be fundamentally selfish and prosociality is fake or always secretly kin-directed

I'm sure I'm missing some and conflating others -- that's the point of asking.

For each reading: say whether it's a platitude, genuinely contentious, or just false, and roughly who would fight about it. Then the kicker: which of these readings, if any, actually supports the use the slogan was put to in my thread, i.e. dismissing group-level explanations of human prosociality as settled-against. My suspicion is the slogan does its rhetorical work by sliding between a true-but-boring reading (genes get copied, fitness is measurable at the gene level, sure) and a strong reading (groups can't be units of selection, so group explanations are illegitimate) that the boring one doesn't establish -- and that the strong one is contested even among gene-centric folks. But tell me if I've got that wrong.

Format stuff: American English please, casual prose is fine, bullet or number the readings so I can reference them in the thread. And at the very end, one last line starting with "Verdict:" -- which reading the slogan was actually being used as in my comment thread, and whether that reading supports the dismissal.
