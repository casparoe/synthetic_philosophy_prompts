I'm putting together a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century philosophy of history and the novel, and the final two weeks pair Tolstoy's War and Peace with Hegel's account of war in the Philosophy of Right. My students always arrive having heard only the cartoon version of Hegel on war, so I want a reading that begins from the actual doctrine rather than the cartoon: Hegel's claim, in the paragraphs on international law, that war is not an absolute evil or a mere external calamity but has what he calls an ethical moment, because in war the state demands that its citizens treat everything finite and particular -- property, private projects, life itself -- as transient, and thereby prevents the ethical life of a people from sinking into the stagnation of a settled, self-satisfied peace. He explicitly contrasts this with the Kantian hope for perpetual peace, which he thinks mistakes a pious wish for a concept. Read the war sections of the novel -- especially the Borodino chapters, Pierre wandering the battlefield as a civilian, and the burning and abandonment of Moscow -- as a sustained, and largely unwitting, test of this doctrine.

Where I most need help is on the double movement. On one side, Tolstoy seems to dramatize exactly what Hegel predicts: the invasion strips away the merely private -- Pierre's inheritance quarrels, Natasha's engagement, the Moscow drawing rooms -- and characters do experience a kind of liberation in that stripping, Pierre most of all. On the other side, the Second Epilogue and the battle descriptions mount an attack on the very historiography Hegel embodies: the great man as the carrier of world history, battles as the work of commanding wills. Hegel famously saw Napoleon as the world-spirit on horseback; Tolstoy's Napoleon at Borodino is a self-deceived actor whose orders mostly never reach anyone. So: does the novel refute Hegel, confirm him at a level Tolstoy didn't intend, or show that his doctrine of war's ethical moment survives the demolition of his theory of great men -- that is, can the ethical effect of war be real even if no one, not even Napoleon, is steering it? Please address where each side would have to give ground, which scenes do the heaviest lifting in each direction, and what the novel's form -- hundreds of pages of ordinary life in which war keeps intruding -- says against a philosopher who treats war in a handful of paragraphs as a conceptual necessity. Do not invent quotations from either text; paraphrase scenes and arguments, and flag where the reading strains. The response should be in American English.
