I've been keeping a private ledger of which so-called generations I actually believe in, and I want to know whether there's a principled theory of cohorts behind my judgments or whether I've just absorbed the marketing. Here are my verdicts.

The Baby Boomers are a real generation. The birth spike after the war is a brute demographic fact, and the bulge has moved through the age structure like something you could track on a graph. The Lost Generation -- those who came of age in the First World War -- is also real, but for the opposite reason: the shared event did the work, and the birth-year boundaries are just how you approximate who was exposed to it. The so-called Silent Generation strikes me as a residual category, a name for everyone left over between two generations that were real, and I don't believe in it. Generation X I do believe in, though I admit the cohort is defined by a stretch of birth years with no founding event and no demographic anomaly. The "68ers" in France and Germany are real -- a cohort forged by participation in (or even just proximity to) a specific convulsion. Everyone under forty is not a generation. The graduating class of 2020, the students whose schooling was interrupted by the pandemic, I keep going back and forth on -- sometimes it seems like the clearest case of a real cohort since the war generations, sometimes like a label that will mean nothing in thirty years. And Generation Alpha, a name I gather was coined by a consulting firm before most of its members could walk, I want to dismiss out of hand -- though I notice my dismissal relies on the name's origin, and I'm not sure origin should matter if the label ends up sticking.

The pair I can't reconcile is Generation X and the Silent Generation. Both are intervals between louder neighbors, both lack a defining event, yet I accept one and reject the other. Something in me wants to say Gen X has a felt identity and the Silents don't, but felt identity seems like the sort of thing a generation could acquire after the fact, and then my criterion would be circular.

Is there a principle here -- for instance, that a generation is real only when shared formative conditions produce correlated dispositions and mutual recognition among members -- or does some single criterion, cleanly stated, get my verdicts mostly right and tell me which ones to surrender? I'd like the principle stated precisely enough that it issues verdicts on cases I haven't listed: the COVID class of 2020, Generation Alpha, the children of the Great Depression, and a birth cohort in a country with no wars, busts, or booms in living memory. And please be honest about which of my judgments no elegant principle can save.
