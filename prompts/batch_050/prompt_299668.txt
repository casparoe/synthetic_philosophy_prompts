I run the internal forecasting function at a mid-size logistics company. My analysts produce probability forecasts for leadership, and lately more and more of the requested targets involve somebody's future decision rather than an external event: the probability that the board approves an acquisition, that our CFO renews a warehouse lease, that I personally select a particular supplier at the end of a review I'm still running. This has produced two recurring failures that I suspect are the same failure. When I try to put a number on my own still-open decision, the number feels bogus in a way I can't articulate — as if assigning 70% to my choosing supplier A amounts to having already decided, and my deliberating as if I haven't is a kind of pretense; and when an analyst forecasts an executive's decision and that executive reads the dashboard, the forecast sometimes changes the decision it's supposed to be about, in both directions. I raised this with a friend who did a philosophy PhD and she said decision theorists have a name for the thought that you cannot treat your own open deliberation as just another fact to be predicted, and that a forecast can become evidence or even a cause in the choice it targets. I want that insight, whatever it precisely is, turned into something my team can actually run.

Please write a decision procedure an analyst can apply in fifteen minutes, before accepting a forecast target, that sorts requested targets into three buckets: straightforwardly legitimate to forecast; forecastable only under handling restrictions (e.g., kept from the decider, or framed in a specified way); and not a forecast target at all — something that has to be handed back to deliberation rather than predicted. Give the tests as questions to ask in a fixed order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and make the procedure specify which distinctions must survive the translation into checklist form — I'd guess the difference between a decision that is genuinely open and one that is settled for practical purposes, and the difference between a forecast the agent will treat as evidence about themselves and one that merely causes them to act differently, are two such distinctions, but correct me if those are the wrong ones. Mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and a senior forecaster has to sign off.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: an analyst forecasting whether the CFO will renew a warehouse lease, where the CFO reads the weekly dashboard; me forecasting my own tie-breaking vote on a supplier decision two weeks before I cast it; and an analyst forecasting which of two routing options a veteran dispatcher will pick, in a case where the dispatcher no longer consciously deliberates about routing at all. Include at least one application where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — ideally a high-visibility, client-facing forecast we currently sell — and close with what the procedure cannot settle, i.e., which disputes about forecasting decisions are really disputes about the nature of deliberation and will survive any checklist. Lead with the procedure's headline rule in the first sentence, then give the full procedure, the worked cases, and the limits, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
