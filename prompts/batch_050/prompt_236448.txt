I am taking a second-year course in animal minds, and last week we discussed the well-known finding that dogs show the so-called guilty look only when their owner scolds them, regardless of whether they actually broke the rule. When someone suggested this still leaves room for a rudimentary form of guilt or shame in dogs, the lecturer shut the discussion down by invoking Morgan's canon, as it is usually stated: "In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development." His point was that associative learning (the dog has connected the tipped-over bin with being shouted at) is a "lower" process than guilt, so the canon settles the matter.

The more I think about it, the less I understand what the canon actually forbids, because everything hinges on what "higher" and "lower" mean and on what kind of principle this is supposed to be. It could be a rule of scientific method, a version of Occam's razor, an empirical claim about a ladder of mental faculties, a special case of evolutionary parsimony, a methodological stance against anthropomorphism, or a claim about when we are licensed to attribute conscious experience at all. Presumably these are not equivalent: a general preference for simple explanations is not the same as a thesis about there being an objective scale of psychological processes, and neither obviously tells you anything about consciousness.

Please separate the distinct precise claims that could be packed into this one sentence. For each candidate reading, I want to know: what exactly it asserts; what "lower" has to mean for the reading to be coherent (simpler mechanism, phylogenetically older, ontogenetically earlier, less cognitively demanding, or something else); whether it is trivially true, defensible but substantive, contentious, or probably false; and whether it would actually justify my lecturer's move of dismissing the guilt interpretation rather than merely advising caution about it. Also say which reading you think people who invoke the canon in animal cognition debates most often have in mind, and whether that reading survives the standard objection that associative explanations can in fact be more complicated, not simpler, than the cognitive ones they replace (as critics like de Waal have argued when warning against what he calls anthropodenial).

(yes, no, or only partly, with a one-sentence reason); Main objection (the strongest single difficulty for that reading). After the table, give me a short paragraph recommending which reading I should press my lecturer on in office hours and why.
