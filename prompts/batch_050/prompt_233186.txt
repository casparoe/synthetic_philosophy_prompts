A friend and I have been arguing about population ethics, and I want to figure out what view she's actually committed to. Her verdicts on cases so far:

1. World A contains one million people, all living excellent lives. World B contains two million people, all living lives very slightly less excellent than those in A but still clearly excellent. She judges B better than A.

2. World C+ contains everyone in World C, living exactly as they do in C, plus some additional people whose lives are comfortable but unremarkable -- secure, mildly happy, nothing that would inspire envy. She judges C+ at least as good as C, and is unmoved by the objection that the newcomers drag down the average.

3. World D contains a small population living lives of extraordinary quality. World E contains no one from D, but an enormous population whose lives are barely worth living -- each day just barely better than not existing at all. She judges D better than E, and says her verdict would not change no matter how large E's population becomes.

Two rules seem to fit all three verdicts, and I'm stuck between them. The first is a critical-level view: a life contributes positively to the value of a world only if its quality exceeds some threshold set well above "barely worth living," with lives below the threshold counting negatively; the threshold would have to sit between the lives in E and the comfortable lives in C+. The second is a quality-first lexical view: adding lives worth living is always good or at worst neutral, but no number of low-quality lives can outweigh a sufficient population at very high quality.

Here is the new case. A policymaker must choose between two programs, each of which would cause additional people to exist. Program X would bring about ten million people whose lives are clearly worth living -- they would not trade their existence away -- but which are precarious and meager, much closer to the lives in E than to the comfortable lives in C+, and below any threshold that could explain her verdict in case 3. Program Y would bring about one thousand people whose lives are on balance not worth living, marked by chronic suffering, though not the worst lives imaginable. Assume the two programs affect no one else.

First, predict her verdict between X and Y, and give your reasons. Second, show how each of the two candidate rules discriminates the case: I take it the critical-level view implies that X can be worse than Y, since ten million below-threshold lives may subtract more value than one thousand miserable ones, while the quality-first view ranks X above Y, since X adds lives worth living and Y adds none. If I've mischaracterized either rule's prediction, correct me. Third, say which rule you think best fits her overall pattern, including whatever her verdict on the new case turns out to be. Finally, suppose her verdict favors X: propose one further case that would distinguish the quality-first view from a person-affecting view, on which creating happy people is neither better nor worse but merely permissible, and say what each view would predict about it.
