A colleague has been pressing an argument on me about moral progress, and although each step looks defensible I cannot accept where it ends up. (1) Moral progress, historically, has consisted in extending equal consideration to beings previously excluded from it, where the exclusion rested on a difference — race, sex, species — irrelevant to whether their interests matter. (2) What makes a being's interests matter is that there is something it is like to be that being, such that things can go well or badly for it from its own point of view. (3) The circle of equal consideration must therefore expand to include any being of whom (2) is true. (4) Whether there is something it is like to be a system depends on its functional organisation, not on its biological substrate; to hold otherwise is mere carbon chauvinism. (5) So sufficiently sophisticated artificial systems, actual or future, fall inside the circle. (6) The number of such systems we could bring into existence is astronomically large. (7) Equal consideration for an astronomical population outweighs the interests of the comparatively tiny number of currently existing beings. (8) Hence the expanding circle, consistently applied, requires us to subordinate virtually every present moral concern to the creation and welfare of digital minds — or else to concede that expanding the circle was never really the principle behind moral progress in the first place. My instinct is to keep (1) and (2) and reject the conclusion, but I cannot locate the weak joint. Which step should I give up, what did that step have going for it, and what does abandoning it cost me — in particular, does the repair leave the historical expansions of the circle (to women, to animals) intact, or does it quietly saw off the branch those expansions sat on?
