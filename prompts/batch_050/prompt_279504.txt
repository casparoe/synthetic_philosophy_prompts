I teach a film-and-philosophy seminar, and next week we are screening Thomas Vinterberg's Another Round (2020), in which four teachers at a Copenhagen school test a half-serious psychological hypothesis — that human beings function best with a small, constant blood-alcohol level — by drinking on a strict regimen during working hours, with agreed rules about when to stop. I want to read it against Plato on drunkenness, who is stranger and more interesting on this topic than my students will expect. In the Laws, the Athenian Stranger defends the supervised drinking party against cities that ban wine outright: led by someone sober, he argues, wine is useful precisely because it dissolves shame and self-command, letting you inspect a man's character quickly and cheaply rather than over years of costly observation, and giving him controlled practice in resisting the strongest pleasures. And in the Symposium, Socrates can drink all night without being visibly affected — the ideal drinker, it seems, is the one drink cannot rule. Read the film through this lens. Does it vindicate the Athenian's wager that regulated intoxication can serve self-knowledge and education, or is it better taken as a demonstration of why Plato insists such a regimen needs a sober master the four men conspicuously lack? Point me to the scenes you would build the discussion around, say where the film complicates or resists the Platonic reading — I cannot settle whether the closing dance is tragic collapse, hard-won liberation, or both — and tell me what the film understands about drunkenness that Plato's framework has no room for.
