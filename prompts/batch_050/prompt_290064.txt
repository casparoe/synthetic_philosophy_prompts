Robert Penn Warren's All the King's Men seems to me to stage an argument between two rival accounts of what the will of the people could be, one carried by each of its central figures. Willie Stark comes to speak as though the people's will were a single, real thing with one voice, and as though he were not its interpreter but its embodiment -- so that opposing him stops counting as disagreement and becomes betrayal of the people themselves. Jack Burden, meanwhile, retreats into the doctrine he calls the Great Twitch, on which human action is nothing but mechanical response with no agency behind it, and so nobody, neither crowd nor politician, ever wills anything at all. I want to read the novel as the collision of these two positions: the populist premise that a popular will exists prior to its spokesman and can therefore be corrupted by him, against the deflationary thought that there is no will to corrupt, only applause and crowd noise -- in which case Stark is not betraying the people's will but manufacturing the appearance of one, and Burden's nihilism is the honest description of what populism actually traffics in.

Please help me test this reading in both directions. Where does the novel support the claim that a genuine popular judgment pre-exists its leader -- I am thinking of the schoolhouse fire escape, built by the contractor Stark had warned against, whose collapse vindicates him before he has any power, as though the crowd had been right about something before anyone spoke for it? And where does the novel cut the other way, suggesting that Stark creates the people he claims merely to find, so that the will is downstream of the voice? A further question: Burden adopts the Great Twitch as an escape from guilt, and the novel clearly treats it as an evasion he has to climb out of. But if his nihilism is a moral failure rather than a theory, does the novel thereby commit itself to something like Rousseau's distinction between a general will and the mere aggregation of what people happen to shout for -- or is that importing more systematic philosophy than Warren's book can bear? I am writing this up as an essay for a political theory journal, and I need the reading to be defensible to Warren scholars rather than merely useful to me; if the framework distorts the novel at some point, I would rather know where.
