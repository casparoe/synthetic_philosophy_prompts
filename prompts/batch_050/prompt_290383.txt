My company is piloting an internal prediction market for product launches. Employees can buy and sell contracts that pay $1 if, say, a feature ships by September; prices move between 0 and $1 and management reads the current price as the company’s probability estimate. An automated market maker supplies liquidity, trades are pseudonymous, and each trade over a certain size has to include a short written rationale. Executives see both the price and the rationales. There’s also a compliance rule forbidding trades based on legally material nonpublic information, even though privately held operational information is exactly the sort of thing the market is supposed to elicit.

What epistemological and philosophical committments does this practice presuppose? I’m especially interested in the apparent tension with no-trade results in economics: roughly, if traders share a prior, know one another are rational, and understand that trade is zero-sum, then the mere fact that someone wants to take the other side can make the proposed trade unravel. Obviously our subsidized, pseudonymous market is not that idealized case, but I’m not sure management has thought through which idealization it is rejecting.

Please reconstruct the market’s implicit theory of knowledge and belief aggregation. Does it treat price as a consensus credence, a weighted poll, a signal of disagreement, or just a managerial dashboard? What must it assume about dispersed private information, sincerity, incentives, expertise, rationality, and the epistemic significance of someone’s willingness to risk money? Which assumptions are strictly required for the practice to make sense, and which merely fit it well? Also say which rival views it rules out or sits badly with -- for example, views on which some employees simply know more and should get fixed authority, or on which deliberation identifies considerations that markets cannot aggregate. Finally, explain what would have to change if the company replaced the market with proper scoring rules for individual forecasts, and then with structured deliberation followed by a public probability judgment.

Don’t turn this into a literature survey or give citations. After the main analysis, include a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "strict_presuppositions": an array of assumptions without which the practice would not make sense
- "merely_compatible_assumptions": an array of assumptions that fit the practice but are not required
- "views_excluded_or_strained": an array of rival epistemological views that the market excludes or makes awkward
- "price_interpretation": one sentence on the best reconstruction of what the price represents
- "no_trade_tension": one sentence explaining where the no-trade concern actually bites
- "compliance_tension": one sentence on the conflict between insider-trading restrictions and information aggregation
- "best_replacement": either "scoring_rules", "structured_deliberation", or "keep_market"
- "replacement_reason": one or two sentences justifying that choice
