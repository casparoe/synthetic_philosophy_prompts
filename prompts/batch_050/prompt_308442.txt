I've been following politics for years, and one practice that keeps bothering me is logrolling -- the ordinary legislative deal in which two representatives, who care about completely different things, trade votes. My senator votes for a farm subsidy she privately considers wasteful because the other senator votes for a transit line he privately considers pointless, and both go home and tell their voters they won. People grumble about it, but nobody proposes abolishing it, and I suspect we couldn't run a legislature without it. What interests me more is the harder version of the same pattern: the "grand bargain," where each side accepts something it doesn't merely dislike but considers morally wrong -- one side tolerates an expansion of something it calls a rights violation, the other tolerates a cut to something it calls a basic duty -- because each gets something it considers morally vital in return. Both sides then describe this as mature compromise rather than as selling out.

Act as a professional philosopher who takes ordinary practices seriously as evidence. My question: what does this practice presuppose about the structure of values? Does logrolling only make sense if the things being traded are matters of mere preference, so that the moment either side genuinely believes a bill involves injustice, the trade becomes corruption? Does the grand-bargain version instead presuppose that values are commensurable -- that some amount of one good can offset some amount of a different evil -- and if so, does that quietly rule out any view on which certain goods are sacred or lexically prior? Or can you reconstruct the practice on a different footing entirely: compromise as respecting the fact of disagreement, or as a second-best response to the impossibility of acting alone? Please distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with, and say what would have to change in political life if we took seriously the view that the traded goods are not tradeable.
