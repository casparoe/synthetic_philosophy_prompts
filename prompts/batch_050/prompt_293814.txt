I'm taking a philosophy of science seminar this term, and we've spent the last two weeks on the problem of verisimilitude — the question of how to make precise the idea that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another. I have to prepare a short exercise for my discussion group, and I'd like your help constructing it. Here's the setup I want to hand them. Consider a toy domain described by a handful of yes/no claims about some observable conditions, and let the "whole truth" be a fixed assignment of truth values to those claims. A natural first pass at measuring closeness to the truth is: the more of these claims a theory gets right (and the fewer it gets wrong), the closer it is. My group already knows that crude versions of this idea run into trouble, but what I want them to see is a subtler problem: even where the measure delivers the intuitively correct ordering between two rival theories, that ordering can apparently be reversed merely by re-describing the same facts in a different vocabulary — using new predicates defined from the original ones, so that nothing about the world changes, only the language in which the truth and the two theories are stated.

So please construct a worked example meeting these specifications: (1) Use exactly three basic yes/no predicates, and write out the true theory as a complete assignment of truth values to them. (2) Give two rival false theories, A and B, such that on the simple "number of basic claims gotten right" measure, A comes out strictly closer to the truth than B — say A gets two of the three claims right while B gets only one. (3) Then introduce new predicates defined in terms of the originals (for instance, a predicate that holds just in case certain of the original predicates agree with one another, or some similar constructed condition), and show that when the whole situation — the truth, theory A, and theory B — is restated in the new vocabulary, the same counting measure now ranks B as strictly closer to the truth than A. (4) Present everything in a compact table or list, so that a student can verify every truth value by hand and check the entire example in under five minutes. (5) After the construction, state explicitly which feature does the work — that is, what property of the defined predicates allows the re-description to flip the count — and explain what happens to the ordering if that feature is removed (for example, if the new predicates are defined so as to vary independently of one another). (6) Finally, say briefly how someone who holds that there is a privileged vocabulary for describing the domain — that some predicates carve nature at its joints while the constructed ones are artificial — would try to resist the example, and give your own assessment of whether that response succeeds.

Two further constraints. First, the reversal must be generated rather than stipulated: don't engineer the definitions so that the desired ranking is simply built in — the flip should fall out of the counting measure once the translation between vocabularies is carried out correctly. Second, please flag any step where the translation between the two vocabularies is doing something more subtle than it first appears, since that is exactly where I expect my group to get confused.
