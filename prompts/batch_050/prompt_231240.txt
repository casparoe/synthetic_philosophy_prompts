A colleague in archaeology has a running series of verdicts about which historical episodes deserve to be called collapses. Her calls so far: the western Roman Empire in the fifth century — collapse; the Classic Maya cities of the southern lowlands in the ninth century — collapse; the Mycenaean palace system around 1200 BC, with its lost script and abandoned administrative centers — collapse; Byzantium after the seventh-century Arab conquests, which stripped it of most of its territory, revenue, and urban life while the state carried on — not a collapse; Rapa Nui before European contact, deforestation and toppled statues notwithstanding — not a collapse, since the islanders and their culture persisted; the breakup of the Carolingian empire after 843 — not a collapse, just a rearrangement of political authority. Next term she has to classify the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991: a superpower's central authority disappeared within months, but its population, cities, literacy, industry, and much of its institutional machinery continued under new management. Predict her verdict; set out the two or three accounts of what collapse is — loss of sociopolitical complexity, demographic or territorial implosion, civilizational discontinuity, or something else — that are each consistent with her six calls so far; say which account best fits the pattern and why; and name the further case, historical or hypothetical, that would most cleanly tell the rival accounts apart.
