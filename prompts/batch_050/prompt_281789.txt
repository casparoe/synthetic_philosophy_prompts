Two friends of mine, both biology PhD students, got into an argument over beers last week thats still going, and I want a ruling. The case they were chewing on: a bacterium in a colony picks up a purely random point mutation, and the mutated enzyme happens to let it digest a sugar in the dish that none of the other bacteria can use. Huge advantage, and the mutant starts taking over. The question: at the very instant the mutation first appears, before any selection has had time to act, does digesting that sugar count as the enzyme's function?

Ana said no. A function is what a trait is there for, and that's a historical fact: it's whatever past instances of the trait did that caused them to get copied. At the first instant, the sugar-digesting is a lucky accident, no function yet. Ben said yes. A function is just whatever a part does that contributes to some capacity of the system you're trying to explain, and right now this enzyme's contribution to the cell's metabolism is digesting that sugar.

Then it went back and forth. Ben said Ana's view makes function talk useless to working biologists, who care about what things do, and it forces her to say the enzyme's function changes retroactively once selection kicks in. Ana said retroactivity is a feature, not a bug: it preserves the difference between what something is for and what it happens to be useful for — your nose holds up your glasses, but holding up glasses is not your nose's function. Ben said fine, but on Ana's view a genuinely novel beneficial trait has no function at all until after it gets selected for, and that just sounds wrong. Then Ana played what she thinks is her best card: Ben's view can't even make sense of malfunction. A diseased heart that can't pump still has the function of pumping, and only a historical view can say that — on Ben's view the broken heart simply doesn't have that function, so there's no sense in which it's failing at anything.

Who's right at each step? Please go move by move, say who's winning each point and why, flag anything important both of them seem to be missing, and give me an overall verdict at the end. Write it in plain continuous prose, like an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
