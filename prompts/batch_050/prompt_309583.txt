I'm putting together a syllabus for a first-year seminar on science and its critics, and the middle unit pairs Mary Shelley's Frankenstein with Dennett's cranes-versus-skyhooks distinction from Darwin's Dangerous Idea. On the surface the pairing seems almost too easy: Victor Frankenstein is the purest skyhook-seeker in fiction, a man who wants mind-first creation, a designed intelligence dropped into the world by a single act of rational will rather than built up by any gradual, algorithmic, bottom-up process. But when I tried to lecture it that way, the reading kept slipping. Victor doesn't conjure the creature from nothing; he assembles him from scavenged materials over years of grinding, iterative work, and the creature's mind -- his language, his moral sense, his self-understanding -- develops slowly through observation, imitation, and reading, which looks much more like a crane-like bootstrapping than like installed design. So I'm no longer sure whether Victor is best read as a believer in skyhooks, or as a crane-operator in denial about the kind of process he is actually running.

Here is what I'd like: a reading of the novel that takes the crane/skyhook distinction seriously as a lens, rather than borrowing Dennett's vocabulary for decoration. Does Victor's fantasy of creation map onto what Dennett means by a skyhook, and if so, where exactly -- in the expectation of a finished, explanation-free product, in the refusal to see intelligence as something that must be earned by accumulation, or somewhere else? Does the creature's self-education (the hovel, the De Lacey family, the books) function as the novel's own argument that minds come from cranes, and does that make the creature himself the book's strongest refutation of his creator's metaphysics? I'd also like the reading to engage Dennett's companion notion of greedy reductionism: the other characters treat the creature as nothing but a mechanism, a heap of animated parts, while the narration steadily shows him to be more than that verdict allows. Is Shelley dramatizing, avant la lettre, the difference between a reduction that explains and a reduction that explains away -- and if so, which characters in the novel stand for which failure?

Two constraints on the answer. First, honesty about strain: the novel predates Darwin, nothing in it is literally about evolution, and Victor plainly works with matter rather than miracles, so the mapping is analogical -- please mark the places where the lens illuminates and the places where it is doing violence to the text, rather than forcing a clean fit. Second, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested: the plots of the novel and the content of Dennett's terms are one thing, but whether Frankenstein is at bottom an anti-reductionist cautionary tale or something more ambivalent is a live dispute among critics, and whether the crane/skyhook framing is even fair to the design intuitions it targets is contested among philosophers -- don't present either contested verdict as the received one.
