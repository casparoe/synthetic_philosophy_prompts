I'm drafting the discussion section of a paper on the Identity of Indiscernibles — roughly, the principle that there cannot be two numerically distinct things that share all their properties — and I want to close the section with a battery of alleged counterexamples followed by one serious new candidate. The cases I already have on the page: Black's thought experiment of a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, standing in no relations that would distinguish one from the other; the appeal to elementary particles of the same kind, which agree in mass, charge, spin, and every other intrinsic feature physics attributes to them; a universe in which history repeats itself exactly and endlessly, so that qualitatively identical epochs occur at different times; and, included only to be dismissed, the two peas in a pod, since real peas always turn out to differ in some microphysical detail. For the purposes of the paper, a counterexample counts only if it tells against a non-trivial formulation of the principle — one that quantifies over intrinsic, relational, spatiotemporal, and dispositional properties, but excludes impure properties like being identical to this sphere, since allowing those makes the principle come out true by stipulation.

What I need from you is a counterexample that is not on my list and not a variant of anything on it. I am especially wary of anything that simply re-runs Black's construction with different furniture — two droplets, two photons, two mirror-image cosmoses — because such cases inherit whatever verdict the spheres receive and add nothing dialectically. Ideally, the new case should pressure the principle from a different direction entirely: perhaps from the structure of space or time itself, from the metaphysics of properties and how finely they are individuated, or from some domain outside physics where qualitative duplication seems to arise for independent reasons. Please set the case out carefully and precisely, state explicitly which formulation of the principle it targets and why it escapes the trivializing worry about impure properties, and then give me your honest assessment of whether it succeeds. If on inspection it covertly smuggles in haecceities, or collapses into a special case of one of the listed examples, I would much rather you say so plainly — the section is stronger with a candid negative verdict than with a padded roster of weak cases.
