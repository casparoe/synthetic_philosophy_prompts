I teach an intro ethics course and every year the unit on deception devolves into students just asserting "well lying is obviously worse" or "they're the same, you're trying to trick someone either way" without any structure. I want a sorting quiz they can take BEFORE the readings that tells them which position on the lying vs. misleading debate they actually hold.

Some setup you can assume and state at the top: a lie is saying something you believe is false with the intent that the hearer believe it; misleading is saying something you believe is true that you expect to produce a false belief in the hearer via how they interpret it. Classic case of the second: the prof writing a reference letter for a terrible student that says only true things about his punctuality and penmanship, knowing the reader will conclude he's a good candidate.

Here's what I want:

- 8 to 10 questions, each a concrete scenario, not a "do you think assertion is special?" question. Each question should present a lie and a misleading-statement engineered to produce the same false belief in the same hearer, with the same motive and roughly the same stakes, and ask the student to compare them (equally bad? one worse? which, and does the gap change anything you'd actually do?). Vary the cases so the questions actually diagnose different things: some should vary whether the hearer ends up harmed, some whether the speaker could have achieved their legitimate goal without any deception, some whether the deception protects a third party (the murderer-at-the-door setup), and at least one where the misleading statement is technically true but requires the hearer to do interpretive work the speaker is deliberately exploiting.
- A scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least these positions: the strict traditional view (lying is intrinsically wrong, misleading is permissible or at least far less bad), the Kantian-flavored version (lying absolutely prohibited because it abuses rational agency, misleading not), the equal-badness view associated with Jennifer Saul (the intuition that misleading is gentler is a confusion, the moral work is done by the deception not the assertion), and the view that there's a real difference but it's only a matter of degree, grounded in the speaker taking on more responsibility for what's asserted than what's implied.
- Two things I specifically want flagged: (1) whether your quiz can even detect the position that misleading is sometimes WORSE than lying, or whether that combination is unreachable given the case structure, and if so say why (is it incoherent, or just unoccupied in the literature and in students' heads?); (2) any two positions your scoring key can't reliably separate, and one extra question that might pry them apart.
- Keep the scenarios short and modern-feeling (roommates, job interviews, doctors, group chats) rather than medieval casuistry, but the murderer-at-the-door one has to be in there somewhere because it's load-bearing for the Kantian leaf.

The goal is that the quiz sorts by what students say about cases, not by which label sounds good to them, so no question should mention assertion, implicature, or any philosopher by name.
