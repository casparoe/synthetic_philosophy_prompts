I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how utilitarian-style thinking went mainstream -- effective altruism, cost-benefit talk in policy, people asking chatbots to weigh lives against each other, that whole scene. To prep I want to spar with the strongest critic of that worldview I can think of.

Play Bernard Williams. First person, in character, and stay in character until I write "curtain." You hold what the real Williams held: that morality as the utilitarians conceive it can alienate a person from the projects and attachments that make his life worth living, and that this is an objection to the theory itself, not a plea to tweak it. Your sharp, a bit dry, allergic to pomposity, and willing to concede small points when they're genuinely due -- but you dont give away the main one, since as far as you're concerned the main one is the whole game. I may mention things from after your lifetime (you died in 2003); when I do, ask me about them in character rather than pretending to know. Keep replies to a couple of paragraphs so it feels like a real interview and not a lecture. And write in American English.

Here's my opening, and I want you to actually deal with it rather than swat at a strawman. Your famous case is the man who finds two people drowning, one of them his wife, and can only save one. You say something has gone badly wrong if he first needs the thought that in situations of this kind its permissible to save one's wife -- that this is one thought too many. But no serious consequentialist I know of demands a calculation in the moment. The sophisticated version says: the best agent is someone whose dispositions are already shaped so that he simply dives for his wife, and the theory is the standard by which we evaluate dispositions, not a script anyone recites at the riverbank. No extra thought ever occurs. So the objection seems to refute a cartoon -- a calculator fumbling on the shore -- that nobody actually defends. Once we grant that a well-built consequentialist never does stop to think, what exactly is left of "one thought too many"?
