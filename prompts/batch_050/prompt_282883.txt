I have a set of firm verdicts about when a pattern of preferences across time is irrational, and I want to know whether any single principle captures them all — or, failing that, which verdict I have to give up. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

First, the classic money pump: someone prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and accepts a sequence of small paid trades that cycles them back to where they started, minus money. Clearly irrational — the paradigm case. Second, the same person, but they know about their own cycle and flatly refuse every trade, keeping A and losing nothing. My verdict: still irrational, because the preferences themselves are defective even though they were never exploited. Third, someone who prefers tea in the morning and coffee in the evening, and reliably reverses accordingly: perfectly fine, no irrationality at all. Fourth, someone who at noon firmly prefers exercising at six, and at six firmly prefers not to — and this reversal happens daily, predictably, and to their regret. Irrational, even though at each moment the preference is locally intelligible. Fifth, Odysseus: someone with exactly that predictable reversal who pays a real cost — a forfeited deposit, say — to bind their later self. Fully rational, arguably more rational than the rest of us. Sixth, someone whose preferences are cyclic but who lives a life in which the options never come in sequence: each pairwise choice is faced once, in isolation, and no exploitation is possible. My verdict, uncomfortably: irrational anyway, for the same reason as the second case. Seventh, someone who has genuine Allais-style preferences over lotteries — violating the independence axiom — but who is never offered the exploitative pair of gambles in sequence. I hesitate, but I lean towards calling this rational, or at least rationally permissible, which sits oddly with my verdicts on cases two and six, since those violations likewise never cost anyone anything.

What I cannot currently do is state the principle that separates case four from case three (both are predictable, repeated preference reversals, yet I condemn one and excuse the other), or explain why unexploited intransitivity is irrational (cases two and six) while unexploited independence violation is permissible (case seven). Exploitability cannot be my principle, since it delivers the wrong verdicts on two, four, and six; but a pure coherence requirement struggles with case five, since Odysseus's diachronic profile is incoherent in just the way I condemn in case four, and paying to make it moot does not remove the incoherence.

Please begin your response with your verdict — the single most defensible principle, or the honest conclusion that none exists — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and no restating of the question. State the principle precisely enough that each clause can be tied to the case that motivates it; apply it to my seven cases and flag explicitly which of my verdicts it overturns; then test it against two further cases I did not consider: a person whose cyclic preferences concern trivial stakes only, and a person whose preference reversals are caused by reliably improving information rather than by mere passage of time. If you find that no single principle does better than a pair of principles (one synchronic, one diachronic), say so and show the division of labour. Use British English throughout.
