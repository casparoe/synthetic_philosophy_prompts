Every time generations come up in my group chat, someone drops the same line: generational labels are just astrology for sociologists. You take an arbitrary birth-year window, slap a personality on it ("Millennials are entitled, Boomers are workaholics"), and people nod along because its vague enough to feel true. My friend used it last week to argue that generational talk should be banned from serious social science altogether, full stop.

Act as a philosopher of social science and audit this analogy carefully -- brainstorm the mapping first, then stress-test it. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the zodiac window, the star-sign traits, the horoscope reader who finds themselves in it? Then tell me where the analogy genuinely bites (arbitrary cutoffs? unfalsifiable trait talk? motivated uptake?) and where it breaks. The obvious candidate disanalogy: cohorts actually share formative events -- recessions, wars, pandemic school years, growing up with or without the internet -- which give us a causal mechanism for cohort effects, whereas astrological signs share, well, nothing that could cause anything. Does that break the analogy or just weaken it? And does the analogy's intended conclusion -- generational kinds are useless, drop them -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it only support something weaker, like the boundaries being conventional while some cohort effects are real? If the horoscope comparison misleads, propose a better analogy (I've heard "statistical regularities across fuzzy clusters" floated, but maybe you have something sharper).
