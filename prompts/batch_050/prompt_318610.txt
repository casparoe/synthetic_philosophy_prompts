I drafted an argument for a paper workshop, and it troubles me because it appears to prove far more than I intended. (1) A choice between options is rational only if the chooser can compare the options' outcomes by values she currently holds, at least well enough to say which outcome she has most reason to bring about. (2) Some choices are transformative in two respects at once: the chooser cannot know in advance what the resulting state is like, and the choice itself will alter the values by which she would assess it. (3) Becoming a parent, losing one's sight, and emigrating to a radically different culture are all transformative in this double sense. (4) For any such choice, the outcome cannot be evaluated by present values (one does not know what it is like), and it cannot be evaluated by future values either, since those values do not yet exist and, once they do, they belong to a self whose standards the present self has no reason to adopt. (5) Therefore transformative choices cannot be made rationally; they are leaps, not decisions. (6) But the difference between transformative and ordinary choices is one of degree, not kind: taking a new job, marrying, and moving to a new city all change one's values somewhat, and in ways one cannot fully anticipate. (7) So the range of rational choice shrinks to the trivial — decisions whose outcomes are already familiar and leave one's values untouched. (8) That conclusion is absurd; yet each step seems forced. If I must choose, I would rather deny (1) than accept that no major life decision can be rational, but I want to know what that costs.
