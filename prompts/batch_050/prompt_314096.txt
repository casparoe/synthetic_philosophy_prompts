I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a piece on the ethics of randomized controlled trials. A widely shared essay I read recently glossed the doctrine of clinical equipoise like this: "Equipoise is the requirement that before a physician enrolls a patient in a trial, she must be genuinely uncertain, in her own mind, about which arm offers the better treatment. The moment any evidence accumulates favoring one arm, her uncertainty is gone, and continuing to randomize patients becomes a betrayal of her duty to give each patient the best available care. This is why critics say equipoise is a failed doctrine: the very first promising result destroys it, so on its own terms almost no worthwhile trial could ever be run to completion. Defenders invented the idea to paper over the conflict between a doctor's obligations and the demands of science, and the conflict remains."

My instinct is that this conflates several things, but I cannot say precisely where. Which parts of the gloss would a careful proponent of equipoise accept, which distort the doctrine as it is actually formulated, and does the essay's conclusion -- that the concept is bankrupt and trial ethics should be rebuilt on other foundations -- follow from the parts that survive correction? In particular, I would like to know whether the gloss gets right what it is that is supposed to be uncertain, and uncertain for whom.
