I've been teaching an introductory philosophy of language course, and we just finished the unit on semantic externalism -- Twin Earth, the division of linguistic labor, natural kind terms, and the slogan that meanings are not in the head. My students grasped the thought experiments fine, but several of them pushed back with roughly the same complaint: the intuitions pump works because it's about a fictional planet with XYZ, and real science never actually confronts us with that kind of choice. I don't think that's right, and I want to build a class discussion around a real case: the 2006 reclassification of Pluto by the International Astronomical Union, when Pluto -- discovered in 1930 and taught to generations of schoolchildren as the ninth planet -- was reassigned to the new category of dwarf planet after the discovery of Eris and other comparable Kuiper Belt objects forced the question of what the word planet was ever picking out.

Analyze that episode through the lens of semantic externalism, in both directions. First, the theory applied to the case: what does an externalist say the word planet referred to between 1930 and 2006, given that speakers' associated descriptions (a fairly large body orbiting the Sun, the ninth such body, and so on) were arguably satisfied by many objects no one knew about? Does the Pluto case vindicate the Putnam-style picture on which reference is fixed partly by the world and by expert practice rather than by descriptions in speakers' heads -- with astronomers playing the role the division of linguistic labor assigns them -- or does it embarrass that picture, since here the experts did not discover what planet always meant but appear to have stipulated, by vote, what it would mean going forward? Pay particular attention to the difference between discovering a fact about a natural kind's underlying nature (as with water and H2O) and negotiating a boundary for a category that may be a conventional rather than natural kind. Second, the case applied to the theory: if planet turns out to be a social or functional kind rather than a natural kind, is the episode evidence that the externalist story only ever worked for a privileged subset of terms, or can the externalist absorb it by extending the account beyond natural kinds? Also say where the theory is simply silent -- for instance, on whether the public's resistance to the demotion (the widespread sense that astronomers had taken something away rather than discovered something) is itself data about meaning or just sentimentality -- rather than forcing it to pronounce.

After the prose analysis, give me a structured summary as a table with four columns: Aspect of the episode; What externalism predicts or implies about it; How well the episode fits (use one of: confirms, strains, or silent); and Why, in one or two sentences. Include at least the following rows: reference of planet before 2006; the role of the expert vote; whether planet is a natural kind term; the public's resistance; and the comparison with the water/H2O case.
