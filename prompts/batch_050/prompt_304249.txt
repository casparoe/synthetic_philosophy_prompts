I'm assembling a small working group — five philosophers, two years of funding — on what contemporary analytic epistemology could learn from the Nyāya theory of pramāṇas, the "instruments of knowledge" (standardly: perception, inference, comparison or upamāna, and testimony or śabda). All of us work in mainstream epistemology; only two of us read Sanskrit, and we'd rather not spend the grant on philology. I need a defended research agenda, not a survey of the classical debates.

Here are the candidate questions I've collected, with my rough glosses so we're all working from the same descriptions:

1. The independence of testimony. Is testimony a genuinely basic source of knowledge, or does hearer knowledge always reduce to perception (of the words) plus inference (to the speaker's sincerity and competence)? The Indian reductionists pressed hard on this; so has the Humean tradition.
2. The individuation problem. By what principle do we count instruments of knowledge — by the causal mechanism, by the type of object known, or by features of the resulting cognition? Different principles yield different lists, and the choice seems to settle in advance whether comparison, memory, or gut intuition "count."
3. The status of comparison. Classical upamāna covers learning what an unfamiliar kind is from a similarity report (being told a gavaya is like a cow, then recognizing one). Is this a distinct way of acquiring classificatory knowledge, or a bundle of testimony and perception? And does its modern descendant — learning categories from exemplars and descriptions — force the same question on us?
4. Exclusivity versus overlap. One classical dispute concerns whether each pramāṇa has its own proprietary domain or whether several can deliver the same object of knowledge, and if they can, what resolves conflicts between them. This looks like an ancestor of our disagreement and defeasibility literatures, but with the sources individuated differently.
5. The exclusion of memory. Nyāya famously refuses memory the status of a pramāṇa because it preserves rather than produces knowledge. Is that exclusion defensible, and does the generative/preservative distinction do real work we should resurrect?
6. Extension to modern instruments. Can the causal-instrumentalist framework absorb sources classical authors never faced — measurement devices, statistical aggregation, search engines — or does it break there in a way that reveals a hidden assumption?

Which three of these should the group prioritize, and in what order? For each, tell me: how many of the others are downstream of it (a settled answer that reconfigures several others beats a settled answer that settles only itself); how tractable it is for epistemologists without Sanskrit, given that the framework has to be reconstructed from translations and secondary literature; and what its payoff would be for live debates in analytic epistemology rather than for Nyāya scholarship as such. Also tell me which of the six you'd drop entirely and why, and what finding in the first year would make you reorder the list.

Lead with your bottom line — the ranked top three — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back to me.
