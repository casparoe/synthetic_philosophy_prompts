For the past seven decades, moral philosophers and economists have been arguing about what Harsanyi's aggregation theorem actually establishes. The theorem shows that if individual utilities satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, social evaluation satisfies analogous axioms, and a Pareto condition holds, then social welfare is a weighted sum of individual utilities. Everyone in the literature agrees about the mathematics. What they have never agreed about is what it proves. Harsanyi and his sympathizers take it to be close to a proof of utilitarianism, with the impartial spectator or original-position construction supplying equal weights. Critics respond along several recurring lines: that requiring the social evaluator to satisfy the axioms of rational choice under risk builds the conclusion in from the start, since those axioms encode a willingness to trade off the good of different people across states of the world; that the Pareto axiom is doing more normative work than its innocuous appearance suggests; and, following Sen and later commentators, that the theorem yields a linear representation of social preference in whatever numerical scales are used for the individuals, but says nothing about whether a common interpersonally comparable scale exists — so the apparent sum is an artifact of notation rather than a discovery that utilities add.

The striking thing is that each side's reply to these objections was worked out by the 1990s, and since then the debate has largely consisted of restatement. Every few years a paper appears showing that some variant of the theorem survives some weakening of the axioms, or that some objection equivocates between the ex ante and ex post readings of the theorem, and the sides remain exactly where they were. I have a working hypothesis about why: I suspect the two camps are not disagreeing about the theorem at all but about what would count as a justification of a distributive principle — one side treating a representation theorem under weak-looking rationality conditions as genuine derivation, the other treating any such result as merely relocating the normative content into the interpretation of the axioms. On this hypothesis the impasse is a disagreement about philosophical methodology wearing mathematical clothes.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive disagreement I am missing — about the formal content of the results, about the meaning of utility under risk, or about interpersonal comparability? I want you to engage with what each side actually argues, not to adjudicate utilitarianism itself, and to say what kind of disagreement this really is: verbal, methodological, or factual about the mathematics. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what progress would even look like — a distinction that would dissolve it, a formal result that would settle it, or an argument that no such thing exists and the disagreement is permanent because the theorem is where normative argument runs out rather than where it begins.
