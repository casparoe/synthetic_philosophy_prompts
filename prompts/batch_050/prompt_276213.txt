I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long feature on how knowledge travels from one mind to another through speech. I have spent several months reading the philosophical literature on testimony, and before I commit to a framing for the piece I want a diagnosis of a debate that, from the outside, looks frozen. Please take on the role of a professional epistemologist who has worked on testimony and is used to advising non-specialist writers. Think this through carefully rather than producing a survey; if it helps, sketch the strongest version of each side to yourself before settling on your diagnosis, but show me only the finished analysis.

Here is the debate as I understand it. Reductionists, in a tradition associated with Hume, hold that a hearer is justified in believing a speaker only when the hearer possesses independent, non-testimonial grounds for taking the speaker to be reliable: observed correlations between assertion and fact, track records, cues of competence and sincerity. Anti-reductionists, in a tradition associated with Reid, hold that testimony is a basic source of justification on a par with perception and memory: absent specific grounds for doubt, a hearer is entitled to accept what she is told without first assembling inductive evidence of reliability. Each side has a stock answer to the other's stock objection. The reductionist replies to the charge that her view makes most of what we know unjustified -- the child who believes her parents, the reader who trusts the newspaper, all of us for facts about our own birth dates -- by scaling down how much independent confirmation is required or arguing that we quietly acquire it early in life. The anti-reductionist replies to the charge of licensing gullibility by building in defeaters, monitoring capacities, or a distinction between entitlement and earned justification. As far as I can tell, no major figure has changed sides in decades, and the arguments on both sides are all known to both.

Two observations from my reporting, offered as hypotheses, not conclusions. First, I suspect the two camps may be answering different questions under one label: reductionism reads most naturally as a thesis about the ultimate grounds of testimonial justification, while anti-reductionism reads most naturally as a thesis about what a hearer must possess or do to be justified in a given instance -- and those can come apart. Second, the literature seems to have been built entirely around testimony between persons, and I think a case it never anticipated may be diagnostic: large language models. A chatbot produces fluent, assertion-like output while arguably having no beliefs, no communicative intentions, and no reputation at stake. If anti-reductionism is correct, are hearers entitled to accept such output by default, and does that consequence vindicate or embarrass the view? If reductionism is correct, does a statistical track record suffice even in the absence of anyone whose word is being taken, and does that show the view never really required speakers at all? My hunch is that this case either exposes a shared presupposition both sides missed or breaks them asymmetrically, and that whichever it does tells us what the original dispute was actually about.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who is right. Is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of epistemic justification, a verbal dispute over words like 'justified' or 'source', two compatible theses mistakenly set against each other, or a standoff between starting intuitions -- say, about the epistemic position of children -- that neither side can argue for without begging the question? Is there a hidden empirical question, about how hearers actually monitor speakers or how children actually acquire testimonial knowledge, doing unacknowledged work? And given your diagnosis, what, if anything, would count as progress: a sharper distinction, an experimental result, a formal model of justification transmission, or should we expect the impasse to persist, and why?

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order: "The state of play" (about 200 words; the strongest version of each position and what each is genuinely committed to), "Diagnosis" (about 400 words; what kind of disagreement this is, engaging my two hypotheses), "The chatbot case" (about 300 words; whether it discriminates between the positions and what it reveals), "What would count as progress" (about 200 words; the specific thing that would move the debate, or a reasoned case that nothing will), and "Bottom line for the piece" (about 100 words; the framing you would recommend to a journalist, stated plainly enough to open the article).
