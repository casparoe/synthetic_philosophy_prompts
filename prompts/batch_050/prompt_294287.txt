So I'm on a working group designing the voting rules for a kind of expert panel (long story, but basically a committee that has to give a single collective verdict on several logically connected questions -- think of the classic court case where three judges separately vote on "a valid contract existed," "the defendant owed damages given the contract," and "the defendant owes damages," and the majority on each premise goes one way while the majority on the conclusion goes the other way). Everyone keeps proposing their favorite aggregation rule -- majority vote on the premises, majority vote on the conclusion, some weighted thing -- and the arguments go in circles because nobody has said what we're even allowed to demand of a rule. Before we adopt anything, I want the full spec written out.

So: what must a judgment aggregation procedure actually deliver to count as adequate? Give me the requirements list -- the desiderata -- with the case or consideration motivating each one. I'm thinking of stuff like: the collective verdict has to be logically consistent and complete; the rule should treat voters symmetrically and not depend on who said what; it shouldn't be manipulable by changing the order or framing of the agenda; it should work on any agenda of connected propositions, not just the toy court case; it shouldn't let one voter be a dictator; it should handle the discursive dilemma rather than just declaring both premise-majority and conclusion-majority outcomes "the answer" depending on convenience. But tell me which of these are genuine requirements versus prejudices of one camp, and whether some of my desiderata are really the same requirement stated twice.

The part I care about most: given the impossibility results in this literature (the ones showing that you can't have universal domain, anonymity, systematicity, collective rationality etc. all at once), which subsets of the desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable? If the list itself is a no-go theorem, say exactly which requirements knock each other out and what the minimal trade-off menu looks like. Then, briefly, score the main contenders against the surviving combinations -- premise-based majority, conclusion-based majority, sequential priority, distance-based rules, supermajority rules -- so I can see which requirements each one sacrifices. A table at the end is fine, but the reasoning about the desiderata themselves matters more to me than the scorecard.
