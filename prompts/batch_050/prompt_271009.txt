I would like help identifying whether a phenomenon I have been circling around in my work on conservative social epistemology has an established name, and if not, I would like a principled coinage.

The phenomenon is a specific failure mode in the reasoning associated with Chesterton's fence. The familiar injunction is that one should not dismantle an institution or practice until one understands the purpose it serves; the implicit assumption is that inquiry into the practice's function is possible in principle, and that the burden of that inquiry falls on the reformer. What I keep encountering, across quite different literatures, are cases where this assumption fails because the practice in question is itself the obstacle to the inquiry. A few instances:

(1) A professional body administers entry requirements whose original rationale no living member can articulate, and which appear on inspection to be pure rent-extraction. However, the body has, over decades, absorbed the apprenticeship records, examination archives, and placement data that would allow an outsider to determine whether the requirements track any genuine competence. The reformer's demand for evidence cannot be met, not because the evidence never existed, but because the institution being questioned is the institution that holds it.

(2) A community maintains a costly ritual whose meaning is transmitted only to initiates, and initiation is conditioned on a commitment not to disclose the meaning to non-initiates. Outsiders considering pressing the community to abandon the ritual face the fence argument in an unusually pure form: they cannot know what the ritual does without becoming insiders, and insiders, by the structure of the practice itself, will not tell them.

(3) A bureaucracy's procedures are so layered that the only people who understand why any given rule exists are those whose continued employment depends on the rule's existence, and who therefore have a standing incentive to report that the rule is load-bearing.

In each case, the injunction to understand before dismantling is not merely imprudent to ignore; it is unsatisfiable from the outside, and its unsatisfiability is produced or maintained by the very thing under scrutiny. This looks to me importantly different from several neighboring notions. It is not mere status quo bias, since that is a psychological disposition of the evaluator rather than a structural feature of the object evaluated. It is not ordinary regulatory capture, since capture concerns the diversion of an institution's aims rather than its epistemic opacity. And it is not Burkean prescription as usually glossed, since the warrant here is not the antiquity or the tacit wisdom of the practice but the contingent fact that the practice has fenced off the evidence by which it might be assessed. There is also a harder edge to the phenomenon worth marking: sometimes the opacity is deliberately engineered (as in my second case, and arguably the third), and sometimes it is an unplanned byproduct (as perhaps in the first), and it is unclear to me whether the normative upshot differs.

My questions, then: Is there an established concept in political philosophy, social epistemology, or the conservatism literature that picks out exactly this configuration — a practice whose resistance to scrutiny is itself a consequence of the practice — as opposed to the general fence heuristic? If so, how does the literature distinguish the engineered from the incidental cases, and does it treat the two as warranting different reform strategies? If there is no such concept, please propose a name, give a definition precise enough to exclude mere obscurity, mere self-interest, and the ordinary fence case, and indicate where you take the boundary of the phenomenon to lie — in particular, whether a practice qualifies only if the opacity was produced by the practice itself, or whether it suffices that the opacity is now sustained by it. I will not be satisfied with a label alone; what I need is the carving.
