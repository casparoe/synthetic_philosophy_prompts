After a long argument about Hofstadter’s account of selfhood, a friend summarized the position this way:

“Hofstadter holds that the self is a strange loop: the brain forms symbols for its own states, and once the symbol ‘I’ becomes rich enough to influence the system it represents, the representation and the represented system fold into one loop. What we experience as a self is the system encountering itself at the symbolic level. But because the ‘I’ is only a symbol and cannot contain every detail of the brain, the self is ultimately a useful fiction created and controlled by the brain rather than something that does genuine explanatory work.”

I objected that the final sentence misrepresents the view, but I could not explain precisely why. Please audit the summary: which parts are accurate, which are understandable simplifications, and which are distortions? In particular, does the symbolic status of the “I” make the self fictional, or can a symbolic pattern have causal and explanatory relevance without implying mysterious downward causation? Does the incompleteness of the self-model undermine genuine self-reference? And does a strange loop require a literal collapse of neural and symbolic levels, or only a recurring interaction between perception, self-representation, and action? I suspect my friend is conflating representational self-reference with executive control, but I would like to know whether that is a serious tension in Hofstadter’s view.
