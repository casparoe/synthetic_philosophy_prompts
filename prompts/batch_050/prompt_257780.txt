I'm TAing a third-year undergrad course called Paradoxes of Infinity, and next week I get one 50-minute section to teach the Pasadena game. Setup I'll give them, roughly: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads comes up on toss n, you win (−1)^(n+1)·2^n/n dollars, where a negative "win" means you pay. So the expectation series is just the alternating harmonic series, which converges conditionally, and by Riemann rearrangement you can reorder it to sum to literally anything — so the expected value isn't infinite, it isn't zero, it just doesn't exist. The students have all seen St. Petersburg earlier in the course and they did series in calculus a year or two ago, but I suspect most of them only half-remember the difference between conditional and absolute convergence.

Don't explain the game to me — plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place before the undefinedness result can even land as surprising rather than as a technicality? I'm torn on whether St. Petersburg should be the launchpad (it's familiar, and "infinite EV" vs "no EV" is the contrast that makes Pasadena interesting) or whether starting there poisons the well by making students file it as "St. Petersburg again, but messier." Also: should the carrying device be a live rearrangement demo on the whiteboard — actually rearranging 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − ... to hit some other target in front of them — and if so, what target value is most pedagogically damaging-in-a-good-way? Rearranging to 0? To a huge number? Or should the carrying example be something more concrete, like a winnings-and-debts ledger, with the formula introduced later? Tell me what the example needs to have and what it needs to not have.
