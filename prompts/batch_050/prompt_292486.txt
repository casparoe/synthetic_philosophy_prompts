For my English class this year, our teacher introduced a unit on interpretation with a view she attributed to twentieth-century literary criticism, roughly as follows: once a piece of writing is published, the author's intentions become irrelevant to what it means. She said that critics call this the death of the author, and that on this view the meaning of a text is whatever readers make of it, so no interpretation can really be wrong, only more or less interesting. She added that people who think intentions matter are committed to something absurd, namely that to understand Hamlet properly we would need Shakespeare to tell us what he meant, and since he is dead we can never understand the play at all. Because she wants us to be open-minded about readings, she concluded that in our essays we should never appeal to what an author intended, and that a reading which finds in a poem something the author could never have thought of is just as legitimate as any other.

I want to take her advice seriously, but I have a nagging feeling that the argument moves a little too fast, and I can't tell whether my doubt is just resistance to a correct view. Could you assess whether this is a fair presentation of the anti-intentionalist position? Specifically: which parts would a careful defender of that position actually endorse, and which parts overstate it or conflate distinct claims? In particular, is there a difference between saying the author's intention does not settle the meaning and saying it counts for nothing, and does the position really imply that no interpretation can be wrong? Also, is the reductio about needing to ask Shakespeare himself one that intentionalists would accept as a characterization of their view, or is it a caricature? Finally, does my teacher's practical conclusion follow from the defensible parts of the view — that student essays should never invoke intention and that anachronistic readings are equally legitimate — or does that conclusion need extra premises she hasn't supplied? If the strongest version of anti-intentionalism doesn't support her classroom rule, what would a rule that does follow from it look like?
