A friend who TAs our phil of science class tried to explain the problem of old evidence to me and I want to know if his version is actually fair. His summary, roughly: "For a Bayesian, confirmation just means raising your credence: E confirms H iff learning E makes you more confident in H. But if E is evidence you already had before the theory came along, your credence in E is already 1, and conditioning on something you're certain of can't change anything, so old evidence literally cannot confirm anything. Think of Einstein's famous prediction of the wobble in Mercury's orbit -- cases like that are the bread and butter of confirmation in physics and Bayesianism gets them exactly backwards. Luckily it's only a problem for evidence gathered before the theory, so it affects a handful of famous historical cases and not everyday science, but even so most philosophers of science treat Bayesian confirmation theory as basically a dead end because of it."

Some of this smells off to me but I can't pin down where. Which parts would a card-carrying Bayesian actually accept? In particular, is the "already certain, so no update possible" step airtight, and is it really only a problem for evidence that predates the theory? Also, the thing Einstein actually did was notice that general relativity *entails* the Mercury result, and discovering an entailment feels like genuine learning even when you already believe both sides of it -- so couldn't the Bayesian just fix things by letting agents have less-than-certain credences about logical truths? Does that actually solve anything or does it break something else in the picture? And once you correct whatever's wrong in my friend's summary, does his big conclusion -- that Bayesianism can't handle the most famous confirmations in science -- still stand?
