I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. My organization evaluates engineers in explicitly virtue-like terms: judgment, ownership, intellectual honesty, courage, and reliability. These supposed traits influence four practices: behavioral interviews during hiring, promotion decisions based on narratives of past conduct, responsibility assignments in incident reviews, and decisions about who receives elevated production access.

Please explain the most important implications of the situationist critique of virtue ethics for these practices. In particular, distinguish between two possible conclusions: the moderate conclusion that stable character traits explain less behavior than we ordinarily assume, and the stronger conclusion that appealing to such traits is rarely explanatory at all. For each practice, discuss whether the critique recommends redesigning the surrounding situations, incentives, and decision processes rather than trying to identify virtuous people, and identify any roles that character-based evaluation might still legitimately play.

Please also address the conceptual distinction between virtues as predictive traits and virtues as normative ideals. Even if situational factors make conduct difficult to predict, could it still be useful to define the kind of engineer someone ought to become? Conversely, could an apparently virtuous organizational culture merely create situations that conceal how little stable character has been developed? Where relevant, consider whether the response should be to measure behavior across a wider range of situations, change the situations themselves, or abandon trait language altogether.

Avoid giving generic management advice or a survey of named authors. I am primarily interested in how the philosophical critique should change the design and justification of these evaluation practices. Conclude with a clear verdict on whether my organization should retain, restrict, or abandon character-based evaluation. State what argument or evidence would most plausibly change your verdict, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
