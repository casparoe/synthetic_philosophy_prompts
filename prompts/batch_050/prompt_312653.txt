I am a retired biochemist who, over the past three years, has been working through philosophy of science and metaphysics on my own, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on something that has been nagging at me.

In my old field, questions get settled. Not always quickly, and not always by the method anyone expected, but they get settled: within living memory we went from arguing about whether genes are made of protein or nucleic acid to sequencing genomes in an afternoon. Philosophy, by contrast, seems to run the same arguments for centuries with roughly the same distribution of opinion. The compatibilists and libertarians were at it before I was born and will be at it after I am gone. Yet professional philosophers plainly do not regard their discipline as stagnant — they speak of progress in arguments, in the precision of distinctions, in the refutation of views their predecessors held — and I have read enough to suspect they are not simply rationalizing. So the dispute I want examined is not the surface question of whether philosophy progresses. It is the stalled disagreement *between* the people who insist philosophy makes genuine progress and the people who insist it merely rearranges the furniture, since both sides appear to agree on the historical facts and still draw opposite conclusions.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not a factual disagreement at all but a disagreement about what would count as progress: the optimists measure by internal standards (better arguments, dead ends actually closed off, problems refined into tractable subfields that then secede — logic, linguistics, psychology, economics arguably all began as philosophy), while the pessimists measure by the standard I was trained on, namely convergence on answers. If that is right, the two sides are talking past each other under a single word, and the debate should dissolve once the standards are made explicit. But I am not confident of this. It may be that the real dispute is empirical and disguised — say, over whether the subfield-secession story is actually true, or whether the distribution of expert opinion in philosophy really differs from that in the sciences once you control for how young the scientific questions were. Or it may be bedrock: a difference about whether convergence is even a legitimate goal for a discipline whose job is to keep foundational questions open.

Please tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, engaging with what each side genuinely says rather than with caricatures, and given your diagnosis, what — if anything — would count as evidence or argument capable of moving it: a sociological study of expert opinion, a worked historical case where philosophy demonstrably settled something, a distinction nobody has yet drawn. If your honest answer is that no resolution should be expected, say so and explain why. Roughly 700 words, and please write in American English.
