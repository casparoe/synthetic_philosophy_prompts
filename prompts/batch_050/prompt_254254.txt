I'm designing a session of a graduate seminar on time in fiction, and I need a serious piece of criticism to argue against. Please take on the role of a literary critic with real training in the philosophy of time — someone who can move between McTaggart's A-series and B-series, branching-time semantics, and the block-universe picture without reducing any of them to a slogan — and give me a careful reading of Borges's story The Garden of Forking Paths. You know the plot: the German agent Yu Tsun, pursued by Captain Madden, visits the sinologist Stephen Albert, who has solved the mystery of Yu Tsun's ancestor Ts'ui Pên — the chaotic novel and the never-found labyrinth are one and the same object, an image of forking time in which a character can die in one chapter and be alive in the next — and Yu Tsun then murders Albert so that the newspaper report of the crime will carry the name of the town to be bombed back to Berlin. I want the reading, not a plot summary.

Here is where I want you to dig. First, settle what the garden is actually a model of: a branching-time structure of the kind formal semantics builds, where the present is a node with many open futures; a many-worlds-style multiverse in which all alternatives are equally actual; or a block universe in which every event simply exists and the forking is only how things look from inside. Before committing, sketch all three briefly, then argue for one. In doing so, apply what I'll call the convergence test: in standard branching models, branches never rejoin, but does anything in the story rule out reconvergence — could a future in which Yu Tsun kills Albert and a future in which he doesn't later converge on the same headline, the same bombing, the same war? And if they can, what happens to the moral weight of the murder? Second, read the story against Borges's essay A New Refutation of Time, where he dissolves time into a series of autonomous instants, each complete in itself, and ends by conceding that time is the very substance we are made of. The essay denies any unified temporal order; yet the spy plot is nothing but unified temporal order — murder, newspaper, bombing — and it runs on train timetables and a narrow head start. Does the story dramatize the essay's thesis, or does it quietly refute it? Is Yu Tsun's guilt even intelligible if the essay is right? Third, take the closing gesture seriously: the narrator bequeaths the garden to various futures but pointedly not to all of them, which suggests the story's time contains futures in which the garden is never written at all. And say something about the frame — a deposition with its first page missing, introduced by a dry historical note — as itself a hole in the temporal record.

Two constraints. Be honest about where the story resists the metaphysics: it is also a clockwork thriller, and I want to know which scenes carry philosophical weight and which are decoration — a critic who finds the labyrinth in every sentence is not doing criticism. And end by telling me what the story adds to the philosophy of time that argument alone does not: what, if anything, do we learn about forking futures by watching a man act inside one that we cannot learn from the formal models? Think carefully, brainstorm the three candidate readings before settling on one, and write the entire answer in American English.
