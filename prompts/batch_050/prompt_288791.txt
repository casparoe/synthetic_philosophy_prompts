I work as a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm facing a professional decision that has started to feel less like a single decision and more like a series of decisions I never quite chose to make. I'd like your advice.

Nine months ago, I learned that the estate of a recently deceased physicist — a major figure whose personal notebooks have never been made public — was willing to grant one outlet exclusive access for a book-length feature. My magazine and one rival publication were both interested, and the estate's lawyer has been negotiating with each of us separately, making clear that only one of us will get access. Since then I have spent most of my working time on this: two research trips to the archives, fees to an independent expert to authenticate the material, legal review of the estate's conditions, and a meaningful chunk of my department's annual budget. From what I can piece together, my rival has made comparable investments.

Here's the problem. Every few months, just as an agreement seems near, the estate returns with new conditions — a higher fee, approval rights over quotations, delays. Each new demand is modest relative to what I've already committed, and walking away would mean that everything spent so far produces nothing, while my rival gets the story. So each time, continuing has seemed like the reasonable choice. Yet when I step back, I notice that I am now deeper in than I ever would have agreed to go at the start, and the end still isn't in sight. My editor is sympathetic but has asked me to justify, in writing, whether we proceed at the next round of demands.

A colleague's take is that quitting now proves the last nine months were wasted, so we should stay in as long as the story's value exceeds the latest increment of cost. Something about that reasoning feels off to me, but I can't fully articulate why — and I also suspect the dynamic with the rival outlet and the estate matters, since the estate plainly does better the longer both of us keep bidding against our own sunk investments.

Should we proceed, withdraw, or try to change the terms of the contest somehow? More generally: if there is a recognizable structure to situations like this one, I'd like to understand it well enough to spot the next one before I'm nine months in. In your answer, please indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis and recommendations, and single out the specific claim you are least sure of.
