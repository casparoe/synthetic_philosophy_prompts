I'm drafting a paper on personal identity and survival, and a recent argument in my philosophy of mind reading group made me realize I don't actually know what theory of survival my own gut reactions commit me to. So here are my verdicts on six cases involving the replacement or copying of my brain; I'd like to know whether any single principle captures them, or whether I have to give one up. First, my neurons are replaced one at a time over the course of a year by synthetic components that exactly replicate each neuron's behavior; I'm awake and conversing throughout and notice no change. Verdict: clearly still me at the end. Second, my brain is scanned destructively and a single upload is run minutes later. Verdict: I died; the upload is a copy that merely believes it's me. Third, my entire biological brain is removed under anesthesia and replaced in one operation by a functionally identical synthetic brain, memories and personality intact. Verdict: I died — though I can't shake the feeling that this is uncomfortably close to the first case, since the endpoint is the same and a year of gradual swaps seems to differ only in degree. Fourth, the gradual replacement proceeds as in the first case, except the discarded biological neurons are kept alive, reassembled in their original arrangement, and the resulting brain wakes up with all my memories. Verdict: the person with the synthetic brain is me, and the reassembled one is a convincing impostor — though I'm uneasy, since the reassembled one has the far better claim to my original matter, and matter seemed to be doing the work in my verdicts on the second and third cases. Fifth, a scan is made non-destructively, and the resulting upload runs in perfect real-time synchrony with my biological brain for a month, every thought occurring in both, until my biological brain is destroyed in an accident. Verdict: I survive in the upload, because my experience was continuous through the shared month — though I notice that by the logic of my second verdict I should presumably say I died a month before the accident. Sixth, the destructive scan happens but the upload is shelved and only run fifty years later. Verdict: still not me, and the delay changes nothing — though I then can't explain why the gap bothers me less in ordinary sleep or a year-long coma.

Is there a coherent account of what survival consists in that gets all or most of these verdicts? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which cases motivate each clause of it, and tell me which of my verdicts the simplest version of the account forces me to abandon — I suspect the first and third can't both stand, and I'm not sure the second and fifth can either. If no single principle works, give me the two or three strongest candidates and say honestly which verdicts each one cannot accommodate. Finally, tell me what your preferred account predicts for two cases I haven't listed: one where the gradual replacement is completed in a single hour rather than a year, and one where two uploads run in synchrony with me during the shared month and only one survives the accident.
