Labs train their chatbots by having humans rate responses and optimizing for high average approval, and then report stuff like average benchmark scores and average satisfaction. But the same labs also run red teams specifically hunting for the worst outputs the model can be made to produce -- the jailbreaks, the one prompt in a million that gets it to help build a bomb. My friend says these two practices are just complementary quality control. I think they presuppose two totally different theories of what it even means for a model to be "aligned" or "safe": one where a model counts as good if it behaves well on average or in expectation, and one where it counts as good only if there's no reachable input that makes it behave terribly.

So: what does each practice actually presuppose? Can you coherently do both, or does the red-teaming practice implicitly concede that average-case training was never going to deliver safety on its own? And if the worst-case view is right, what would training and evaluation have to look like instead -- is the averaging practice just a pragmatic approximation, or does it rest on a genuinely different picture of what we're trying to guarantee?
