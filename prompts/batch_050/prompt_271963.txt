Dennett's account of the self as a center of narrative gravity compares selves to centers of gravity in physics: abstracta rather than inner objects, posits that help us interpret and predict a system without being literal parts of it. This seems to me to have a deflationary consequence that Dennett elsewhere ignores. No physicist explains a satellite's orbit by citing forces exerted by the satellite's center of gravity, and nobody holds a center of gravity responsible for anything; so if the self really is that kind of posit, it can neither cause behavior nor be a fit target of blame and praise. Yet Dennett devotes entire books to defending human freedom and moral responsibility, apparently on behalf of beings whose selves are, on his own view, explanatory fictions rather than causally efficacious things. One of these commitments has to give, and I cannot see which. Does the center-of-gravity analogy actually entail that the self is causally and morally inert, so that Dennett's responsibility talk survives only as a useful manner of speaking — or is the mistake in my reading of the analogy, and if so, where exactly does it go wrong? I am not looking for a survey of the interpretive options: give me your verdict on which reading of Dennett is correct and defend it. Please answer in exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
