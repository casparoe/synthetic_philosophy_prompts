Earlier this year I took over as administrator of the benevolent fund attached to my professional association — a modest charitable trust that makes discretionary grants to members facing hardship or unusual expense. Having sat through two rounds of applications, I find that my gut verdicts are firm but do not obviously cohere, and I should like to know what principle, if any, I am actually applying. Here are the cases (details altered, amounts in sterling):

(a) A member with a mobility impairment applied for £4,000 towards a powered wheelchair. She is strikingly cheerful and reports being content with her life, but cannot leave her flat unaccompanied. I approved it without hesitation.

(b) A member with severe depression applied for £4,000 for home improvements which he believes would lift his mood. Everything I have read suggests the lift would be real but brief. I declined it, though not without unease.

(c) A member applied for £4,000 towards a pilgrimage her faith treats as a once-in-a-lifetime obligation, which she said it would devastate her to forgo. I voted to fund it, on the strength of how much was at stake for her.

(d) A member of refined and expensive tastes — a trained wine palate, miserable in the absence of what most would call luxuries — applied for a subsidy. I declined it firmly and felt no unease at all.

(e) A member with a long-standing disability, to which she has thoroughly adapted, applied for equipment costing £3,000 that would restore a capability she lost but, by her own candid admission, would change her felt quality of life very little. I approved it.

(f) A widowed member asked that £2,000 be spent on her disabled son's specialist equipment rather than on the family holiday that would, on any plausible measure, have produced far more enjoyment per pound for her and her three children. I agreed.

Cases (b) and (c) are the pair I cannot reconcile: in one I discounted a large claimed welfare stake, in the other I honoured one, and I cannot yet say what distinguishes them beyond my own sympathy. A sixth application is now pending: a member who is profoundly lonely has asked for £900 to join a club and travel to its gatherings — cheap, and likely to transform his daily experience.

My questions: is there a currency of advantage — welfare, resources, capabilities, or some hybrid — on which all six verdicts, and ideally the pending case, come out as I have them? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause of it, and tell me what it delivers for the lonely member. If no single principle saves every verdict, say which verdict you would overturn and why. And if you think my pattern is better captured not by any theory of equality but by some neighbouring notion — sufficiency, say, or priority to the worse-off — I should like to hear that case made.

Please answer in British English.
