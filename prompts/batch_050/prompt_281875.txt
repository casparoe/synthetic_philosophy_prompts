Last month I bought a bedside lamp marketed as a "limit lamp": it switches itself off after one minute, back on after a further half minute, off again after a further quarter minute, and so on, completing infinitely many switches within two minutes. It arrived, it performs exactly as advertised, and now my household is deadlocked over a question the instruction manual refuses to answer: when the two minutes are up, is the lamp on or off? The manufacturer has stopped replying to my emails. I would like to convert my frustration into something more useful than a refund dispute.

Write a deadpan product-recall notice of roughly 500–600 words, issued by a fictional national consumer-safety authority, announcing the recall of this lamp. It should be formatted like a real recall notice (a hazard summary, a description of affected units, instructions to consumers, a remedy or refund section), and the humor has to come from getting the underlying philosophy right rather than from mocking a caricature: the switching schedule settles what happens at every moment before the limit but, as Benacerraf pointed out in reply to Thomson, entails nothing whatsoever about the lamp's state at the limit itself; Thomson's original suggestion that the situation is somehow contradictory should appear only as the agency's exaggerated official position, one a specialist will recognize as contested; and the notice may gesture at the deeper dispute over whether any supertask is even physically or metaphysically coherent, so long as it does not simply declare that dispute settled by bureaucratic fiat. Good material includes: a hazard warning that the product's final state is "underdetermined by the manufacturer's specifications"; instructions advising consumers not to infer the lamp's terminal state from its complete switching history; a remedy section in which the refund depends on the lamp's state at the limit, with the obvious practical difficulty handled in the most bureaucratic way possible; and at least one line where the agency quietly overreaches — asserting as regulatory fact something that is actually an open philosophical question — without breaking its deadpan register.

The piece should be funny to someone who has read the Thomson–Benacerraf exchange and still fully readable, and still funny, to someone who has never heard the word "supertask." A reader should be able to tell what is being ironized — the gap between a complete description of a process and any conclusion about its limit — without the notice ever winking at them.

After the recall notice, and clearly separated from it, add a short author's note of about 100–150 words distinguishing what the piece treats as settled (that the switching sequence alone does not determine the lamp's final state) from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers who work on supertasks (whether Thomson's argument shows an actual contradiction or merely a surprising underdetermination, and whether supertasks are possible at all), so that no contested position is smuggled in as the consensus view.
