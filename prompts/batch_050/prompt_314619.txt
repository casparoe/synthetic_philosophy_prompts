I'm grading essays for an intro philosophy class and want a second pair of eyes on one of them. The exam question was:

Explain G.E. Moore's proof of an external world, and evaluate the most serious objection to it. Does Moore succeed against the skeptic?

Here's the student's answer:

"Moore's proof is his famous performance where he holds up each of his hands in turn and declares them to be hands, concluding that at least two external objects exist, and therefore an external world exists. He says a proof is legitimate when the premises are known to be true, are different from the conclusion, and the conclusion follows from them, and he claims his proof satisfies all three conditions. The skeptic's standard reply is that Moore can't really know the premises, since for all he knows he might be dreaming. Moore basically turns the tables here: since the skeptical argument just assumes we could be radically deceived without showing it, it's actually the skeptic who begs the question. Moore also takes his proof to refute idealists like Berkeley, because once you've proven that two hands exist, you've shown physical objects exist independently of anyone perceiving them. He does admit the proof doesn't explain exactly how he knows he has hands, but argues this doesn't matter, since you can know things you can't prove. Personally I think the dreaming objection still works, because Moore never actually rules out the dream hypothesis, so in the end I find the proof unconvincing."
