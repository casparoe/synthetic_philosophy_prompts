Okay so I'm a novelist — two literary sf novels out, third one stuck in a drawer — and the stalled book is a first-contact story, which means I've been rereading Peter Watts' Blindsight and getting quietly depressed about how well it already does the thing I wanted to do.

Quick sketch in case you're rusty on it: humanity meets the scramblers, aliens who are superintelligent, converse fluently, outmaneuver the human crew at every turn, and yet — the book keeps insisting — there's nobody home, no inner experience whatsoever. The narrator Siri had half his brain removed as a kid and experiences his own mind as something he infers from the outside more than inhabits. By the end the book floats the idea that consciousness itself is an evolutionary dead end: expensive, slow, and the universe belongs to the things that never bothered with it.

Read the novel through the access vs. phenomenal consciousness distinction and global workspace theory. Concretely:

1. Block's separability claim — access consciousness (information available for reasoning, reporting, guiding action) vs. phenomenal consciousness (raw what-it's-like). The scramblers look engineered to be all access, no phenomenality. Does the book actually pull that off, or does it cheat? I'm thinking of the stretch where the crew entertains the possibility that the scramblers' fluent replies are a Chinese Room situation, and of how seemingly gleeful and cruel their maneuvering feels — does Watts smuggle experience back in to keep them menacing?

2. On global workspace theory, consciousness just is the global broadcast and integration of information for flexible control. If that's right, are the scramblers even coherent? Anything integrating that much information, that flexibly, is conscious on the GWT picture. So the novel reads like a thought experiment the theories answer differently: Block-style seperatists can have scramblers, GWT people have to say they're impossible or misdescribed. Is that a fair framing, and where does the book itself sit — is Watts implicitly committed to a theory, or deliberately exploiting the gap between them?

3. What about Siri himself? He's presented as a halfway case — access mostly intact, phenomenality thinned out. What would Block say about him, and does that category even make sense on GWT, or does the theory force him fully onto one side?

4. The ending's consciousness-as-liability move: for evolution to select against consciousness, doesn't the book need phenomenality to be causally inert or at least detachable from the cognition — and does it ever argue that, or just assert it? If phenomenal conciousness comes along automatically once you've got global broadcast, you can't select it away without wrecking the intelligence too. Is that the book's fatal flaw or am I missing something?

And the selfish part: I want aliens that survive this scrutiny. What would a GWT-consistent version of the scramblers look like — something conscious, but in a form we'd have real trouble recognizing — and is that scarier or less scary than what Watts wrote? Also, tell me honestly where the book resists the reading. I don't need a book report that flattens it into an illustration of Block.
