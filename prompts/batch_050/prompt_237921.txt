I'm a graduate student in philosophy preparing a short essay for an interdisciplinary workshop on AI and persuasion, and I'd like comments on a draft before I submit it. The draft defends a position I'm tentatively calling the doctrine of argumentative transparency: a superintelligent AI system, when advising humans, should be required to present its recommendations in the form of explicit arguments whose premises the human can inspect and whose inference steps are simple enough to verify, and it should be prohibited from using any persuasive means that bypass this -- no tailored emotional framing, no strategic sequencing of information, no exploiting cognitive biases it has detected in the user. My argument is that persuasion which outruns the listener's ability to evaluate it is a form of domination, even when the content is true and the advice is good, because the human ends up complying rather than genuinely endorsing; and that a sufficiently capable system should always be able to find the transparent route, so we lose nothing by mandating it. A colleague pushed back with two objections I haven't fully answered: first, that transparency is itself a rhetorical style, and a superintelligence could pick which true premises to disclose in a way that steers you just as effectively as framing would, so the doctrine might only prohibit the visible manipulation while licensing the invisible kind; second, that there are cases -- a panicked patient refusing emergency surgery, a voter facing a genuinely complex policy choice under time pressure -- where insistence on full argumentative transparency looks less like respect and more like abandonment. I've tried to reply that these cases show we need the doctrine to include a duty of simplification rather than an exception to it, but I'm not confident the reply works.

Please read the draft position as stated above (I haven't pasted the full essay, but the position is summarized faithfully) and give me comments of three kinds: where the argument is weakest or most vulnerable to counterexample, what the strongest version of my colleague's two objections would look like if developed more carefully, and whether my "duty of simplification" reply actually saves the doctrine or concedes it. Be candid rather than encouraging; I'd rather have the workshop's objections in advance from you than in person. Please avoid suggesting further reading or citing specific philosophers -- I want your assessment of the reasoning, not a literature review.
