I am a novelist currently in a dispute with the screenwriter adapting my book, and the dispute has forced a conceptual question on me that I would like clarified on its own terms, independent of the contract: when do two sequences of events instantiate the same mechanism, as opposed to merely resembling one another? We agree the film must preserve the novel's central mechanism — the way the protagonist's deception destroys her marriage — but we disagree about what preservation requires. Below are my verdicts on cases. I want to know what principle, if any, generates them, and which of them I must surrender.

My novel runs thus: the wife conceals a debt; the husband discovers the concealment (not the debt) through a stray bank letter; and the discovery, rather than the debt itself, ends the marriage.

Case one. Transplant the story to a space station: the wife hides a contraband cargo, the husband stumbles on a shipping manifest, the marriage collapses. Every surface detail changes; every link — concealment, accidental discovery, disproportionate reaction, collapse — is preserved. My verdict: same mechanism.

Case two. Keep the setting, but have the husband hire a detective. Coarsely described, concealment–discovery–collapse still holds, yet I say the mechanism has changed: at the joint that matters, the causal work is done by different means. Accident and design are not interchangeable parts.

Case three. The letter reaches the husband by a gust of wind rather than because the wife's evasiveness made him suspicious enough to open it. Outcomes identical. Verdict: different mechanism. A chance-mediated link and a psychology-mediated link do the same work in the plot summary but not in the story.

Case four. Compare an overheard-prophecy story, where the precautions a character takes bring about the foreseen event, with a time-travel story where an agent's attempt to prevent an event is, via a closed causal loop, its cause. Despite the exotic physics, I say these share a mechanism: the agent's response to a prediction feeds back into the predicted outcome.

Case five. Now compare two precautions-backfire stories: in one, the backfiring runs through the agent's psychology (his fear makes him cruel, which provokes the rebellion he feared); in the other, the gods simply ensure the prophecy holds whatever anyone does. I want to say: different mechanisms. But I notice this may convict me in case four, since the time loop and the overheard prophecy also run through different substrates. I do not think cases four and five can both stand, and I cannot see which to keep.

Case six. The screenwriter's draft preserves every causal link but transplants the story to a setting — a culture, a technology — in which the concealment could not plausibly go undiscovered for long, or in which its discovery would not plausibly destroy a marriage. The arrows are drawn exactly as in my novel, but the world no longer underwrites them. My instinct says the mechanism is gone; the screenwriter says the graph is the mechanism.

Is there a single principle here? Candidates I can gestured at but cannot sharpen: identity of causal topology; identity of counterfactual dependencies under intervention; identity of entities and activities at the relevant grain of description. Each seems to rescue some verdicts and condemn others, and each leaves me unable to say what fixes the grain — since any two stories share a mechanism if described coarsely enough, and no two share one if described finely enough. Please state the best candidate principle precisely, identify which of my six verdicts it must overturn, and tell me what the repaired version predicts for two cases I have not listed: an abridged plot summary that omits the middle link (is a mechanism sketch the same mechanism as its expansion?), and a sequel in which the same deception, discovered by the same means, is met this time with forgiveness — the same mechanism interrupted, or a different one altogether?
