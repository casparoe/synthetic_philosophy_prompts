I'm an epidemiologist by training, and most of my working life runs on the machinery of modern causal inference: directed acyclic graphs, the do-operator, d-separation, screening off, and the usual taxonomy of redundant causation (early and late preemption, backup processes, joint sufficient causes). A colleague in philosophy of mind recently tried to get me worried about Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument — roughly, the worry that if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, there is no causal work left for mental properties to do unless the mental is identical to something physical. I'd like to understand the argument properly, but I keep translating it into my own vocabulary as she talks, and I'm not sure whether the translation is faithful or whether I'm missing what makes it a philosophical problem rather than a modeling confusion.

So here is what I'd like: restate the exclusion argument as precisely as you can in the vocabulary of causal graphical models. In particular, I want to know how to draw it. Is the physical cause's sufficiency a deterministic arrow from brain state to behavior? Is the mental property a separate node with its own arrow into the outcome, and if so, does the argument amount to the claim that conditioning on the physical node screens off the mental node — i.e., that intervening on the mental variable while holding the physical fixed changes nothing? Does the exclusion principle (no event has more than one sufficient cause, barring genuine overdetermination) correspond to anything a causal modeler would recognize, or is it an extra premise our machinery simply doesn't encode? And does the supervenience relation — the mental being fixed by the physical without being identical to it — show up in a DAG at all, given that it's usually described as determination rather than causation?

Then I want the residue report. Which parts of the argument translate cleanly into interventions and d-separation, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts seem to have no counterpart in this framework at all? My suspicion is that the translation quietly begs a question: a graph only lets me ask what happens under an intervention on the mental node, but if the mental supervenes on the physical, such an intervention may be impossible in principle — like trying to intervene on a variable that is a deterministic function of its parent. Tell me whether that impossibility is where the real philosophical action is, whether it's an artifact of forcing metaphysics into a tool built for manipulable variables, and whether the exclusion argument survives as a genuine problem once everything that does translate has been translated — or whether what's left over is a defect in the framework, a defect in the argument, or both.
