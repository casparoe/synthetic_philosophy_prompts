I recently took an environmental ethics seminar where we encountered the argument that wild-animal suffering may dominate the moral ledger of nature: most wild animals belong to r-selected species that produce enormous numbers of offspring, nearly all of whom die young and unpleasantly, so that an acre of thriving habitat may be, in expectation, an acre of net negative welfare. On that view, one might actually reduce suffering by reducing habitat. Our seminar split between people who took this as a serious policy conclusion and people who treated it as a reductio of the whole welfare-accounting approach, and I've been going back and forth with a friend about which reaction is right. To sharpen my own intuitions, I'd like you to write a piece of satire that forces the question.

Write a parody investment prospectus for a fictional asset-management firm, "Verdant Reclamation Partners," whose business model is acquiring biodiverse land and converting it into low-biomass surfaces (parking lots, sealed gravel beds, salt pans) as a humanitarian intervention, then selling certified "suffering offsets" to conscientious consumers who want to neutralize the wild-animal suffering their lifestyles fail to prevent. The voice should be impeccably deadpan corporate -- the calm, upbeat, risk-disclosing register of a real prospectus -- and the piece should never wink: no named real people, no strawmen, no moment where the firm admits it's doing anything but rigorous applied ethics. The humor must come from getting the underlying arguments right, so that someone who has read the wild-animal-suffering literature laughs in recognition rather than wincing at a misunderstanding: r-selection and juvenile mortality, the idea that ecosystems are Malthusian rather than Edenic, uncertainty about insect sentience and how to discount for it, and the total-versus-average utilitarianism tension lurking under any claim that fewer lives means less suffering. Crucially, the satire should be fair: the firm's finest print should quietly engage the sophisticated objections rather than pretend they don't exist -- displaced animals fleeing into neighboring habitat, edge effects, the spike of mortality during the conversion itself, the possibility that the whole calculus is too uncertain to act on -- each answered with serene, internally consistent reasoning. At least once, follow the firm's logic somewhere genuinely uncomfortable (a reader should catch themselves nodding along and then stop short).

Structure the piece with these sections, in this order, with these rough lengths: "Letter from Our Founder" (~150 words, a warm personal origin story involving a formative encounter with a starving fledgling), "The Opportunity" (~150 words, market-sizing wild suffering the way one might size the carbon market), "Methodology: Measuring What Matters" (~200 words, the firm's proprietary welfare metrics, including how it handles the insect-sentience question), "Frequently Asked Questions" (~150 words, including a polite answer to "Isn't this monstrous?"), and "Risk Factors" (~100 words, legally-styled disclosures that happen to restate the best objections to the position). Around 750 words for the prospectus in total. After the piece, add a short section titled "Author's Note" (~120 words) identifying which real arguments each joke or clause depends on, so I can check the satire against the seminar readings.
