A friend from my old reading group and I have this running game where we trade thought experiments about pain and pleasure, because neither of us can settle whether pain is bad because of how it feels or because of how we react to it. She has very settled intuitions and I basically have none, so I've started collecting her verdicts like data. Here's what she's said so far:

- Normal person, hand on a hot pan, ordinary searing pain: bad, obviously.
- Hot shower after a long cold bike ride: good, obviously.
- Post-surgery patient on morphine who says the burning pain is still right there, same spot, same intensity, it just doesn't bother her in the slightest and she's indifferent to whether it stays or goes: not bad. Totally neutral, like noticing a clock ticking.
- A guy at a chili cook-off whose mouth is on fire and who is loving every second of it, goes back for thirds: good, or at least not one bit bad.
- A long-term addict who compulsively chases his drug but tells you flat out the high does nothing for him anymore, no pleasure, just a flat nothing, and still he spends every evening pursuing it: not good. Him chasing it doesn't make it good, she says.
- A depressed patient who reports her morning tea still tastes faintly pleasant but has zero motivation to ever make it, wouldn't cross the room for it: mildly good anyway. The pleasantness is there even if the caring isn't.
- Someone on a new medication with a constant, free-floating, objectless unpleasant feeling — no body part hurts, nothing bad happened, it's just a low awful hum she desperately wants to stop: bad, clearly, even though no pain is involved.

Now the case we got stuck on. There's a real-ish condition (pain asymbolia, from certain kinds of brain damage) where people still feel pain as pain — they can tell you where it is and how intense, they call it pain — but it has no sting for them: they don't mind it, don't flinch, never lift a finger to avoid it. Marta is like that, with one twist: she's read up on her condition and reflectively judges that these pains are bad for her. She'll sincerely say she wishes theyd stop, that her life would be better without them, and she means it. But in the moment she feels zero aversion, and when her doctor offers a pill that would erase the sensations entirely she shrugs, says sure maybe, and keeps forgetting to pick up the prescription. Indifferent in the moment, opposed on reflection.

So: what does my friend say about Marta's pains? More than the verdict itself, I want to know what rule she's actually running — bad iff it feels unpleasant, bad iff you reflectively endorse being rid of it, bad iff you're disposed to avoid it, or something else — and which of those rules fit all seven verdicts equally well. Give me a prediction with the reasoning behind it, tell me which of the earlier cases is the closest analog to Marta's and why, and then name the one further case I could put to her that would settle which rule it really is (and what she'd say on each rule, so I know what answer to listen for).
