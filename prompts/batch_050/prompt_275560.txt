More and more, companies that use chatbots for customer service have the bot open with something like "Hi, I'm an AI assistant" — and some jurisdictions now legally require bots to identify themselves as bots, even in cases where users genuinely couldn't have told the difference. What does a disclosure norm like that presuppose about behavioral criteria for mind? On a Turing-test-style view, a system whose behavior is indistinguishable from a minded being's is, for practical purposes, as good as minded — but then forcing it to out itself seems to presuppose that something beyond behavior matters, something no test could detect. Reconstruct the implicit theory of mind behind the practice: is it that passing behavioral tests is only ever evidence, never constitutive? That people have a right to know what they're "really" talking to no matter how it acts? Say whether the norm is coherent on its own terms, whether it can be justified without smuggling in a kind of dualism its defenders would probaly disavow, and what customer service (and our obligations to the systems we talk to) would have to look like if we actually took Turing's criterion at face value.
