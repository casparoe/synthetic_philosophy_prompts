Suppose you are advising a philanthropic funder who can support a small research cluster -- say, half a dozen political theorists and empiricists for three years -- on sortition and lottocracy: the use of lotteries rather than elections to select officials, legislators, or citizen assemblies. The theoretical literature has grown quickly, but it is strikingly disjointed. Proponents argue variously that lotteries achieve descriptive representation, break the grip of party machines and campaign finance, produce better deliberation than elected bodies, and embody a distinct ideal of political equality in which every citizen has an equal chance to rule. Critics raise worries about competence, accountability (randomly selected officials cannot be voted out), the legitimacy of binding decisions made by bodies the public did not choose, and whether a sample of a few hundred can genuinely 'stand for' a population of millions when the sampled body deliberates and changes its members' minds.

Which open questions should the cluster prioritize, and in what order? I am less interested in a survey of the debate than in a defended agenda. For each question you prioritize, explain: whether it is upstream of other questions (for instance, does the empirical track record of deliberative minipublics matter at all if the legitimacy objection cannot be answered, or vice versa?); whether it is tractable by theorists, by empirical methods, or only by real-world experimentation at the municipal level; and what findings over the next few years would move it up or down the list. Please also identify at least one much-discussed question in this literature that you think is overrated -- that is, one whose resolution would change little for anyone deciding whether to actually implement a lottery-based institution -- and say why.
