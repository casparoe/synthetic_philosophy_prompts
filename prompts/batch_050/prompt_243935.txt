I am a retired physicist, and in my retirement I have been reading the philosophy of fiction, which I find both delightful and maddening. I want you to argue, carefully and in some depth, for the position that fictional names such as Sherlock Holmes genuinely refer — not to flesh-and-blood people, past or present, but to abstract artifacts: entities created by authors, much as a theorem or a symphony is created, and thereafter available to be discussed, misremembered, translated, and adapted. I associate this view, rightly or wrongly, with philosophers such as Peter van Inwagen and Amie Thomasson, though you need not concern yourself with attributing it precisely.

Your argument should do four things, in continuous prose.

First, explain how the view handles the obvious difficulty that Sherlock Holmes, as Conan Doyle describes him, is a concrete detective who smokes a pipe and lives in Baker Street, whereas an abstract artifact can surely do none of those things. I gather the standard move is to distinguish the properties the character has in the story from the properties the abstract object actually has — being a detective belongs to the first list, being created in the 1880s and being discussed in this letter to the second — and I want you to state this distinction precisely enough that it does not collapse into a mere relabeling of the problem.

Second, defend the view against the incompleteness objection. The stories settle neither Holmes's blood type nor the number of hairs on his head, and this seems to make the fictional character unlike any real person. Explain whether incompleteness is a defect for the abstract-artifact view or, as I suspect, actually a point in its favor, since abstract objects can be genuinely indeterminate in respects where a concrete person cannot be.

Third, account for sentences that cross the boundary between story and world, such as the claim that Sherlock Holmes is more famous than any living detective, or that most readers picture him wearing a deerstalker he rarely wears in the original tales. These seem to be straightforwardly true assertions about the character, not about anything in the fiction, and I want your argument to show that the artifact view handles them more naturally than the rival view on which Holmes does not refer at all and fictional discourse is a species of pretense or make-believe.

Fourth, and here my professional past intrudes: in the nineteenth century, Le Verrier hypothesized a planet, Vulcan, orbiting inside Mercury, to explain anomalies in Mercury's orbit. No such planet exists. My instinct as a scientist is that Vulcan and Sherlock Holmes are importantly different cases — that Le Verrier's error and Conan Doyle's creation should come apart semantically, even though both names might seem, at first glance, to dangle in the same way. Argue either that the abstract-artifact view vindicates this instinct (and say exactly what distinguishes creating a character from mispositing a planet), or that my instinct is wrong and the two cases must be treated alike, in which case tell me which treatment both should receive.

Please write about 800 words. Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; I would like continuous prose, structured the way a careful letter or essay is structured, with the argument carried by the paragraphs themselves.
