In my philosophy of religion reading group we recently worked through the argument from divine hiddenness — roughly: a perfectly loving God would ensure that no one fails to believe in him through no fault of their own; but such nonresistant nonbelief clearly exists; so, probably, there is no perfectly loving God. When we reached the conclusion, one member waved it off with a line I have since noticed everywhere in these debates: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. He said it as though it settled the matter, and the discussion stalled, because nobody could say exactly what had just been asserted.

I would like that slogan turned into a menu of precise claims. Distinguish the different theses it could be expressing — at minimum, I suspect, a bare logical point (from "we have not found evidence of X" it does not follow that X is absent), a probabilistic point about when a failed search licenses any updating at all (presumably only when the search was of a kind likely to have found the evidence if X were real), a claim about the particular case at hand (that we have not, in fact, conducted the relevant search for God, or that no such search is possible), and perhaps a stronger epistemological claim (that in domains like religion, apparent silence should be treated as evidentially neutral by default). For each reading, state it precisely, assess whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false, and then say whether that reading — if granted — would actually block the hiddenness argument. My suspicion is that the readings true enough to be undeniable are too weak to stop the argument, and the readings strong enough to stop it are contestable; tell me whether that suspicion is right.

Two complications I want the analysis to engage rather than bypass. First, the slogan seems to presuppose that the "absence" in question is the failure of a search we performed. But the hiddenness argument does not concern records, arguments, or traces we went looking for; it concerns the apparent absence of a relationship — an experience of God's presence — that a loving parent-like being would arguably supply unbidden. Does the slogan survive the shift from "evidence one might fail to find" to "evidence that should have found us"? Second, consider whether the slogan cuts in the opposite direction here: if God's existence predicts readily available evidence and God's nonexistence predicts its absence, then the absence is not neutral but differential — in which case absence of evidence just is evidence of absence, and the slogan's only defensible reading concedes the point it is deployed to deny. Work through whether that inversion succeeds.

Present the readings as a numbered list I can bring back to the group, each entry ending with a verdict on its dialectical force, and close by saying which reading my interlocutor most plausibly meant and how the conversation should proceed if he insists on it.
