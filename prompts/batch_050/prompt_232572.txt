I write about physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I have been asked to deliver the keynote at the annual awards dinner of a professional association of science journalists. The audience is genuinely mixed: roughly half working reporters, half scientists who serve as judges and sources, plus their families. The organizers have given the evening the theme "When the Data Don't Decide" and asked me to speak to it for about twelve minutes.

The theme is, at bottom, the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I want the speech to take that idea seriously rather than use it as a decoration. I want to build the speech around three things: the Duhemian point that no experiment ever tests a hypothesis in isolation, because every test presupposes a background of auxiliary assumptions about instruments and conditions, so that a failed prediction can always be blamed on the auxiliaries rather than the theory; the stronger Quinian claim that in principle any theory could be held onto come what may if one is willing to make enough adjustments elsewhere; and the awkward fact, which the relativists and the cheerleaders both mishandle, that scientists nonetheless converge on judgments all the time, and usually for good reasons that are real but not mechanical.

Please write the speech. The philosophical material should do structural work: it should constrain what I can honestly say in both directions. It must not slide into the conclusion that science is just politics or that any theory is as good as any other, because I don't believe that and it would be false; but it also must not retreat into the naive view that the facts simply announce themselves and that journalists merely transmit them. The speech should land for the reporters without boring the scientists, and land for the scientists without embarrassing me in front of them. Include one concrete recommendation about newsroom practice that follows from the argument -- something a desk editor could actually adopt -- and a single memorable line that a reader with no philosophy background could carry away and repeat. Somewhere in the middle, voice the sharpest objection a physicist in the room would raise, and answer it without conceding the whole case. No quotations, no name-dropping beyond what is strictly necessary, and no jargon that I would have to pause and define.
