So I retired from my chemistry professorship three years ago and, having too much time on my hands, joined a philosophy reading group at the public library. We're on Galileo now, specifically the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615), and I keep chewing on this bit:

"Now the Bible, merely to condescend to popular capacity, has not hesitated to obscure some very important pronouncements, attributing to God himself some qualities extremely remote from (and even contrary to) His essence. Who, then, would positively declare that this principle has been set aside, and the Bible has confined itself rigorously to the bare and restricted sense of its words, when speaking but casually of the earth, of water, of the sun, or of any other created thing?"

Here's my issue. At least three people in the group -- and honestly me too, at first -- reached for the same analogy to explain what Galileo is up to: scripture is like a parent explaining something to a small child, or like a pop-science book. You tell the six-year-old the sun "goes to sleep" at night, you write in the trade book that electrons "orbit" the nucleus, and it's not a lie, it's pitching the material to the audience. The analogy then gets used to do real work: since God was pitching to ancient shepherds and not to astronomers, any passage where the Bible talks about the physical world can be discounted whenever it conflicts with what we measure, full stop, no residual problem. One guy in the group basically thinks Galileo has dissolved the whole science-religion tension with this move, definately for cosmology and probably for everything else empirical too.

I'm a scientist by training and a philosopher by three years of Tuesdays, so I want this audited properly. How far does the analogy actually hold, taking the passage above as Galileo's own statement of the view?

Concretely: give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the parent/author, the child/reader, the simplified story, the true account the child could in principle get later. Then tell me where the mapping strains or breaks. Some things that nag at me: a parent eventually expects the kid to learn the real story, but is there any "growing up" available to the Bible's audience on Galileo's picture, or is the condescension permanent? A pop-science writer could have written the accurate version and chose not to, but does Galileo think God could have written an astrophysically correct Bible, and would that even have served what he calls the primary purpose of the sacred writings? Also, the analogy makes the simplification a mere teaching device, but Galileo seems to want the conclusion that the Bible *cannot err* when rightly understood -- does the parent-child picture actually support that stronger claim, or does it only support the weaker claim that the Bible is allowed to be loose about physics?

Finally: does the sweeping conclusion my reading group drew (any physical claim in scripture can always be discounted, tension dissolved) survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If the conclusion survives only in a narrower form, state the narrower form precisely. And if you think there's a better analogy for what Galileo is doing in this passage, give it and say why it fits better.

Please answer in American English, and feel free to be blunt about where my group's hero is hand-waving.
