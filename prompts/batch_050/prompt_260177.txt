I am a land surveyor by trade, with no formal education in philosophy, but for several years I have been turning over a puzzle about what we owe to future generations, and I have arrived at a position I now intend to defend in public. Next month I will speak for twenty minutes at an open meeting of my county's long-range water planning commission, which is choosing between an expensive reservoir project and a restrictive conservation regime. My thesis is that neither option can be condemned on the ground that it harms future people. Whichever policy we adopt will alter settlement patterns, livelihoods, marriages, and childbearing so thoroughly that, a century on, largely different individuals will exist under each policy; no one alive then could truthfully claim they would have been better off had we chosen otherwise, since under the other choice they would never have existed at all. I conclude that the familiar language of what we owe our grandchildren is confused, and that if conservation is right, it must be justified impersonally: a flourishing future is simply better than a degraded one, not because anyone in it has a claim against us.

The audience will include the county's chief planner, an economist by training who thinks in cost-benefit terms; a retired family-court judge; two organizers from a regional environmental group who frame everything as intergenerational justice; an insurance actuary active in civic life; and a number of farmers and parents with no theoretical commitments but strong intuitions.

Prepare my question-period briefing. Give me the objections this particular audience will raise, in roughly the order I am likely to hear them, flagged for how much damage each could do, and phrased in the vocabulary of the person raising it: the judge will speak of standing and injured parties, the actuary of risk and expected value, the organizers of justice and responsibility. For each, give me a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and where the reply is weakest. Tell me which premise of my argument is most exposed. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it comes from the judge and involves a case much closer to home than population policy — and how I might handle it if it comes. Please write your answer in American English.
