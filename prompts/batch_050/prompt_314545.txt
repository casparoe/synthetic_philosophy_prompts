I'm designing the final unit of an undergraduate course on self-knowledge, and I want to replace the usual lecture with a classification exercise. Please write a decision tree on the question of whether — and why — we can rely on introspection, which takes a respondent from a widely shared starting commitment to a named position in the debate.

Structure the tree as follows:

1. Begin at a root almost everyone in the room would accept (something like: I generally know what I believe, want, and feel better than anyone else does), and end in named positions. Each internal node should be a single diagnostic question about a concrete case or a specific claim — never a request for a label. A respondent should be able to answer every question before ever hearing of the positions it distinguishes.

2. Build the tree from the logical space first: identify the points at which the positions genuinely come apart, and only then locate positions at the leaves. Combinations of answers that no one defends should appear as marked dead ends, each with a sentence or two diagnosing why the combination is empty — incoherent, unstable under pressure, or merely unexplored.

3. The diagnostic questions should draw on a range of failure and success cases rather than a single intuition. Please include at least the following: (a) post-hoc rationalization, where people sincerely explain a choice by reasons that demonstrably played no role in producing it; (b) the phenomenon whereby answering "do I believe that p?" seems to be settled by thinking about p itself rather than by inspecting oneself; (c) whether one can be wrong about one's own current pain or about the detailed character of one's ongoing visual experience; (d) avowed attitudes that diverge from what behavior and reaction-time measures reveal; (e) deliberation, where making up one's mind seems to fix what one will do without any self-observation at all.

4. The leaves must include, at minimum: a perceptual or inner-sense model of introspection; a constitutivist account on which self-ascription partly makes the mental state what it is; an account on which self-knowledge is a matter of rational agency or commitment rather than detection; an expressivist treatment of first-person utterances; a deflationary interpretivism on which we self-ascribe in much the way we ascribe states to others; and an empirically minded error theory that takes the rationalization evidence to discredit introspection wholesale. If you think a further position deserves its own leaf, add it.

5. At the end, flag any pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate with the questions you have written, and state what further case or claim would be needed to split them.

6. For each leaf, add one sentence stating the most awkward consequence the view has to swallow with respect to one of the cases listed in (3).

The audience is second-year undergraduates who have taken one course in philosophy of mind but have no prior exposure to this debate. Aim for six to ten internal nodes.
