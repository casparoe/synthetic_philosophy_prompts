I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm trying to get a clear conceptual understanding of a worry about iterated amplification and distillation (IDA) as an alignment strategy. As I understand the scheme: amplification means a human, assisted by copies of the current model, breaks a hard task into easier subtasks and combines the results; distillation means training a fresh model to imitate the amplified system's behavior; then you iterate. Please explain the following three issues, in enough depth that I come away understanding the actual structure of the debate rather than a summary of slogans.

First, the decomposition circularity. To break a problem into subproblems whose answers can be composed into a correct answer to the original, the decomposer seemingly needs to already grasp the shape of the solution. Decomposing "is this argument sound?" into lemma-checking subtasks, for instance, presupposes that you know the argument's dependency structure. If decomposition is only reliable when performed by something that more or less understands the answer already, what work is amplification actually doing? Explain how defenders of the scheme respond — for example, by appealing to the gap between generating a solution and verifying one, or to decompositions of oversight rather than of the task itself — and whether these responses dissolve the circularity or merely relocate it one level down.

Second, distillation fidelity. In the amplified system, whatever competence and trustworthiness exists seems to live partly in the decomposition-and-recombination procedure itself, not in any single component. When you distill by training a model to match the amplified system's outputs, are you compressing that procedure into the weights, or merely fitting a lookup table over observed input-output pairs? Explain under what conditions imitation of outputs plausibly preserves the underlying competence — and alignment-relevant dispositions like honesty or corrigibility — and under what conditions it produces a model that matches behavior on-distribution while lacking whatever made the amplified system safe to consult.

Third, the interaction between the two over many iterations. Even granting that each individual step is sound in isolation, does small per-step error or value drift compound through the loop, the way repeatedly re-encoding a file with a lossy codec eventually destroys it? Is there any principled reason to expect the iteration to converge to a fixed point that still reflects the original human overseer's values, or is that an empirical hope riding on each step being conservative enough?

Throughout, feel free to use analogies from software systems — recursion, lossy compression, test-generation asymmetries, compiler correctness — where they genuinely illuminate, but please flag explicitly where an analogy misleads, since I suspect my instincts from engineering could steer me wrong here.
