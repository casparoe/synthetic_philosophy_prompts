The passage that caused all the trouble was this one, which one of the participants read aloud twice:

"Whatever we conceive as existent, we can also conceive as non-existent. There is no being, therefore, whose non-existence implies a contradiction. Consequently there is no being, whose existence is demonstrable."

Their exchange ran as follows. (1) Maya argued that Cleanthes' reasoning simply conflates conceivability with possibility: the fact that we can form some thought of a thing's non-existence does not show that its non-existence is genuinely possible, since conceivability is a notoriously fallible guide to modal truth, and so the passage does nothing to dislodge the claim that a necessary being could exist. (2) Dev replied that this defense backfires, because the contingency argument itself runs on modal intuitions of exactly the kind Maya is now distrusting: its premise that the universe is contingent rests on the thought that the universe could have failed to exist or could have been otherwise, and if conceivability-based evidence is inadmissible against the necessary being, it is equally inadmissible for establishing the contingency of the cosmos, in which case the argument never gets started; Cleanthes' point, Dev said, is really about partiality, since whatever we can imagine away, deity included, cannot be necessary on pain of special pleading. (3) Maya answered that there is a principled asymmetry: the defender of the cosmological argument does not merely report an inability to conceive the first cause otherwise, but offers independent argument that contingent reality requires a necessary ground -- every member of the universe is the kind of thing that comes into and passes out of existence, and a totality composed entirely of such things cannot account for itself -- whereas the case for the universe's contingency and the case for the necessary being's necessity do not stand or fall together, since only the former is a bare modal intuition. (4) Dev's final response was that Maya had not answered Cleanthes at all but merely re-asserted the argument Cleanthes was attacking: the allegedly independent argument presupposes that contingent things demand an explanation of the whole series rather than member-by-member explanations, which is precisely the principle of sufficient reason that the critic denies, and her claim that a totality of contingent parts must be contingent shows at most that the universe might not have existed, not that anything exists necessarily -- the conclusion of the argument remains exactly as vulnerable to the conceivability objection as it was before she spoke.
