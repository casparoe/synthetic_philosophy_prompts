I'm a PhD student in logic (mostly proof theory, so this is adjacent rather than my home turf), and I keep seeing Gödel's ontological argument pop up in popular writing. A recent magazine piece put it like this:

"Gödel defined God as the being that possesses all positive properties, and then, using nothing beyond the standard axioms of modal logic, proved that such a being must exist. The proof is formally valid, so the only escape is to deny that the properties Gödel lists — omniscience, omnipotence, moral perfection — really are positive, which is hard to take seriously. In effect, Gödel settled the existence of God by pure logic, in roughly the way Anselm tried to: existence turns out to be built into the very definition of God."

My instinct is that this is mostly fine as a gloss and that philosophers of religion are just being picky when they grumble about it — after all, the formalization has been machine-checked, so surely the popular version can't be that far off. But I have a nagging feeling the bit about "standard axioms of modal logic" is doing more work than it admits, and I'm not sure the comparison to Anselm is right either. How fair is this summary, actually? Please break down what's accurate, what's a subtle misrepresntation, and what a defender of Gödel's argument (or at least a careful commentator) would want corrected.
