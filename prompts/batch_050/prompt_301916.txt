I'm a retired research chemist (40 years in materials science) who has fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole since retiring, and I now run a little discussion group at our local library -- about a dozen adults, mixed backgrounds, no philosophy training, meeting for 90 minutes once a month. We've just worked through Leibniz's Monadology (the 1714 one, in an old translation I found online), and the section that really grabbed people was near the end, where he argues roughly that when you trace back the reasons for contingent things, the chain of explanations can't just keep going forever or rest on more contingent things, so the sufficient reason for the whole series has to lie outside the series, in something that exists necessarily. What struck me is that about half the group immediately felt the pull of this and half just shrugged and said the universe might not need a reason at all, full stop. Meanwhile I'd told them earlier about the kalam version of the cosmological argument (everything that begins to exist has a cause, the universe began to exist, etc.) and nobody much cared -- as a scientist I'm not that moved by it either, since the whole thing seems to hinge on whether actual infinities are impossible, and I lost my fear of infinities decades ago in calculus class. But the contingency argument got under people's skin, and I want to lean into that.

So: design me an activity or game for this group that makes the shrug-half actually feel the force of the contingency version before anyone names it. My vague idea is something built around chains of "why" questions -- maybe with real (small) stakes, tokens or poker chips or something, where people experience the difference between pushing the explanation one step further back and actually ending it, and where somebody inevitably tries the move of saying the whole chain just doesn't need a reason, and the group has to reckon with whether that's a legitimate stopping point or a refusal to play. But I don't want to lecture about the principle of sufficient reason up front -- the rules of the activity should make them run into the regress problem themselves, the way you'd design a physics demo so the surprising result happens before you write the equation on the board. Whatever structure you propose, it should be runnable by a non-philosopher facilitator (me) with people who won't do any homework.

Please include: the full rules and materials, a rough timeline for the 90 minutes, what I should and shouldn't say while it's running, and a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the argument -- the PSR, the claim that the series can't be self-explanatory, the leap to a necessary being, and the standard escape hatches (brute facts, the Hume-ish point that once you've explained each part you've explained the whole, the objection that "necessary being" doesn't get you to anything remotely like God even if it works). Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- with this crowd I can already picture one guy answering every why-question with "because of the Big Bang" and another one bringing up quantum indeterminacy within five minuites, so advice on handling both would be great -- and say which feature of Leibniz's actual argument the activity can't reproduce and I'll just have to state outright. If it helps, I'm happy for the debrief to end on the live question rather than a resolution, because frankly I'm still not sure where I land on it myself.
