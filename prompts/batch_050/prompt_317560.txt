I sit on my city's algorithmic accountability commission, and next week we issue our first real decision. Last fall one of the city-contracted autonomous delivery shuttles braked late at a crosswalk and seriously injured a cyclist. The investigation turned up nothing: the vendor met every spec in the procurement contract, the engineers tested well beyond what was required, our deployment officer followed the approval checklist to the letter, and the decision logs show the system doing exactly what it was designed to do -- the crash was a rare sensor-fusion edge case no one had any reason to forsee. Nobody was negligent, but the cyclist is still in rehab and the public wants a name.

Three options are on the table: (1) hold the deployment officer who signed off answerable, on the theory that someone in the chain must be sanctionable or deterrence and public trust both collapse -- our chair calls this the "designated answerer" model; (2) declare it a no-fault accident, compensate the cyclist from an insurance fund, and discipline no one; (3) refuse to assign anyone responsibility for this case but impose strict liability plus a mandatory bond on vendors going forward, so answerability sits with whoever profits from deployment.

Draft the commission's decision memo recommending one of these (or a principled hybrid) as both the disposition of this case and our standing policy. Give a bottom line council can vote on, and engage the strongest argument for whichever option you're rejecting rather than a strawman of it. End with a short paragraph on what would change the memo's verdict -- e.g. what fact about how these systems actually fail, or which consideration about what responsibility is *for*, would push it toward a different option.
