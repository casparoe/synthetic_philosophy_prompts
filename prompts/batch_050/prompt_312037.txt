ok so I'm a software engineer, mostly backend plus some time spent poking at smart contracts and crypto protocols. Zero formal philosophy. I keep running into these two decision theory thought experiments people call "counterfactual mugging" and "XOR blackmail" and I have a strong hunch that both of them are secretly just protocol design problems wearing a costume.

The first one, as I understand it: a perfect predictor flips a coin. If heads, she predicts whether you'd pay her $100 in the tails world, and if she predicts you would, she gives you $1000. If tails, she actually asks you for the $100, and you get nothing either way. The interesting move is paying up in the tails world even though the $1000 was never on the table *for you*, because that policy is what gets the money in the heads world.

The second: some agent knows whether a disaster is coming to your house. She commits to sending you a "disaster incoming, pay me $20 for the info" email if and only if (disaster XOR a hidden coin flip she did), so that getting the email doesn't actually tell you whether the disaster is real, but paying her still looks weirdly attractive to certain decision theories.

Here's what I actually want: restate both of these in the vocabulary I work in — commit-reveal schemes, oracles, trusted execution environments, preimages, incentive compatibility, that whole toolbox. Like, Omega is basically a perfect oracle plus a TEE that can simulate my decision procedure, right? And the coin flip is a commitment to randomness that stays sealed. Do it as precisely as you can for both cases.

Then the important part: the residue report. What doesn't survive the translation? My suspicion is that once you write these down as protocols, the "weirdness" mostly evaporates — paying in the tails world is just following the strategy you would have committed to ex ante, same as honoring a losing leg of a contract. But I also suspect something genuinely does get lost, like maybe the whole thing hinges on the predictor simulating *me* specifically, and there's no clean analog of "being simulated" in protocol-land, or the XOR one needs some notion of the email being *about* something that doesn't map onto anything. Tell me which parts of the philosophical debate have no counterpart in my vocabulary, and whether that means my toolbox is missing a concept or means the philosophers are arguing about something a protocol designer would correctly regard as a non-issue.
