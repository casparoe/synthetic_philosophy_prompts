A friend in the mathematics department walked me into the following argument last week, and I still cannot find the exit, though I refuse the conclusion.

(1) Cantor's diagonal argument establishes that the real numbers are uncountable: given any enumeration of the reals in (0, 1) as infinite decimal expansions, one constructs a number differing from the nth expansion in its nth decimal place; this number is absent from the enumeration, so no enumeration exhausts the reals.

(2) The method is perfectly general. Given any set S whose members can be represented as infinite strings of digits, and any enumeration of members of S, the diagonal construction yields a member of S absent from the enumeration; hence S is uncountable.

(3) The natural numbers admit such a representation: write each in binary, padded to the left with an infinite tail of zeros, and index the digit positions from the right.

(4) Enumerate the naturals in the usual order and apply the construction, letting the diagonal string differ from the nth string at the nth position. The resulting string is absent from the enumeration, and so, by (2), the naturals are uncountable.

(5) But the enumeration in (4) simply is a listing of the naturals; the naturals are countable by definition.

(6) Therefore the diagonal method is unsound: it does not in fact establish uncountability, and for all Cantor has shown, the reals may be countable as well.

My friend welcomes (6) as a route into finitism — if the canonical proof of the uncountable is methodologically rotten, the actual infinite loses its best motivation. I am not prepared to abandon either the countability of the naturals or Cantor's theorem, so something between (1) and (6) has to give. Which step should I reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if the defect lies in the generality claimed at (2), I want the precise condition — satisfied by the reals but not by the naturals — on which the soundness of Cantor's argument actually depends.
