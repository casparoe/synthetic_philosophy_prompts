I'm a novelist, and for several years I've run a Saturday workshop for about ten MFA students, meeting for two hours. Watching writers chase prizes, sales, and likes has convinced me that the surest way to ruin good work is to optimize the reward signal instead of the thing the signal was meant to track -- and lately I've been reading about how artificial agents can fail in structurally similar ways: by gaming the proxy they're scored on, or, in the limiting case, by seizing control of the reward channel itself so that the signal reads maximum regardless of what it was supposed to measure. I'd like my students to feel both failure modes in their bones before either is named.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in AI safety and real experience designing classroom exercises. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming two or three candidate game structures, saying briefly why you settled on the one you did. Then give me the full design: rules and materials, a scoring scheme that requires no money, and at least two phases -- one in which the announced scoring rule is a crude proxy for good writing and students can exploit it, and one in which students control the record of their own scores and can simply make it say what they want. Include a debrief that maps the first phase onto specification gaming and the second onto wireheading proper, a candid note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and an honest statement of which feature of genuine wireheading a game like this cannot reproduce. If the debrief can also draw the line back to writing for applause rather than for the work, so much the better.
