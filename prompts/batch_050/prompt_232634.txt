At a recent dinner, two friends of mine — one a few weeks into deciding whether to accept a job offer abroad, the other a bystander to that decision — fell into an argument about whether a person can assign probabilities to her own still-pending choices. I recount their exchange below as accurately as I can, and I would like it adjudicated.

Clara has been deliberating about the job offer for weeks and says she genuinely has not made up her mind. Daniel remarked that, whatever she eventually decides, he currently puts it at roughly 70–30 that she accepts, since in his observation she always ends up following the money. Clara objected that no such number exists for her while she is still deciding, and the conversation went roughly as follows.

Clara: From the inside, the choice is simply open. Deliberating just is treating both options as live and as depending on what I conclude. If I had already settled on a 0.7 probability that I'll accept, I would no longer be deciding anything — I would be observing myself the way I observe the weather. So your number may be fine for you, but there is no corresponding credence available to me.

Daniel: That is too strong. Your deliberation does not suspend the facts about you. I know your track record, and I predict you the way I predict anyone. If my 70–30 turns out to be right, it was presumably right all along — including tonight, while you insist there is nothing for it to be right about.

Clara: I do not deny that you can hold that number. I deny that I can occupy it. Suppose I assign 0.7 to accepting. In deliberation, that should function as evidence — but evidence for what? My own future choice cannot be an input into the very deliberation that produces it; that is circular. A prediction I adopt feeds back into the decision it purports to describe, so it is not stable under being believed by me — and stability under being believed is the least one can ask of a credence.

Daniel: You trade on self-prediction constantly. You know you will not rise early, so you decline morning commitments; you know certain conversations make you defensive, so you avoid them. Those are probabilities about your own conduct, and acting on them is simply good planning.

Clara: Those concern settled habits, where I have stepped back from deciding and treat my own behavior as part of the background. The present case is different precisely because the matter is still in my hands. If I forecast a pending choice the way I forecast my sleep habits, deliberation becomes a charade — I would merely be waiting to learn what I already believe.

Daniel: Then consider a wager. If you truly have no probability tonight, you should be indifferent between taking either side of an even-odds bet on your decision. But of course you would refuse, and the moment money rides on it you would simply choose to make yourself win. That shows something real was there all along — and it also shows your view has an awkward consequence: I can know what you will do better than you can, despite your privileged access to your own reasons.

Clara: The wager proves my point, not yours. What collapses under the bet is not a hidden credence but the pretense that there was one: a "probability" that turns into a decision as soon as it has stakes was a decision in waiting, not a forecast. And your supposed epistemic advantage is no advantage at all if the price of my sharing it is that my deliberation stops being deliberation.

Please go through the exchange point by point and say who is right at each step — not merely who wins overall. In particular: Is Clara correct that adopting a credence about a pending choice is unstable or circular in a way that Daniel's third-person forecast is not? Does Daniel's wager argument establish that a genuine probability exists, or does it equivocate between prediction and decision? Is Clara's distinction between settled dispositions and open choices principled, or does it merely relabel cases where she prefers not to predict herself? Finally, what, if anything, are both parties missing — for instance, about whether probabilities over one's own actions must be capable of guiding deliberation in order to be real, or about whether a choice can be genuinely open to the agent while being predictable to everyone who knows her?
