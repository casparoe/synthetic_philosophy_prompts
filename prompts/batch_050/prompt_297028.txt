Two mates of mine (both software engineers, no philosophy training) got into an arguement at the pub last week about mind uploading, and it's still rumbling on over WhatsApp. I've stayed out of it so far, but I'd like an actual verdict. You're a professional philosopher specialising in personal identity and philosophy of mind. Think carefully: for each move below, first restate it in its strongest form, then rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a short justification, and at the end say who's closest to right overall and what both of them are missing. Don't just fence-sit.

The setup they both accepted: a company offers a "Neurobridge" procedure. Instead of scanning and copying your brain in one go, synthetic neurons are swapped in for your biological ones one at a time over about a year, while you're awake and living normally, each synthetic neuron taking over the exact functional role of the one it replaces. There's also a cheaper "redundancy" version where synthetic neurons are added alongside your biological ones and gradually take over as the biological ones die off, rather than being swapped directly. Tom says both versions are obviously survivable. Priya says neither is, and you've paid for an elaborate funeral. The exchange, roughly as it went:

1. Tom: if replacing neurons one by one kills you, name the neuron whose replacement is the lethal one. There isn't one. There's no moment of death, no break in experience, no gap anywhere, so there's no death. Any theory that says otherwise has to locate a magical threshold, and it can't.

2. Priya: that argument proves too much. Tiny steps can add up to a huge change — that's how erosion works, and going bald, and every sorites case ever. And notice what the endpoint is: a functional replica. If a scan-and-copy of you produced in one afternoon would just be a copy, then arriving at exactly the same endpoint slowly doesn't stop it being a copy. Gradualness changes how the copying happens, not what it produces.

3. Tom: biology already replaces your matter constantly — proteins, membranes, atoms turning over all the time. If sameness of stuff is what survival requires, you've died thousands of times since breakfast. Survival has to be about the pattern and its continuity, and the procedure preserves that perfectly.

4. Priya: look at the redundancy version, where it's obvious what's really going on. The synthetic network is a set of new parts trained up by the old brain; when the last biological neuron dies, the thing left running is something that was only ever a passenger. The swap version hides this by doing the replacement faster, but the same thing happens: a successor gets constructed alongside you and inherits your life. Tom conceded the redundancy version "feels worse" but said feelings aren't arguments.

5. Tom: at no point during the year does the patient notice anything. Every single evening they go to bed feeling entirely themselves. If Priya is right, the patient is being fooled from the inside about their own continued existence, and there's no fact they could ever access that marks the moment they "died". That consequence is absurd — survival can't be completely invisible to the survivor at every instant.

6. Priya: first-person testimony can't settle identity, because a perfect copy would also wake up feeling continuous with your past — that's precisely why the testimony is worthless. People with confabulated memories feel continuous with a past that isn't theirs. Feeling like the same person is exactly what any adequate successor would feel. The patient's confidence after the procedure is evidence of nothing except that the engineering worked.

Please adjudicate. A few things I specifically want addressed: does the difference between the swap and redundancy versions make any real philosophical difference, or is Tom right that it's just an intuition pump? Whose move does the most work in the overall dispute? And is there a position neither of them considered — they both seem to assume survival here must be all-or-nothing, and I'm not sure that assumption was ever argued for.
