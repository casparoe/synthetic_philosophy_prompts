In a commentary piece I keep seeing cited in media-literacy discussions, the author argues that anxiety about deepfakes is overblown by leaning on a single analogy: deepfakes are to video what Photoshop is to photographs. Just as we absorbed decades of doctored images without ceasing to treat photographs as evidence, the argument goes, we will absorb synthetic video without ceasing to treat footage as evidence; the technology changes the cost of fakery, not its possibility, and so nothing epistemically fundamental is happening. A colleague of mine objects that the analogy fails at exactly the load-bearing point. Her view, roughly, is that recordings matter not because they are hard to fake but because of their role as a backstop to testimony: people are kept (somewhat) honest by the standing possibility that a recording could surface and contradict them, and even in the Photoshop era, video continued to play that role. If deepfakes let anyone dismiss genuine footage as fabricated, the backstop dissolves even if no fake is ever actually produced, and testimony loses the constraint that recordings used to impose on it.

Please audit the analogy in the commentary piece rather than just picking a side. First, give the correspondence table: what in the deepfake case is supposed to map onto Photoshop, onto the photograph, onto the public's adjustment period, onto the residual trust we still place in images. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic -- consider at least the difference between altering existing footage and generating footage of events that never occurred, the difference in who can produce a convincing fake and at what cost, and the difference between doubting a particular image and having a standing reason to dismiss any inconvenient recording. Third, assess whether the analogy quietly imports features of the Photoshop case (a detectable chain of custody, a professional gatekeeping layer, a still-functioning backstop in some other medium) that the deepfake case lacks. Fourth, take my colleague's backstop objection seriously: does the claim that video functioned as a check on testimony survive the observation that staged and selectively edited footage existed long before deepfakes, and if the objection holds, does it show the commentary's conclusion was being carried by the analogy rather than argued for? Finally, if the Photoshop analogy cannot do the work asked of it, say whether there is a better analogy for the epistemic situation deepfakes create -- one that captures both the genuine continuity with older manipulation techniques and whatever is genuinely novel -- and note what policy or media-literacy conclusions, if any, the better analogy would actually license.
