You are a humor writer with a scholar's command of the applied-ethics literature on torture. Before drafting, take a moment to plan: list the five idealizations built into the standard ticking-time-bomb hypothetical (the scenario in which a captured terrorist is presumed to know the location of an armed bomb, the clock is short, no other interrogation method can work in time, and the case is used to argue that torture can be permissible -- which critics answer by noting that these conditions never jointly obtain in practice and that accepting the scenario paves the way for an institutionalized torture apparatus), and assign one idealization to each clause of the piece. Then write the piece.

Write a parody of an official regulatory bulletin issued by the fictitious Bureau of Hypothetical Exigencies, the government office charged with certifying that a given ticking-time-bomb scenario qualifies, under its Standards for Permissible Emergencies, as one in which torture may be contemplated. The humor must come from the Bureau's deadpan insistence on enforcing, as literal certification criteria, the assumptions philosophers quietly build into the thought experiment: that the suspect's guilty knowledge is certain rather than suspected, that the bomb's detonation is imminent enough to exclude lawful methods, that torture is stipulated in advance to succeed, that every alternative (rapport-based interrogation, leniency offers, deception) has been duly exhausted on paper, and that the permission can be contained to a single case without any risk of a standing practice. Let the Bureau also administer a judicial warrant scheme requiring a judge's signature at any hour, treating sleepiness as compatible with impartiality. Exaggerate these real features of the debate rather than inventing new ones, so that a reader familiar with the literature recognizes that the satirist understands what is being satirized and finds the satire fair. The irony should remain legible: without ever stating the standard objections outright, a reader should finish each clause having felt why the certified conditions are unobtainable, why interrogators cannot know what the scenario says they know, and why a one-time exception does not stay one-time. Do not name any real person; the target is the scenario and the style of argument built on it, not an individual. Keep the register flat, procedural, and unsmiling throughout, with forms, schedules, and sub-headings doing the comedic work.

Present the piece as exactly five numbered clauses (Clause 1 through Clause 5), each with a short bureaucratic heading and two to four sentences of regulatory prose, totaling roughly 600-700 words, with no introduction before Clause 1 and no concluding remarks after Clause 5.
