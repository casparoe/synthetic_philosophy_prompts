In software engineering we have our own version of an unresolvable argument: after a serious incident, some of my colleagues insist on a "blameless postmortem" that traces the failure to systems, incentives, and processes, while others argue this lets genuinely negligent engineers off the hook and that character and competence must be part of any honest account. Fifteen years in, neither side has convinced anyone. I recently realized this mirrors the older and darker Browning–Goldhagen debate: Browning's *Ordinary Men* explains the Reserve Police Battalion's participation in mass killing through situation, conformity, and incremental escalation, while Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* explains it through a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism that made the killers willing. Both draw on much of the same archival record. My question is not who was right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, in both cases: is it an empirical dispute about what the evidence shows, a methodological dispute about what counts as an adequate explanation of action, or a deeper standoff between two explanatory frameworks -- situational versus dispositional attribution -- such that the same facts keep getting redescribed rather than weighed? Please ground the diagnosis in what the two sides actually argue, and then tell me what progress would even look like given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the standoff, a kind of evidence neither side has considered, or an argument that some debates about human conduct are structural rather than resolvable.
