I am a novelist, and there is a phenomenon I keep exploiting without having any vocabulary for it. A short story of mine was workshopped as fiction and admired; when a small magazine later reprinted the identical text presented as a found diary fragment, readers found it almost unbearable — same sentences, different weight. I notice the same thing with blurbs: a first chapter reads one way if you trust the narrator survives and another if the jacket copy has told you she does not. It seems a layer of meaning travels with the packaging — genre label, author attribution, circumstances of presentation — rather than with the words themselves, and readers feel genuinely cheated when the packaging turns out to have misled them, even though nothing "in" the work changed. Does this configuration have an established name, and is there a recognized way of dividing what a work communicates into the part fixed by its text and the part supplied by the frame around it? A bare label will not satisfy me: I want the nearest concepts, how this differs from each of them, and where the boundary sits — for instance, whether the author's name on the cover counts as part of the work itself. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
