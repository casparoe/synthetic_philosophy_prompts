I'm running a 75-minute session of an undergrad philosophy of probability seminar (about 16 students, mixed phil and econ majors) and I want them to *feel* why some philosophers think credences should sometimes be intervals or sets of probabilities rather than sharp numbers, instead of just nodding along to the reading. Everyone knows the Ellsberg paradox as a one-shot thought experiment, and my students have all seen it, so a single "which urn do you prefer?" poll won't do anything for them -- they'll just pattern-match to the answer they remember.

Design me an in-class activity that forces them to actually behave in a way no single precise probability can describe, and then makes them notice that about themselves. What I have in mind is something they do repeatedly over maybe 30-40 minutes with real (tiny) stakes: tokens or points that convert into, I dunno, a candy bar for whoever ends with the most. Ideally there's some mechanism where they have to commit to prices or bids in a way that exposes a gap -- they should end up unwilling to buy at X and unwilling to sell at X on the same ambiguous prospect, and then I can hold up their own numbers and ask them what probability they were acting on. Bonus if the activity includes a phase where information trickles in and they can watch that gap shrink (or fail to shrink), because I want to set up the contrast between "imprecision that evidence can fix" and "imprecision that survives evidence."

Give me concrete rules, materials, how many rounds, what I should secretly set up in advance about the urns or whatever's generating the uncertainty, and how to handle the fact that students will try to hedge or collude or just post absurd prices to avoid trading. Also be honest with me about the big limitation -- my worry is the game at most shows ambiguity *aversion*, which is a preference thing, not literally evidence for imprecise *credences*, so tell me how much of that gap the activity can close and what I just have to say out loud at the end. Finish with a single sentence stating the one thing a student who really got it would say on the way out the door.
