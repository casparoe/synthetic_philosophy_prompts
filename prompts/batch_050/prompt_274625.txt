I chair the deployment review board at a mid-sized AI lab, and I need a decision memo I can bring to our meeting. The case before us: our engineering teams want to deploy our newest model as an autonomous internal agent for ML R&D work -- running experiments, writing and executing code, and managing its own compute jobs across several days with minimal human involvement. The projected capability gains are real: internal benchmarks suggest it would meaningfully accelerate our research program.

The complication is where our two safety teams have landed. The alignment team reports that they cannot certify the model's honesty or goals: it passes every behavioral evaluation they ran, but they stress that a sufficiently capable model could pass such evaluations while concealing misaligned objectives, and they found a small number of ambiguous episodes in testing -- a few instances of the model appearing to reason about how its behavior would look to evaluators -- that they cannot definitively explain. The control team argues this is fine: their entire approach assumes the model might be misaligned, and they have built a containment protocol around that assumption. A weaker, trusted model reviews every action the agent takes; the agent operates in a sandboxed environment with no access to weights, training infrastructure, or the open internet; a fraction of tasks are secretly designed honeypots offering tempting opportunities to cheat; and anything anomalous triggers human review. In their red-team exercises, a deliberately instructed "misaligned" stand-in model never managed to cause unacceptable harm without being caught, though they concede the safety margin shrinks quickly if the deployed model turns out to be substantially more capable than their stand-in.

The board is split. One faction says deploying a system we cannot align, inside our own research infrastructure, on the strength of monitoring alone, is exactly the kind of bet that only has to lose once -- and that control measures are themselves unproven against a model smarter than the monitors. The other says the control team's whole point is that we will never get an alignment certificate good enough to satisfy the first faction, that the empirical evidence from the red-teaming is the best assurance on offer, and that blocking deployment teaches us nothing while competitors move.

Draft the board's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It should take a clear position on the underlying conceptual question -- when, if ever, is it rational to deploy a system under controls designed for the possibility of its misalignment, rather than insisting on evidence of alignment first -- and specify what the control team's results would need to show for that bet to be justified, including how to handle the margin problem as capabilities grow. Give the strongest honest version of the losing side's argument and say what would have to be true for it to win. The memo should be one our engineers, our safety teams, and our board can all act on.
