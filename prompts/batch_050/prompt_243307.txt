In The Fixation of Belief (Popular Science Monthly, 1877), C. S. Peirce mocks Francis Bacon's picture of inquiry as mechanical rule-following: "That we have only to make some crude experiments, to draw up briefs of the results in certain blank forms, to go through these by rule, checking off everything disproved and setting down the alternatives, and that thus in a few years physical science would be finished up — what an idea!" Modern Bayesian convergence results — Doob's consistency theorem, the Blackwell–Dubins merging-of-opinions theorem — are often read as vindicating a subtler version of the hope Peirce ridicules: feed different Bayesians the same growing stream of evidence and their priors wash out. But the fine print matters. Merging requires that each agent assign positive probability to the truth, that both update on a shared evidence stream within a correctly specified model, and it comes with no finite-time rate.

I advise a small foundation that can fund exactly one three-person research group for three years. The funder's endpoint is practical rather than purely theoretical: better institutional handling of persistent expert disagreement, of the sort seen in forecasting tournaments, risk assessments, and policy panels. Which open problem in the vicinity of these theorems should get the grant — learning under model misspecification, disagreement over what the evidence is or how it bears on hypotheses, the absence of convergence guarantees at decision-relevant timescales, or some problem I have not named — and in what order should the runner-ups fall? Please defend the ordering with explicit criteria, such as closeness to the applied endpoint, tractability for a group of that size, and how much other work depends on resolving the problem first, and say where those criteria conflict and how you would weigh them. Finally, state what result or argument would change your top pick, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. I would like the answer in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — and in American English.
