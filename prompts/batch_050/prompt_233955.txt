A few months ago I fell down a YouTube hole about AI risk and got properly stuck on one thing: people who worry about powerful AI keep saying we should get it to "avoid side effects" or "minimise its impact on the world", and I can't work out how you'd ever define that without basically listing everything humans care about. Like, a robot crossing a room to fetch coffee moves every air molecule in the room, might knock a vase, might save a vase — where's the line, and who drew it? Anyway, slightly rashly, I've agreed to run a session on this for the after-school coding club at my nephew's school. Kids about 14-16, decent little programmers, zero philosophy, and most of their opinions come from short videos so they're a mix of hyped and sceptical.

I don't want the idea explained to me — I want a plan for how I should explain it to them, since I get one hour and no second chance. What has to be in place before "low impact" even makes sense as a design goal? My guess is they first need to see why "just tell it not to break anything" doesn't work, but maybe there's something even more basic I'm skipping. What's the one example to build the whole session around — I'm torn between the classic coffee-fetching robot and a cleaning robot in a hospital ward, so tell me which carries it better and what the carrying example needs to have and definately needs to not have (e.g. if anyone dies in the story, they'll spend the whole hour arguing about trolleys instead). Then the misconceptions: I'm predicting "side effects just means the bad stuff", "can't you make it ask a human before doing anything?", and "everything changes the world so the whole idea is daft" — tell me the order those will show up in, where in my sequence each one gets formed, and exactly where I should head them off rather than waiting for them. Also: how do I check at the end whether they've grasped the actual puzzle — that spelling out impact seems to demand a full picture of what humans value, unless there's some clever way around it — rather than just nodding along? And what should I deliberately leave out at this level: do I even mention that researchers have proposed specific mathematical versions of this, or does that muddy it for a first pass? Please write the answer in American English, since that's what my slides have to be in.
