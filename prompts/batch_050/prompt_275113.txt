I have been reading about the dollar auction, the game in which an ordinary dollar bill is sold to the highest bidder, with the twist that the second-highest bidder must also pay their bid while receiving nothing. As I understand the analysis, once two bidders are involved, each faces the same calculation: dropping out guarantees a certain loss equal to one's current bid, whereas continuing to bid at least preserves the possibility of winning the dollar and recouping what has already been committed. This seems to me to demonstrate that taking sunk costs into account is sometimes genuinely rational, since walking away would mean treating the money already bid as a pure, unredeemed loss. Yet decision theorists and behavioral economists routinely hold up the sunk cost fallacy as a paradigmatic error and advise ignoring irrecoverable past expenditures entirely. If the dollar auction shows that escalating one's commitment in order to recover sunk costs can be the rational course, on what grounds do these theorists continue to condemn that reasoning? Should they not qualify their advice to exempt precisely these competitive situations, where abandoning a prior investment appears to be the costlier choice?
