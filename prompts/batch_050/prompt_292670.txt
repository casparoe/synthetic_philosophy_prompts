I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm developing an interactive feature to accompany a reported piece on the ethics of pest control: everything from backyard mosquito spraying and bedbug fumigation to agricultural neonicotinoids and laboratory work on fruit flies. The feature is a "where do you actually stand?" quiz, and I'd like you to build the full instrument for me.

Here is what I need:

Eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete predicament rather than an abstract principle. For example, a reader might be asked what they'd do about a wasp nest beside a toddler's play area, whether a purely cosmetic lawn-grub treatment differs morally from protecting a food crop, whether a glue trap is worse than a fast-acting spray, whether eradicating an invasive hornet to protect native bees is a reason for or against intervention, or whether farming crickets for protein is an improvement over conventional livestock. No question should ask anything like "do you think insects feel pain?" or "do you believe insects have rights?" -- the point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by the labels they find flattering. Each question gets two to four answer options.

A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions. The leaves must include at least: a hedonic utilitarianism that counts insect suffering at face value and lets the enormous numbers drive the conclusion; a precautionary view that gives uncertain insect sentience enough weight to forbid routine killing but not defensive killing; a threshold view on which insect interests count but are always outweighed by nontrivial human interests; a contractualist or community-of-rights view on which insects simply fall outside the moral circle; an ecocentric view that evaluates killing by its effects on populations and ecosystems rather than on individuals; and a virtue- or care-based position focused on the character of the killer and the manner of killing rather than on outcomes. If your tree produces combinations of answers that no one seems to occupy, say so briefly and diagnose why: incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. And flag any pair of positions your questions cannot really separate -- for instance, I suspect the virtue position and the threshold position may give identical answers to every realistic case, and if so I'd rather be told that than have the difference papered over.

Two further constraints. First, the diagnostic work should be done by questions on which the positions genuinely come apart -- aggregation across vast numbers, how to weigh uncertain sentience, intended versus merely foreseen harm, whether the victim's status as an invader or a "pest" changes anything -- and each branch question should be phrased so a reader can answer it without knowing which label it funnels toward. Second, a note for my fact-check desk: wherever the quiz presupposes an empirical claim, mark what is settled (that insects exhibit nociception and avoidance learning, say) as distinct from what remains genuinely contested among researchers (whether any insect has conscious experience of pain, and in which orders), and make sure no branch of the tree quietly treats a contested verdict on insect sentience as established consensus.
