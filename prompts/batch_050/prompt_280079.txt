Two philosophers of biology have been arguing for years about whether DNA is literally a code. The first, the semanticist, holds that talk of genetic information is not a façon de parler: genes carry coded instructions, the correspondence between codons and amino acids is arbitrary in the way that a genuine code must be, the cell contains machinery that reads and translates a message, and this is why genes -- unlike, say, the altitude at which a plant grows -- can be said to specify traits and to explain why organisms resemble their parents. The second, the deflationist, replies that "code" and "information" here are heuristics borrowed from engineering: the genetic code's assignments are fixed by chemistry and evolution rather than by anything like convention, every causal process can be redescribed as a signal carrying information about its causes, so calling DNA a code singles it out without justification, and what is doing the real explanatory work is ordinary causal-mechanistic detail about transcription and translation.

Draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. The document must do the following:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should be substantive, not vacuous: facts about molecular biology and about the practice of genetics that both parties accept (consider, for example, what both will say about the near-universality and evolvability of the codon table, about alternative splicing and RNA editing, about overlapping reading frames, about noncoding and regulatory DNA, and about the predictive successes of molecular genetics). Include at least one agreed claim about what each side's vocabulary is good for in practice.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one precise point of dispute, phrased so that each side would accept the formulation of its own position. Disagreements worth considering include: whether arbitrariness of the codon assignments is enough for genuine semantic content; whether the deflationist can recover the privileged status of genes in explanation, or is committed to a causal-parity thesis on which cytoplasmic and environmental factors are equally "informational"; whether the semanticist's account survives the fact that the same stretch of DNA is "read" differently in different contexts (splicing, editing, overlapping genes); and what either side should say about epigenetic marks and whether they too carry information. Where a disagreement turns out, on inspection, to be about words rather than the world, say so explicitly and identify which one.

3. A list of cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, name a concrete case, finding, or line of argument that both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts or would have to concede. Candidates include: engineered organisms with reassigned or expanded genetic codes; the analogy (or disanalogy) between the genetic code and genuinely conventional codes like Morse; and whether information talk has ever guided a discovery that a purely mechanistic redescription would have missed or gotten wrong.

The tone should be that of two careful philosophers who want the residual disagreement stated as exactly as possible, not that of a moderator declaring the debate resolved. Where the collaboration would genuinely fail to produce agreement on even the framing, note that rather than forcing a formulation.
