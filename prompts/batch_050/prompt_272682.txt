I am a journalist who covers economics and political ideas, and I am preparing a long feature on the recent revival of serious socialist thought — market socialism, participatory planning, workplace democracy proposals, and the like. While researching, I have been reading John Stuart Mill's late, posthumously published Chapters on Socialism (1879), and one section in particular struck me as a better piece of journalism than anything I am likely to write. In it, Mill sets out what he takes to be the real difficulties any socialist scheme must confront: he asks how a community would allocate labour among its members when some work is unpleasant, whether people would exert themselves with equal energy once the connection between individual effort and individual reward is loosened, and whether a population no longer restrained by private economic prudence would grow until the gains of common ownership were eaten away. Notably, he insists throughout that these are questions to be answered by the socialist, not proofs that socialism is impossible, and he elsewhere warns that communism at its best must be compared not with private property as it actually exists but with private property as it might be under a just regime.

Here is what I want from you. Before I interview anyone, I want the specification, not the pitch: write the desiderata that any adequate theory of a socialist economy would have to meet to deserve serious coverage — the problems of allocation, incentives, and motivation it must solve or dissolve, the empirical findings about human behaviour and about historical attempts it cannot simply wish away, the distinctions it must respect (for instance, between common ownership of capital and common management of work, or between equality of outcome and equality of sacrifice), and the cases it must get right. For each desideratum, give the consideration behind it, and say whether it is a genuine requirement any theory in this space must meet or merely a prejudice of one camp — Mill's Victorian assumptions about work and character included, where you think that is what they are. Where you suspect two or more desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly; if the honest conclusion is that no scheme can meet the whole list, that is itself the story. Then, briefly and without pretending to be exhaustive, tell me how the main contemporary contenders — market socialism, centrally or participatorily planned models, and the social-democratic position that the best achievable socialism is a reformed capitalism — fare against the list, so that I know which desiderata do the most work in separating them. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard; I can fill in the scoring myself after the interviews.
