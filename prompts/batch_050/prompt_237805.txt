You are a philosopher of science with particular expertise in the demarcation problem, and I would like you to reason carefully through the following exercise rather than reach for a textbook answer. I am a teaching assistant for a philosophy of science seminar, and all term the professor has been issuing verdicts on which fields, theories, and practices count as science. Her verdicts so far: astrology as actually practiced, with its endless explaining-away of failed predictions -- not science. Eighteenth-century phlogiston chemistry -- science, even though false, because it stuck its neck out and got it wrung. Psychoanalysis as practiced in the 1930s, equally compatible with a patient loving or hating his father -- not science. Nineteenth-century celestial mechanics -- science, and she stresses that this verdict holds even though its practitioners repeatedly shielded Newton's theory by positing unseen planets, one of which was triumphantly found and one of which never was. Intelligent design -- not science. A present-day parapsychology lab that pre-registers its hypotheses, publishes its null results, and welcomes replication attempts -- science, she insists, merely unsuccessful science; the subject matter being disreputable is irrelevant. A believer who accepts every radiometric dating result but holds that the world was created with the appearance of age -- not science. Pure mathematics -- not empirical science, though she is careful to add that this is no insult, since demarcating science from non-science is not the same as separating sense from nonsense. Finally, a lone crank with a genuinely risky, falsifiable theory that no journal will touch and no community works on -- science, she says, just ignored science.

Next week she will unveil this term's test case, and I want to predict her verdict before she gives it. The case: a growing research community in fundamental cosmology, mathematically rigorous, housed in elite departments, with its own journals, conferences, and internal standards of peer review. Its central posit -- that our universe is one among a vast multiplicity -- is invoked to explain why our physical constants appear fine-tuned. By its practitioners' own admission, no experiment with current or foreseeable technology could confirm or refute the posit, and they increasingly defend the programme by appeal to explanatory unification, mathematical elegance, and the fertility of the formalism.

Here is what I want from you. Begin by brainstorming the candidate rules her track record might embody -- I can think of at least three: a Popper-style falsificationism, a Lakatos-style appraisal of research programmes by their progressive or degenerating trajectory, and a Kuhn-style appeal to paradigm-governed puzzle-solving communities; there may be a fourth centred on institutional method and epistemic virtue. For each candidate rule, state what it predicts about the new case and check it against every earlier verdict, flagging any verdict that strains it. Then give me your best-guess prediction of her actual verdict, with your reasoning about which rule the track record most plausibly encodes -- I note, for instance, that her verdicts on the parapsychology lab, the lone crank, and continental drift (which she called science throughout, even while the community reasonably rejected it) seem to point somewhere, though I am unsure where. Close by describing the single further case that would most efficiently discriminate between your two leading candidate rules, and what each possible verdict on that case would reveal.
