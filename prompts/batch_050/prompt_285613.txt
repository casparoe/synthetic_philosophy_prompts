I've been reading the decision theory corners of LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for years, and I'm comfortable with the standard zoo: counterfactual mugging, where a predictor who has already done you a favor in another branch asks you to pay up in this one, and XOR blackmail, where someone sends you selectively filtered bad news and demands payment not to have sent it. But I keep running into a third configuration that doesn't seem to be either of these, and I don't know whether it has a name. In this configuration there is no predictor, no adversary, and no one who profits from your payment in the branch you're actually in. Instead, the structure is generated entirely by the agent's own values. A concrete instance: I care about what happens in branches other than the one I occupy, at least a little. I learn that had a coin come up the other way, a version of events would have unfolded in which some stranger receives a large benefit; in the branch I'm actually in, that benefit goes to no one, and instead I am presented with an opportunity to incur a real cost whose only justification is that, before the coin was flipped, I would have wanted to bind myself to a policy of paying in exactly this situation. Another instance: a donor sets up a commitment where a charity in the actual world gets nothing, but her payment is "owed" to the counterpart-world in which the coin landed differently and the charity was funded. Nobody is mugging her; the mugger-shaped hole in the story is filled by her own counterfactual-caring preferences.

Is there an established term for this — the self-administered or exploiter-free version of the counterfactual mugging structure? I've seen pieces of it discussed under updatelessness and under acausal reasoning, but those seem to name the decision theory or the mechanism rather than the situation itself. If there is no established name, I'd like one coined, with a precise definition. Either way, what I really want is the carving: how exactly does this configuration differ from an ordinary counterfactual mugging, in which a powerful outside agent engineers the asymmetry and captures the payment, and from XOR blackmail, in which an adversary exploits the informational structure of your policy? Does the absence of a beneficiary in the actual branch make the payment more rational (no one is profiting from your exploitability, so there's no incentive being rewarded) or less (you're burning resources for a world that by construction never happens)? And where are the edges of the concept: does it cover cases where the "other branch" is not a physical possibility but merely a hypothetical the agent finds morally salient, and does it cover the temporal version, where the counterfactual self is replaced by one's own past self's ex ante preferences?

One more thing I'd like the answer to address: whether this phenomenon should be classified as a decision problem at all, or whether it's better understood as a question about the structure of values — that is, whether an agent who pays in these cases is being exploited by a quirk of their decision theory, or is simply revealing that their preferences range over possibilities rather than actualities, in which case the "mugging" framing smuggles in a normative judgment. If the literature has treated it under the second description rather than the first, pointers to that framing (without needing citations, just the names of the relevant ideas) would be welcome.
