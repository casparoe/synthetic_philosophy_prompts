I am setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate course on probability and inductive logic. The exam is closed-book, and students have 50 minutes and 30 marks for this question. Write one exam question on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, followed by a model answer and a marking scheme.

Build the exam question around this scenario, which students will not have seen before: a factory produces metal cubes whose side lengths vary randomly between 1 and 2 cm, and three employees propose three different ways to apply the principle of indifference -- one takes the side length to be uniformly distributed, one the face area, one the volume. The question should require students to (a) compute the probability that the side length exceeds 1.5 cm under each parameterization and state plainly that indifference has yielded three inconsistent answers; (b) articulate precisely what the paradox threatens -- the claim that a state of ignorance determines a unique rational prior -- and distinguish this from the weaker claim that ignorance sometimes underdetermines it; (c) evaluate two proposed resolutions that the question should present: first, the production manager's argument that, since the machine is calibrated by setting the side length directly, physical facts about the mechanism single out the uniform-over-side-length distribution and dissolve the paradox; second, a statistician's proposal that the correct prior is the one invariant under changes of measurement unit. Students must then (d) reach and defend a verdict on whether the paradox refutes the principle of indifference outright or only shows that it needs supplementation.

The model answer should be realistic for a strong student writing under the stated time constraint. The marking scheme should allocate the 30 marks across the four components and should name, for full marks, the specific moves a top answer must make: the three numerical answers; the observation that the manager's resolution changes the subject by appealing to empirical information the principle was supposed to do without, rather than vindicating the principle on its own terms; and the recognition that unit-invariance points toward a log-uniform rather than uniform prior, together with some assessment of whether smuggling in a symmetry constraint is a fix or a concession. It should also specify mark deductions for identifiable errors: conflating Bertrand's paradox with the reference class problem, dismissing all parameterizations as equally valid and concluding that probability is meaningless without argument, and treating the inconsistency as a mere rounding or measurement artifact.
