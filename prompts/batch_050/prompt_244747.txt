I'm writing a survey chapter on the paradox of sad music -- the puzzle of why people deliberately seek out music they describe as sad, and report valuing the experience, when sadness is ordinarily a state we take pains to avoid. Here are the solutions I have so far: (1) the formalist answer -- what we savor is the beauty of the music's construction, and its sadness is a quality we merely perceive in the sound, no more distressing to perceive than the sorrow in a painted face; (2) catharsis -- the music provides a safe occasion to discharge pent-up feeling, and the pleasure lies in the release; (3) the make-believe answer -- in listening we only imagine feeling sad, or imagine a persona within the music who feels it, and imagined emotion is not real suffering; (4) the mixed-rewards answer -- the sorrow the music elicits is genuine but outweighed by compensating goods: understanding the emotion, savoring it, the reassurance that we are still capable of deep feeling, a sense of communion with the composer; (5) the debunking answer -- the paradox is manufactured by loose talk, since listeners are never really saddened at all: being moved by music lacks the cognitive content of actual sadness, and listeners, when asked, report predominantly pleasant or bittersweet states. Is there a sixth solution I'm missing? It should be genuinely different from these -- not a rewording, special case, or conjunction of any of them -- and I'd like you to say how it relates to the entries already on the list and whether you think it survives the obvious objections.
