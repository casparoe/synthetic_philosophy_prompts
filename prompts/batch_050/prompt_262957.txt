Our physics club got into this huge argument about the many-worlds interpretation and I'm worried we've accidentally proven its internally inconsistent. Heres the argument: (1) In many-worlds, whenever a quantum measurement happens, the universe splits into one branch for each possible outcome. (2) A measurement where the amplitudes give 70/30 odds still splits into exactly two branches, same as a 50/50 measurement -- one branch where outcome A happens, one where B happens. (3) If there's one branch per outcome and I'm equally "me" in each branch, then the probability of ending up in a given branch is just 1 divided by the number of branches. (4) So EVERY two-outcome measurement gives probability 1/2 for each outcome, no matter what the amplitudes are. (5) But the Born rule says the probability is the square of the amplitude, which is usually not 1/2, and the Born rule is obviously right because like a century of experiments confirm it. (6) So many-worlds contradicts the Born rule, which means many-worlds is false. This feels like it proves way too much -- the physicists who take many-worlds seriously can't all have missed something this simple. Which step goes wrong, and why does it seem so convincing? And if you reject (3) or (4), what do many-worlds people actually say probability even MEANS when every outcome definitely happens somewhere?
