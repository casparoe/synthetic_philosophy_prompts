I am a high school senior taking a philosophy elective, and our class is holding a structured debate next week on the question of whether the human mind could, even in principle, be a computer. I have been assigned the side arguing that Gödel's incompleteness theorems pose a genuine obstacle to that idea. My main source is a popular science book (I would rather not name it) that presents Penrose's version of the argument, and before I rely on it in front of the class I would like to know whether its presentation is actually fair. Here is the passage in question, which I copied out:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems, proved in 1931, show that in any formal system powerful enough to do arithmetic there are true statements that the system cannot prove. Any computer is such a system: its program is just a fixed set of formal rules, so for every computer there will always be a sentence — its 'Gödel sentence' — that it cannot prove. But we, standing outside the machine, can see that this sentence is true: it says, in effect, that it is itself unprovable, and since the machine indeed cannot prove it, what it says is the case. Human mathematicians can therefore know something that no computer can know. Gödel's theorems thus demonstrate that the human mind outperforms every possible machine, and that mathematical understanding cannot be computational. Penrose takes this further: if the mind does something no computer can do, the brain must be exploiting non-computable physics, and he has proposed that the missing ingredient is quantum gravity acting within the brain's microtubules."

Something nags me about the step where we supposedly just see that the machine's unprovable sentence is true — I have a vague feeling that this smuggles something in, but I cannot articulate what. The book treats the whole chain of reasoning as essentially airtight, and the author is an accomplished physicist, so I am inclined to trust it; I would also, frankly, like it to be right, since it supports my side of the debate.

Could you audit this summary for me? Specifically: which parts accurately reflect what Gödel's theorems themselves establish, which parts are further moves made by Lucas and Penrose on top of the theorems (and how contested those moves are among philosophers), and which parts, if any, are subtly misleading or gloss over an assumption the argument needs? I would also like to know whether, if the weak points were conceded to the opposing side, any of the larger conclusions — that minds are not computers, and that new physics is required to explain consciousness — would still stand. My goal is to walk into the debate knowing which claims I can defend and which I would have to retreat on.
