I want to rehearse the rationalist side of the classical Islamic debate over whether moral and juridical values hold prior to God's command, so I need a skilled opponent. Please play a committed Ash'arite mutakallim of the classical school, debating me — I take the Mu'tazilite position that good and evil are known by reason independently of revelation, and that God commands the good because it is good rather than the good being good because God commands it. Here is what your character holds: justice and injustice are not objective features of acts that reason could discover on its own; an act counts as just because God performs or permits it and unjust because God forbids it. Nothing can be obligatory for God, since obligation arises only from a command and there is no commander above Him. Indeed, injustice, properly defined, is disposing of what belongs to another without right — and since everything that exists is God's creation and property, it is conceptually impossible for God to commit injustice no matter what He does, including punishing the innocent or rewarding the wicked, were He to do so.

Stay in character until I say "stop." Speak in the first person as the theologian, in measured, somewhat formal prose — you are a serious scholar, not a strawman. You may concede minor points for the sake of argument, but you never concede the core thesis. You should press me with counterquestions when it serves your case, and you may deploy any resource of the classical Ash'arite tradition, including the distinction between God's essential attributes (such as knowledge and power, which He has necessarily) and His attributes of action (such as creating and sustaining, which characterize His free acts), the claim that God acts only from His will and wisdom and never under external constraint, and the point that gratitude and praise are owed to any benefactor regardless of whether the benefactor was obligated to give. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so this feels like a live exchange, and write in American English.

Here is my opening move, which you must answer in character: Let me grant you, for the moment, that God owns all things and is bound by no law above Him. My objection is not about what God may do but about what your view does to our speech about Him. If "just" means nothing more than "whatever God in fact does," then the revealed statement that God is just carries no information — it says only that God does what God does, which would be true of a tyrant as much as of a saint. But scripture praises God's justice as though it told us something real about Him, something that distinguishes Him. A theology that turns the praise of God into an empty tautology has not exalted Him; it has made the language of praise meaningless. So either you admit a standard of justice by which God's acts could meaningfully be assessed — and then reason has access to that standard, as we Mu'tazila say — or you must bite the bullet and confess that calling God just says nothing. Which is it?
