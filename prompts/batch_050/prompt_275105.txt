I served on a jury last month, and the experience has left me preoccupied with what knowledge actually amounts to. The decisive witness identified the defendant correctly, but only through a chain of lucky accidents in how she perceived the scene — she was right, yet I couldn't bring myself to say she knew what she claimed to know. Since then I've been collecting cases and recording my verdicts, and I'd like to know whether any coherent principle unifies them. My verdicts:

First, my officemate's smart display has been offline for three days and keeps announcing a cached forecast of rain. This morning he glances at it, believes it will rain today, and it does rain. He doesn't know. Second, in a museum where every meteorite in the hall is a replica except one, a visitor points at the single genuine specimen and says, in effect, that one is a real meteorite. She doesn't know, even though she's right. Third, a friend holding one ticket in a million-ticket lottery tells me, before the drawing, that he knows his ticket lost. I say he doesn't know that. Fourth — and this is where my trouble starts — I also think I know, right now, that my flight leaves tomorrow at 6 a.m., even though cancellations and my own misreadings give that belief roughly the same objective error rate as the lottery case. Verdicts three and four are the pair I cannot reconcile. Fifth, a colleague proves a theorem using a lemma she believes on good evidence; the lemma is false, but the theorem happens to be true for reasons she never noticed. She doesn't know the theorem — or at least doesn't know it on the basis of that proof. Sixth, when a scrupulously reliable source tells me a fact I have no reason to doubt, and it is true, I do know it.

Is there a single principle, or a single clause-by-clause analysis of knowledge, that delivers all of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, say which clause each of my cases motivates, and be honest about which verdicts, if any, it has to sacrifice — I suspect three and four cannot both survive. Finally, test it on a case I haven't decided: a thermometer develops two independent faults that exactly cancel, so its readings remain true. Does a nurse who relies on it know her patients' temperatures?
