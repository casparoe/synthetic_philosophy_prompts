Ok so here's a setup I keep arguing about with a friend who does policy work. Grant, just for the sake of argument, the strongest defensible version of "national character": average personality-trait differences between countries are real but small, typically well under half a standard deviation, completely dwarfed by within-country variation, and maybe partly an artifact of people in different cultures using survey response scales differently (what counts as a "strongly agree" in one place might be a "mildly agree" somewhere else).

Given only that, which alleged practical implications actually go through, and which don't? Run through at least these: (1) hiring and team composition decisions in multinational companies, (2) diplomatic and negotiation strategy, (3) immigration policy, (4) tailoring marketing to different countries, and (5) the casual generalizations about peoples you find in travel writing and everyday talk. Rank them from most to least defensible and explain what drives the ranking. In each case, where exactly does the inference break down — is it the tiny effect sizes, the ecological fallacy of applying group averages to individuals, the measurement problems, or something else entirely?

Two more things. First: is there any domain where acting on differences this small is not merely statistically dumb but straightforwardly unjust, and what marks that domain off from the rest? Second: does your ranking change if it turns out the measured differences mostly reflect reference-group effects (people rating themselves against their neighbors rather than against some absolute scale) rather than "real" trait differences?
