Something's been bugging me about how constitutional law actually works and I want someone to think it through with me.

Here's the setup. In a lot of countries there's a charter of basic rights, and courts can strike down laws that a democratically elected parliament passed, if the law violates one of those rights. The charters tend to say the rights belong to people just in virtue of being human -- the state doesn't grant them, it only recognizes them -- and some of them (the ban on torture, say) can't be suspended even in a national emergency. The whole point of the arrangement seems to be that rights can't be traded away just because doing so would make the majority better off: a law can serve the public interest beautifully and still get thrown out.

Now, Jeremy Bentham thought this whole picture was confused. His view, roughly, was that rights are made by law and nothing else: no legislator, no right, just people loudly wanting things and calling their wants rights. Talk of rights existing before or above the law struck him as empty -- his verdict on natural rights was that they're plain nonsense, famously nonsense hoisted up on stilts -- and worse than empty, since anyone can dress up any demand as an imprescriptible right and then refuse to bargain.

So: does the modern practice actually presuppose the thing Bentham said was incoherent? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. When a court strikes down a rights-violating law, what does it have to think rights *are* -- pre-legal moral facts that the charter merely describes, or just higher-ranking law that happens to be harder to amend? Try the Benthamite redescription on for size: maybe judicial review is just courts applying one bit of positive law (the charter) against another, no natural rights required. Does that redescription cover everything, or are there parts of the practice -- the "inherent to being human" language, the non-derogable rights, rulings that go beyond anything the charter's text clearly says -- that only make sense if rights exist independantly of enactment? Also, the charters themselves let most rights be limited when doing so is proportionate to some pressing social goal, which sounds awfully like Benthamite balancing -- so is the practice presupposing two inconsistent theories at once, one where rights are trumps and one where they're weights? Finally, if we rebuilt the system on Bentham's view from scratch, what would concretely have to change, and would anything we care about be lost?
