I co-run a monthly philosophy reading group at our public library -- mix of retirees, a couple of nurses, a guy doing an open university degree, nobody with any formal philosophy. We've been working through Mill's On Liberty and next session is the harm principle. Instead of building the evening around the famous "prevent harm to others" sentence from chapter one (they'll all have half-heard it already and will nod along without thinking), I want to anchor the whole session on this less-quoted bit from the start of chapter four:

"Each will receive its proper share, if each has that which more particularly concerns it. To individuality should belong the part of life in which it is chiefly the individual that is interested; to society, the part which chiefly interests society."

Here's what I need: don't explain the passage to me, plan how I should run the ninety minutes so that the passage does the work. The thing I care about is that little word "chiefly." My worry is that the group arrives expecting Mill to draw a clean line -- stuff that affects only you on this side, stuff that affects others on that side -- and if they meet this passage cold, "chiefly" reads like Mill fudging his own principle the moment it gets hard. I want the session ordered so that by the time we read the passage aloud, they've already felt the problem it's solving and "chiefly" lands as the honest move rather than the cop-out. So: what has to be in place before the passage can do that, and in what order (I'm assuming the basic principle first, but then what -- the difference between being harmed and being offended? the difference between law and social opinion as enforcement? something else?).

Also give me the one example to build the evening around. It needs knock-on effects on other people that are real and visible, but that shouldn't count as setting back their interests -- I'm thinking something like a neighbour whose drinking worries his family and makes him an unreliable friend, but feel free to propose better. Whatever it is, say what the example must have and must NOT have (e.g. if the guy drives drunk it collapses into the easy case and the session dies).

Then the misconceptions, in the order this group will actually produce them, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off rather than letting it take root. My guesses: "harm just means upsetting people," then the swing to "well everything affects someone eventually so Mill's principle protects nothing," then "Mill is saying nobody's allowed to even try to talk you out of your choices," and probably someone treating "interests" as "whatever I happen to want." Tell me where I'm wrong about the order or missing one.

Finally: one question to end on that would show whether they've got the passage or just agreed with it, and what I should explicitly leave on the table this session and say we're leaving (the utilitarian grounding? whether the principle admits exceptions? the chapter two speech material?). We're definately not doing readings lists, just the plan.
