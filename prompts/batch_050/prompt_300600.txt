In 1961, Elias Crowe, a night watchman in our county with no criminal record and no money for a real defense, was convicted of a payroll robbery on the strength of one eyewitness and a jailhouse informant. He maintained his innocence until his death in prison in 1974. In 2019, a law school's innocence clinic proved him innocent, and next month the county is unveiling a memorial stone for him in the courthouse square -- in front of the very building where he was convicted. I taught Elias in high school and have been asked to give the main address, about eight minutes.

The complication: the law students who won the exoneration, several of whom will be sitting in the front row with their professors, asked to open the ceremony by reading aloud a passage from Glaucon's challenge to Socrates in Book II of Plato's Republic (Jowett translation), because they say Elias's life is the experiment Glaucon describes. The passage is this:

"They say that to do injustice is, by nature, good; to suffer injustice, evil; but that the evil is greater than the good. And so when men have both done and suffered injustice and have had experience of both, not being able to avoid the one and obtain the other, they think that they had better agree among themselves to have neither; hence there arise laws and mutual covenants... it is a mean or compromise, between the best of all, which is to do injustice and not be punished, and the worst of all, which is to suffer injustice without the power of retaliation; and justice, being at a middle point between the two, is tolerated not as a good, but as the lesser evil, and honoured by reason of the inability of men to do injustice. For no man who is worthy to be called a man would ever submit to such an agreement if he were able to resist; he would be mad if he did." Later in the same speech, Glaucon imagines a man who could act with impunity and refuses, and says the onlookers would think him "a most wretched idiot, although they would praise him to one another's faces, and keep up appearances with one another from a fear that they too might suffer injustice."

This passage will hang over my speech whether I address it or not, and I want to address it. Glaucon's account says the law is a bargain each of us grudgingly accepts because we fear being defenseless victims, and Elias lived out precisely "the worst of all" -- he suffered injustice with no power of retaliation while the covenant that was supposed to protect him did the suffering to him. Worse, Glaucon's onlookers warn that a crowd's praise of a wronged innocent can be self-interested fear dressed up as reverence, which is a uncomfortably plausible description of a county honoring a man it killed.

Write the address so that the passage does real structural work: the speech must take seriously the possibility that the memorial is exactly the self-protective appearance-keeping Glaucon describes, and must say what -- if anything -- distinguishes honoring the dead from insuring ourselves. It must not claim that Elias is watching, at peace, or compensated; he died in prison, and the family in the second row knows it. It must not concede that his steadfastness was the idiocy Glaucon's onlookers would call it, but it must earn that refusal rather than assert it. And it must land for the mixed audience: the family and elderly neighbors who remember the trial, the courthouse staff who work in that building daily, and the lawyers and students who will catch every allusion -- without turning into a Plato lecture.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order; the quoted passage does not count toward any length:

1. "What the covenant cost him" -- the speech's opening, roughly 200 words.
2. "Glaucon's suspicion of this ceremony" -- the middle movement, roughly 250 words.
3. "What we owe a man we cannot repay" -- the close, roughly 250 words.
4. "Speaker's notes" -- roughly 150 words explaining the choices you made in handling the passage, the family, and the two audiences, and flagging anything you deliberately left unsaid.
