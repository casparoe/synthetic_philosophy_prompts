I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, currently drafting the second chapter of my dissertation, and my committee has asked me to include a short "position statement" laying out my commitments on the access/phenomenal consciousness debate before I start arguing from them. I've written the statement below, but I'm increasingly worried that I've assembled it piecemeal from different literatures over the years and that it may not survive scrutiny as a package. What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture: tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and for the genuine ones, what the least costly revision would be. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally urgent, and identify which single change would do the most to stabilize the whole set.

Here is the statement as it currently stands:

(a) Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness is genuine and not merely terminological: a state can be phenomenally conscious without being poised for use in reasoning, report, and rational control of action.

(b) Global workspace theory is the best scientific theory of consciousness currently on offer. Consciousness, at bottom, is what happens when a representation is broadcast widely enough to be available to the whole suite of consumer systems.

(c) Phenomenal consciousness is rich: our experience outruns what we access. I take the partial-report findings in the Sperling paradigm to show that subjects see more of the display than they can ever report, and I think the usual deflationary responses to this fail.

(d) Report, or at least availability for report, is our only non-question-begging evidence for attributing phenomenal consciousness to a system. I'm generally suspicious of alleged phenomenology that could not in principle leave any behavioral or report-based trace, because claims about it are untestable.

(e) I'm not any kind of dualist about the two kinds of consciousness: the same neural and functional machinery that realizes the workspace plausibly realizes phenomenal consciousness as well. Access and phenomenal consciousness are conceptually distinct but empirically entangled, and I don't think we should posit two separate substrates.

One more thing: I've deliberately written the statement without deciding in advance which commitment I'd abandon if push came to shove, because I want to see where the fault lines actually are rather than steer you toward a conclusion I'd find comfortable. If some of these turn out to be inconsistent only under a particular reading of one of them, say so explicitly and tell me which reading rescues the package and what it costs.
