I'm putting together a lecture for my constitutional theory seminar and I keep running into the same move, in the readings and from my colleagues: a constitution is like Ulysses tying himself to the mast -- the people, in a moment of clarity, bind themselves so they can't act on the sirens' song later (populist surges, wartime panic, inflationary temptation, whatever). It's almost always deployed to legitimize entrenched, hard-to-amend constraints on majority rule. Audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table first: who or what is Ulysses (the founding generation? "the people" as a continuing agent?), what are the ropes, what's the song, and who plays the crew with wax in their ears -- courts, maybe? Then the disanalogies. The ones that jump out at me: Ulysses bound himself, whereas constitutions mostly bind people who weren't at the founding; Ulysses *wanted to hear* the song and just not act on it, which isn't obviously what entrenchment does; and the whole precommitment story assumes the earlier self is the more rational one, which is contestable when it's 1789 talking to us. Tell me which of those breaks actually matter for the legitimizing use and which are just pedantic, whether the conclusion survives once they're on the table, and if there's a better analogy for constitutional precommitment (a pilot locking the cockpit? a treaty?) say what it buys and what it costs.
