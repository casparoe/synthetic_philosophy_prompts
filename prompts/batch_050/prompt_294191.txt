I'm a high school senior who has been self-studying epistemology and decision theory over the past year, mostly out of frustration with how the probability unit in my statistics class treats "learning" as always a matter of coming to know some fact for certain. Real life never works like that: the radiologist squinting at a blurry scan, the judge weighing a nervous witness, me trying to tell whether the sky at dusk is grey or faintly violet. After working through these examples, I've come to accept the position that Jeffrey conditionalization is not merely a fallback for edge cases but the correct general account of rational belief updating, with ordinary conditionalization on certainties as a special limiting case — one that, strictly speaking, never occurs. My reasoning is that a norm of belief revision that presupposes something agents never actually have (infallible observation) cannot be the fundamental norm, and that treating probability shifts on a partition as the basic unit of evidence both matches phenomenology and avoids the awkward question of how certainties are ever established in the first place.

What would count against this position? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be, from fatal down to merely awkward. I'm aware of some candidate pressure points — the fact that successive Jeffrey updates on different partitions need not commute, so that the order in which uncertain evidence arrives can change where you end up; the rigidity assumption, which seems to do real work but is hard to justify from inside the theory; and the possibility that any Jeffrey update can be re-described as ordinary conditionalization on a suitably refined space of possibilities, which might mean my position collapses into a notational variant of the orthodox view rather than a genuine rival to it. But I'm not sure how deep any of these cut, which of them a committed defender would concede as genuine costs versus dismiss as misunderstandings, and whether there are defeaters I haven't thought of at all.

I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. It seems to me that almost any observed pattern of belief change could, after the fact, be redescribed as a rational Jeffrey update given some cleverly chosen partition and some suitable shift in degrees of belief — and if that's right, does the view actually constrain anything, and does it matter for a normative (rather than descriptive) theory if it doesn't? What I would personally find most persuasive is a concrete sequential-update or decision scenario where faithfully following Jeffrey's rule produces a result that seems clearly irrational, and where the standard defensive maneuvers fail. If no such case exists, I want to know what kind of thing would have to be true for my position to be wrong at all — and if the honest answer is "very little," whether that should bother me.
