I am a novelist, some two years into a book whose central set piece is the deliberations of a parliamentary select committee in an invented country. The committee has been convened to consider a bill that would criminalise a private, consensual adult practice which the majority of the public finds repugnant but which causes no demonstrable harm to anyone outside its participants. I have six committee members to write, and I want each to embody a genuinely distinct position on whether the law may enforce morality as such — not six shades of vaguely liberal or vaguely conservative temperament. My problem is architectural: I need to know which questions actually prise the positions apart, so that I can place each character by their answers without ever letting them announce what they believe.

Please construct a decision tree that does this. Build it from the logical space rather than from a roll-call of names: first settle the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions within that space. The tree should begin from the opening sentence of Chapter I of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty:

"The subject of this Essay is not the so-called Liberty of the Will, so unfortunately opposed to the misnamed doctrine of Philosophical Necessity; but Civil, or Social Liberty: the nature and limits of the power which can be legitimately exercised by society over the individual."

The first branch must turn on this sentence. Mill frames the question as the power of society, not of the state, and I want the tree to exploit that: does a character read "the power which can be legitimately exercised by society over the individual" as covering the criminal enforcement of a community's shared morality as such — which is roughly the reading Devlin needs — or as already foreclosing it, as Hart would insist? Later branches should then separate the positions on grounds this sentence leaves open.

Requirements:

- No more than eight branch questions. Phrase each as a concrete case or claim a committee member could answer without knowing any of the labels — for instance, whether the fact that a practice would provoke public outrage if widely known counts in favour of prohibiting it, or whether a society that ceased to share a common morality would thereby be weakened in a way the law may guard against.
- The leaves must include, at minimum: Hart's position, Devlin's position, James Fitzjames Stephen's, the offense principle associated with Feinberg, legal moralism of the perfectionist kind (law as promoting genuine human good rather than appeasing majority sentiment), and straightforward legal paternalism. If one of these cannot be reached by your questions, say which and why.
- Note every unoccupied combination of answers and diagnose each briefly: is nobody there because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate — I would rather merge two characters than write two who turn out to hold the same view in different vocabulary.
- Finally, tell me in two or three sentences whether you think Mill's opening sentence, fairly read, already settles anything in this debate, or whether both Hart and Devlin can honestly claim it.

Apart from the quoted passage, which does not count against it, keep the whole instrument to under nine hundred words. End with a single final line that summarises, in one sentence, the deepest fault line your tree exposes.
