I chair my country's national bioethics council, and the Ministry of Health has formally asked us to advise on whether to retain our prohibition on compensated surrogacy and paid egg donation. The trigger is a draft bill that would replace the ban with a regulated market on broadly American lines: enforceable surrogacy contracts, payment to surrogates beyond documented expenses, and market-priced compensation for egg donors rather than the fixed, capped sum we currently permit on the fiction that it merely covers costs. Our existing position is the familiar European one — altruistic arrangements tolerated at most, contracts unenforceable, intermediaries criminalised. The council must issue its decision memorandum this quarter, and the ministry has signalled that it will treat our recommendation as the basis of its response to the bill.

This lands the week after a long and genuinely unresolved argument with an old friend. Her view was that the ban is paternalism dressed up as protection: women are permitted to undertake dangerous and intimate paid labour in other industries, the claim that paid surrogates are exploited is an empirical one the ban prevents us from ever testing properly, and prohibition's clearest actual effect is to export the practice — our citizens travel abroad, return with children whose legal parentage and citizenship are left in limbo, and our courts improvise a cleanup. My view was that a gestational market is not just another labour market, that payment under conditions of economic need corrodes the consent on which any defensible arrangement depends, and that some relationships are damaged by being priced even when both parties freely agree. We ended with what I think is a real crux neither of us could dissolve: whether the harms I fear are features of markets in reproduction as such, or only of badly regulated ones.

Draft the council's decision memorandum. It should recommend one of three options — retain the prohibition, permit altruistic arrangements only, or approve a regulated compensated market subject to specified conditions — and defend the recommendation in terms a legislator could act on, with a clear actionable bottom line. It should state the underlying philosophical issues precisely rather than by slogan (commodification, exploitation under economic pressure, the weight of reproductive autonomy, the standing of the resulting child's interests), and it must give the strongest consideration on the losing side in a form its advocates would recognise as their own. If you recommend approval with conditions, make the conditions concrete enough to appear in a schedule to the bill — covering, for instance, how genuine consent is to be secured and verified, what happens when an arrangement breaks down mid-pregnancy, and how parentage is established. Please write in formal British English.
