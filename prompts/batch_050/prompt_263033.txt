I coordinate staff support at a 700-person hospital, and leadership has bought a workshop series marketed as “modern Stoic resilience” for burnt-out employees. The trainers’ basic exercise is a control sort: identify what is up to the person right now, separate judgments and actions from outcomes, and make plans with a “reserve clause” rather than treating success as guaranteed. They insist this doesn’t mean passivity toward bad conditions. A charge nurse on our advisory group thinks that’s exactly how management will use it: unsafe staffing gets classified as outside your control, and workers are coached to accept it calmly. I can see the distinction she’s worried about, but our six staff advisors are not philosophers and currently make these calls based on intuition.

Write a reusable triage procedure the advisors can follow during a fifteen-minute first conversation, about thirty times a week. It should give the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, and how to classify cases as coaching-only, escalation-only, or mixed. The procedure needs to preserve the important parts of the dichotomy of control without collapsing influence into control, confusing acceptance of a fact with endorsement of an arrangement, or treating every external outcome as something nobody should try to change. Include clear stopping rules for safety risks, discrimination, legal issues, and mental-health concerns, plus what advisors should write in the confidential note and when my office director has to sign off. Then run the procedure on three cases: a nurse who says chronic understaffing is harming patients and is being told to “work on acceptance”; a surgeon whose grant was rejected and now intends never to apply again; and a scheduler who is obsessed with a rude colleague’s opinion of them but hasn’t reported specific misconduct. At least one application should produce an answer hospital leadership probably won’t like, and finish by saying what kinds of disputes the procedure cannot settle.
