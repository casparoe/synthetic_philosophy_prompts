I sit on the grants jury for a regional arts fund in the US, and three cycles ago we opened the "interactive media" category to video games, which sounded great at the time and has been a headache ever since. The problem is we know how to judge a film or a sculpture, but with games the jurors keep talking past each other. One of us says the art is in what's on the disc, so you judge it like any authored artifact. Another says no, the art is what the player does, so a game where the interesting stuff only emerges in play has to be judged on that, even if no two players see the same thing. And a third juror basically judges whether she had a meaningful time, which feels like judging a restaurant by one meal. Last cycle we fought for an hour over whether a speedrun of a game could itself be the artwork being submitted, and honestly we just guessed.

What I want is an actual procedure our jurors can follow, grounded in whatever aesthetics and philosophy of art have to say about agency, interactivity, and the ontology of works that only exist when someone operates them. Concretely: a set of questions or tests a juror runs in order when a submission lands on their desk, that tells them (a) what the candidate artwork even is in this case -- the code, a playthrough, the space of possible playthroughs, a specific performance by a player -- and (b) which of its features count as aesthetic properties of the work versus just facts about one person's session. Constraints are real: jurors get maybe 6-8 hours with each submission, most of them are not gamers, decisions happen quarterly, and each of us has to write a short justification that the fund director signs off on and that applicants can appeal. So the procedure has to produce defensible verdicts, not just vibes.

Show me the procedure working on three cases like ones we've actually had: a tightly scripted narrative game where the player's choices barely change anything, a systemic sandbox where the developer just built a physics and let go, and a submission that's a recording of one player's extraordinary playthrough of a competitive game. Include at least one case where the procedure spits out a verdict I'm not going to like, and say so. And mark the points where the procedure runs out and a juror just has to make a judgment call, because I'd rather know where those are than pretend they arn't there.
