Here's my position. In my paper I defend the following thesis: Descartes' cogito establishes, with certainty that even an all-powerful deceiver could not undermine, that a thinker exists whenever a thought is occurring. My reasoning is that doubting, being deceived, or even being mistaken about everything else all count as instances of thinking, so the very attempt to doubt that one is thinking performs the act it tries to doubt. I've even coined a term in my paper for this: I call a proposition "deceiver-proof" if performing any mental act aimed at rejecting it counts as confirming it. My thesis is that "thought is occurring now" is deceiver-proof in this sense, and that the cogito therefore delivers at least one genuine certainty.

Before I commit to this, I want to know what would count against it. Specifically:

1. What observations, arguments, or possible cases would have to hold for my thesis to be false? I'm aware of the famous objection that Descartes is only entitled to claim that thinking is happening, not that an "I" is doing it (the Lichtenberg-style worry). Does accepting that objection merely shrink the conclusion, or does it actually destroy the certainty I claim? I want to know whether my thesis survives in the weaker form "thought is occurring" or whether even that fails.

2. There seem to be at least three distinct things the cogito might be taken to prove: (a) that a thought is occurring at this moment, (b) that there is a single subject having that thought, and (c) that this subject persists through time. My paper claims only (a), and concedes (c) immediately, since any belief that I existed five minutes ago relies on memory, which a deceiver could fake. But is even (b) — the existence of a unified subject at a single instant — an extra commitment beyond (a)? If so, where exactly does the step from (a) to (b) introduce something doubtable?

3. Is my "deceiver-proofness" test itself vulnerable? Could a proposition pass the test — any attempt to doubt it performs it — and still be false? For instance, does the test conflate the impossibility of doubting something with its truth? I want to know whether the cogito's force is epistemic (I can't doubt it) or metaphysical (it must be true), and whether my paper needs to distinguish these.

4. What would a committed defender of the cogito concede as genuine defeaters, versus what would they dismiss as missing the point? Please sort the possible defeaters from fatal, to seriously damaging, to merely awkward.

5. Finally, has the strong version of the thesis — that the cogito gives us an indubitable foundation — been qualified into something so minimal that it's unfalsifiable and therefore empty? If the cogito only ever proves that this very episode of thinking exists right now, does that conclusion do any philosophical work at all, or is it a certainty that buys you nothing? I'd like an honest verdict on whether my thesis risks anything, and whether it matters if it doesn't.
