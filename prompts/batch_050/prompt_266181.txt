So I was at a pub quiz afterparty with some mates and a friend of mine who's doing a physics PhD -- argument about whether an AI could ever in principle do everything human mathematicians do. Someone shut the whole thing down with 'well, Gödel proved there are true things that can't be proved, so machines can never do what minds do'. Everyone nodded like that was checkmate, and it's been bugging me ever since because I'm fairly sure that's not what Gödel showed at all, but I couldn't articulate the difference on the spot.

What I want is the slogan taken apart properly. Lay out the different precise claims that 'there are true things that can't be proved' could be making, from the boring to the wild. I figure there's at least: the actual theorem-level claim (something like: any consistent, effectively axiomatisable system rich enough for arithmetic contains a sentence that is true in the standard model but unprovable in that system), then progressively looser readings -- there are truths no formal system can prove, there are truths humans can grasp that no machine can (this seems to be the Lucas/Penrose route my friend was taking), some vague claim about rationality outrunning rules, and the fully mystical one where it becomes a point about the limits of knowledge in general, or even a free pass for religious belief. For each reading, tell me whether it's a theorem, a defensible philosophical thesis, a contested interpretation, or just wrong, and what extra premises it needs to get off the ground.

Crucially: work backwards from the rhetorical use. Which of these readings, if any, would actually support the move my friend made -- from incompleteness to 'no machine can replicate mathematical minds'? I want to know what's settled mathematics, what's a live but minority philosophical position, and what's a popular myth that wouldn't survive a philosophy of maths seminar. Please don't blur those three categories or present the contested stuff as established fact just because it's famous. And if the Lucas-style argument depends on a step that most specialists reject, say so plainly and say which step, rather than doing the both-sides thing.

One more thing: the slogan trades on 'can't be proved' as if proof were one thing. Spell out how much of the confusion comes from sliding between provable-in-this-system, provable-in-any-system-we'd-accept, and provable-at-all, because I suspect that slide is doing all the work in the pub version.
