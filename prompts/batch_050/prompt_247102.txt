I spent my career as an experimental chemist and have turned to philosophy in retirement, and I keep running into a configuration I lack a word for, so I am writing to ask whether it has one. The pattern is this: someone advances a thesis which, if true, severs the connection between its own subject matter and the very utterance or judgment by which it is asserted. Three instances from my reading. A philosopher argues that conscious experience causes nothing in the physical world — yet he presumably came to write down that conclusion because of what his experiences were like. An acquaintance argues that introspection is thoroughly unreliable, and supports the claim with evidence about what careful attention to his own mind reveals. A third writer argues that our moral convictions are products of evolutionary pressures aimed at survival rather than truth, and offers this as a conviction of his own, arrived at by those same pressures. In each case the conclusion is not strictly self-contradictory: the world could be exactly as the thesis says, utterance and all. Nor is it mere hypocrisy, since the speaker may be perfectly sincere. The trouble seems to be that the thesis, if believed for the reasons the speaker believes it, leaves the speaker's own judgment unexplained or accidentally placed — as if a man were to saw through the branch and then remark, from mid-air, that the view is excellent. Does this configuration have an established name? How does it differ from ordinary self-refutation, from performative contradiction, and from simply having an unreliable belief? And is the defect a logical one that refutes the thesis, or merely an epistemic embarrassment for the person asserting it — with what consequences for cases where only some of a theory's defenders are caught by it?
