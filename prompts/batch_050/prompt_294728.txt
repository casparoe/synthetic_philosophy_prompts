My friend Dana and I have been having this running argument about what money shouldn't buy since our town flooded last spring and the hardware store guy tripled the price of generators overnight. We're both in a reading group doing that Sandel book, and I've been keeping a list of her verdicts because she swears she's being consistent and I think she's not. So far: the generator thing, wrong, obviously — her words were "he's not charging for generators, he's charging for being flooded." A government-run scheme where the state pays kidney donors a fixed fee and allocates the organs by medical need — she says that's fine, actually good. Selling your vote — absolutely not, even if both parties walk away happy, because "guess who'd be selling and who'd be buying." Paying a line-stander to wait overnight for concert tickets — fine, mildly gross but fine. And airlines bidding up compensation for volunteers on overbooked flights instead of bumping people — totally fine, better than the alternative.

I told her she's basically running Aquinas's rule from the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q.77, a.1), which I found while trying to prove her wrong. He says: "if the one man derive a great advantage by becoming possessed of the other man's property, and the seller be not at a loss through being without that thing, the latter ought not to raise the price, because the advantage accruing to the buyer, is not due to the seller, but to a circumstance affecting the buyer. Now no man should sell what is not his, though he may charge for the loss he suffers." So my read is: you're allowed to charge for your costs, not for the other guy's need, because his need was never yours to sell. That explains the generators and the kidneys and the flights, I think. But Dana insists her rule is about which goods shouldn't track wealth at all, not about where the seller's profit comes from — and honestly her vote verdict does seem to need something more than Aquinas, since nobody's desperate there, it's just that elections aren't supposed to be bought. So maybe there's a second rule doing the work, or maybe Aquinas's line isn't even the rule behind the price cases and I read it too fast.

Anyway here's why I suddenly care: her old state university just floated a pilot where, after filling most of the class the normal way, they'd auction off the last few percent of seats among applicants who clear the academic bar, with the proceeds going to financial aid. We're going to litigate this at the next meeting and I want to call her verdict in advance. So: what does Dana say about the seat auction? Which of the earlier cases is the closest analog — the overbooked flight, since it's monetized access to a scarce thing, or the vote, since it's a good some people think shouldn't track money? If her track record genuinely underdetermines it, tell me what principle she'd be adopting with each answer, and give me the further hypothetical that would actually settle which rule she's running. Also, is the Aquinas passage even doing what I think it's doing, or have I stretched "selling what is not his" past breaking point?
