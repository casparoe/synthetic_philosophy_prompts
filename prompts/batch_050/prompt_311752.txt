After my father's long illness a few years ago, I began writing down instructions for my own care in case I am ever unable to speak for myself, and the exercise has left me stuck on a question I cannot find discussed anywhere in plain language, though I find it hard to believe nobody has thought about it. What I keep noticing is that my judgment about whether a person's earlier instructions should bind their later self seems to depend entirely on how the change of mind came about, and not on anything about the earlier and later states themselves.

Three examples to show what I mean. First, the usual case: a woman at forty writes that if she ever develops dementia and no longer recognizes her family, she does not want her life prolonged; at seventy she has dementia, appears cheerful, and in her own way asks to keep living. I have found some discussion of this case, but it always turns on whether she is still the same person, or whether her younger self could really know what that life would feel like from the inside. That is not my question. Second, a man at thirty, a devoted climber, makes his wife promise that if he ever becomes someone who wants to quit out of fear, she should push him to continue; at fifty-five, after watching two friends die over the years, he has changed gradually and sincerely, with no illness and nobody pressuring him, and almost nobody, I think, would enforce the promise. Third, suppose that same man reaches that same cautious state of mind not through years of experience but through a head injury, or through a manipulative group that worked on him over a winter. Then, I think, nearly everyone would side with the earlier self and try to undo the change.

So here is the pattern: the earlier wish and the later wish can be held fixed, and my verdict flips with the path from one to the other. Is there an established name for this distinction, between changes of mind that unfold within the ordinary course of a life and changes that are, so to speak, done to a person, and does any serious line of thinking hold that the causal history of a preference, rather than its content or its sincerity, settles how much authority the earlier self keeps? I can see the edges are murky: a brain tumor seems clearly external, but what about falling in love, a religious conversion, having a child, emigrating? These are not illnesses, yet they can change a person past recognition, sometimes without their ever having chosen it.

A bare name would not settle anything for me. I want to know which existing ideas come closest, how the pattern I have described differs from each of them, where the distinction breaks down, and whether it can bear the moral weight my three verdicts assume, or whether those verdicts cannot in fact be jointly defended. Please answer in ordinary continuous prose, as one would in a letter: no headings, no lists, no bold text, and no tables.
