As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past two years catching up on metaphysics, I keep returning to what I gather is called the special composition question: under what conditions do several objects together compose a single further object? My laboratory instincts rebel at both extremes I have encountered — that any scattered plurality whatsoever automatically forms a thing, and that nothing but simples exists — yet every intermediate answer I have found seems to draw its line somewhere suspiciously arbitrary. I am organizing a reading group for fellow retired scientists, none of whom know the standard terminology, and I want to sort them by what they actually believe about cases rather than by which label sounds respectable to them.

Please construct a decision tree that begins with the composition question itself and leads to named positions: unrestricted composition (any plurality composes something), compositional nihilism (nothing composes anything), the organicist restriction (only living activity unifies parts), a contact- or fastening-based restriction, and brutal composition (the view that composition just happens in some cases with no further principle). Every branching node must be a question about a concrete case that a physicist can answer without knowing where it leads — for instance, whether two billiard balls resting against each other form one object, whether a dead dog differs from the living dog of a moment before in being composed at all, whether a swarm of midges, a galaxy, or a disassembled ship with its planks stacked in a warehouse each counts as a single thing. At each leaf, name the position and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment. Flag explicitly any pair of positions your tree cannot separate with questions of this kind, and note any combination of answers that no one seems to defend, saying briefly whether the gap looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. One additional wrinkle: include somewhere in the tree a question about whether it can be genuinely indeterminate whether composition has occurred, since I suspect at least two of my colleagues secretly hold this and I want the tree to catch them.

Format: the tree itself as an indented outline with each node phrased as a question, followed by the flagged-separations and unoccupied-combinations notes. No more than 1,000 words in total, and no references to particular philosophers or texts — the group will not have read them.
