Ok so I teach a second-year epistemology course, about 18 students, and I have one 75-minute session free next month. I want to run an in-class game or simulation that makes students feel the difference between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber -- the distinction between just not hearing the other side, versus having been trained (by your own information environment, and by people you trust) to actively distrust the other side when you do hear them. On paper students nod along and treat these as basically the same thing, and I want them to run into the difference with their own decisions before I ever name it.

Design me something runnable. The mechanics matter more than the theme, but it needs to be playable with no tech beyond paper, cards, and maybe a whiteboard, and it should work even if two or three students don't show and the group is lopsided. The key thing I'm after: there should be a phase where some players are simply cut off from information (the bubble part), and then a later phase where those same players recieve the missing information but from a source the game has given them good reason to discount -- and the point is they should hesitate or refuse in a way they couldn't have predicted about themselves at the start. If your design can arrange it so that a player who is actually right gets discredited and frozen out, even better, because that's the moment I want to talk about afterwards. Whatever it is, it should involve real choices with small stakes (points, candy, whatever -- suggest something) rather than hypothetical talk, and the students should not be told what the game is "about" beforehand.

Give me the full rules, setup, and turn structure, a rough timeline that fits 75 minutes including the debrief, and then the debrief itself: which moments in the game map onto which parts of the bubble/echo chamber distinction, in what order to ask about them, and how to handle the student who says the distrust was just rational given what happened in the game (someone always says this and honestly they have a point, so tell me what to do with it rather than how to shut it down). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be honest about which feature of real echo chambers a 60-minute game can't reproduce -- I suspect it's the part where distrust gets built up over months of social belonging rather than 20 minutes of points, but tell me what you think the irreplaceable ingredient is.

One more thing on format: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no tables. Just continuous prose, like you're writing me a long email. I know that's annoying for a rules-heavy thing but I want to be able to read it aloud and adapt it, so compress the rules into flowing description rather than a spec sheet.
