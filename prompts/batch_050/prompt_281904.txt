I am a novelist at work on a book whose two central figures are a Rotterdam harbor engineer and a Kyoto tea-ceremony instructor, and I have been reading about cross-cultural pragmatics so that their scenes together ring true rather than trade in caricature. A well-regarded popular book on the subject — I would rather not name it, as I want the account judged on its merits — summarizes the relevant theory as follows:

"According to Grice's cooperative principle, conversation works because speakers obey four maxims: be truthful, be informative, be relevant, and be clear. A white lie breaks the first of these, so on Grice's account every white lie is a small communicative failure — the exchange has, strictly speaking, misfired. Brown and Levinson showed that politeness is really the management of 'face': indirectness is the polite strategy for delivering unwelcome messages, because it softens the threat to the hearer's dignity. Cultures differ in how much indirectness they demand. The Dutch sit near the direct end and the Japanese near the indirect end, which is why a Japanese refusal is so often phrased as an acceptance deferred. It follows that indirect cultures run on polite fictions — that Japanese politeness is institutionalized insincerity in a way Dutch bluntness is not — and that the direct style is, morally speaking, the more honest one."

Much of this matches what I half-remembered from other reading, but the moral conclusion at the end struck me as a non sequitur dressed up as a corollary, and I cannot tell whether my unease is justified or is merely the defensiveness of a writer fond of his Japanese character. Could you audit the passage for me? Sort it into what is sound, what is simplified but defensible, and what is actually mistaken. In particular: are Grice's maxims best understood as rules speakers obey, and is the book right that a white lie counts on his framework as a communicative failure? Is there a distinction — between flouting a maxim and violating one, say — that the summary is quietly depending on while ignoring? And does indirectness of the Japanese sort involve any intent to deceive at all, or has the book run together two phenomena that pragmatics keeps apart? I would also like to know whether anything in Brown and Levinson's framework, as summarized, licenses the step from "indirectness is a politeness strategy" to "indirect cultures are less honest," or whether that step is the author's own. Finally, once the passage is corrected, what survives of the claim that bluntness is morally superior? Is there a serious philosophical case that white lies are wrong, and if so, would it even bear on a contrast between Dutch and Japanese communicative norms, or only on choices that individuals make within either culture? I ask partly as a citizen and partly as a craftsman: if the correction has consequences for how honest dialogue can be rendered on the page, I would like to know them.
