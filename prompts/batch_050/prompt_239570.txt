Over the past year I've had the same slogan thrown at me in two very different rooms, and I want to understand what I was actually being told.

At a meditation retreat, the teacher repeatedly said "the self is an illusion," usually to console people: there is nothing that could be annihilated at death, so the fear of death rests on a mistake. A month later, in a cognitive science seminar, the same slogan came up in defense of a different conclusion: since the self is an illusion, the feeling that "I" authored my decisions is post-hoc, and so our practices of blame and desert are built on a fiction. Same five words, doing very different argumentative work.

What I'd like is a careful disambiguation of the slogan, laid out as a menu rather than an argument for one reading. Please:

1. Enumerate the distinct precise claims "the self is an illusion" could be making. I'd like the readings organized by what, exactly, is being denied: an unchanging essence underlying the stream of experience; a single unified subject at a time (rather than a loose confederation of processes); a persisting bearer that makes the person at the reunion the same person as the child in the photographs; an author of decisions located where introspection puts one; or a well-defined narrative character answering to one's name. If you think a reading belongs on the menu that doesn't fit this classification, add it.

2. For each reading, give a verdict: trivially true (no sensible view ever denied it), substantive and defensible, substantive and probably false, or confused. Briefly note which intellectual traditions would endorse or contest it, without needing citations.

3. Distinguish carefully between the "illusion" and "nonexistence" versions of each reading. An illusion, in the ordinary sense, is a misrepresentation of something real -- the bent stick in water exists even if it isn't bent. On which readings of the slogan is something genuinely there but misdescribed by experience, and on which is nothing there at all? And address the obvious regress: an illusion seems to require someone being misled -- who is misled on the strongest no-self readings, and is this a real problem or a verbal one?

4. Finally, map the menu onto the two uses I described. Which readings would actually support the retreat teacher's consolation about death, and which would support the seminar's skepticism about blame and desert? If the readings needed for the two conclusions differ -- or if one conclusion requires a reading you judged defensible and the other requires one you judged false -- that's the part I most want spelled out.

Please write this up as something I could bring to office hours: a structured list with short explanations, not an essay defending your preferred view.
