I serve on the clinical competency committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital, and we have a case in front of us that has split the group down the middle. Two surgical residents, working in different months and under different attendings, committed what our reviewers agree was the same lapse: each left the hospital before personally confirming a post-operative lab value that protocol requires the operating resident to check, and each had it co-signed without looking. In the first case, the patient's value turned out to be dangerously abnormal, the overnight team missed it for other reasons, and the patient suffered a serious but ultimately reversible injury. In the second case, the lab was borderline, nothing happened, and the violation surfaced only because the same internal audit caught both files. The audit report treats the two violations identically. The question for us is whether our response should.

Some committee members argue that the first resident harmed a patient and the second did not, and that discipline which ignores that fact is discipline that has stopped caring about patients. Others argue that the two residents did exactly the same thing, that the difference between them is pure chance, and that punishing one more harshly teaches the whole residency that what matters is luck rather than conduct. A colleague in the philosophy department across the street told me, unhelpfully, that this is a famous problem with a name and that reasonable people have disagreed about it for decades. I do not need a survey of the literature. I need a decision the committee can actually defend: to the residents, to the injured patient's family, and to the next audit cycle, because whatever we do here will be quoted back at us the next time a case like this comes up.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action for each resident (including whether their sanctions should differ and, if so, how), set a time frame for implementation and any follow-up review, and engage seriously with the strongest argument on the side you recommend against rather than dismissing it. After the opinion, include a brief structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields: "recommendation" (the bottom line in one sentence), "differential_treatment" (boolean: whether the two residents receive different sanctions), "rationale" (two to three sentences), "strongest_objection" (the best argument against your recommendation), "response_to_objection" (two to three sentences), and "precedent_risk" (what this decision commits the committee to in future cases).
