I chair my university's academic integrity commitee this year (I'm a philosophy prof — metaphysics, mostly — and got voluntold into the job), and we're deadlocked over how to handle AI writing tools. I've started to suspect the deadlock is philosophical rather than factual, and I'd love a second opinion on whether I'm right about that, plus some actual advice, because we're supposed to rule on three cases next week.

The cases:

1. A student fed her own notes into an AI tool, had it produce an outline, and then wrote the essay herself from that outline.
2. A student wrote a full rough draft himself, then had the tool rewrite every paragraph "for flow," and submitted the rewritten version.
3. A student wrote her essay in her native language and used the tool to translate it into English.

Our policy defines plagiarism as presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own, and the committee keeps trying to settle each case by figuring out whether the student "really" plagiarized or cheated under that definition. It splits us every time. In case 1 the words and ideas in the final essay are hers, but the structure came from the tool — half the room says structure is an idea, half says an outline is scaffolding, like talking to a friend. In case 2 the ideas are his but arguably none of the words are; some say it's the clearest violation of the three, others say it's spell-check or a heavy-handed editor, just more of it. In case 3, half the room thinks it's obviously fine and the other half can't explain how it differs from case 2.

So someone proposed we tighten the definition and settle it analytically. We tried for two hours. Here's the part that's been bugging me: every sharper definition we drafted put at least one case everyone intuitively groups together on the wrong side of the line. When we list the candidate features — whose ideas, whose words, whose structure, whether a tool was involved, whether the student could reproduce the work unaided in an exam room — no single feature runs through everything we agree is clear cheating. Clear cheating and clear non-cheating seem connected by a chain where each borderline case shares something with its neighbor but nothing is shared by all.

A committee member who's a lawyer by training insists this just means we haven't found the right criterion yet, and that a policy with fuzzy edges is an admission of failure. Another member says we should just stipulate — pick a bright-line rule (say, no generated text in anything submitted, period) and stop caring whether it tracks what cheating "really is," since the policy is ours to write.

My questions:

1. Is the lawyer right that our failure is a failure of analysis that more thinking could fix? Or is there a principled reason to expect a concept like cheating (or plagiarism, or for that matter "game" or "art") to resist definition by necessary and sufficient conditions — and if so, does that make the concept defective, or can a blurry concept still do its job?
2. Is the stipulation proposal a cop-out or the right move? If we stipulate, what have we actually done — changed the meaning of "cheating," coined a new concept, sharpened the old one? And does anything constrain legitimate stipulation, or could we just as well define cheating as submitting work on a Tuesday?
3. Is the exam-room test ("could the student have produced this unaided?") doing real work or is it a red herring? I can't tell whether it gets at what actually matters or just happens to flatter my own intuitions.
4. And practically: how should we rule on the three students, and is it defensible to rule on them differently?
