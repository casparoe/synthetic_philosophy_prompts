For years I have been informally collecting my own answers to what van Inwagen calls the special composition question -- under what conditions do some things add up to a further thing? -- and I would now like to know whether my answers embody any coherent principle, and what adhering to such a principle would cost me.

Here are my verdicts, as I find them:

1. My kitchen table consists of planks screwed together years ago. The planks compose a further object, the table. Yes to composition.
2. The heap of gravel at the end of my driveway is just stones piled up. There is no further object, the heap -- there are only the stones, arranged heapwise. No.
3. Two paperbacks leaning against each other on the shelf, their covers in full contact: no further object. Contact alone composes nothing.
4. My wristwatch is currently disassembled for cleaning, its parts spread on a tray. The watch still exists. Scattering the parts has not destroyed it.
5. A mail-order kit containing exactly the same parts, never yet assembled, sits on a tray at the factory. There is as yet no watch there.
6. The cat asleep on the chair is, beyond any doubt in my mind, one object and not merely many cells arranged catwise. This is the verdict I hold most firmly.
7. The jigsaw puzzle on the dining table is complete except for one piece lying beside it. The puzzle already exists, missing a piece -- I do not think that slotting in the last piece creates anything, only finishes it.
8. My wedding ring and the Rosetta Stone: there is no object they jointly compose.
9. The swarm of bees moving across the field as one dark mass: I genuinely cannot decide.
10. Conjoined twins who share a liver: one organism or two? I cannot decide this either, and I notice my reluctance to answer feels different from case 9.

Where I feel the strain. Verdicts 1 and 2 seem to turn on fastening versus mere piling, but verdict 4 breaks any fastening principle, since the watch's parts are not fastened to anything at all. Verdicts 4 and 5 force the difference into the past -- the parts on the two trays are arranged identically, so if one tray holds a watch and the other does not, composition must be partly a matter of history. But that strikes me as strange: whether there is something on this tray now should depend on how things are now, not on where the parts have been. Meanwhile, verdict 6 tempts me toward the answer that composition happens exactly when the goings-on among some things constitute a life -- but that answer appears to sacrifice verdicts 1, 4, and 7, and possibly to force an answer on 10 that I may not want.

What I am asking for. First, one or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them mechanically to new cases, with an explicit note of which verdict motivates each clause. Second, for each candidate, an honest ledger: which of my verdicts it preserves, which it overturns, and -- if it cannot save them all -- which single verdict is cheapest to abandon and why. Third, its rulings on cases 9 and 10, and on at least two cases I have not listed (for instance, a campfire, or the Mississippi River). Fourth, a verdict on my guiding hunch: that the correct principle should be non-disjunctive and present-tense -- a single condition on how things now stand, rather than a list of composition-permitting relations or a fact partly about the past. Is that hunch worth keeping, or is my case list already proof that it cannot be satisfied?
