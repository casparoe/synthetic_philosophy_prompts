I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on a dissertation chapter about Dennett-style skepticism toward qualia, and I keep circling back to the Chase and Sanborn passage in "Quining Qualia," where the two coffee tasters each report that the coffee now tastes worse to them, one attributing the change to the coffee's flavor itself and the other to a shift in his own standards and reactions, with Dennett concluding that there is no further fact of the matter about who is right because first-person judgment is the only instrument we have and it cannot arbitrate the dispute. I have the strong intuition that this argument equivocates, and I have been testing that intuition with a variant I devised: imagine a third taster, Eleanor, who is fitted with a perfect memory aid that lets her replay, with complete fidelity, her total reactive disposition profile from five years ago, and who, when she drinks the coffee now, responds in every behavioral, classificatory, and hedonic respect exactly as her younger self did when the coffee tasted good to her, yet insists that something has nonetheless shifted. My question is whether Dennett's argument can still be run against Eleanor, or whether it depends essentially on the epistemic messiness of ordinary memory and changing tastes, in which case it shows at most that we cannot *know* when qualia change, not that there is nothing for such a change to consist in.

Here is what I would like from you. Rather than have my own hunch confirmed, I want the strongest case I can get on the other side: please argue that Dennett's quining strategy succeeds even against the Eleanor variant, that is, that even under idealized conditions of perfect mnemonic and dispositional control, the distinction between a change in the quale and a change in one's reactions to it remains without content, so that Eleanor's conviction of a residual difference is either incoherent or reduces to something fully capturable in functional and dispositional terms. Steelman the position; do not rest the case on the mere claim that we lack third-person access, since that is exactly what I am trying to get past. If you think the argument succeeds only for one of the two original tasters but not the other, or succeeds against Eleanor only on a reading of qualia that a realist need not accept, say so and defend that restriction as the strongest available version of the view.

Two further requests. First, please end by stating explicitly what would change your mind: which consideration, thought experiment, or distinction, if it could be made good, would force you to conclude that the Eleanor case does reveal a coherent residue that quining cannot dissolve, and where in your own argument you judge the reasoning most likely to break down under pressure. Second, please write the whole reply as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no numbered lists, just connected paragraphs.
