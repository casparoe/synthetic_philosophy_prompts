So I run this little meetup at my company, we call it "stupidly big questions night" — about 10-12 devs and a couple of designers, we meet in a conference room for 90 minutes once a month. No philosophy background among us, including me, but people are sharp and they love systems-y stuff.

Next session I want to do the cosmological argument, specifically the contingency flavor — the "why is there anything at all rather than nothing" line of thinking. The hook I want to build around is a passage from Leibniz's essay "On the Ultimate Origination of Things" (1697). Paraphrasing since I don't have the text in front of me: he asks you to imagine a geometry textbook that has existed forever, where every copy was transcribed from an earlier copy, forever back. His point is that even though each copy is fully explained by the one before it, the whole infinite chain still isn't explained — why geometry books at all, why this content, why any of it? You never escape the need for an explanation outside the series, no matter how far back you go.

Design me an activity where the group *feels* that. Concretely:

- No lecture up front, and nobody should hear "cosmological argument" or even "Leibniz" until the debrief. I want them to build the regress themselves, the way the geometry book example does — every thing explained by a prior thing, and then the dawning realization that the whole setup is still unexplained.
- Ideally it exploits that we're engineers. I was vaguely thinking something like: they spec out a simulated world where literally every event inside has a complete in-world causal explanation (initial state + rules), and then the floor gets opened to "ok so what explains the initial state and the rules" — but you probably have better mechanics than that, so go with whatever works. There should be a genuine moment where the infinite-regress move occurs to *them* as a fix, and then they discover for themselves why it doesn't feel like one.
- Then a debrief that maps moments from the activity onto the actual argument: the principle that everything contingent has an explanation, the regress step, the "explain the totality, not each part" step, and the conclusion that something non-contingent has to be doing the explaining. For each step show them where it can be resisted and what it costs to resist it there (brute facts, denying the principle for the whole while keeping it for parts, questioning whether "the totality" is even a legit object of explanation, etc.).
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this (e.g., someone immediately yelling "God of the gaps!", someone conflating this with the kalam first-cause version which I know is a different argument, the activity collapsing into a debate about the Big Bang) and how to handle each.
- Be honest about which feature of the argument the activity *can't* reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud — I suspect the intuitive pull of "there must be a reason" is easy to stage but the modal logic of contingency/necessity isn't.

One more thing: since none of us can check you, please be upfront about what's textbook-settled here versus what's genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this — like, don't present the principle of sufficient reason or the success of the argument as the consensus view if it isn't, and flag where the experts themselves split. Also flag anywhere your activity design leans on a contested reading of Leibniz rather than the standard one.
