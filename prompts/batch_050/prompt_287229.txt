I run a local philosophy meetup that meets in the back room of a pub, and in a few weeks it's my turn to give the talk. My thesis, which I've sort of arrived at on my own after watching a lot of physics YouTube and reading pop science books, is that the direction of time isn't a basic feature of the universe — it's just entropy. There's no real "flow" of time, we just happen to live next to a very orderly past and a disorderly future, and everything we call the arrow of time (remembering the past, eggs breaking but never unbreaking, causes coming before effects) is downstream of that one fact. Basically my line is: stop asking what pushes time forward and start asking why the Big Bang was so tidy.

The audience is not physicists, which cuts both ways. It's roughly: a retired mechanical engineer who did thermodynamics professionally and will know the subject better than me, a bloke who's very into quantum mechanics and thinks consciousness collapses wavefunctions, a woman who's read a lot about eternal recurrence and Nietzsche and will want to connect everything to that, and then half a dozen regulars who are smart but will mostly follow along and chip in with things they've half-remembered from documentaries. Twenty minutes of me talking, then an hour of questions.

Prepare me for that hour. I want the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come up, phrased the way these specific people would put them (the engineer won't say "the Past Hypothesis", he'll say "hang on, entropy of what exactly, because the universe is expanding and my old textbook says equilibrium is maximum entropy so how was a hot dense soup low entropy?"). For each one tell me what I should just concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is on shaky ground and I should keep it short rather than bluff.

I suspect the things that'll come up are: the laws of physics being reversible so how does entropy even get a direction, why was the early universe low entropy in the first place (I know "it just was, that's a boundary condition" sounds like cheating), whether my view means the future is somehow less real, and something about time's arrow in small systems. There'll be others I've not thought of. Also tell me which objection is the one that could genuinely sink the talk — the one where if the engineer lands it and I fumble, I look like I don't understand my own thesis — and give me the two-minute version of the answer I need to have rehearsed for it.
