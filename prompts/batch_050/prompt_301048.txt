In an essay I am editing, the author leans heavily on the claim that social media is a digital Panopticon. The argument runs as follows: Bentham's design placed inmates in cells permanently visible to a central tower whose occupant they could never see, so that each prisoner, never knowing whether he was being watched, came to watch himself; Foucault took this as the emblem of a form of power that needs no violence because its targets internalize the gaze and discipline themselves. Social media, the author says, reproduces this arrangement almost exactly: users are perpetually on display, can never know who is looking at any given moment, and so curate their own conduct in advance, policing their speech and self-presentation without anyone having to order them to. The conclusion drawn is that contemporary surveillance is disciplinary power in Foucault's strict sense, that users are its docile and normalized subjects, and that individual efforts to resist (curating one's privacy settings, posting less, quitting a platform) are therefore beside the point, since the disciplining has already been internalized.

I suspect the analogy is doing more work here than it can bear, and I would like an audit of it. Set out the correspondence explicitly in prose: what in the social media case plays the role of the tower, the guard, the cells, the inmate's uncertainty about being observed, the inspection, and the internalized gaze. Then identify where the mapping breaks. Some candidates that occur to me, which you should assess rather than assume: in the Panopticon the many are visible to the few, whereas on social media visibility is largely reciprocal and the many watch the many; the inmate's visibility is imposed and unwanted, whereas much online visibility is actively sought and even paid for; the Panopticon's watcher is a single institution with reformative aims, whereas the relevant observers online are a shifting mixture of peers, employers, advertisers, and ranking systems with no unified purpose; and the prisoner internalizes a norm he would be punished for violating, whereas users respond to quantified feedback (counts of likes and followers) that rewards rather than merely punishes. For each disanalogy, say whether it is fatal to the author's specific conclusion or merely a superficial mismatch, since not every disanalogy matters equally. In particular, consider whether the analogy quietly smuggles in the older, repressive model of power that Foucault was explicitly trying to move beyond, and whether Foucault's own emphasis that power is productive -- that it makes up kinds of subject rather than merely suppressing a pre-existing self -- actually fits the social media case better than the author's use of the Panopticon image does. Finally, give a verdict: does the conclusion that users are self-disciplining subjects whose internalization makes individual resistance futile survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the Panopticon is the wrong emblem, is there a better one from within or near Foucault's own repertoire (the examination, the confessional, the norm rather than the tower) that would carry the author's argument more honestly?

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would in a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, even for the correspondences, which should be worked through in sentences.
