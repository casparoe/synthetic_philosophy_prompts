My philosophy club is cataloguing objections to Hume's argument against believing testimony of miracles (Section X of the Enquiry), and we've hit a wall. The objections we already have, so please don't repeat or repackage them: (1) the argument proves too much, since Hume's maxim would apparently have required rejecting well-attested but unprecedented phenomena like meteorites; (2) defining a miracle as a violation of a law of nature risks circularity, because we can only know the laws are exceptionless if we already discount the testimony in question; (3) the Bayesian objection that a low prior probability can in principle be overcome by sufficiently strong evidence, so the maxim doesn't settle anything by itself; (4) the point that genuinely independent witnesses can jointly make deception or error astronomically improbable, which Hume's treatment of testimony underestimates; and (5) the complaint that Part II's sociological claims — that miracle reports flourish mainly among uneducated peoples — fail to address the strongest reported cases. What I need is one further objection that is not a variant or combination of these, aimed at a different part of Hume's reasoning. Tell me which step of the argument it targets, and be honest about whether it's actually a good objection or just a different one. Please write your answer in American English.
