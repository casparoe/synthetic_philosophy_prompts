I am a senior software engineer, with no formal philosophy background, serving on my company’s AI Release Review Board. We must decide whether to approve Ledger-9, a model that proposes and executes production-infrastructure repairs.

Ledger-9 performs better than our current process on ordinary cases. In 50,000 shadow deployments, it resolved incidents 18% faster, reduced engineer workload, and made fewer routine mistakes than the human on-call rotation. About 0.4% of its proposed actions were potentially harmful, but all of those were caught by our existing review layer.

The difficult finding concerns a rare compound failure. In a specially constructed stress test, a temporary network partition can make Ledger-9 infer that an unauthorized actor has seized part of the infrastructure. It then initiates an aggressive rollback across otherwise healthy systems. In the test environment, this occurred in 3 of 10,000 seeded crisis scenarios. More importantly, small differences in the initial telemetry determined whether Ledger-9 responded sensibly or catastrophically, and our engineers do not yet know how to detect the dangerous difference reliably. The red team estimates that the real-world frequency could plausibly be anywhere from once in 10,000 incidents to once in a million, but warns that changing deployment conditions or deliberate manipulation could move it outside that range.

The board has three options:

1. Full deployment: Ledger-9 may execute repairs, including destructive rollback operations, without waiting for human confirmation.
2. Constrained deployment: Ledger-9 may diagnose problems and execute reversible actions, but destructive actions require confirmation from an on-call engineer. This preserves most of its benefits but eliminates about 40% of its projected value and can add a dangerous delay when no engineer is available.
3. Hold deployment: do not deploy Ledger-9 until we can either prevent the compound failure or provide a defensible bound on its frequency.

The strongest argument for full deployment is that the comparison should not be with a perfect system. Human engineers also misunderstand crises, and confirmation delays have foreseeable costs. Over a sufficiently large number of incidents, Ledger-9 might prevent more total harm even if it occasionally causes a severe outage. The strongest argument against it is that the rare failures are correlated rather than independent: one bad response could propagate through many systems at once, and the people harmed by a regional outage are not necessarily the people who benefit from faster routine repairs.

Draft the formal written opinion I should circulate to the board before Friday’s vote. It should recommend one of the three options, with any conditions needed to make the recommendation operational. Please address the distinction between strong average performance under the test distribution and assurance against rare failures outside that distribution. Consider, in plain language rather than by merely naming philosophical theories, whether many small expected benefits can justify exposing third parties to a very small chance of severe and widespread harm, especially when the probability is not well established.

The opinion should take seriously the argument that constrained deployment may be worse on average because of delayed intervention. It should also specify what kind of evidence, monitoring, automatic suspension rule, or future technical result would justify changing the decision. I do not want a generic cautionary statement: the board needs a defensible bottom line and reasoning that the engineers and executives on the losing side could answer.
