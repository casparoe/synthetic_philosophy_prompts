A friend of mine has an argument that voting for the lesser evil is obligatory, and I'm pretty sure it proves too much, but I can't pin down where. It goes like this. (1) If an action is available to you, costs you almost nothing, and has a significant expected benefit for other people, you're obligated to do it. (2) In a close election, the winner might be decided by a single vote -- and if it is, then your vote for the lesser evil is the one that decides it. (3) The difference between a decent candidate and a terrible one, spread over millions of people, is enormous. (4) So even after you discount by the small chance that your vote is the decisive one, the expected benefit of voting for the lesser evil is still significant. (5) So by (1), you're obligated to vote for the lesser evil. But now look: (6) by (1) plus the same arithmetic, you're also obligated to spend election day driving strangers to the polls, skip work to phone-bank, and donate your vacation fund to the campaign, since each of those has at least as much expected benefit per hour and dollar as casting one vote. (7) And it obligates you to vote for the lesser evil no matter how rotten both candidates are, so long as one is a bit worse. Those last two conclusions seem nuts to me. He says bite the bullet. I'd like to keep the duty-to-vote conclusion at (5) if I can, but I don't see a principled place to cut between (5) and (6). Which step goes first, why does it look fine when it isnt, and what does rejecting it actually cost?
