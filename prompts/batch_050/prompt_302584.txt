I run the pilot-project review team for a midsized city transit deparment, and every proposal shifts risk onto people who never opted in: delivery robots reduce van traffic but crowd sidewalks, autonomous shuttles may cut ordinary crashes while introducing weird software failures, and so on. Turn the ethics of nonconsensual risk imposition into a procedure we can actually run in one 90-minute meeting, without pretending that public consultation counts as individual consent or that a positive overall benefit automatically settles things. The procedure needs ordered tests for whether a risk is genuinely new or merely rearranged, who bears it, whether the exposed people get the benefit, how probability and severity trade off, when compensation or insurance can help, and when uncertainty itself is a reason to stop; include a rule for cases where total risk falls but risk rises for a vulnerable group, plus explicit hard stops and points where judgment cannot be eliminated. Then apply it to a robot pilot that cuts delivery-van miles by ten percent while adding a small low-speed collision risk borne mostly by blind pedestrians, and an autonomous shuttle expected to save 0.2 statistical deaths per year while introducing an unquantifiable catastrophic failure mode. Make at least one application produce an answer my team probably won’t like. Format the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: items 1–3 give the ordered procedure, item 4 handles the robot case, and item 5 handles the shuttle case and states what the procedure cannot decide.
