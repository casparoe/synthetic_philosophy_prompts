I'm a PhD student in political theory, working on the border between the neo-republican literature on freedom and the study of large-scale institutions, and I keep running into a configuration I can't cleanly classify. Consider three cases. First, a gig-economy driver whose account can be suspended by an automated fraud-detection system at any time, with no human reviewing the decision and no meaningful appeals process; the system happens never to have flagged him, but whether he works next month is entirely at the mercy of a threshold he cannot see, influence, or reason with. Second, a welfare recipient whose benefits depend on a scoring algorithm administered by an agency that follows its own rules perfectly and exercises no discretion whatsoever — everything is rule-bound and predictable — yet the rules themselves were set by a distant committee, are changed frequently and without consultation, and she must continually shape her life around anticipating the next change. Third, a tenant whose landlord is not a person but a property-management firm so proceduralized that no individual employee has the power to be arbitrary; the "whim" has been engineered away, but the tenant's dependence and exposure feel structurally identical to having a master.

My difficulty is this. The neo-republican account I was trained on defines unfreedom as subjection to another's arbitrary will — domination — and it usually illustrates this with personal relationships: the slave with a kind master, the wife with a tolerant husband. That analysis seems to presuppose an agent whose will one is tracking. But in my cases there is arguably no will to track, only a process; and yet the condition of the driver, the recipient, and the tenant seems to be what the republican framework was built to capture, not a different phenomenon. Meanwhile, the Hayekian tradition explicitly holds that impersonal forces of this kind do not compromise freedom at all, because coercion requires a person's design. So I appear to have a phenomenon that one major tradition defines out of existence and another describes only through cases that don't quite match it.

Is there an established name for this configuration — unfreedom-by-dependence where the dominating party is a procedure rather than an agent — and if so, how is it distinguished from ordinary domination on the one hand and from mere constraint or bad luck on the other? If there isn't a settled term, I'd rather have a careful carving than a label: what exactly is doing the work in these cases (is it the absence of recourse, the unilateral power to change the rules, the need to anticipate, or the dependence itself?), and where are the boundary cases — would a perfectly transparent algorithm still count, would an unchangeable bureaucratic rule still count, and does anything turn on whether a human somewhere could in principle intervene? I'd also like to know whether the answer changes if the system's designers intended the outcome it produces, versus cases where no one intended it at all.
