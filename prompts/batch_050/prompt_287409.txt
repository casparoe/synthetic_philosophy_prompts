Earlier this year my son's endocrinologist and our insurer both declined growth hormone for him. He has idiopathic short stature: healthy by every test, simply tracking far below the height curve, in a range where regulators have actually approved the treatment but coverage remains discretionary. When I pressed, the medical director gave me an analogy I have since heard repeated almost verbatim by two pediatricians and seen in a popular bioethics book: practicing medicine is like working as a mechanic, not a tuner. The mechanic restores a car to factory specification; the tuner pushes it beyond spec, and while nobody should be jailed for hot-rodding, it is not repair, no one owes it to you, and it falls outside what the workshop exists to do. Illness, on this picture, is departure from the body's factory specification, therapy is restoration to it, and my son -- nothing broken, nothing below spec for a human body, just short -- is a tuning request. The conclusion the analogy was made to carry is a strong one: the therapy/enhancement line is not an arbitrary bureaucratic boundary but tracks something real, and institutions like insurers may honorably stop at it.

I would like a full audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on my son's case. Please give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of factory specification, of wear and malfunction, of the service manual, of the mechanic's professional duty, of the owner who commissioned the build in the first place. Then the disanalogies, separated into the ones that matter for the intended use and the ones that do not. Some I suspect are load-bearing: a car has a designer who fixed its specification, whereas "species-typical normal functioning" has to be constructed statistically or evolutionarily, and both constructions are contested; a car's spec is independent of what other cars are like, whereas human normality is relativized to a reference class (age, sex, population) whose choice can be gerrymandered; repair restores a prior state the machine once had, while much accepted therapy (vaccination, myopia correction, psychiatric medication for someone who was never well) does not restore anything and arguably raises the patient above their own baseline. Note also where the analogy quietly smuggles in assumptions: that departure-from-spec is detectable independently of anyone's values, that the tuner/mechanic line aligns with the permissible/impermissible line rather than merely redescribing it, and that whoever declines a tuning request is simply following the spec rather than making a judgment about which specs are worth meeting.

Finally, tell me whether the coverage conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Does the analogy justify a principled stopping point, or does it only relabel the disputed boundary in mechanical vocabulary -- and if it fails, is there a better analogy in circulation for what the therapy/enhancement distinction is trying to do (an attorney restoring a client's rights versus winning them new ones, a steward maintaining an endowment versus growing it), or should the distinction itself be abandoned in favor of deciding coverage case by case on welfare and fairness grounds?
