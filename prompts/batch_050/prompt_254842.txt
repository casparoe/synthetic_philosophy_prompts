QUESTION: Explain Dennett's four kinds of creatures -- Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian. For each kind, state the generative-and-selective mechanism that defines it and how it goes beyond the previous kind. Then classify the following and explain your classifications: (a) a moth species whose wing coloration shifts over generations as trees darken with soot; (b) a rat that learns a maze by trial and error; (c) a chess player who mentally rehearses a line before touching a piece.

STUDENT'S ANSWER: Dennett orders creatures by how sophisticated their problem-solving equipment is, and each creature belongs to exactly one of the four kinds. Darwinian creatures rely entirely on natural selection: variation and selection across generations shape their behavior, with no learning in the individual. Case (a) is Darwinian, since the moth population changes through differential survival, not through any individual moth's efforts. Skinnerian creatures can learn within a lifetime through reinforcement: behaviors followed by reward are strengthened. Case (b) is Skinnerian, since the rat's successful turns are reinforced by food. Popperian creatures, named for the philosopher of science, can represent candidate actions in an inner environment and test them in imagination rather than in the world, so that bad ideas are eliminated instead of the creature itself. Case (c) is Popperian, because the chess player lets hypothetical moves fail in the head instead of on the board. Gregorian creatures, named for Richard Gregory, are distinguished by possessing language, which allows them to share their Popperian trials with others. Humans are the only Gregorian creatures. Since the chess player is a language-user, she is also Gregorian, but her chess deliberation itself counts as Popperian because it is internal.
