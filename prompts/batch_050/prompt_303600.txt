I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I recently got into a long argument with a colleague in computer science about whether a content-recommendation algorithm "really wants" to maximize user engagement or whether that's just a convenient way of talking. That argument made me realize my students can all recite Dennett's three stances — physical, design, intentional — and can all parrot the slogan that patterns are real if they license compression, but almost none of them can say what work the two ideas do for each other. So for this year's final I want one essay question, answerable in about 90 minutes, that forces them to connect the intentional stance to Dennett's "mild realism" about patterns rather than treating them as separate lecture topics.

Here is the setup I'd like the question to use (feel free to refine it): an engineer builds two different programs that both predict the behavior of the same target system — say, a smart thermostat — with equal accuracy. The first tracks a pattern best described in design-stance terms (setpoints, sensor states, scheduled cycles); the second tracks a pattern best described in intentional-stance terms (the thermostat "believes" the room is too cold, "wants" to reach 72 degrees, and "concludes" it should run the furnace). A third, brute-force lookup table also predicts the thermostat perfectly but tracks no pattern at all. The question should ask students to determine: (a) on Dennett's account, which of these three predictors succeeds because it has latched onto a real pattern, and whether equal predictive success plus different carving means both pattern-trackers describe real patterns or whether only one can; (b) what follows for the reality of the thermostat's beliefs and desires — are they made real by the usefulness of the intentional pattern, made unreal by the fact that the design stance also works, or is that a false dichotomy; and (c) whether Dennett's mild realism about patterns genuinely splits the difference between instrumentalism and robust realism about propositional attitudes, or whether it collapses into one of them under pressure. The question should explicitly forbid credit for bare summaries of the three stances.

Please deliver: the exam question itself (with the scenario stated cleanly enough that students can't misread it), a model answer of roughly 600 words written at the level of your best student, and a marking scheme out of 25 points built around five must-hit moves — with the points each is worth and a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker answers botch each move. The scheme should make explicit what separates an A answer from a B answer: I'd like the A/B line to turn on whether the student sees that the hard case is the *coexistence* of two successful pattern-trackers, not merely whether they can define the stances. Everything should be written in American English.
