In three weeks I'm giving a twenty-minute talk at my school's humanities symposium, followed by fifteen minutes of questions, and I want to go in prepared. My thesis is a qualified attack on the "man of his time" defence -- the familiar move of excusing figures like Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus on the grounds that they simply held the views everyone held back then. I argue that this defence should be replaced by what I call the contemporary-witness test: if people living in the same period and society, with access to roughly the same facts, recognized the practice in question as wrong and said so at the time, then the figure cannot plead his era as an excuse, though he may still plead other things (like the difficulty of acting differently, or mitigating achievements). On my test Jefferson fares badly, since antislavery arguments were available and he made some of them himself while continuing to enslave people; Churchill's record on India fares badly for similar reasons; Columbus is a harder case that I use to show where the test strains. I also concede a weakened version of the original defence for cases where dissenting witnesses were absent or genuinely inaudible to someone in the figure's position.

My audience is a panel of three judges plus classmates. Two of the judges are history teachers, and both have warned our class repeatedly against presentism -- judging the past by standards that only exist now -- so I expect them to be my sharpest critics even though my test is designed to avoid exactly that. The third is a philosophy teacher who likes pressing people on whether their distinctions do real work. The students will mostly ask whichever question sounds cleverest.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from this particular audience -- not generic objections from the internet -- ordered roughly by how damaging they'd be to my thesis if I answer them badly. Phrase each objection the way that person would actually phrase it (the history teachers talk about anachronism, context, and the difference between understanding and judging; the philosophy teacher talks about counterexamples and whether a criterion is doing explanatory work). For each objection, give me: a reply I can deliver in under two minutes; one thing I should openly concede rather than fight; and a note on where my reply is soft, so I know which follow-up to brace for. Then tell me which single question I'm hoping nobody asks, and sketch the best available answer to it in case someone does.
