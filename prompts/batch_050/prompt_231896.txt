I serve on the ethics committee of a private fertility clinic, and we have been asked to issue a written opinion on the following case. A couple, both of whom live with a well-characterized, dominantly inherited pain disorder — recurrent episodes of severe pain beginning in adolescence, manageable but not curable, with normal life expectancy and cognition — have undergone IVF. Of their embryos, the only one viable for transfer carries the mutation, as they knew it might. Both parents have the condition themselves, describe their lives as genuinely worth living, and want to proceed. The clinic's physicians are willing; the question has been referred to us because our charter requires committee sign-off when a transferred embryo is known to carry a serious heritable condition.

At our last meeting, two members laid out the positions the rest of us are split between. Dr. A argues that we should decline: refusing harms no one, since if we refuse there is no child who exists to be wronged, whereas proceeding guarantees that a person will experience serious suffering who would otherwise not have existed at all. On her view, the asymmetry settles it — bringing a sufferer into existence is a harm, while declining to bring a happy person into existence wrongs nobody — and she notes that the couple's wish to raise a child can be met through donor embryos or adoption. Dr. B argues that we should approve: one cannot wrong a person by giving them a life that person would, like the parents themselves, endorse as worth living; refusing implicitly declares the parents' own lives worse than never having existed, which they understandably experience as an affront; and a fertility clinic is not competent, nor authorized, to judge which prospective lives are worth starting. He adds that Dr. A's asymmetry, taken seriously, would condemn reproduction whenever any suffering is guaranteed — which is to say, always — and that a principle implying no one should ever have children cannot be the right tool for deciding one couple's case.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the full committee: a clear recommendation to approve, decline, or approve with conditions, with the reasoning laid out formally. The opinion should engage the strongest version of the losing side rather than dismissing it, should say whether the committee may rely on Dr. A's asymmetry in this case without thereby committing the clinic to her conclusion in general, and should close with criteria the clinic could apply to future cases involving known heritable conditions, so that this decision does not remain a one-off.
