I cover science and economics for a general-interest magazine, and twice a year I run a numeracy workshop for early-career reporters at a journalism fellowship -- 16 people, 90 minutes, a classroom with movable tables and a big whiteboard. This year's theme is averages that lie, and I want to spend the whole session on ergodicity, the whole Ole Peters time-average-vs-ensemble-average thing I covered at a talk once. The reporters should leave physically feeling that a bet can be great for the average of the room and quietly terrible for nearly every actual person in it, and that this is arithmetic, not some psychological bias or a story about risk tolerance. I don't want to lecture it at them. I want the room to generate the phenomenon themselves and only then have me name what just happened.

My seed idea, stolen from that talk: everyone starts with 100 tokens; each round you flip a coin, heads your stack goes up 50%, tails it drops 40%. My back-of-envelope math says the expected stack grows 5% a round, but someone who wins exactly half their flips still loses about two-thirds of their stack over 20 rounds, so the room's average should climb while most people bleed out -- with one lucky reporter carrying the whole average on their back. Please act as a veteran game designer who also knows the ergodicity economics literature cold. Think carefully, brainstorm two or three different activity structures first (improve on my coin game or scrap it if you can do better), say briefly why you picked the winner, then give me the full design: exact rules, materials, the payoff scheme (no real money, but I can do small stakes like good chocolate or first pick of the coffee), number of rounds, and crucially the mechanics of how results get posted on the whiteboard in real time so the divergence between the room's average and the individual trajectories becomes undeniable before I say a word about it. Include the debrief: how to map specific moments of the session onto the formal time-average/ensemble-average distinction, and how to handle the inevitable smart-aleck who says this is all just risk aversion and expected utility already covers it -- ideally so that objection becomes the point of the session rather than a rebuttal to it.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (my guess: the arithmetic of repeatedly multiplying by 1.5 and 0.6 defeats half the room, but your the designer) and how to engineer around each failure mode, plus one feature of the real ergodicity point the game cannot faithfully reproduce, which I'll just have to assert in the debrief. Fair warning: last year I ran a monty hall simulation and half the session got eaten by arguements about the rules, so simpler beats realistic even if you have to sacrifice some richness.
