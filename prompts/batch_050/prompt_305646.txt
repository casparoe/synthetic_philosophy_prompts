I teach a philosophy of biology course and every spring we do a unit on the definition of life, and every spring I write down where I stand on the problem set so the students can have at it. This year I want to actually check my own list before I hand it out, because I have a nagging feeling something in it doesn't cohere. Here it is: (1) I'm convinced by the Darwinian criterion — something is alive iff it belongs to a lineage capable of reproduction with heritable variation and selection; life is essentially the stuff evolution happens to. (2) I'm also convinced viruses are not alive. They're parasites on cellular machinery, they're on the wrong side of the line, and all the "giant virus" discoveries haven't moved me. (3) I want the definition to be intrinsic: whether something is alive should be a fact about that thing, so that in principle you could tell whether a single isolated entity — a lone bacterium on a petri dish, a single Martian crystal-thing — is alive without knowing anything about where it came from or what its relatives are like. (4) I think mules and other sterile hybrids are obviously alive, which everyone agrees with, but I mention it because I want the definition to handle individuals and not just species. And (5) I'm sympathetic to strong artificial life — a sufficiently self-maintaining, self-replicating software system could literally be alive, not just simulate life.

So: does this package hang together? I'm not asking you to relitigate each thesis on its merits — I know the standard objections. I want an audit of the combinations. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other and how badly, which ones only look like they're in tension, and for the real tensions, what's the least costly revision — which commitment would you drop or weaken, and what do I lose by doing so? Please rank by severity rather than treating everything as equally problematic. And write it up in American English, since this may end up circulating with my course materials.
