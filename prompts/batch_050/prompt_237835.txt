I have been trying to work out my considered verdicts on which acts are supererogatory, and I would like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. A passerby who ruins an expensive suit jumping into a pond to save a drowning child: not supererogatory, merely obligatory. A healthy person who donates a kidney to a stranger: supererogatory. A colleague who stays two hours late to help me meet a deadline at real inconvenience to herself: supererogatory, though I cannot say why the suit case isn't. A wealthy person who gives ten percent of her income to effective charities: admirable, but I hesitate to call it supererogatory rather than simply decent; one who gives ninety percent clearly exceeds duty. A daughter who spends five years caring for a difficult, declining parent: supererogatory, even though no single day's sacrifice would have been. A soldier who throws himself on a grenade: beyond duty. A bystander who dashes into traffic to push a child clear, at grave risk to himself: somehow this feels obligatory to attempt, or nearly so, which sits badly with the kidney case, where the cost is lower and the good comparable. And a person who quietly forgives a large debt owed by a struggling friend: supererogatory, though if the friend were starving I would call forgiveness obligatory. State a principle — or a small family of candidates — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, identifying which clause of the principle each case motivates, and say plainly which verdicts you would overturn in the name of simplicity. Then tell me what the surviving principle implies about two cases I have not listed: a bystander who calls emergency services rather than intervening personally at some risk, and a professor who writes unusually detailed comments on every student paper, year after year, at significant cost to her own work.
