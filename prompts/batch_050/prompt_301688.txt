I'm taking a seminar on comparative democratization, and our professor has been running us through a classification exercise: she presents a hypothetical country, we debate, and then she gives her verdict on whether it counts as "democratic backsliding" as opposed to democratic breakdown, ordinary policy shift, or no democratic change at all. Her verdicts so far have been consistent, she insists, but she won't tell us the principle. Here they are. Country 1: an elected president fires independent prosecutors, packs the constitutional court with loyalists, and brings most private media under owners friendly to the government, while elections remain free and the opposition can still win; verdict: backsliding. Country 2: a long-standing authoritarian regime permits a small number of independent newspapers and releases some political prisoners, then stagnates at that level for a decade; verdict: not backsliding, since there was no meaningful democracy to erode from. Country 3: the military dissolves an elected parliament overnight and rules by decree; verdict: not backsliding but breakdown, which she treats as a different category. Country 4: extreme partisan polarization, open contempt for opponents, and the collapse of informal norms of mutual toleration, but every formal institution remains intact and the incumbents scrupulously follow the written rules; verdict: not (yet) backsliding. Country 5: an executive uses emergency powers of dubious legality to postpone elections indefinitely, citing a security crisis that most observers consider manufactured; verdict: backsliding.

Next week she will give us this case: a popular incumbent wins a free and fair re-election, then amends the constitution -- through a supermajority in the legislature and a court-supervised referendum, all formally lawful -- to abolish term limits and restructure the electoral commission, whose members must now be approved by the governing party. The courts and press remain formally independent, but journalists increasingly self-censor after a few high-profile libel suits, and the next election is procedurally clean though fought on a visibly tilted playing field. I'd like you to predict her verdict and reconstruct her reasoning for me before the class meets.

Please structure your answer in five sections, in this order: "Candidate principles" (roughly 150 words -- state at least three distinct principles that are each consistent with all five prior verdicts, such as a formal-illegality rule, a level-of-democracy baseline rule, an election-centered rule, or a liberal-institutions rule); "What each principle predicts" (roughly 120 words -- where the principles diverge on the new case); "Closest precedent" (roughly 80 words -- which earlier verdict is the strongest analog and why); "My prediction" (roughly 100 words -- your best guess at her verdict, with your reasons); and "The discriminating case" (roughly 60 words -- one further hypothetical whose verdict would distinguish your favored principle from its strongest rival).
