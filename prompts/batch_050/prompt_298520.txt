The exam question was:

"In his essays 'Of Money' and 'Of the Balance of Trade,' Hume argues against the mercantilists. Explain the specie-flow mechanism he describes and what it implies about the mercantilist policy of maintaining a trade surplus. Does Hume think changes in the money supply have real effects on economic activity? Explain your answer with reference to the timing of monetary changes and price changes."

The student's answer:

"Hume's specie-flow mechanism works like this. Suppose a country runs a persistent trade surplus. Gold and silver flow in, raising the domestic money supply. By the quantity theory of money, which Hume originated, the domestic price level rises in proportion. Higher prices make the country's exports expensive abroad and foreign goods cheap at home, so the surplus erodes and eventually reverses. The mirror-image process corrects deficits. Because the adjustment is automatic, the mercantilist goal of accumulating bullion via a favorable balance of trade is self-defeating: any inflow of coin just inflates prices until trade rebalances.

As for real effects: Hume is a neutral-money theorist. Since prices are determined by the quantity of money, increasing the money supply merely raises prices in proportion and changes nothing real — money is a veil. He concludes that the money supply is irrelevant to a nation's industry and that governments have nothing to gain from monetary expansion. What actually makes a nation rich is its labor, manufactures, and commerce, not its stock of precious metals; the mercantilists simply confuse money with wealth. A country's money supply will settle at whatever level its trade requires, and any attempt to push it higher only produces inflation."
