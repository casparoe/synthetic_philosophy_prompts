So for english class we watched 12 Angry Men (the 1957 one) and it got me thinking about this philosophy thing I read about called "naked statistical evidence." The idea is roughly: evidence can make something super probable and yet it still feels wrong to convict on that alone. Like the blue bus case -- someone gets hit by a bus, you can't ID it, but you know one company runs 80% of the buses on that route, and courts don't want to hold the company liable just on that. Or the gatecrasher one where almost everyone at a rodeo snuck in without paying, so any random attendee probably didn't pay, but convicting them on that alone feels messed up even though it's like 99% likely.

Read the film through this lens. The jurors go from 11-1 guilty to not guilty without any new evidence, they just re-argue the same stuff (the knife, the old man's testimony, the el train, the woman's glasses). Two readings I can see: (a) the film agrees with the intuition behind the gatecrasher case -- probability alone isn't enough, you need some actual account of what happened that night, and the prosecution never had one that hung together; (b) the film isn't about that at all, Juror 8's whole point is just that the evidence never reached "beyond a reasonable doubt" even statistically. Which reading do the scenes actually support, and where does the film resist being shoved into the blue bus framing? Also: does the movie kind of cheat by making it seem like the kid probably really is innocent, when the whole point of the gatecrasher puzzle is the person probably IS guilty and conviction still feels wrong?
