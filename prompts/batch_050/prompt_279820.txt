So I'm in this undergrad bioethics seminar and last week we did the unit on defining death, brain death vs the cardiopulmonary criterion and all that. After class my roommate (he's an EMT, so he's seen actual death pronouncements) walked me through this argument and I literally could not find the hole in it, and it's been bugging me for days because the conclusion seems insane. Here it is, as close to his version as I can reconstruct:

(1) Death has to be a biological fact about the organism. Whether grandma is dead at 3:14pm is a fact about grandma's body, not a fact about the people standing around her -- otherwise "she died" would be reporting something about the doctors, which is absurd. (2) The standard circulatory criterion says death is the *irreversible* cessation of circulation and breathing -- that's why we don't call someone dead mid-CPR when there's a decent chance of getting them back. (3) But "irreversible" just means it can't be reversed. If some intervention could restart the circulation, then the cessation wasn't irreversible, full stop. (4) Whether circulation *can* be restarted depends entirely on what's around: someone who arrests alone in a remote cabin is gone, but a person in the exact same physiological state in a cardiac ICU, with CPR and ECMO on hand, is a resuscitation candidate. (5) So the very same bodily state counts as death in the cabin and as not-death in the ICU. (6) That means death is relative to available technology and other people's choices, which by (1) is not a biological fact at all. His conclusion: there is no moment of death, only a moment when we *decide* to stop treating someone -- "dead" is a social and legal status wearing a lab coat.

Every step feels right when I look at it individually, but the conclusion can't be right -- we pronounce deaths, we harvest organs from donors only after declaring them dead, the whole thing would fall apart if death were just a decision we make. I feel like something slippery happens around (2) and (3) with the word "irreversible" but when I push on it he just says "ok, define irreversible in a way that doesn't smuggle in the relativism you're trying to avoid," and I can't. So: which step do you actually reject, why does it look so innocent, and what does rejecting it *cost* me? Because I suspect every way out here costs something -- either you bite some version of his bullet, or you end up saying nobody is ever really dead until their cells rot, which seems just as crazy. And fair warning, I'd really like to keep the intuition that the cabin guy and the ICU guy aren't in different ontological categories, so if that's what has to go, tell me plainly.
