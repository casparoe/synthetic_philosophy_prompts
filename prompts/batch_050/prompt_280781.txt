Every year our philosophy department ends the spring term with a "symposium" -- a long dinner at which the wine flows freely, in a nod to Plato. This year our dean, a teetotaler who has read just enough Plato to be dangerous, has proposed making the event dry, and she plans to attend to defend her proposal. I have been elected to give the after-dinner speech answering her. Her case rests on a passage from Book I of the Laws (649b, in R. G. Bury's translation), where the Athenian Stranger describes what wine does to a man:

"he becomes filled with high hopes and a sense of power, till finally, puffed up with conceit, he abounds in every kind of licence of speech and action and every kind of audacity, without a scruple as to what he says or what he does."

Write the speech (about five minutes). It must take the dean's passage seriously rather than dodge it: the Athenian says this not to condemn drinking parties but to justify them, since a soul's grip on shame and self-restraint can only really be tested, and trained, where those things are genuinely at risk -- which is why his ideal city permits wine under the supervision of the sober and forbids drinking in solitude. The speech has to work for a mixed room: colleagues who know the Laws well enough to catch a misreading, the dean, who knows only her one passage, and spouses who know neither. The philosophical point should do real structural work -- the speech should itself, in its tone and conduct, enact the distinction Plato draws between drinking as licence and drinking as a governed test of character -- rather than treating the Laws as decoration. End with a toast the dean can join without surrendering her passage.
