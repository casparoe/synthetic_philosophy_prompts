In a philosophy-of-perception seminar, we have been discussing Molyneux’s problem through a less-quoted remark from Book II, Chapter IX of John Locke’s An Essay Concerning Human Understanding: “We are further to consider concerning perception, that the ideas we receive by sensation are often, in grown people, altered by the judgment, without our taking notice of it.” One member of the seminar, Dr. Vale, has given verdicts on four stipulated cases. First, Mara, blind from birth but highly skilled at distinguishing cubes from spheres by touch, gains sight and is immediately shown one cube and one sphere; Dr. Vale says Mara cannot yet tell which is which by sight alone. Second, after a week of simultaneously watching and touching the objects, Mara can sort them visually; Dr. Vale says this is now genuine visual recognition rather than mere verbal inference. Third, Tomas, sighted from birth, has often seen a distinctive unfamiliar solid but never touched it; when blindfolded, he recognizes it by touch on the first attempt, and Dr. Vale says this is immediate tactile recognition, not inference from memories of touch. Fourth, Liora, also blind from birth, is fluent in tactile raised-line drawings of cubes and spheres; upon gaining sight, she is shown the actual solids and, according to Dr. Vale, still cannot identify them visually before watching them being handled.

The disputed new case concerns Liora and drawings rather than solids. Immediately after gaining sight, before any period of looking at her hands or at three-dimensional objects, Liora is shown visual versions of the same sort of raised-line diagrams she knows tactually: one line drawing of a cube and one line drawing of a sphere. There is no color, shading, motion, or accompanying tactile input, and she must say which drawing depicts the cube. What do you predict Dr. Vale will say, and why? Please reconstruct the rule that best explains her earlier verdicts, identify at least two other rules that fit those verdicts but diverge on the drawing case, and compare their predictions. I am less interested in the textbook answer to Molyneux’s original question than in whether the decisive consideration is a newly opened sensory modality, the difference between solid form and depiction, asymmetry between vision and touch, or the kind of unnoticed judgment Locke describes. If the earlier verdicts underdetermine the prediction, say so, and propose one further case that would best distinguish the leading interpretations.
