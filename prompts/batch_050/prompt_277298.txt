I serve as secretary to the Allocations Board of a consortium of four research groups — call them Astronomy, Bio, Chem, and Dynamics — that share a university computing cluster. Our charter lets the Board adopt a change to the scheduling and cost-sharing rules only if the change is a "guaranteed improvement for all four groups." A consultant's memo once glossed that standard for us in terms of safe Pareto improvements: roughly, a change that leaves each group better off than the status quo regardless of how the other groups subsequently behave, rather than one that pays off only if everyone plays along. I increasingly doubt that our actual practice matches that definition, and I want a prediction of how we will rule on a pending proposal. Our record:

1. Overnight batch scheduling. Moving large jobs to a batch window raised every group's expected throughput, and each group would have gained even if it alone had adopted the new submission discipline while the others kept their old habits. Approved unanimously.
2. A priority lane for short jobs. Expected gains for all four groups if all participated — but had Bio joined the scheme alone while the others stayed on the old system, Bio's long jobs would have been squeezed and Bio would have ended up strictly worse off than the status quo. Approved 3–1, over Bio's dissent.
3. A revised maintenance-window scheme. Strictly positive expected gains for Astronomy, Bio, and Dynamics; Chem exactly indifferent. Dynamics' gain, however, depended on an outside vendor's response time: in the slow-response scenario Dynamics would have been slightly worse off than the status quo, though well ahead in expectation. Dynamics argued against the scheme. Rejected.
4. A consolidated reservation and accounting system. Deterministic, strictly better for every group under joint adoption, and each group would have gained even adopting it alone. All four groups acknowledged that the shelved "fair-share draft" would have been strictly better still for every one of them. Approved anyway; the chair remarked that the Board measures proposals against the status quo, not against hypothetical alternatives.

Now pending: a burst-capacity proposal. Groups may claim idle nodes above quota, financed partly by selling surplus capacity to an external spot market and splitting the proceeds. All four groups are strictly better off in expectation under joint adoption; each group strictly gains even if it adopts the new claiming discipline alone; and no group's position depends on how the others behave. Astronomy's net position, though, rides on external spot prices: in a low-price year it ends slightly below the status quo, in a normal or high-price year well above it, strictly positive in expectation. Astronomy drafted the proposal and is lobbying hard for it.

Please give me: (a) your predicted verdict, with a rough confidence; (b) the two or three principles, each consistent with all four prior rulings, that the Board might be running — including, if it survives, a no-downside-risk principle and a no-imposition-on-a-dissenter principle — and what each predicts here; (c) the closest precedent and what makes it a confounded one; and (d) the future case, or pair of cases, that would most efficiently tell the surviving principles apart. Note that the consultant's own test delivers a clear verdict on this proposal even though the Board's practice may not be the consultant's test. If the record underdetermines the prediction, or if my gloss of the framework is itself muddled in a way that corrupts the question, say so plainly rather than forcing a tidy answer.
