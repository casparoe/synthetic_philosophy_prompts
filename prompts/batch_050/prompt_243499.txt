I'm a novelist working on a book about a lighthouse keeper in the 1970s, and part of my "research" (procrastination) has been rewatching survival movies. Last week I put on Cast Away again, the Tom Hanks one, and this time it hit me as almost a feature-length illustration of the tool analysis from Being in Time, which I half-remember from a philosophy class twenty years ago. Chuck on the island is constantly having his stuff break or fail him -- the ice skate blade he repurposes, the rope, the raft, the FedEx packages he raids -- and the moment something breaks is exactly the moment he seems to really SEE it for the first time as an object with properties, rather than just transparently using it. Meanwhile Wilson the volleyball starts out as pure equipment (something to stop the bleeding hand, IIRC) and becomes the only "person" in his world.

So here's what I want: read Cast Away through Heidegger on readiness-to-hand, presence-at-hand, and the breakdown of equipment (conspicuousness, obtrusiveness, obstinacy, if you want to get technical). Which scenes dramatize the tool analysis best, and where does the film genuinely push back? Does that break the Heideggerian reading or deepen it? If the film shows something about our relationship to things that the hammer analysis in Being and Time misses -- something about isolation, or time, or making tools into companions -- say what it is.
