I run the responsible sourcing team at a mid-sized European clothing company. About forty supplier arrangements cross my desk each quarter — new factories, renewals, subcontracting proposals — and my team of three has maybe two days per case, no economists, and a board that signs off only on rejections of deals above a certain size. The situations we face almost never involve outright coercion or legal violations; our audits catch those. The hard cases are the ones where everyone consents and everyone is better off: the workers prefer the job to their alternatives, the supplier makes a margin, we get a price that keeps us competitive — and yet the wage is a fraction of any credible living-wage estimate, and it is low precisely because the workers' alternatives are desperate.

I want a decision procedure, grounded in what the philosophical literature on exploitation actually says about mutually beneficial transactions, that my team can run on a proposal and defend to the board. The literature seems to agree that consent plus mutual benefit does not settle the matter — a deal can leave both parties better off and still take unfair advantage — but it disagrees about what makes such a deal wrong, and I need the procedure to be honest about that rather than smuggling in one answer. In particular, I would like it to engage seriously with: the problem of the baseline (fair relative to what — the market rate, a hypothetical competitive price, the workers' needs, or a fair division of the surplus the deal creates); the non-worseness constraint and its micro/macro problem (we can rarely worsen an individual worker's lot by offering a job, yet cancelling or overpaying at scale may shrink employment, trigger automation, or move production elsewhere, so what counts as 'worse' and for whom); the worry that refusing to engage with desperate workers can be morally worse than dealing with them on imperfect terms; and the question of how much weight the quality of consent deserves when refusal was never a live option.

The deliverable should be a sequence of questions we ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, clear stopping points where the procedure outputs reject or escalate, and explicit markers at the steps where the answer is a judgement of value rather than fact — I would rather the procedure visibly hand those to our ethics committee than pretend a checklist settled them. It should say what to do when we cannot get the numbers (living-wage estimates for a region often do not exist, and surplus figures are guesswork), and it should flag the cases where the philosophical positions genuinely diverge, so we know which step of the procedure a different theoretical commitment would change.

Then run the procedure on three cases: first, a garment factory paying roughly double the prevailing local alternatives but under half of a contested living-wage estimate, with systematic compulsory overtime; second, a network of subcontracted homeworkers on piece rates, mostly women, whose hourly earnings fluctuate around the local minimum and who strongly prefer the arrangement to factory work; third, a supplier offering genuinely good wages in a depressed region where our exit would eliminate most formal employment in the town. I want at least one of these to come out with an answer I will find uncomfortable, in either direction, if that is where the reasoning leads. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — which decisions will remain contested no matter how well it is designed.

Please write in a formal register, and in British English.
