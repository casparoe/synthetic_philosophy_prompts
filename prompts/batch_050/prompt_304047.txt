So a friend of mine was explaining this thing called the unilateralist's curse to me at dinner, and I want to know if his version of it is actually fair. His summary went roughly like this: imagine a bunch of altruistically motivated people who each privately form their own noisy estimate of whether some intervention is worth doing — say, funding a particular risky research program that only needs one funder. Anyone can act alone without consulting the rest. The person most likely to act is whoever has the most optimistic estimate, and since estimates are noisy, the most optimistic estimate is probably an overestimate. So the program tends to get funded even when the group's average judgment would be that it's a bad idea, and this gets worse the more people are in the pool. His takeaway was that whoever ends up pulling the trigger is basically the sucker who got fooled by their own over-optimism — the same way the winner of an auction tends to overpay — and so the rational thing for do-gooders in these situations is to never act on their own initiative and instead wait until a consensus forms.

I have a gut feeling he's got the basic mechanism right but that the conclusion is doing more work than the mechanism supports, though I can't pin down where. So tell me, and please actually commit rather than giving me a menu of interpretive options: which parts of his summary are accurate as a description of the curse, which parts subtly distort it, and where exactly his "so never act unilaterally" takeaway breaks down? In particular I'm suspicious of two things — the framing of the person who acts as irrational or duped, and the jump to consensus as the remedy — but I might be wrong about either, so give me your definite verdict on both, plus whether his overall conclusion follows once the accurate parts are stated properly.
