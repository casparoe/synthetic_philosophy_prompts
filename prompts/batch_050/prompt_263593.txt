I chair the board of a mid-sized charitable foundation. Every spring we fund exactly one large program from a shortlist, and this year the choice has come down to two:

(a) An intensive rehabilitation program that would restore independent living to roughly 40 stroke survivors per year, over five years -- an enormous change in the life of each recipient, from dependence to something close to their previous functioning.

(b) A hearing-aid subsidy program that would reach roughly 3,500 elderly people with mild-to-moderate hearing loss over the same period -- a modest but real improvement for each, at far lower cost per person.

The cost totals are comparable. Our junior analyst has tried to settle the question numerically: using published survey instruments, she assigned each beneficiary an expected gain in self-reported life satisfaction (on a 0–10 scale, converted to "welfare units"), summed the units within each program, and produced a table showing program (b) delivering about nine times the total welfare per dollar. She argues the board is morally required to fund (b), and has said so in a memo to staff.

Half the board is persuaded; half is uneasy but cannot articulate why. One trustee says the whole exercise is "adding apples and oranges across skulls" and that her sum means nothing. She retorts that we are going to compare across persons no matter what we do -- even choosing (a) out of intuition is an implicit comparison -- so refusing to add numbers does not avoid interpersonal comparison, it just hides it.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board before our vote. It should:

1. Give a bottom-line recommendation on two questions: whether the analyst's table may be treated as a measurement of what it claims to measure, and whether it should nonetheless play some role in our decision (and what role).
2. Take seriously, and answer, the trustee's objection that cardinal welfare units cannot be meaningfully compared across persons -- while also taking seriously the analyst's rejoinder that any decision between these programs is an implicit comparison, so that the real choice is between explicit and implicit comparison, not comparison and none.
3. Distinguish, in terms a non-philosopher board can follow, between the different things a 0–10 life-satisfaction score might be tracking, and say which of these the addition across persons is most and least defensible for.
4. Propose a decision rule the board can actually adopt this spring and reuse in future years -- one that does not pretend we have no way to weigh 40 large gains against 3,500 small ones, but that also does not dress up a contested value judgment as arithmetic. State explicitly which premises of the rule are measurements and which are policy choices we are owning.
5. Apply the rule to this case and give a recommendation on (a) vs (b), including an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side.

The opinion should run to two or three pages. I want a document the board could vote on, not a literature survey.
