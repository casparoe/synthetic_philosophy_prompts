Ok so I run a small philosophy meetup, mostly software people who've read a lot of LessWrong (me too, obviously, I'm one of them). Next session is on Quine and gavagai. Here's the problem: this crowd already thinks it knows gavagai, because it gets name-dropped on LW all the time, but what they've actually absorbed is the pop version -- "you can't tell whether gavagai means rabbit, undetached rabbit part, or rabbit-stage, words don't carve reality at its joints, language is hard, whatever." That's a claim about ambiguity and category boundaries. Quine's actual claim is way weirder: it's not that the linguist can't find out what the word refers to, it's that there is no further fact to find out, and (this is the part that really melts people) it applies to your own words too, not just Jungle Language.

I have about 90 minutes, whiteboard, no assigned reading, and I can't assume anyone's read Word and Object. Don't explain the argument to me -- I know the argument. Plan how I should teach it.

Specifically I want:

1. The order the ideas have to come in and why. E.g. my instinct is that you need the field linguist setup and the assent/dissent stuff in place before "why not just ask?" makes sense as a question, and you need underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence distinguished from the stronger indeterminacy claim before anyone can state the thesis without overstating it. But I might have the dependency structure wrong, so tell me what actually has to precede what.

2. The one example that carries the whole thing. Default is obviously the gavagai case itself, but if you think something else should do the load-bearing work (the proxy function bit? the "you can permute the ontology" move? a home-language case?) argue for it. Whatever you pick, say what the example must have and must NOT have to avoid misleading them.

3. The misconception map, in the order this audience will actually form them. My guesses: (a) "this is just epistemic modesty about field linguistics," (b) "pointing more carefully or gesturing at rabbit-parts vs wholes settles it," (c) "it's basically Kripkenstein's plus/quus thing" (they will bring up rule-following, guaranteed, one guy always does), (d) "so Quine is a skeptic who thinks meaning is fake" vs the naturalistic reading, (e) the LW-specific one where they nod along because they think it's just the map/territory thing again. For each, where in the sequence do I head it off -- before they form it or after it's out in the open -- and with what move.

4. What I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level (my candidates: the Davidsonian responses, the "was Quine even right" literature) and how to flag those as real open questions rather than dodge them.

5. One question I can ask at the end that distinguishes "got the actual thesis" from "got the bumper sticker."

Give me the plan as a markdown table with these columns: Step | What I introduce | What must already be in place (dependency) | Misconception headed off here, and the move that does it | Quick check it landed. Then after the table, a short JSON object with fields: carrying_example (string), why_not_the_obvious_alternative (string), topics_to_refuse (array of strings), end_of_session_question (string), and biggest_failure_mode (string -- the single most likely way this session produces confident nodding and zero understanding).
