Next month I'm presenting to my college's electoral reform working group, and I'd like you to act as a social choice theorist who has also spent years advising real committees -- someone who knows the formal literature cold but also knows which objections actually sink proposals in a room full of non-theorists. The background: our faculty senate is currently elected by plurality with a top-two runoff, and there is broad agreement that this is producing perverse outcomes (candidates winning with tiny pluralities, widespread talk of "wasted votes" and tactical desertion). My proposal is to replace it with majority judgment: every voter assigns every candidate a letter grade from A to F, the candidate with the highest median grade wins, and ties are broken by successively removing one median-grade ballot from each tied candidate until the medians separate. The audience is about twelve people: three political scientists who work on electoral systems, two economists (one of whom does mechanism design), a statistician, our registrar, and several senior administrators whose primary concerns are transparency, ease of explaining results to the campus, and the risk of a contested outcome. There are no philosophers in the room.

The centerpiece of my pitch, and the part I most need stress-tested, is a three-step argument that majority judgment escapes the classic impossibility results. First: the Gibbard--Satterthwaite theorem assumes voters submit ordinal rankings, so its manipulability conclusion simply does not apply to a graded ballot -- we have stepped outside the theorem's framework rather than being defeated by it. Second: even informally, grading blunts strategic voting, because the median is robust -- moving your grade of a candidate up or down does nothing unless you cross the median, and inflating your favorite's grade never requires burying a rival, so the runoffs' incentive to dishonestly tank the strongest opponent disappears. Third: whatever residual misgrading remains is unsystematic and washes out across a large electorate. My instinct is that step one is the load-bearing claim and also the one most likely to be wrong or at least badly overstated, but I don't know exactly how the mechanism designer in the room will put the objection, and I don't want to learn during the Q&A.

Please think carefully about this from the perspective of each constituency in the room, and begin by brainstorming the full space of objections -- formal, incentive-theoretic, empirical, procedural, and purely presentational -- before narrowing down. Then draft my prep sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise (not the ones a conference of voting theorists would raise), ordered by how much damage each would do to the proposal's chances, each phrased the way that person would phrase it -- the mechanism designer's version will sound different from the registrar's. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in two minutes, flag where the reply is soft or relies on a premise I can't fully defend, and tell me whether this is an objection I should concede gracefully up front or one I must hold the line on. If any step of my three-part argument is simply false as stated, correct it before I embarrass myself, and tell me the strongest defensible version of the claim. Finally, include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- the one where my best honest answer is weak -- and what I should say anyway.
