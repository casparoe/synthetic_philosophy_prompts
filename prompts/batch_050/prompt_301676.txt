I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I've been on a weird criminal law kick since a coworker mentioned that executives can go to jail for stuff they didn't personally do. That led me to United States v. Park (1975), the Supreme Court case where the CEO of Acme Markets was convicted because food in the company's Baltimore warehouse was exposed to rodent contamination. From what I can tell the salient facts are: Park never went near the warehouse, he'd delegated sanitation to subordinates he regarded as dependable, and the FDA had actually warned him in writing back in 1970 about similar conditions at a different warehouse in Philadelphia. The company pled guilty, Park fought it and lost, and the Court upheld his conviction under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act without requiring proof that he intended or even knew about the violations.

Here's the passage that snagged me. The Court says the Act punishes "neglect where the law requires care, or inaction where it imposes a duty" (that's Burger quoting Morissette v. United States in the Park opinion). And I can't figure out what that sentence is doing. If Park is being punished for *neglect* — for failing to take care he should have taken — then isn't that just negligence, i.e. a fault standard, not strict liability at all? Which would mean the famous "strict liability CEO case" is secretly a mens rea case with the bar set low. But if it's genuinely strict liability, then the word "neglect" is doing no work and Park was convicted essentially for holding a job title while mice did their thing somewhere in a 874-store operation.

So what I want is an analysis of the case through the mens rea question, run in both directions. First: what would the main theories of criminal culpability (strict liability, negligence, recklessness) each say about whether Park deserved conviction on these facts, and does the 1970 FDA warning letter change which theory is actually in play — does it convert this into recklessness because he was on notice, or is that a stretch? Second: is Burger's "neglect" line a genuine reconciliation of strict liability with the intuition that punishment requires fault, or is it rhetorical cover — dressing up a no-fault rule in the language of blame? Third, and this is the part I actually care about: what does the case show about the theory? If the only way to make Park palatable is to redescribe it as negligence, does that mean pure strict liability for people (as opposed to fines for corporations) is indefensible, or does the case point to some third thing — liability for voluntarily taking on a role whose duties you could have structured your company to fulfill? Please be disciplined about the mapping: tell me where each theory is silent instead of making it talk. And one wrinkle: Park's defense was basically that he delegated to competent people, which as an engineer sounds... exactly like what running a large org requires? If delegation itself is the culpable act, I'm not sure any large company could be run legally, so say something about that too.
