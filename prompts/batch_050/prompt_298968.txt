I'm a retired field biologist (thirty years counting seabirds, if you're curious) who fell into philosophy about two years ago, mostly through a reading group that meets at the library. I've also ended up editing the letters page of our local naturalists' society newsletter, and the two hobbies have collided. Members keep sending in sentences that feel broken in a specific way, and after reading around I gather philosophers call this Moore's paradox: things along the lines of "the ospreys have abandoned the nest, but I don't believe they have," or "I believe the water level has dropped, and it hasn't."

What I want is a procedure our two volunteer copy editors (neither has studied philosophy) can follow to decide, when a letter contains one of these constructions, whether it's genuinely defective and should be queried with the author, merely odd but fine to print, or actually no problem at all. Please base it on whatever the philosophy of language and mind actually establishes about these cases, and write it as a sequence of tests in order, where each test says what to look for and what verdict to give before moving to the next one.

It has to cope with at least these wrinkles, because I've collected them from real letters:

1. Both forms -- "p but I don't believe p" and "p but I believe not-p" -- if the theory treats them differently, the procedure should too.
2. Tense shifts, like "the ospreys left, but I didn't believe they would," which strike me as perfectly sensible.
3. Other people's beliefs: "the ospreys left, but Margaret doesn't believe it."
4. Weakened versions: "the ospreys left, but I doubt it" or "I suspect they left, and they did."
5. Someone hypothesizing rather than asserting, as in "suppose the lake froze over and nobody believes it did -- then what?"
6. Cases where the first half isn't an assertion at all, like a hedge or a guess.

Run the finished procedure on three worked examples, and include one where it hands back a verdict that would annoy me (I'd rather keep letters intact than query them, so probably one where it tells me to query a sentence I find charming). Also mark clearly the points where the procedure can't decide and an editor just has to use judgment, because I don't want it pretending to settle what it can't. And tell me in a sentence or two what the underlying explanation of the absurdity is doing in each step, so the editors understand the point of the test rather than running it mechanically.

Keep the whole thing under 700 words, including the worked examples -- it has to fit on one printed page we can tape to the desk.
