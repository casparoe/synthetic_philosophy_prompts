I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about persuasion tech — dark patterns, hyper-targeted ads, AI companions that are scarily good at getting people to buy stuff or stay subscribed. The piece keeps circling one question: when does trying to change someone's mind stop being legit persuasion and start being manipulation? Everyone uses these words like the line is obvious, but when I actually press people on it, they draw it in completly different places.

So I want to build a little "where do YOU draw the line?" quiz for readers to run alongside the article. Here's what I need:

- 8 to 10 questions, each one a concrete scenario with 2-4 answer options phrased as verdicts on the case (this is fine / this is manipulation / it depends on something specifiable), never as endorsements of a theory. Nobody should need to have read any philosophy to answer. Cases I'd like used: a grocery store putting candy at kid-eye-level by the checkout; a friend saying "after everything I've done for you, you owe me this one"; a website that makes the cancel button nearly impossible to find; a politician giving a fiery emotional speech in which every factual claim happens to be true; an AI assistant that notices you seem down and times its product suggestions for when you're most likely to say yes. You can tweak those and add a couple of your own, but keep them everyday-ish — no trolleys, no brains in vats.
- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions in the debate. Leaves that must appear: the view that manipulation means bypassing or short-circuiting someone's capacity to deliberate; the view that manipulation is about applying pressure — making the option you don't want them to pick costly — as opposed to trickery; the view that what makes influence manipulative is that it's hidden from the target; the view that exploiting known glitches in human cognition is manipulation even when everything the influencer does is out in the open; and a deflationary skeptic leaf for readers whose answers suggest they don't think there's a real line at all, just influence we like vs. influence we don't.
- The whole point is to sort readers by their verdicts on cases, not by which label sounds impressive, so nothing like "do you value autonomy?" as a question.
- Flag any two leaves the quiz can't reliably tell apart, and any plausible answer pattern that doesn't land on any leaf — and for those, tell me whether the pattern looks incoherent or just unoccupied.
- Since this runs in a magazine: for each leaf, one sentence a lay reader will actually understand, plus one thing from my beat (advertising, UX design, political messaging, AI assistants) that the view would condemn and one it would let slide.

End with a single last line starting "Summary:" naming the one diagnostic question that does the most sorting work in the quiz.
