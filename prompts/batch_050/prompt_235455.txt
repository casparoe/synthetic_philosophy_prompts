I am assembling examples for a seminar on whether mathematics can be genuinely explanatory in empirical science, as the so-called enhanced indispensability argument requires. My list so far: (1) the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas, where number-theoretic facts about primes are said to explain why 13- and 17-year cycles evolved rather than 12- or 14-year ones; (2) the hexagonal structure of honeycombs, where the honeycomb theorem — that the regular hexagonal grid partitions the plane into equal-area cells with the least total perimeter — is said to explain why bees build as they do; (3) the Königsberg bridges, where elementary graph theory explains why no walk crosses each of the seven bridges exactly once. Supply a fourth example whose structure differs from all three: it should not be an optimization result like (2), nor a sheer impossibility result like (3), nor rest on number-theoretic facts like (1). State the empirical phenomenon and the relevant mathematics precisely enough that one could check whether the mathematics is doing explanatory work or merely representing facts already explained by the underlying physics or biology, and then assess briefly how your example fares against that challenge. Do not offer a variant or combination of the three cases above. At most 400 words.
