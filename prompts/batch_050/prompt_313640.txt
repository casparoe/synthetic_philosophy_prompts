Bernard Williams famously argued that a man who saves his drowning wife rather than a stranger, but only after pausing to confirm that morality permits him to do so, has had one thought too many: the motivating thought should simply have been that it was his wife. Critics have replied that this objection trades on an unrealistic picture of moral psychology, or that it conflates what motivates an action with what justifies it. Write an essay on a less-discussed variant of this controversy: retrospective moral scrutiny. Imagine a rescuer who acts instantly and wholeheartedly, moved only by the thought that it is his wife — but who, that evening, sits down to check whether the rescue was morally permissible, and resolves that he would have been obliged to let her drown had the calculation come out the other way. Is this after-the-fact bookkeeping one thought too many in Williams's sense, even though it played no role in motivating the action? Or is retrospective justification-seeking not only innocent but perhaps required of a morally serious person? Your essay should weigh the strongest considerations on both sides — including what this case reveals about the relationship between motivation, justification, and integrity — and reach and defend a conclusion about whether Williams's objection extends to retrospective deliberation.
