I am a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on supererogation, and I find that my own judgments across cases resist tidy systematization. I would like your help doing some reflective equilibrium on them. You should approach this as a moral philosopher with particular expertise in the structure of duty and the beyond-duty. Please think carefully rather than reaching for the textbook definition. Begin by brainstorming, briefly, the main candidate principles for drawing the duty/supererogation boundary (cost-based, blame-based, reason-balance-based, and any hybrid you think worth considering), and only then work through my cases. I want a principled answer, not a tour of the literature.

Here are my verdicts, which I report with reasonable but not total confidence:

1. Donating a kidney to a stranger one has read about: clearly supererogatory.
2. Donating a kidney to one's own child: not supererogatory. I would think worse of a parent who declined.
3. A strong swimmer who happens by rescuing a drowning child at real but moderate risk to herself: obligatory.
4. A soldier throwing himself on a grenade to save his comrades: supererogatory, indeed paradigmatically so.
5. Keeping a promise even when unforeseen circumstances make doing so ruinously expensive: obligatory, the cost notwithstanding.
6. Donating forty percent of one's income to effective charities: supererogatory.
7. Driving three hours out of one's way to return a stranger's lost dog, at trivial risk: supererogatory, though only just.
8. Whistleblowing on one's employer's dangerous fraud when doing so will end one's career: I go back and forth, but I lean toward obligatory.
9. Confessing to a crime one's brother committed in order to spare him prison: not supererogatory; it is wrong, whatever else it is.
10. A surgeon who regularly works through the night for her patients beyond what her contract and professional norms require: supererogatory.

My tentative principle, which I suspect needs repair, is the standard tripartite one: an act is supererogatory if and only if it is morally good, not required by duty, and its omission merits no blame. Two things trouble me. First, cases 2 and 5 together seem to defeat any principle keyed to cost, since both are enormously costly yet neither strikes me as beyond duty; but then what distinguishes them from 1 and 4? Second, the no-blame condition seems to misfire in both directions: I want to blame the withholding parent of case 2 without conceding that kidney donation is in general obligatory, and in case 10 I do not blame the surgeon who goes home at a reasonable hour, yet I also feel there is a residue of "she really shouldn't have to, but someone must" that the tripartite account flattens.

Here is what I want from you:

(a) State precisely the principle, or the two strongest competing principles, that best capture the pattern in my verdicts. Indicate which case motivates each clause of the principle.

(b) Be honest about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and say whether you would overturn the verdict or complicate the principle. I suspect 2 and 5 force a choice here, and I want to see you make it rather than gesture at both.

(c) Test my tentative tripartite principle against the cases and either repair it with the fewest possible amendments or explain why it is beyond repair.

(d) Apply your repaired principle to four cases I have not listed: an extravagant tip on a modest restaurant bill; anonymously paying off strangers' medical debt; declining a lucrative job abroad to remain near an ailing parent; and a lifeguard who rescues a swimmer while off duty. For each, give the verdict and the clause that does the work.

(e) Finally, say a word about whether your principle yields a sensible category for the mirror-image phenomenon: acts that are somewhat bad but not forbidden, such as being consistently brusque with colleagues. If your principle is silent on this, say whether that is a gap or a virtue.

Please structure your answer with explicit labels for each deliverable, and where your own judgment diverges from mine on a case, flag it rather than smoothing it over.
