I'm trying to find out whether there is an established term for a pattern I keep noticing in philosophical argument, and if not, how best to carve it out. The pattern: a thought experiment contains a feature that the author explicitly sets aside as irrelevant to the conclusion — both sides stipulate that it shouldn't matter — and yet the intuitive force of the case seems to depend almost entirely on that feature, so that quietly rewriting the case without it, while leaving in place everything the author says is doing the work, deflates the intuition completely. Two instances. First: a speaker argues that some practice is intolerable by telling the story of one named child it harms, complete with the child's morning routine and aspirations; pressed on the argument, the speaker insists the story is mere illustration and that everything rests on the bare statistics of expected harm — but when the audience is given the statistics alone, its verdict noticeably softens. Second: in a dispute about whether a perfectly reconstructed copy of a person would be the person, one side describes the copying process in the vocabulary of annihilation, the other in the vocabulary of going under anesthesia and waking up; both insist that only the facts about continuity matter and that their choice of description is neutral, yet each audience finds the corresponding verdict obvious.

Note what the pattern is not: it isn't just any thought experiment built to provoke an intuition (too broad), nor a general point about how presentation affects judgment (too broad, and pitched at the wrong level — this concerns a detail whose irrelevance is conceded within the argument itself), nor the simple use of evaluatively charged words, since the work can be done by narrative structure or selection of detail without any loaded vocabulary. Nor is it automatically a vice: sometimes the vivid-but-irrelevant feature is used openly, precisely in order to argue that it shouldn't matter. What I want identified is the configuration where the bracketed feature silently carries the persuasive weight. If some existing label fits, say which fits best and exactly where the pattern diverges from each neighboring concept; if nothing fits, coin a term with a definition and boundary cases, including how to tell this apart from a detail that turns out to be genuinely relevant after all. A bare name won't satisfy me — I want the carving.

Please also say what would change your verdict: what evidence would show there is already a well-established term I've missed, or that the pattern collapses into one of its neighbors. End with a final line of the form \answer{label — established / nearest-fit / coined}.
