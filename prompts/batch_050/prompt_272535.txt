I sit on the research ethics committee of a university medical center. Over the past two years we have begun receiving protocols that involve reading or modifying brain activity: consumer EEG devices used to monitor attention, trials of adaptive deep brain stimulation, and studies that decode intended speech from motor-cortex signals in paralyzed patients. Last year the committee adopted a short charter of what it calls neural rights, organized around four protections: cognitive liberty (persons must remain free to use, refuse, or discontinue interventions targeting their own minds), mental privacy (information about a person's mental states must not be extracted or shared without consent), mental integrity (interventions must not impair a person's mental functioning against their interests), and psychological continuity (interventions must not disrupt a person's sense of being the same self over time). The charter is two pages of aspirational prose and has proven nearly unusable in review meetings. Members invoke whichever clause supports their intuition, and we have approved and rejected similar protocols on inconsistent grounds.

Please turn the charter into a review procedure we can actually run. The committee has ten members with mixed backgrounds (clinicians, a lawyer, a philosopher, community representatives), meets monthly, and can devote roughly twenty minutes of structured discussion per protocol. Specify: the questions the assigned reviewer should ask of each protocol, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; at which questions an unsatisfactory answer ends the review with a rejection and at which it triggers escalation to the full committee; and which questions the procedure deliberately declines to settle because they require judgment that cannot be proceduralized. Where one of the four protections is genuinely ambiguous in application, say so rather than papering over it, and identify which distinctions in the charter must survive translation into procedure and which can safely be dropped.

Then apply the procedure to three protocols: (i) a study fitting medical residents with consumer EEG headbands that stream attention data to the device vendor's servers, with participation nominally voluntary but strongly encouraged by the residency director; (ii) a trial of adaptive deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression, in which earlier patients' families reported the patients seemed changed in ways the patients themselves welcomed; (iii) a study decoding attempted speech from motor-cortex implants in locked-in patients, where the decoding pipeline cannot reliably distinguish intended communication from private inner speech and will inevitably capture some of both. Flag any case where the procedure yields a verdict the committee will dislike, and state plainly what the procedure cannot do.
