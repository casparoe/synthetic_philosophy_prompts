I would like you to construct a thought experiment to specifications, and then demonstrate that it works. The topic is the puzzle of imaginative resistance: the observation, familiar from discussions of fiction and imagination, that readers will imagine almost any empirical falsehood an author asks them to — faster-than-light travel, a society that finds knife wounds trivially survivable, a world where lead floats on water — yet balk when a story flatly asserts, as true in the fiction, a moral judgment they take to be false, such as a narrator's calm pronouncement that an act of gratuitous cruelty was right. The puzzle is why moral falsity in fiction generates resistance that empirical falsity does not.

Your task:

1. Construct a short fictional scenario, no more than one paragraph, engineered so that a typical competent reader will (i) readily imagine, as true in the story, a wildly false empirical claim, and (ii) strain against, or outright refuse, imagining as true in the story a moral claim that is no more obviously false in actuality. The constraints: the two claims must occupy structurally identical roles in the story (each asserted by the same narrator, at the same level of explicitness, with the same narrative authority); the story must contain no signal of an unreliable narrator, no irony, and no genre conventions — magic, science fiction, fable — that would let the reader defuse the empirical claim by relocating it into a world where it is true after all. The asymmetry must arise from the content of the claims, not from any cue about how to read them.

2. Demonstrate that the case meets each specification: state the empirical claim and the moral claim, describe the expected reader reaction to each, and explain why the reaction is asymmetric despite the structural parity.

3. Diagnose what is doing the work. In particular, adjudicate between two explanations: first, that moral falsity in fiction produces a distinctive, sui generis resistance that empirical falsity does not; second, that the asymmetry reduces to impossibility — the moral claim, if genuinely true in the fiction, would describe an impossible world, and we cannot imagine impossibilities — whereas the empirical claim merely describes a nomologically impossible but metaphysically possible world.

4. Then stress-test the impossibility explanation against the observation that readers happily imagine fictions whose contents are arguably flatly impossible — time-travel stories with grandfather paradoxes, stories with inconsistent mathematics. If impossible fictions can be imagined, impossibility alone cannot explain the resistance. Say whether your constructed case helps locate the residual explanation — for instance, in the reader's unwillingness to take up an evaluative perspective, or in fiction's convention that moral claims are exportable to the actual world while empirical claims are quarantined — and identify which single feature of your case is load-bearing, such that removing it would collapse the asymmetry.

5. Conclude with the strongest objection a defender of the impossibility view could press against your case, and your assessment of whether the case survives it.
