For the past year I have been part of an interdisciplinary project (two philosophers, a logician, and several legal scholars) trying to build a formal framework for counterfactual reasoning about specific individuals -- the sort of reasoning courts use when they say that had the supplier delivered on time, this very plaintiff would have fulfilled the contract. Almost immediately the two philosophers deadlocked, and they have not moved since. One insists that when we evaluate what would have happened to the plaintiff, it is the plaintiff himself -- numerically the same person -- who figures in the counterfactual scenario; we simply stipulate that we are talking about him under different circumstances, the way Kripke says we point at Nixon and ask what might have happened to that very man. The other insists, following Lewis, that nothing exists in more than one possible world: strictly speaking the plaintiff inhabits only the actual world, and it is a counterpart of him -- a sufficiently similar person in a sufficiently similar world -- whose timely delivery settles the counterfactual. She adds that this is a feature, not a bug, since it dissolves the puzzle of how one and the same thing could have incompatible properties like actually-late and possibly-on-time.

What frustrates me is that neither side can land a blow. The transworld-identity philosopher presses the charge that nobody cares, when wondering whether they could have been a doctor, about what happens to some merely similar person -- the counterpart theorist replies that de re modal representation via counterparts captures everything legitimate in that concern, and that the residue is just sentiment about the words "himself" and "his." The counterpart theorist demands a criterion of transworld identity that doesn't collapse into arbitrary stipulation; her opponent replies that stipulation is all we ever needed, since possible worlds are not foreign countries we discover with a telescope. Meanwhile the logician on the project keeps insisting the whole fight is notational, since anything provable in one framework can apparently be mirrored in the other, and has threatened to translate both sides' claims into a single neutral formalism to prove it. My own tentative hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about the metaphysics of worlds at all but about which ordinary-language intuitions get to count as data to be preserved and which get classified as loose talk to be explained away -- but I could be wrong, and I may just be redescribing the impasse rather than diagnosing it.

So here is what I want: a diagnosis, not a verdict. Using what each side actually says (feel free to reconstruct their best arguments rather than the versions I've sketched), tell me what kind of disagreement this is -- substantive, verbal, a clash of starting intuitions, a methodological dispute about the data of metaphysics, or two parties answering different questions under one heading -- and engage with the logician's intertranslatability claim specifically: does formal equivalence settle anything, or can two expressively equivalent theories still disagree? Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a case where the two frameworks deliver different verdicts on the same de re modal claim, a principled criterion for whose intuitions count as data, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed. Finally, a practical coda: for the applied legal project, does it actually matter which ontology we adopt, and if not, what does that indifference show about the debate? Please write your answer in American English.
