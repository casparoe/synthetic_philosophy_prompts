For a workshop on the badness of death, I have been asked to circulate a short statement of my own position so the other participants can stress-test it. Here is the statement:

1. Hedonism about welfare: how well a person's life goes for them is exhausted by the pleasure it contains and the suffering it avoids.
2. Deprivationism about death: when death is bad for the one who dies, it is bad because it deprives them of future life that would have gone well for them; the relevant comparison is with the life they would have led had they not died then.
3. An experience requirement: nothing can be good or bad for a person unless it makes a difference, at some time, to how things are experientially for that person.
4. Verdict: an unanticipated, painless death in one's sleep can be a grave misfortune for the victim.
5. Verdict: the badness of a death scales with the amount and quality of future life lost, regardless of the victim's own attitude toward dying.
6. Verdict: the Lucretian symmetry argument fails; not having been born earlier is no misfortune at all, whereas dying early often is one.

Please audit this set for coherence. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and which pairs merely appear to conflict but can be reconciled on inspection? For each genuine tension, indicate how severe it is, what the cheapest revision that restores consistency would be, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Finally, tell me which single change would do the most to clean up the position as a whole.
