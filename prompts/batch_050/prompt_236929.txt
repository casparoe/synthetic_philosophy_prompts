My reading group spent last month on the ethics of blame -- in particular on when someone lacks the standing to blame others for wrongs they themselves are connected to. My friend Priya has developed strong intuitions on a series of cases, and I want to figure out what rule she's implicitly running, so I'm writing down her verdicts and testing them against new scenarios. Her verdicts so far: (1) A CEO who quietly embezzled company funds last year has no business sternly rebuking a mid-level employee caught doing the same -- the rebuke itself is objectionable even though everything the CEO says is true. (2) A reformed shoplifter who stole compulsively as a teenager, was never caught, but has since made anonymous restitution and now volunteers counseling at-risk youth, is fully entitled to condemn a neighbor's ongoing shoplifting. (3) A senator who cheated on her taxes a decade ago, confessed publicly, paid back everything with penalties, and built her subsequent career on fiscal transparency, may blame a current opponent for tax fraud -- and Priya adds that it matters that the confession preceded any accusation. (4) A general who ordered a war crime and feels no guilt about it is in no position to denounce a foreign general for an identical order, even if the denunciation would do good. (5) A parent who smokes two packs a day can still warn their teenager against smoking, because the warning is advice rather than blame and involves no claim of moral superiority.

Now consider this case: Marcus has never once stolen anything in his life, but his circumstances have never made stealing tempting -- he has always been comfortable. In an honest moment, he tells friends that if he'd grown up in the poverty and chaos his cousin Danny grew up in, he'd almost certainly have become a thief too, just as Danny did. When Danny is caught stealing, Marcus angrily berates him as a disgrace to the family. Does Marcus have the standing to blame Danny? I'm fairly sure two different rules both fit Priya's five verdicts -- one on which standing depends only on one's actual record of wrongdoing, and one on which it also depends on one's counterfactual or dispositional character -- and that they diverge here. I'd like you to (a) spell out the candidate rules that fit the verdicts, making sure each genuinely covers all five; (b) say what each predicts about Marcus; (c) give your best guess about which verdict Priya would deliver, with your reasons; and (d) name one further case, distinct from Marcus's, that would most efficiently discriminate among the rules you take most seriously.
