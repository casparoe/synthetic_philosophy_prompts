I'm a journalist who covers science and philosophy of mind, and I'm writing a long piece on the ethical implications of panpsychism — the view, treated by its proponents as a serious candidate solution to the hard problem, that experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of matter. At a workshop last month, a philosopher I interviewed sketched the argument below, half-seriously, and it has been bothering me since, because each step seems defensible on panpsychist assumptions while the conclusion is one nobody in the room was willing to accept. I'd like your help diagnosing it for the article.

Please answer as a professional philosopher with a strong footing in both philosophy of mind and normative ethics. Think carefully before settling on a verdict: I'd like you to first sketch, briefly, the two or three steps you consider the most plausible candidates for the failure point, and only then commit to a diagnosis. Here is the argument, as I've reconstructed it from my notes:

(1) Suppose panpsychism is true: some form of experience is present wherever there is matter, down to the elementary processes described by fundamental physics.

(2) Experience, wherever it occurs, admits of valence — of going better or worse for the subject — because valence is not an extra ingredient added by brains but a basic feature of what experience is. So even the simplest microphysical events involve a faint positive or negative quality for the experiencer.

(3) The badness of suffering does not depend on the intelligence, complexity, or narrative self-awareness of the sufferer; a simple pain is bad in proportion to how bad it feels, not in proportion to the sophistication of the being who feels it.

(4) The number of microphysical events in any macroscopic region is astronomically larger than the number of macroscopic experiencers. A single cubic meter of rock or air contains more of these events, per second, than there have been seconds since the Big Bang.

(5) Moral value aggregates: a sufficiently vast number of tiny bads can outweigh a small number of large bads, just as vast numbers of tiny goods can outweigh a few large ones.

(6) Therefore the moral weight of the microphysical world swamps everything we ordinarily care about. The suffering of every human and animal alive is a rounding error compared to what is happening, moment by moment, in the walls of the room, and an ethics that took panpsychism seriously would be almost exclusively concerned with the physics of ordinary matter rather than with sentient creatures.

I cannot accept (6), but I also can't see which step a panpsychist is entitled to reject without giving up something that motivated the view in the first place. If I had to protect some premises, I'd most want to keep (1), since it's the hypothesis my article is about, and (3), since denying it looks like special pleading for complex minds. I'm less attached to the rest.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Name the step you would reject, explain why it looked plausible — I want to be fair to my interviewee — and tell me what rejecting it costs: which commitments a panpsychist must abandon or revise to take that exit, and whether the exit is also available to non-panpsychists. If you think the argument is actually sound, say so, and tell me what accepting (6) would concretely require.

Finally, please be explicit about your epistemic state: state how confident you are in your diagnosis (roughly, on a percentage scale), and single out the one step or claim in your own answer where you feel least sure of yourself, with a sentence on why.
