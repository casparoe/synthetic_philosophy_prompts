I have no formal education in philosophy, but for years I have been privately preoccupied with the question of why there is something rather than nothing. Last weekend, over dinner at my place, two friends of mine — neither of them trained in the subject either — fell into an argument about it, and I listened without taking a side. I have reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can, and I would like you to referee it.

(1) Elena opened by saying the question is the one problem every worldview fails. Everything we encounter is contingent and explained by something else that exists: matter by fields, fields by laws, and if you reach for God you have merely added one more existent whose presence itself cries out for explanation. In her view, the regress can only end in a state of nothing, because nothing is the one condition that asks for no explanation. So why reality contains anything at all is a genuine question that has no answer.

(2) Marcus replied that Elena's regress-ending move quietly assumes that nothingness is the default condition of reality — one genuine option among others, and the only one that comes for free. But nothingness, he said, is not a scenario at all; there is no empty arena sitting there, waiting either to be filled or to be left empty. Worse, nothing is not even the limit of less and less: picture deleting the contents of reality one item at a time — the final deletion is unlike all the previous ones, because it does not merely remove the last object but also removes space, time, laws, and possibilities themselves. So the comparison "something versus nothing" never gets off the ground, and the question is malformed rather than deep.

(3) Elena conceded that nothingness might not be a coherent scenario but said this cuts in her favor. Suppose there were nothing at all; then it would be true — it would be a fact — that nothing exists, and a fact is not nothing. The supposition defeats itself, so nothing is impossible, and the question "why is there something?" turns out to have an answer after all: there had to be something, because there is no alternative.

(4) Marcus answered that this argument trades on an ambiguity in the word "true." We, situated inside a world rich with facts, can truly describe a nothing-scenario as containing nothing; but that scenario itself contains no facts and no truth-bearers, nothing in which a truth could consist. Describing an empty box does not put the description inside the box. He added that even if the argument worked, it would be a hollow victory by Elena's own lights: she had wanted to know why this contingent world exists, and "nothing is impossible" at most establishes that something or other must exist — it says nothing about why the something has the particular character it does, which was the itch she started with.

At that point dessert arrived and the conversation moved on, but I have been stuck on it ever since. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four numbered moves, say whether it succeeds, partially succeeds, or fails, and give your reasoning in a sentence or two. Then tell me three things: (a) whether the original question genuinely depends on treating nothingness as a default, as Marcus claims; (b) whether Elena's self-defeat argument about facts in a nothing-world survives Marcus's empty-box reply; and (c) what, if anything, both of them are missing. Conclude with your assessment of who is more right overall, stated as a final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: [Elena / Marcus / even split]
