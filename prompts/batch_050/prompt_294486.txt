I am helping design a governance charter for a general-purpose AI assistant deployed across many countries, religions, and subcultures. One faction on the design team proposes a principle they call symmetric experimentation: the assistant should help each competent user pursue that user’s own conception of a good life, while enforcing only the minimum cross-cultural rules needed to prevent coercion, domination, deception, and serious harm to third parties. When users’ values conflict, the system should prefer option-preserving compromises over averaging everyone’s preferences into a single social utility function. Another faction objects that this supposedly liberal neutrality is an illusion: deciding which harms are serious, which choices are competent, and which compromises preserve options already smuggles in the developers’ moral theory. They also worry that leaving communities free to experiment may merely empower local majorities to stifle dissidents, while a highly persuasive AI could manufacture the very preferences it claims neutrally to serve.

The debate has turned on this passage from Chapter III of John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty: “That mankind are not infallible; that their truths, for the most part, are only half-truths; that unity of opinion, unless resulting from the fullest and freest comparison of opposite opinions, is not desirable, and diversity not an evil, but a good, until mankind are much more capable than at present of recognising all sides of the truth, are principles applicable to men’s modes of action, not less than to their opinions. As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them.”

Write an essay evaluating whether symmetric experimentation is the right value-aggregation principle for such an AI. Interpret Mill’s argument carefully rather than treating “experiments of living” as a slogan, and consider whether his epistemic case for diversity supports an AI that remains neutral among users’ ideals or instead an AI that actively protects and cultivates individual self-development. Address the strongest objections concerning adaptive preferences, conflicts among experiments, vulnerable minorities, future people, and the impossibility of wholly neutral infrastructure. Compare symmetric experimentation with both a single global utility function and simple deference to the immediate user. Reach a definite conclusion about which principle the design team should adopt, even if your verdict involves substantial qualifications. Please write in American English.
