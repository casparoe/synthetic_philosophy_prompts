You are a philosopher specializing in rational choice theory and the normativity of practical reasoning, with particular expertise in diachronic rationality. I am preparing a position statement for a workshop and would like a rigorous consistency audit before I circulate it. Please think carefully and methodically: first enumerate the candidate pairs of commitments that might pull against each other, then assess each, and only then deliver your final verdict.

Here is the setup my statement assumes. Consider three options A, B, and C, and an agent whose preferences run B strictly preferred to A, C strictly preferred to B, and A strictly preferred to C. A dealer offers, in sequence: swap A for B at a small fee; then swap B for C at a small fee; then swap C for A at a small fee. An agent who accepts all three ends up where she began, holding A, minus three fees — a strictly dominated result. That is the money pump argument's standard case against cyclic preferences.

My commitments are these:

1. Cyclic preferences over options that vary along multiple dimensions can be fully rational. There is nothing defective about the pattern B≻A, C≻B, A≻C as such; pairwise comparisons can each track a legitimate dimension without the cycle registering any mistake.

2. Nevertheless, an agent who actually completes the pump — accepting all three swaps and finishing with her original holding minus the fees — has gone wrong rationally, not merely prudentially. I want this verdict to hold even when the offers arrive one at a time as genuine surprises, so that the agent never foresaw the sequence as a whole.

3. What blocks the inference from (2) to the irrationality of cyclic preferences is sophisticated choice: a rational agent with such preferences who does foresee the sequence will decline the first swap, anticipating where acceptance leads. So the preferences themselves never get a rational agent pumped; foresight severs the link the pump argument needs.

4. Practical rationality is synchronic through and through. Whether a choice is rational is wholly determined by the agent's beliefs, evidence, and preferences at the moment of choice. There are no irreducibly diachronic requirements: no standalone norms of plan-stability, intention retention, or cross-temporal consistency.

5. A mere liability to exploitation is not a rational defect. That some possible sequence of offers would leave an agent worse off than she started shows only a prudential vulnerability, not any mistake in her preferences or her choices.

My questions: Do these five commitments hang together? For each pair you judge to be in genuine tension, state the tension precisely, explain why the usual motivations for the two commitments cannot be jointly satisfied, and rate its severity. For each pair whose tension is merely apparent, say what distinction dissolves it. Then identify the single cheapest revision — the smallest change to one commitment that restores consistency — and state what it costs me in terms of positions I can no longer hold. I am not attached to any particular commitment, so do not pull punches, but do not flag everything either: I want a discriminating audit, not a list of every conceivable friction.
