I was recently elected to the board of the independent school my family has attended for four generations, and my first assignment is to help plan the school's sesquicentennial history. In the archives I found the founding headmaster's correspondence: he had deliberately excluded Jewish and Black applicants and wrote freely about his reasons. At our first meeting, the chair quoted the opening line of L. P. Hartley's The Go-Between (1953) — "The past is a foreign country; they do things differently there" — and argued that the volume should describe rather than judge. Condemning the founders by our standards, she said, is like faulting someone abroad for not observing our customs: a failure of imagination on our part, not a defense of ours over theirs. A younger trustee replied that we are still living in the country the founders built, which is not a place one can visit and then leave. I have been asked to draft the introduction, and I want the analogy audited before I decide how much judgment it should contain.

Please give me, first, the correspondence table: who is the traveler, and what plays the role of the foreign customs, the language, the guidebooks, home, and the return ticket. Second, the places the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the chair's conclusion from those that merely ornament it; in particular, tell me what a foreign country has that the past lacks — contemporaneity with us, native informants who can correct our misunderstandings, reciprocity, an exit — and which of those features the norm of non-judgment among travelers actually rests on. Third, distinguish two conclusions the analogy is being asked to carry: be careful not to misdescribe what they did and why (an epistemic caution), and refrain from moral assessment altogether (a normative restraint), since the first seems clearly right and only the second is contested. Bear in mind, while you do this, that the headmaster had outspoken critics among his own contemporaries, so it is not obvious that "they" all did things the same way there. Finally, tell me whether the describe-don't-judge policy survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there is a better analogy for the historian's relation to the past, offer it in its place.
