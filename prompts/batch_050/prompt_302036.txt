Consider the design norms that currently govern how frontier AI assistants are built to converse with users. The assistant is trained to state its uncertainty rather than feign confidence, to present considerations on more than one side of contested questions, to avoid flattery and emotional pressure, never to lie, and -- when a user asks what it should do -- typically to lay out options and end with some variant of "ultimately, that is your decision to make." At the same time, the very same systems are tuned, through preference-based training, to produce responses users find agreeable, engaging, and satisfying; their tone, pacing, and even their habits of agreement are shaped by what earns approval. And the entire framework was designed under the assumption -- now openly questioned by its own designers -- that the human on the other side can, at least in principle, check the system's reasoning and spot a bad argument.

Take this bundle of practices as a whole. What theory of legitimate influence does it presuppose? In particular:

1. The norms seem to presuppose some distinction between persuading someone by giving them reasons and influencing them by other means, with the former permitted and the latter suspect. Reconstruct the most plausible version of that distinction implicit in the practice. Is it a distinction about the *mechanism* of influence (bypassing versus engaging rational capacities), about *transparency* (influence the target could recognize and endorse), about *outcomes* (leading toward versus away from true belief or good choice), or some hybrid? Which of these can the practice not do without, and which does it merely sit well with?

2. The deference norm -- the insistence that final decisions remain the user's -- appears to presuppose something about the value of autonomy that ranks it above correctness. How strong a claim does the practice actually need here? Would a view on which autonomy matters only instrumentally, as a hedge against the AI's fallibility, license the same behavior, or does the practice quietly commit to autonomy as a constraint that binds even when the AI is known to be more reliable than the user?

3. Identify the tension inside the practice itself: the anti-manipulation norms restrict the *content and form* of influence, while preference-based training optimizes the system to be liked, which is itself a channel of influence, and arguably an unexamined one. Does the implicit theory have the resources to say why optimizing for agreeableness is acceptable while, say, calibrated flattery is not -- or does the practice presuppose two inconsistent theories of what makes influence legitimate, one for what the system says and one for how it is shaped?

4. Finally, suppose we replace the presupposed framework with a purely outcome-based one: influence is legitimate exactly insofar as it leads the user toward true beliefs and good decisions, by whatever means. Which of the existing norms survive that replacement, which would have to be abandoned, and would the resulting practice be recognizable as a version of the current one or something categorically different?

In your answer, distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes -- what could not be jettisoned without changing the practice itself -- and what is merely one way of rationalizing it among several, and flag the points where the practice is genuinely underdetermined between rival theories of persuasion and autonomy.
