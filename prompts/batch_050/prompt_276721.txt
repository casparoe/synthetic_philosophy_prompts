Is there an established name for the following move, or is it just garden-variety hypocrisy? I got into an argument about this with a coworker and realized I don't have a word for the exact thing that bugs me. The pattern: a region inside a country wants independence, and its whole case rests on the idea that the people living there should get to decide their own future by a vote. Fine, that's a principle I can take seriously. But then someone asks the awkward follow-up: okay, suppose you leave -- what if a corner of your territory, an island group or a valley, votes overwhelmingly to stay with the old country? Does that corner get to stay, i.e. secede from your new state in turn? And the striking thing is that the independence movement almost always answers no -- our territory is a single unit, its borders are inviolable, the vote binds everyone inside them. Which is word for word the argument the parent country was making against *them* a moment earlier. Quebec is the cleanest case I know: in the run-up to the 1995 referendum the Cree and Inuit communities up north made very clear they wanted to remain in Canada, and the sovereigntist response was basically that Quebec's territory was indivisible. And you see the same shape elsewhere -- during the Scottish debate there was real talk in Shetland and Orkney about staying with the UK if Scotland left, and after the Catalan push there was that half-joking Tabarnia thing, except the Val d'Aran is an actual place with its own language and its own distinct status, so it wasn't entirely a joke.

So my question: does this specific configuration have a name of its own? I don't mean hypocrisy or special pleading in general -- I mean the recursive thing, where the principle invoked at level one gets denied at level two, by the same people, using the same words they just rejected. I suspect it's adjacent to the more general puzzle of who gets to be "the people" that decides in the first place, so if there's literature on that I'd like to know how this particular move fits into it. And if there's genuinely no established term, coin one, but do it properly -- give me a definition that separates it from plain inconsistency, and some boundary cases so I can tell where it stops applying. Like: is it still the same phenomenon if the sub-region's majority was arguably engineered by decades of settlement policy? What if the sub-region isn't contiguous and would leave an enclave? What if the parent country cheerfully supports the sub-region's right to stay purely to sabotage the secession, which it obviously does sometimes -- does the bad faith on that side change anything? One more thing: if my description of any of these cases is off, or if the whole framing rests on a confusion (maybe international law already settles the recursive question and I'm just ignorant of it), say so plainly rather than politely working around it.
