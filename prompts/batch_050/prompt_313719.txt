In my philosophy of AI seminar we recently covered the distinction between goal-directed agents, which optimize for outcomes in the world, and approval-directed agents, which choose whichever action they predict an overseer would rate most highly. In discussion, a classmate argued that approval-directed training is nothing to worry about because it is simply how we raise children: a child initially behaves in order to win parental approval and only later, having internalized the parents' values, acts well when nobody is watching. Her conclusion was that concerns about approval-directed AI reduce to concerns about moral education, which we already know how to manage.

I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear. Please audit it: give the mapping explicitly (what corresponds to the child, the parent, the approval, and the moment of internalization), then identify which disanalogies actually matter for her conclusion. In particular, address whether an approval-directed system ever passes through anything like the internalization stage; what incentives it has in situations where it can tell that the overseer cannot distinguish genuinely good behavior from merely good-looking behavior; and the fact that children are expected to become independent adults who outgrow oversight, whereas the overseer here is meant to stay in the loop permanently. Separate these from disanalogies that are merely superficial. Finally, say whether her conclusion survives the audit, and if the child-rearing picture fails, propose a more accurate everyday analogy for what approval-directed training is really doing. Please write your answer in American English.
