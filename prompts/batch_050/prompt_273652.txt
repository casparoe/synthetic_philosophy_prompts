I spent my career as an experimental biologist -- marine invertebrate sensory systems, mostly -- and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind, in particular the attempts to naturalize mental content. One problem keeps nagging at me, and I would like you to construct a thought experiment that puts it under the microscope. Here is the setup as I understand it. Purely informational or causal theories of content struggle with the fact that any neural state co-varies with many things at once: the frog's famous prey-detection circuit fires at flies, but equally at small dark moving specks, at nutritious items, at shadows of a certain size. Teleosemantics promises to break the tie by appeal to what the mechanism was selected for. What I want to know is whether that promise survives a case built precisely to strain it.

So: construct for me a thought experiment meeting all of the following specifications.

First, the case must involve a single perceptual or neural state in a single organism, and it must be biologically realistic -- a hypothetical species is fine, even preferable, but the physiology and the ecology should be plausible to a working scientist. No Swampman-style lightning-strike duplicates, no Twin Earth, no miracles, no stipulated content.

Second, engineer the case so that two candidate contents for the state are genuinely coextensive in the organism's actual environment, yet come apart in the following specific way: the state reliably carries information about F, but the historical selection pressure that explains why the mechanism exists and persists was exerted through G, where F and G happen to coincide in the actual environment without being the same property. I want the coincidence to be doing real work, not to be decorative.

Third, and this is the crux, the case should be built so that a selection-historical teleosemantics and an informational or asymmetric-dependence theory deliver different contents for the very same state, and neither verdict is obviously absurd. If your construction lets one theory answer trivially while the other flounders, it has failed.

Then do the demonstration. State the organism, the mechanism, and the selection history explicitly. Give each theory's verdict and show, step by step, that the verdict follows from the theory's actual commitments rather than from your having stipulated it. Identify the single load-bearing feature of the case -- the feature that keeps F and G coextensive where they need to be and apart where they need to be -- and tell me what happens to each theory's verdict if that feature is removed. Finally, play defense attorney for whichever theory comes out looking worse: how would its proponent attack the case, and is the attack a principled move or an admission that the theory cannot do what it advertised?

Some context for what the case is for: I lead a small reading group of fellow retirees, scientists all, and we are working through the naturalized-content literature. I want a case I can present to them that forces the question of whether teleosemantics genuinely resolves the indeterminacy that defeats informational theories, or merely relocates it one level up -- into questions about what, exactly, selection selects for. Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or two; the analysis can be as long as it needs to be.
