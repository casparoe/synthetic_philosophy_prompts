I'm preparing a short position statement on infinity for a philosophy of mathematics reading group, and before I circulate it I'd like a consistency audit of the commitments I plan to defend. Here is what I currently hold:

1. Finitism about the physical world. The universe contains only finitely many physical things and events; nothing infinite is ever physically instantiated. Appeals to actual infinity in physics are at best convenient idealizations.

2. Platonism about the mathematical infinite, including Cantor. The diagonal argument is a straightforwardly valid proof, and its conclusion should be read literally: the real numbers genuinely outnumber the natural numbers, and both infinities exist as completed totalities, in whatever sense any mathematical objects exist.

3. The moral of Hilbert's hotel. Infinite sets behave strangely but are fully coherent. When intuitions trained on finite collections — like "a full hotel cannot accommodate another guest" — clash with the mathematics of the infinite, the intuitions simply lose, wholesale. The lesson of such thought experiments is to retire those intuitions, not to restrict the mathematics.

4. The Euclidean common notion that the whole is greater than any of its proper parts is a self-evident constraint on any legitimate notion of size or quantity. Galileo's observation that the perfect squares can be paired one-to-one with the natural numbers does not show that this principle fails; it shows only that we have not yet settled on the correct way to measure the sizes of infinite collections. A satisfactory theory of infinite size must be one on which the natural numbers come out strictly more numerous than the squares.

5. Literalism about the continuum in physics. General relativity models spacetime as a continuum, and I take our best physics at face value: spacetime points are real, and there are as many of them as there are real numbers. This is not, in my view, a mere modeling convenience.

6. Determinacy of the continuum hypothesis. There is one real set-theoretic universe, and the continuum hypothesis is either true or false of it; the independence results show only that our current axioms are incomplete, not that the question lacks an answer.

My questions: Which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which pairs merely appear to be but dissolve on inspection? Please order any genuine tensions by severity, and for each one say what exactly would have to give — the least costly revision that restores consistency, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the position. Finally, if a single revision would relieve more than one tension at once, identify it. I am not attached to any particular commitment here, so please do not pull punches, but also do not manufacture tensions: I want to know which of my views actually constrain each other.
