**Exam question (10 points):** State the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. Be explicit about why it is a problem for expressivism in particular, rather than for non-cognitivist or anti-realist views generally. Then describe one expressivist response to the problem and present one objection to that response.

**Student's answer:**

Expressivism holds that moral sentences do not describe moral facts; uttering "lying is wrong" expresses a non-cognitive attitude, such as disapproval, toward lying. Since the sentence's meaning is exhausted by the attitude it expresses, it is not in the business of being true or false.

The Frege–Geach problem is that moral sentences occur not only asserted on their own but also embedded in larger sentences, and expressivists cannot say what they mean there. Consider: "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong. Lying is wrong. So getting your little brother to lie is wrong." This argument is valid, and its validity requires that "lying is wrong" mean the same thing in the antecedent of the first premise and as the second premise standing alone. But in the conditional, the speaker does not express disapproval of lying — someone entertaining the conditional might approve of lying. Since the expressivist identifies the sentence's meaning with the attitude it expresses, and no attitude is expressed in the antecedent, the sentence changes meaning between occurrences and the argument commits the fallacy of equivocation. Strictly speaking, then, the problem is that conditionals cannot contain sentences lacking truth values: a conditional's antecedent must be capable of truth or falsity, and on expressivism "lying is wrong" is not.

The best-known reply is Blackburn's. He holds that uttering the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude: roughly, disapproval of accepting the antecedent while refusing to accept the consequent. Anyone who accepts both premises but rejects the conclusion thereby disapproves of their own combination of attitudes; their moral sensibility is internally incoherent. This restores the argument's validity: since no one can accept the premises and reject the conclusion without condemning their own attitudes, the conclusion must be accepted by anyone who accepts the premises — which is just what validity consists in.

One objection is that Blackburn's account ties the inference to the attitudes of whoever is reasoning. Two speakers who share no moral attitudes can both accept the conditional without incoherence, so the account seems to make a matter of logic depend on a contingent moral sensibility. Logic, the objection says, should hold independently of anyone's attitudes.
