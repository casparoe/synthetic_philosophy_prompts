I am preparing to lead a single eighty-minute seminar for first-year undergraduates on the paradox of fiction -- the question of how we can feel genuine emotions for characters and events we fully believe do not exist. The students will have done no prior reading in aesthetics or philosophy of emotion, and I cannot assume they have ever encountered the idea that emotions carry cognitive commitments. Plan the seminar rather than delivering it. Specifically, I want:

First, the explanatory arc. The paradox has the structure of an inconsistent triad -- we seem to have real emotions for fictional characters, such emotions ordinarily require believing their objects exist, and we do not believe fictional characters exist -- and I want the session to make each claim feel individually irresistible before the inconsistency is sprung. Lay out the order in which the pieces must be introduced, and for each step state what it depends on: in particular, where the cognitive-commitment claim about emotion must be established (and how, given that students will arrive thinking of emotions as mere feelings that need no justification), and why the triad must be assembled before any solution is even mentioned.

Second, the carrying example. Choose the one case the whole session is built around, state what it must have (the audience's full and unwavering awareness of fictionality, an emotion that persists on repeated exposure rather than decaying, no sensory trickery doing the work) and what it must lack, and explain why it should not be a horror-film fear case -- my worry is that jump-scares and startle responses give students an easy physicalist exit that bypasses the paradox entirely.

Third, the misconception map. Predict the objections in the order this audience will raise them -- I expect, roughly: that we temporarily half-believe the fiction is real, that the emotions are therefore not real emotions, that people are simply irrational and the paradox dissolves, and that identifying with the character explains everything (which seems to me to presuppose exactly what needs explaining). For each, indicate where in the arc it will predictably surface, whether to head it off in advance or let it arise, and what question or demonstration defuses it without prematurely settling the triad. Also tell me how to keep the session from sliding into the neighboring but distinct question of why anyone seeks out sad or frightening fiction at all.

Fourth, a control case to deploy mid-session: some contrast (a documentary about real suffering, or a vividly imagined hypothetical about a real person) that lets students isolate whether fictionality itself is doing the philosophical work, rather than distance or passivity.

Finally, an end-of-seminar check that would show they grasped the structure of the problem rather than merely acquired an opinion about it -- ideally a novel case they must diagnose as either generating the same triad or not -- and a note on what I should openly decline to cover at this level, including whether to adjudicate among the standard families of solutions or leave the triad unresolved on the table.
