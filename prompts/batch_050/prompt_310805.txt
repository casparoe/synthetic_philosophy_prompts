(1) Rewarding people based on a measurable proxy is completely fine in principle, as long as the proxy genuinely correlates with the thing you care about. Like if a company pays workers for hitting daily step counts because healthier staff take fewer sick days, and steps really do track health, I don't see a problem with the setup itself.
(2) When schools tie teacher pay or evaluations to standardized test score growth, the teaching-to-the-test and narrowing of the curriculum that follows isn't an implementation failure or a few bad apples -- it's the predictable result, and that's a reason the policy is a mistake even if the scores do normally correlate with learning.
(3) I'm less worried about this stuff at the individual level. I track my own habits (pages read, hours studied) and I trust myself to notice when I've started gaming my own metric instead of doing the real thing, so self-imposed metrics are basically safe.
(4) The right fix when a metric gets gamed isn't to abandon measurement, it's to use a basket of several diverse metrics so that gaming all of them at once is harder than just doing the real work.
(5) Some things genuinely can't be captured by any metric or rubric -- like whether an essay is actually insightful -- and forcing rubrics onto them actively makes them worse, so those domains should just be left to holistic judgment.

Tell me which pairs of these are in real tension and how bad it is in each case, and which apparent tensions actually dissolve if you look closer (I suspect at least one of mine only looks contradictory).
