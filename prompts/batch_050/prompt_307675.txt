The exam question as set (25 marks; candidates were asked to write roughly 350 words):

Sentences such as "It is raining, but I don't believe it is" are said to be Moore-paradoxical. In the material first published as Part II of Ludwig Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations (the section now printed as "Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment," section x), Wittgenstein responds to this puzzle with an observation to the following effect: an utterance of "I believe that p" in the first-person present tense is used in much the way the outright assertion that p is used; and yet to suppose or hypothesize that one believes that p is not at all the same as supposing or hypothesizing that p. In the same stretch of text he remarks that a verb meaning to believe falsely would have no significant first-person present indicative. (a) State the puzzle precisely: explain why the Moore-paradoxical sentence is not an outright contradiction, and say where its absurdity lies if not in its truth-conditions. (b) Set out Wittgenstein's diagnosis, making clear what work is done by the contrast between asserting that p and merely supposing that one believes that p. (c) Use the diagnosis to explain why no comparable absurdity attaches to "It was raining, but I didn't believe it was" or to "It is raining, but she doesn't believe it."

The candidate's answer:

Moore's puzzle concerns sentences like the one in the question. Such sentences are not contradictions, because belief is not factive: it can perfectly well be raining while I fail to believe that it is, so both conjuncts can be true together. The puzzle is that no one who understood the words could sincerely assert the sentence. The absurdity therefore attaches to the asserting of it rather than to what is asserted, which may for all that be true.

Wittgenstein's diagnosis is that in the first-person present tense "I believe that p" is used interchangeably with the assertion of p itself, usually as a somewhat tentative or hedged version of that assertion. On this reading, someone who utters the Moore-paradoxical sentence asserts that p and in the same breath declines to stand behind that very assertion. The absurdity is practical, not logical: the speaker is doing and undoing the same speech act at once, much as one would in saying "It is raining — no, I take that back."

The shifts of tense and person fall out naturally. In "It was raining, but I didn't believe it was," the belief-clause is an ordinary backward-looking report of a past state of mind, and in "It is raining, but she doesn't believe it," the clause describes another person's state, which the speaker is in a position to report. In neither case does the belief-clause double as an assertion about the weather, so nothing goes wrong.

A further consequence of Wittgenstein's account is that present-tense avowals of belief never describe the speaker at all: like a cry of pain, "I believe that p" gives verbal expression to something rather than stating a fact about an inner condition. This, I take it, is why he says a verb meaning to believe falsely could have no first-person present indicative. Where there is no report, there is nothing to get wrong: since an avowal makes no claim about the avower, the possibility of error does not arise, and one's present-tense avowals of belief cannot be mistaken.
