You are a philosopher of probability with deep expertise on the connection between rational credence and objective chance, preparing teaching materials for a graduate seminar. I need a self-locating flowchart that lets students sort themselves into a position on when, and why, rational credence should defer to objective chance, without the students needing to know the names of any positions in advance.

Build the flowchart from the logical space rather than from the literature: devise a sequence of diagnostic questions, each a concrete case phrased in plain language, on which theories of the chance–credence link genuinely come apart. Your questions must include the following cases, though you may sharpen or add to them: a crystal ball with a perfect track record shows that a genuinely chancy coin will land heads, while the chance of heads remains one-half; a demon reveals that the world is fully deterministic and hands you the complete microstate, raising the question whether any non-trivial chances survive; evidence that bears on an outcome only by way of the chance (long-run frequencies of a process) contrasted with evidence that bypasses the chance entirely (a reliable symptom of the outcome itself); and a chance distribution that assigns positive probability to the falsity of the very chance-theory that generated it.

The terminal positions your tree must reach include: Lewis-style deference restricted by an admissibility clause, on which crystal-ball information is simply inadmissible; the view that deference is constitutive of chance, so that anything not rationally deserving deference is not chance at all; the view that chance claims tacitly presuppose the absence of undermining information, so that a world containing the crystal ball has different chances than it appears to; an accuracy-first justification, on which deferring to chance is rational because it minimizes expected inaccuracy; and a deflationary position on which trusting chances is just ordinary defeasible induction with no special principle behind it.

Deliver the result as exactly five numbered items, one per terminal position, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should be two to four sentences stating (i) the path of answers through your diagnostic questions that leads to that position, written so a newcomer could follow it, (ii) the name of the position, and (iii) its single most awkward commitment. If two of the required positions cannot be separated by any well-formed diagnostic question, merge them into one item, say explicitly that the tree fails there and why, and use the freed slot for a combination of answers no one occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Think carefully about the full space of possible answer patterns before committing to your five items, but present only the final list.
