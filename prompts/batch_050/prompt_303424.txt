I am assembling the problem set for a graduate seminar on population ethics, specifically on cases that test whether a larger population of happy people is better than a smaller one. Below are the cases I already have. For each I include the structure I take it to have, so you can see what is covered:

1. The Repugnant Conclusion (Parfit): for any population of people with excellent lives, there is a possible, vastly larger population of people with lives barely worth living that ranks as better on total-welfare reasoning, since enough barely-positive lives can sum to more value than fewer wonderful ones.
2. The Mere Addition Paradox (Parfit): adding people with good lives, while leaving everyone else's welfare untouched, seems not to make things worse; iterating this and equalizing welfare leads step by step to the Repugnant Conclusion, so one of the apparently innocent steps must be rejected.
3. The Sadistic Conclusion (Arrhenius): some principles imply that it can be better to add people with lives of pure suffering than to add people with lives well worth living, because the sufferers drag the average down less than the happy-but-below-average newcomers would.
4. The Very Repugnant Conclusion (Arrhenius): at some population sizes, a slight drop in the welfare of the many can be compensated by adding ever more people with negative welfare, so a world mixing a few comfortable lives with multitudes of miserable ones outranks a smaller world of universally good lives.
5. The Non-Identity Problem (Parfit): a policy that predictably produces a worse-off future generation cannot be said to harm that generation, since a different policy would have caused different people to exist, and no one in the worse-off group can say they would have been better off otherwise.
6. The Procreation Asymmetry: we take ourselves to have strong reason not to bring a miserable child into existence but no corresponding reason to bring a happy child into existence, and most systematic principles either fail to recover this asymmetry or recover it only at the cost of implying that any life containing suffering should not have begun.

What I need is one further case that is genuinely new relative to these: not a relabeling of one of them, not a sorites-style chain of small additions or small welfare drops (structures 1, 2, and 4 have that covered), and not merely an instance of the general puzzle about whether existence can be better or worse for someone than nonexistence (structure 6). Ideally it should exploit some feature of population comparison that the list does not touch — for instance risk and uncertainty about who will exist, distribution across time rather than across persons, choices affecting a fixed set of people whose membership is known only probabilistically, or comparisons where one of the outcomes contains no people at all.

Present the case concretely enough that a seminar could argue about it (with made-up numbers if that helps), state precisely which intuition or principle it puts pressure on, and explain why it is not a special case of anything on my list. Then give me your honest assessment of whether it survives scrutiny — including the strongest way a defender of the targeted principle could defuse it.
