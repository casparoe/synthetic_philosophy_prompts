I run training and compliance for a mid-sized manufacturer, and we keep hitting the same wall: someone passes the written test on a procedure, signs the acknowledgment form, and then doesn't do the thing on the floor. Lately I've been reading about Wang Yangming's claim that knowledge and action are one -- roughly, that if you genuinely know something (that it matters, or how to do it), you act accordingly, and not acting just shows the knowledge was never really there, only borrowed secondhand. I want to take that seriously as a design principle rather than a slogan. Write me a procedure I can run whenever an incident review concludes "employee was trained on this": a sequence of questions for deciding whether the knowledge was incomplete (and what kind -- heard it as words, never practiced it under real conditions, something in the environment blocking it), and what evidence counts as real knowing versus knowing-in-quotes. Include the cases where the Wang Yangming frame clearly does not apply and I should stop diagnosing and just fix the incentive structure. Then run it on two examples: a tech who skips machine lockout steps when the line is backed up, and a manager who sincerely endorses our anti-harassment policy but keeps covering for a high performer who violates it. Flag where the procedure has to hand off to judgment. Also be honest about where this translation of Wang Yangming is most likely to break -- which step you'd least bet on, and what result or argument would make you scrap the whole approach.
