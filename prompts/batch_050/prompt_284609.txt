In my physics class we learned that the centrifugal force you feel in a turning car or on a spinning merry-go-round is not a real force at all but a "fictitious" one that only appears because your frame of reference is rotating. At the same time, our teacher told us about devices that seem to detect rotation without comparing anything to anything else: a gyrocompass settles on true north, Foucault's pendulum slowly changes its plane of swing, and astronauts in a windowless spinning station can tell they are rotating because objects drift sideways and they feel pulled outward. In the Scholium to the Definitions of his Principia, Isaac Newton describes a bucket of water hung from a twisted cord: the water's surface stays flat when the bucket spins but the water is still, and becomes concave only once the water itself is whirling, which Newton took to show that rotation can be absolute rather than merely relative to surrounding objects.

My question: what view of space does this whole practice presuppose? When physicists call centrifugal effects fictitious yet still treat rotation as something an enclosed observer can measure outright, are they committed to Newton's absolute space as a kind of container that bodies accelerate with respect to, or would a purely relational view -- on which rotation means rotation relative to the distant stars and all the matter in the universe -- license exactly the same practices? Please reconstruct the implicit metaphysics of how rotation is handled in ordinary physics, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what, if anything, would have to change if absolute space turned out not to exist. Please answer in American English.
