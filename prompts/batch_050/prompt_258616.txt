Bit of a weird one. I'm a software engineer (backend, no philosophy background) and I've somehow ended up in the middle of what seems to be a hundred-year-old argument, and I honestly can't work out what anyone in it is actually disagreeing about.

Context: last year I was writing some procedural generation code and needed to pick a "random" chord of a circle. Turns out there are at least three perfectly reasonable ways to do that. Pick two random points on the circumference and join them. Or pick a random radius, pick a random point along it, and draw the chord perpendicular there. Or pick a random point inside the circle and make it the chord's midpoint. Each one feels like THE uniform way of doing it until you try the others, and they genuinely give different answers — famously, for the probability that a random chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, you get 1/2, 1/3 or 1/4 depending on the method. I checked this in my own simulation and it definately holds up.

So I googled it, and apparently this is Bertrand's paradox, goes back to some French mathematician in the 1880s, and it was meant to kill something called the principle of indifference — the idea that if you've got no reason to favour any possibility over the others, you should assign them all equal probability.

Here's where it gets strange. From old lecture notes and forum threads and one scanned PDF from the 70s, there seem to be roughly three camps:

1. The "paradox is fatal" camp: there's no such thing as random, full stop. Every probability claim only makes sense relative to a specified method or measure, the principle of indifference is dead, and anyone still reaching for it is confused.

2. The "some problems have a right answer" camp: this physicist (Jaynes, I think?) argued that if the problem has genuine symmetries — your answer shouldn't change if you move the circle around the page or draw it bigger — then requiring invariance under those symmetries actually singles out one of the three methods (the 1/2 one, apparently), so for well-posed problems the paradox dissolves.

3. The "you're both confused" camp, who say probability is just about frequencies, or just about degrees of belief, so there's no fact of the matter about "the" random chord and the whole fight is a category error.

Now the thing that keeps bugging me: nobody in this argument disagrees about any of the maths. Everyone accepts the three methods give three different answers. Everyone accepts Jaynes's invariance calculation does what he says it does. And yet this has apparently been going for over a century, every argument is out in the open, and nobody has moved an inch. It feels exactly like certain arguments at work — like whether a bug is "in" our service or "in" the framework — where the facts are settled and the argument carries on anyway.

So my actual question is: what kind of disagreement is this? Some options I've been turning over:

- Is it secretly an empirical disagreement — about how often real problems actually come with enough symmetry for camp 2's move to get a grip?
- Is it verbal — camp 1 and camp 2 just mean different things by "random" and by "the problem", so there's nothing left to resolve once you say that out loud?
- Is it a disagreement about what probability even is — which you'd think is camp 3's knockout point, except camp 3 never seems to actually win?
- Or is it bedrock intuition — some people just feel total ignorance should pin down your beliefs uniquely, others just feel it can't, and there's no argument either side can still give the other?

My own half-baked hypothesis, for what it's worth: this is like arguing about what the default value of a parameter should be when the parameter shouldn't have a default. "Pick a random X" needs the distribution passed in; camps 1 and 3 accept that, and camp 2 is claiming that for some X the type system forces one specific distribution. If that's right then the whole century-long fight is really about whether "well-posed problem" is a real type or just a vibe. But I've got no training in any of this, so tell me if I'm talking rubbish.

Given your diagnosis — what would progress even look like here? A new formal result? A distinction everyone should've been making all along? Or is this one of those debates we should expect to run forever, and if so, why?
