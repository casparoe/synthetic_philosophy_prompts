Last night a friend and I ended up in a three-hour argument about Hempel's ravens, and she walked me into an argument I cannot escape but whose conclusion I refuse to accept. She laid it out like this:

(1) A universal generalization is logically equivalent to its contrapositive: "All ravens are black" says exactly what "Everything non-black is a non-raven" says — same truth conditions, no difference in content.
(2) If evidence confirms a hypothesis, it confirms any hypothesis logically equivalent to it. Confirming a claim while failing to confirm something that says the very same thing would be incoherent.
(3) Finding an instance of a generalization — an object that satisfies both its antecedent and its consequent — confirms it, at least somewhat. So finding a non-black thing that is not a raven confirms "Everything non-black is a non-raven."
(4) A white shoe is a non-black thing that is not a raven. So observing one confirms "Everything non-black is a non-raven," and by (1) and (2), it confirms "All ravens are black."
(5) But that same shoe is also a non-white thing that is not a raven, so by perfectly parallel reasoning it confirms "Everything non-white is a non-raven," which contraposes to "All ravens are white."
(6) No single observation can confirm both "All ravens are black" and "All ravens are white," since (given that ravens exist) they cannot both be true.
(7) Each of (1) through (3) looks undeniable, so the ordinary notion of confirmation must be incoherent: there is no such thing as inductive evidence, and what we call confirmation is an illusion.

Her conclusion seems to prove far too much — it would dissolve not just the ravens puzzle but the whole idea that observation supports generalizations. Yet I can't locate the misstep. If forced to keep one premise, I'd most like to keep (1), and I suspect the trouble is somewhere in (3) or in the move to (6), but I can't say why.
