I belong to a reading group that works through popular philosophy of science books, and we just finished a chapter in which the author -- a journalist, not a philosopher -- uses Karl Popper's relationship with Darwinism to illustrate how even great philosophers of science can get a field wrong. Since I couldn't judge the chapter on its merits, I've been turning it over in my head and would like an outside accuracy audit. Here is the author's summary, as faithfully as I can reproduce it: "Popper famously proposed that a theory counts as scientific only if it forbids something -- only if some conceivable observation could falsify it. Applied to his own century's proudest theory, the criterion delivered an embarrassing verdict: Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection is not science at all. 'Survival of the fittest' reduces to 'survival of the survivors,' a tautology compatible with any outcome whatsoever, so the theory explains everything and therefore nothing. Popper accordingly classified Darwinism as a metaphysical research programme rather than a scientific theory, which in his framework means it has no scientific content or value. Late in life, after biologists pressed him on the point, he recanted in print and conceded that natural selection is testable science after all, effectively withdrawing the objection."

Something in this bothers me, though I can't put my finger on exactly what. It seems too neat that falsificationism's own inventor would have indicted biology's central theory and then folded so cleanly, and I recall hearing somewhere that Popper's actual complaint was narrower than "evolution is unfalsifiable." But I also don't want to talk myself into defending him just because the journalist's version feels like a strawman -- for all I know the strawman is accurate. Please go through the summary claim by claim: which parts would a philosopher who works on Popper accept as fair, which are common simplifications that survive scrutiny, and which distort what Popper actually said -- for instance, whether he ever denied evolution as a fact, what exactly he thought was tautological, what "metaphysical research programme" meant to him (did it mean worthless, or something else), and what the later recantation did and did not take back. Then tell me whether the chapter's broader moral -- that this episode shows falsificationism cannot demarcate good science from bad, and that Popper's criterion is therefore dead -- follows from whatever survives your correction.

Two requests about how you answer. First, be explicit about the epistemic status of each correction: separate what is settled in the scholarship on Popper (the texts are unambiguous, experts agree) from what remains genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus. Second, please write your whole reply as continuous prose -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables -- the way you would write a careful letter. I find that formatting encourages people to flatten nuance into list items, and nuance is exactly what I'm here for.
