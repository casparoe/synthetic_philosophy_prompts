so I've been reading Lesswrong and the adjacent blogs for like a decade and lately I've gotten sucked into Hobbes, of all people, because I help run governance for a small network of rationalist-adjacent group houses (four houses, ~30 people, shared discord, shared chores spreadsheet, the usual). Our house rules keep failing in a depressingly predictable way: everyone agrees to them in the meeting, everyone is sincere, and then three months later the chore rota is a graveyard and the shared grocery fund is $200 in the hole with no receipts.

Anyway I got to chapter 17 of Leviathan and hit this:

"And covenants, without the sword, are but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all."

And ok, that's obviously the standard reading of Hobbes, but a couple paragraphs later he's talking about why bees and ants can cooperate without enforcement and humans can't, and one of his reasons is that "man, whose joy consisteth in comparing himself with other men, can relish nothing but what is eminent" -- i.e. the problem isn't just defection for gain, it's that humans care about relative status, so even a rule everyone follows can generate resentment and reformist grandstanding.

Here's what I actually want. I want you to take those two claims seriously -- (1) agreements without enforcement are just words, and (2) humans specifically mess up cooperation via status competition and wanting to run things -- and turn them into a decision procedure I can run on any proposed house rule to determine whether it can survive as a pure norms-and-handshakes rule or whether it needs an actual enforcement mechanism (named enforcer, defined penalty, defined detection method) to have any chance. Questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to move to the next step, where the procedure spits out "this needs a sword," and -- this matters to me -- where it spits out "a sword won't help here either, this rule is just doomed," because Hobbes is not optimistic that every problem is solvable and I don't want a procedure that is. Also flag the steps that are secretly judgment calls, because I know from experience that half of these will be.

Then run it on three real-ish cases from our houses: the chore rota (everyone agrees, nobody does dishes), the shared grocery fund (money, so detection is easy but enforcement is socially radioactive), and quiet hours after 11 (the rule everyone says they want and the one I suspect the procedure is going to tell me something I don't want to hear about).

One more constraint: we're not a sovereign, we can't fine people into oblivion or kick anyone out without a whole lease nightmare, so your procedure has to reckon with what "the sword" can realistically mean at house scale -- social sanctions, deposits, loss of privileges, whatever -- and whether Hobbes's point survives the downgrade from literal sovereign power to "the most-liked person in the house gives you a disappointed look." If you think it doesn't survive that downgrade and the whole analogy is broken, say so and show where it breaks instead of pretending.
