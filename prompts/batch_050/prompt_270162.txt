Last night I got into a long and unresolved argument with a friend who works as an economic consultant, and I would like help turning my side of it into something more rigorous than a grievance. We were arguing about Milton Friedman's famous claim that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant, and that a theory should be judged only by the accuracy of its predictions. My friend defends a strong version of this: if the model predicts well, complaining about its assumptions is just philosophizing. I objected that some unrealistic assumptions are harmless simplifications while others are doing all the work, and that you cannot tell which is which by looking at predictive track record alone. In the heat of the argument I improvised a distinction and a test, and I want to know whether they can be made operational.

My distinction was between what I called load-bearing and cosmetic assumptions. A cosmetic assumption is one you could relax -- make it a bit more realistic -- and the model's conclusions would shift only slightly. A load-bearing assumption is one where a small step toward realism flips or destroys the conclusion; the prediction is an artifact of the simplification rather than a discovery about the world. My proposed test was: take each obviously false assumption, perturb it slightly in the direction of realism, and see what happens to the output. My friend replied that this test is itself usually impossible to run outside economics, that I have no account of what counts as a small perturbation or which direction counts as toward realism, and that my distinction collapses back into his position whenever the model predicts well.

Here is my situation. I work as a policy analyst at a state agency, and roughly twice a month I have to write a two-page memo recommending whether the agency should rely on a specific quantitative model -- economic forecasts, epidemiological projections, traffic models, cost-benefit analyses submitted by contractors. I typically have a few days per memo, no ability to rerun the models myself, and access to the model documentation but rarely the code. What I want is a decision procedure I can actually follow: the questions to ask in order about a model's unrealistic assumptions, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, and a clear rule for when the verdict is rely, rely-with-caveats, or do-not-rely. The procedure should tell me what to do when I cannot run my perturbation test (which, realistically, is most of the time), and it should honestly mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, rather than pretending those steps are mechanical.

Please also address my friend's objections: is the load-bearing/cosmetic distinction coherent, does it smuggle predictive success back in through the rear door, and can toward realism be defined without already knowing the truth the model is supposed to approximate? Then apply the finished procedure to three worked cases: a rational-agents-with-perfect-information model of consumer response to a tax change that has a decent forecasting record; a pandemic model whose assumption of homogeneous mixing is known to be false and whose headline projection was far off; and a traffic-flow model that treats drivers as identical particles but accurately predicts congestion on a specific corridor. Make sure at least one case yields a verdict I will find uncomfortable -- either vindicating my friend or condemning a model I would have wanted to trust. Please write your answer in American English.
