I work as a software engineer and have no formal philosophy background, but I host a small podcast where I interview academics, and next month's guest is a political theorist who defends what I understand to be the game-theoretic reading of Hobbes's state of nature: roughly, that the war of all against all does not require anyone to be aggressive or malicious, because it arises from rational self-preservation under mutual uncertainty — no enforcer exists, so even peaceable people have reason to strike first, the way a coordination problem collapses when no party can trust the others to stand down. She argues against the rival reading on which Hobbes's conclusion depends on a gloomy moral psychology of vanity and appetite, or on his theology.

Draft nine to ten interview questions. I want to avoid anything generic and anything that merely invites her to restate the view. Please make sure the set as a whole covers the following pressure points, in whatever order flows best:

1. Which of Hobbes's causes of quarrel — competition, diffidence, glory — her reading treats as load-bearing, and whether the argument survives if Hobbes is granted only diffidence.
2. What I think of as the iterated-game objection: if the state of nature is an ongoing interaction rather than a one-shot encounter, why wouldn't conditional cooperation emerge on its own, without any sovereign? Does the reading quietly depend on assuming short time horizons or anonymous encounters?
3. Whether the argument is fundamentally epistemic — about not being able to tell who poses a threat — rather than about incentives, and what that implies about the sovereign as a remedy: does the sovereign solve an information problem, and if so, why couldn't reputation or conventions solve it instead?
4. The regress worry: whatever diffidence makes subjects unable to trust one another seems to apply equally to trusting the sovereign, so why doesn't the same logic dissolve the commonwealth? (I keep mapping this onto a distributed system with no trusted coordinator and no reliable way to distinguish honest nodes from hostile ones — feel free to have the questions use or correct that analogy where it helps.)
5. Whether she takes the state of nature to be a historical claim, a thought experiment, or an analytical device, and whether the reading's plausibility changes across those.
6. A closing question asking her what textual evidence or argument would push her toward the rival moral-psychology reading.

For each question, give the answer you would most expect from her, plus a follow-up keyed to that answer that presses the point further. After the list, tell me which question you are least confident will land as intended — where your prediction of her reply is most likely to be wrong — and what she could plausibly say in the interview that would make you restructure the set.
