Here is the student’s answer: Lakatos’s discussion of Euler’s polyhedron formula is meant to show that mathematics advances through conjectures, attempted proofs, counterexamples, and improved versions of the claim. A counterexample is not simply an observational fact coming from outside mathematics; it usually comes from examining the proof and deciding which objects or assumptions it did not cover. Concepts like polyhedron are proof-generated because the proof prompts us to tighten, stretch, or divide them, so a theorem can change in content even while its wording remains fixed. This is what Lakatos means by quasi-empirical: mathematical knowledge is revisable through rational criticism, although the criticism is internal to mathematical proof rather than based on sensory experiment. Fallibility is only one part of this. A proof might contain an ordinary mistake, but Lakatos’s stronger point is that an apparently valid proof can teach us that its theorem should be reformulated. Once the relevant definitions have been repaired, however, the resulting theorem and proof are deductively certain and no longer exposed to revision. Thus his account mainly describes the growth of informal mathematics and does not challenge the certainty of completed formal proofs.
