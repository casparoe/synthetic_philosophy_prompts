I'm the education director at a mid-sized human-rights museum. We've got permanent galleries on the transatlantic slave trade, the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, and the Rwandan genocide, plus rotating exhibits. I manage about forty volunteer docents, we get school groups most weekdays, and my board includes members of several survivor communities who definately do not agree with eachother about much.

The single most common thing that goes wrong on our floor is comparison. Visitors ask docents which one was the worst. A teacher wants us to tell her class that slavery was the worst atrocity in history so the kids will, as she puts it, get how serious it is. A community group petitions for a gallery on an event we don't cover and frames any hesitation as us saying their suffering doesn't count. Last year two survivor communities clashed over a joint commemoration because each read the shared stage as a ranking. And my board keeps floating a scale-of-evil interactive exhibit for fundraising reasons, which honestly makes me want to quit.

The philosophy here, as I understand it, is genuinely split. One side says comparison is unavoidable, even necessary: if we can't say some things are worse than others we can't allocate attention, guilt, or reparations, and refusing to compare is its own kind of evasion. The other side says ranking atrocities is obscene, that each one is singular, and that comparisons always collapse into competitive victimhood or get weaponized to minimize somebody's dead. I don't want a lecture settling which side is right. I want a floor procedure my docents and I can actually run.

So write me that procedure: the questions to ask in order whenever a comparison comes up, whether it's a visitor's question, a teacher's request, an exhibit proposal, or a commemoration plan. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands off to a human being because the question is one of values rather than protocol. Keep the distinctions that actually matter even for docents with no theory background -- my own hunch is that comparing death tolls, comparing moral culpability, comparing causes and mechanisms, and comparing suffering are four different activities that need different handling, but tell me if I've carved that wrong. And mark the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the teacher who wants the worst-in-history line for her lesson, the joint-commemoration fight, and the board's scale-of-evil interactive. Include at least one case where the procedure spits out an answer I'm not going to like. Finish with what the procedure can't do -- the situations where I just have to decide something myself and live with it.
