I'm a PhD student in political science (comparative politics, mostly clientelism in southern Italy) and I've agreed to run one session of a grad seminar on corruption next term. Instead of assigning the usual journal articles I want to open with The Godfather -- the first film -- and read it as a case study in whether our standard concept of corruption travels. Here's what I'm stuck on. The canonical poli-sci definition (Nye and basically everyone after him) is misuse of *public* office for private gain. But Don Corleone holds no public office at all; he's running something closer to a para-state. So right away there's a puzzle: when he trades favors with Bonasera the undertaker in the opening scene, is that corruption, clientelism, or just a Maussian gift economy -- the gift that creates an inalienable debt and binds giver and receiver -- that liberal political science mislabels because it only recognizes state institutions? And notice the film complicates the obvious answer: when the Don finally calls in Bonasera's debt, the service he asks for is preparing Sonny's body for burial, which is perfectly legal and arguably decent. So the friendship framing isn't always a euphemism for crime.

I'd like you to read the film through this debate and tell me what reading I'd be giving the students. Some things I want covered: (1) Does the film itself distinguish between the Don's favor-bank and the garden-variety corruption of the judges and cops on his payroll, or does it treat them as one system -- and should we? (2) Mauss says the gift obligates; but the Don's gifts arguably can't be refused, so is the film showing us a gift economy from the inside as *coercive*, i.e. is the anthropologist's romanticization the thing the film is actually correcting? (3) The opening has Bonasera saying he believed in America and the courts let him down, and the whole film sets up American law and Sicilian justice as rival normative orders with the audience pushed to sympathize with the wrong one. Does the film take a position on the universality question -- i.e. does it vindicate the relativist line that corruption norms are local, or does Michael's arc (and Kay watching from outside) amount to a verdict that the system was rotten all along?

One more thing: I've been playing with a test I call the Bonasera test -- an exchange of favors counts as corrupt iff fulfilling the return obligation requires the beneficiary to do something they have independent moral reason not to do. On my test, the Bonasera favor comes out non-corrupt, the drug-trade refusal becomes really interesting (Vito frames it as moral but his stated reason is losing his political protection, i.e. prudential), and the payroll judges are the only clear corruption in the film. I'm sure this test fails somewhere I haven't seen -- where does it break, does it classify the film's other exchanges right, and is there a better criterion that keeps the interesting cases interesting rather than just collapsing everything into "all mafia activity is corruption," which I find analytically lazy? Feel free to be blunt, I'd rather get torn apart now than infront of the students.
