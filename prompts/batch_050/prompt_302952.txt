I cover science and big ideas for a general-audience magazine, and before that I spent about a decade as a software engineer, so I still think in interfaces, abstract data types and type classes whether I like it or not. I'm researching a piece on a spat in the philosophy of maths and want it straight in my own head before I interview anyone. The spat, as I understand it: you can build the natural numbers out of sets in more than one equally good way, and on the standard construction where each number is just the set of all smaller numbers, 2 turns out to be literally a member of 4 — while on another perfectly good construction it isn't. So the question 'is 2 an element of 4?' seems to have no real answer at all, which apparently is the embarassment that pushed people towards saying numbers are nothing but positions in a structure: what matters about 2 is purely that it follows 1, precedes 3, is half of 4, full stop, and any construction that delivers those relations is as good as any other.

Here's what I want. Translate this whole debate — the multiple-constructions problem, the structuralist move, and the main objections to structuralism — into the vocabulary I actually think in: interfaces versus implementations, representation hiding, type classes, coercion and subtyping. And do it honestly, not forcing the fit. For each element, tell me what maps cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart in programming at all. My own guess, for seeding: 'is 2 an element of 4?' is exactly like asking an implementation-detail question about something you only ever touch through an interface — like using `is` in Python to peek at object identity, where small-integer caching can make the answer leak out, but no sane programmer would treat that as a fact about the number. I'm less sure what to do with the follow-up problem that the 2 of the natural numbers and the 2 of the reals seem to be the same thing living in different structures — in my world that's coercion between types, but does that mapping survive scrutiny? The thing I keep tripping over, though, is this: in software the question of which implementation is the real one simply doesn't arise, because we wrote all of them ourselves, whereas the philosophers seem to think that with numbers there's a genuine fact of the matter they keep failing to pin down. Does that difference dissolve their puzzle — is the whole thing a category error the programming framing exposes — or does it show the translation leaves out the one thing they actually care about?

Please take a definite line. I don't want a tasteful survey of the options; I want a verdict on whether the interface/implementation way of seeing it dissolves the philosophers' puzzle or misses its point, argued properly, with a note where you're leaning on a contested reading of the debate. And write it in British English, please — the piece is for a London outlet and I'd like the working notes to match.
