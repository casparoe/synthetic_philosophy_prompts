So I've been reading Cicero's De Senectute (the Cato the Elder dialogue on old age) and I keep thinking about the current debates in the US over aging politicians — should there be a maximum age for serving in the Sentate, mandatory retirement ages for judges, cognitive testing for presidents, that kind of thing.

In the dialogue Cicero has Cato push back on the complaint that old age pulls you out of public life. His answer is basically that the really important business of the state isn't done by muscle and stamina but by counsel, judgment, and authority, and those are things old men have more of, not less. But he also concedes that mental powers genuinely do fail some people, and his move there is roughly that the failure isn't old age's fault — it's the fault of that particular person's infirmity, the same way a sick young man isn't an indictment of youth. And he's big on the idea that old age should be resisted the way you'd fight a disease: stay mentally active, keep learning (he makes a point of Cato taking up Greek late in life).

So here's my question: if Cicero were alive today watching these debates, what do you think his position would actually be? A few things I'm genuinely unsure about:

1. Would he back mandatory retirement ages, or would he see them as punishing everyone for a defect only some people have — like convicting all old people because a few decline? His own framing seems to cut against blanket rules, but I might be missing something.

2. His defense of the old statesman leans hard on a distinction between deliberation and execution — old men are for advising, not doing. But modern offices like the presidency kinda fuse both. Does that break his argument, or would he just say the presidency should be restructured so the elder counsels and younger people carry things out? And would he accept that tradeoff?

3. Rome had no retirement from the Senate at all — you served until you died. How much of his view is just that institution rationalizing itself? Can you separate the philosophy from the setup it was defending, and if not, how much should we trust it as advice for us?

Try to reason from what's actually in the text rather than giving me a generic 'ancients respected their elders' take. And if you think Cicero's position has a real internal tension here, don't smooth it over — point at it.
