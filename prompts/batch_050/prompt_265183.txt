I teach political philosophy at a mid-sized university and, somewhat to my own surprise, recently found myself elected to the executive council of a scholarly association of about four thousand members. At our last meeting, after a dispiritingly acrimonious debate over whether to move our annual conference from in-person to hybrid, one council member proposed that we start settling certain association decisions with internal prediction markets: members would buy and sell contracts that pay out conditional on measurable outcomes, and the council would follow the prices rather than arguing from intuition. She pitched it with the slogan that we should vote on our values but bet on our beliefs. I am sympathetic enough to want the proposal taken seriously, but I do not trust the council, myself included, to improvise this responsibly. What I would like from you is a standing written procedure the council could run on any proposal to use a market, in under an hour, with no quantitative expertise on hand: the questions to ask in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a firm refusal to use a market, and where it instead terminates by sending the question to an ordinary vote or to officer judgment because the issue is one of values rather than of fact.

The procedure needs to be honest about the distinctions that must survive translation into committee language. At minimum it should force us to ask: whether the question at hand is actually a factual one about which policy best serves a goal we already share, or a disguised contest over which goal to pursue; whether there exists an outcome measure we could specify in advance and actually observe within a time horizon relevant to the decision; whether a market drawing on perhaps thirty or forty genuinely active participants is thick enough to aggregate information or thin enough for one committed member with a large play-money budget to push around; whether the market's own price could change the outcome being predicted (since we would be watching the prices while deliberating); and whether the prediction is conditional on our own decision in a way that makes the contract's terms self-referential or impossible to settle. Please also mark explicitly the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and a person simply has to decide, and flag which features of the underlying theory you have deliberately dropped in the simplification and what that costs us.

Then run the procedure on three proposals the council has actually floated: first, a market on whether the hybrid conference format will raise total attendance and graduate-student participation over a two-year window, with the outcome deciding whether hybrid becomes permanent; second, a market on whether a candidate for our executive director position will still hold the job in three years, to be used as the basis for whether we make the offer at all; third, a market on whether moving our flagship journal to open access will increase its submissions within eighteen months, with the market advising but not deciding. I expect the procedure will not treat these three alike, and I want you to include at least one application where it yields a verdict I or my colleagues will dislike, and to say plainly what a procedure of this kind cannot deliver for a volunteer body like ours. Please conclude with a final line in exactly the following form: \answer{Case 1: [market or no market]; Case 2: [market or no market]; Case 3: [market or no market]}
