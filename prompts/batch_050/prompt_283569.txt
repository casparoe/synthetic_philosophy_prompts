So I got into an argument with a friend who works in finance about the GameStop squeeze in early 2021, and I realized we were talking past each other because I kept thinking about it in terms of the no-trade literature (Milgrom-Stokey, Aumann-style agreeing to disagree, that whole cluster) and he kept saying none of that applies to a squeeze. I want someone to actually work through this carefully. Take the GameStop episode -- short interest reportedly above 100% of the float, a loosely coordinated crowd of retail buyers on Reddit driving the price from around $20 to several hundred in days, hedge funds like Melvin getting margin-called and forced to buy back at catastrophic prices, brokers eventually throttling buy orders -- and analyze it as a test case for no-trade theorems. Map it properly: who are the traders, what's the information structure (the short interest was public knowledge! the Reddit thesis was posted openly!), and under what assumptions would the theorem say this volume of trade should never have happened? If everyone is rational and it's common knowledge, why does anyone take the other side of the Redditors' buy orders, or of the shorts' positions?

Then do the part my friend gestured at but didn't rigorously do: say exactly where the theorem's assumptions break in this episode, and be specific about which break does the work. Is it that a short squeeze is mechanical rather than informational -- shorts have to buy back no matter what they believe, so it's not really "trade on disagreement" at all? Is it that the retail side had non-informational motives (entertainment, spite toward hedge funds, diamond-hands commitment) which makes them effectively noise traders, a category the theorem assumes away? Or is it heterogeneous priors, since both sides saw the same public data and just refused to converge? And then run it back the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory? My worry is that no-trade results have an unfalsifiable escape hatch -- anytime real people trade in massive volume, you just say "noise traders" or "non-common priors" and the theorem survives. Does GameStop show the theorems are vacuous in practice, or does it actually illustrate their logic by being precisely the kind of case that needs an assumption-violation to exist at all? Also connect it to Grossman-Stiglitz if you can: the Reddit crowd arguably did produce information (the original deep-value thesis on GME), and markets that perfectly revealed information would have given them no reason to bother -- is the squeeze what the return to information-gathering looks like, or is that stretching the paradox past where it goes?
