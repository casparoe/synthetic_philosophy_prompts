I spent forty years as a molecular biologist before retiring, and in the last year or so I have been working my way through the philosophy and historiography of science, which I neglected shamefully during my career. In a reading group with some friends from my old department, we recently discussed Herbert Butterfield's notion of the Whig interpretation of history: the tendency of historians to write the past as though it were aimed at the present, sorting historical actors into those who helped bring about what we now take to be correct and those who obstructed it, and judging them accordingly. I found the idea immediately plausible, but our discussion stalled when we tried to apply it to an episode I thought I knew intimately: the discovery of the double-helical structure of DNA in 1953.

Let me set out the episode as I understand it, in case my memory of the details matters. Watson and Crick, working at Cambridge, arrived at the structure largely by model-building informed by X-ray diffraction data produced at King's College London by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins; Franklin's data, including the famous Photograph 51 and her measurements in an MRC report, reached Cambridge without her having been asked, and she was herself close to the answer when the Cambridge model was published. Franklin died in 1958, and the 1962 Nobel Prize went to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins. What interests me historiographically is that this episode seems to have been narrated twice in succession. The mid-century telling was a straightforward triumphalist story: brilliant young men racing Linus Pauling to the secret of life, with the diffraction people as supporting cast. The later telling, roughly from the 1970s onward and accelerated by Watson's own memoir, inverted it: Franklin became the wronged heroine whose data was appropriated and whose contribution was erased, with Watson recast as something closer to the villain.

Here is what I would like analyzed. Take Butterfield's critique as the lens and examine both tellings of the episode through it. The first telling is Whiggish in an obvious way, but my real question is whether the second telling is Whiggish in exactly the same way: it seems to me to keep the past organized around present concerns, merely swapping the heroes and villains, so that Franklin is judged by how well she anticipates what we now regard as the right outcome, rather than understood in terms of what the problem looked like to her in 1952 (she had, after all, reportedly drafted something like a joke obituary for the helical hypothesis). If that is right, does the episode suggest that revisionist history is often just inverted Whig history, and does Butterfield's framework have the resources to say what a genuinely non-Whiggish history of the double helix would even look like, given that we cannot un-know that the model was correct? Please be disciplined about the mapping: say precisely which features of the episode the Whig concept illuminates, which it misdescribes or leaves untouched, and where the concept is simply silent rather than forcing it to speak. I would also like the analysis to run in the other direction: state what this episode, taken as a case, does for or against Butterfield's thesis — confirmation, anomaly, or a case he never anticipated. Begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. And since I am new to this field and cannot tell confident scholarship from confident bluffing, please indicate how confident you are in your main claims and single out the specific point on which you are least sure.
