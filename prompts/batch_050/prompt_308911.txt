Yesterday a friend and I spent hours arguing about the 2016 ProPublica investigation into COMPAS, the recidivism-risk algorithm used in parts of the US criminal justice system. ProPublica analyzed scores from Broward County, Florida, and found that Black defendants who did not reoffend were roughly twice as likely as comparable white defendants to be labeled high risk; the tool's maker responded that the scores were equally well calibrated across racial groups, meaning a given score corresponded to the same actual reoffending rate for both groups. My friend insists this case is a clean, worked example of the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness: two reasonable fairness criteria, provably incompatible given unequal base rates, both sides talking past each other, case closed. I think that reading is too tidy, but I lost the argument on the details.

Please analyze the ProPublica–COMPAS dispute through the lens of the impossibility theorems, in both directions. Map the episode onto the theory precisely: which fairness criterion ProPublica's analysis operationalized, which one the company's defense invoked, and why the theorems say these cannot both be satisfied when base rates differ. Then tell me where the mapping breaks down or runs out: whether the theorems actually settle who was right or merely redescribe the disagreement in formal terms, what they are silent about (the choice of criterion as a normative question, the provenance of the base rates themselves, the difference between scoring defendants and sentencing them), and whether the episode tests the theorems in any way -- for instance, whether a real institution's need to pick one criterion under political pressure is something the formal results anticipated or ignored. Throughout, distinguish what is mathematically settled from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested normative verdict as if it followed from the theorems.
