Yesterday evening I had a long and inconclusive argument with a friend about scientific priority. We had been talking about the Newton–Leibniz dispute and the Darwin–Wallace episode, and at some point she cut the whole discussion short with a phrase I keep turning over: the idea was in the air. Her point, as I understood it, was that simultaneous or near-simultaneous discovery shows that major findings are inevitable products of their era rather than achievements of particular minds — that calculus, natural selection, and the rest would have arrived when they did no matter who happened to be alive — and that in consequence (i) quarrels over who got there first are historically parochial and philosophically empty, (ii) the outsized credit we give to individual discoverers is unjustified and should really attach to the broader intellectual community, and (iii) the very notion of genius is a romantic fiction.

I felt she was moving much too fast, but when I tried to say which step was illegitimate, I found I could not even state clearly what the slogan itself claims. That is what I would like your help with.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that "the idea was in the air" might be expressing — for instance, a counterfactual thesis (had Newton and Leibniz never lived, calculus would have appeared around the same time anyway), a thesis about causal sufficiency (the state of mathematics circa 1670 fully determined the outcome, individual contributions being interchangeable), an inductive thesis (independent multiple discovery is so common that we should infer inevitability in each case), a deflationary thesis about discovery itself (the "discovery" is mostly assembling pieces that were already lying around, so there is little left for any person to have done), and a purely normative thesis (whatever the causal story, institutions of individual credit are unfair). For each candidate thesis, I would like to know: what exactly it commits one to, whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; whether the historical fact of simultaneous discovery is actually evidence for it (and whether it might in some readings be evidence against it); and, crucially, whether it would support the three conclusions my friend drew, or whether she needs more than one reading at once — perhaps an implausibly strong causal thesis to get (i) and (iii) while only a modest normative thesis gets (ii).

Two further complications she brushed past, which you might address: first, whether the slogan applies equally to inventions (the telephone, the light bulb) and to discoveries of purportedly mind-independent truths, or whether the inevitability claim is more plausible for one than the other; and second, whether there is a reading on which the slogan is true but irrelevant — where inevitability of the result coexists with genuine differences in what individuals contributed (speed, rigour, generality, the willingness to publish) that might still ground differential credit even if firstness itself should not.

I am not asking for a history of any particular dispute; I want the disambiguation done cleanly, with verdicts, so that I can tell which version of her claim I should actually have been resisting.
