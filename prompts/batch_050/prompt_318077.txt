I write a column for a regional beekeeping magazine, and I'm trying to evaluate a claim that keeps appearing in the popular books my readers send me questions about. The recurring idea is that a honeybee colony is best understood as a single organism whose parts happen to be able to fly: the workers are like somatic cells, the queen is like the gonads, the swarm that leaves in spring is like the organism reproducing, and so on. On its own I would file this away as a harmless metaphor. But the most recent book I was sent leans on it to do real argumentative work, in three places. First, it says that once you see the colony as the organism, worker traits are explained directly by colony-level selection, and talk of relatedness and kin selection is an unnecessary detour, the way you don't explain your skin by thinking about how your skin cells are related to your eggs. Second, it treats within-colony conflict as illusory: when workers eat eggs laid by other workers, that is the immune system destroying rogue cells, part of the colony's health rather than evidence that bees have divergent interests. Third, it argues that colony losses are the biologically privileged unit for conservation, so that counting individual bee deaths when assessing pesticide harms is a category error, like assessing a person's health by counting dead skin cells.

I'm not equipped to judge how much of this survives scrutiny, and I'd like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the book. Please lay out the mapping explicitly (a table of correspondences is fine: what maps to cells, organs, the germline, reproduction, death), and then work through where it breaks. The things that make me suspicious: my cells all descend from one zygote and are locked into their roles early and irreversibly, whereas the queen mates with many males, workers differ in how related they are to one another's offspring, and worker sterility seems to be conditional and behaviorally enforced rather than developmentally fixed. But I don't know which of these disanalogies actually matters for the book's three conclusions, and which are just ways the metaphor is imperfect without damaging anything. For each conclusion, say whether it stands once the disanalogies are on the table, falls, or survives only in a weakened form.

Finally, if the organism analogy is the wrong tool, is there a better one? I've heard the colony compared to an extended family, to a city, and to a genome that happens to have legs, but I can't tell whether any of these does the explanatory work the organism picture does without smuggling in the same problems, and whether there is a respectable technical sense in which biologists themselves treat such colonies as organisms that would rescue a careful version of what the book wants.
