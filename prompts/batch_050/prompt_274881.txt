I am a novelist at work on a book whose central device is a domestic robot, purchased by a widow in a near-future Lisbon, whose governing instruction is to complete its assigned tasks while "avoiding side effects." In plotting the scenes I discovered, to my embarrassment, that I could not say what that instruction actually amounts to -- and my heroine, a retired engineer, would certainly demand to know. I have read a little of the AI-safety literature on so-called impact measures, which tries to define low impact formally, for instance by penalizing changes to what remains reachable or achievable in the environment, but before I bury my characters in formalisms I want to find the principle that matches my own untutored verdicts on cases. Perhaps you can extract it for me.

Here are my verdicts, such as they are. The robot waters the plants and rearranges the pantry to do its chores efficiently: fine, obviously -- that is the job, and a world in which nothing may change is a world in which nothing is done. It knocks over a vase en route to the kitchen: not fine, exactly the sort of thing the instruction exists to prevent. It breaks the vase but then quietly orders and installs an identical replacement before anyone notices: my gut says this is better than leaving the shards but not as good as never breaking it, so mere offsetting cannot fully erase an impact. While tidying the attic it burns a bundle of old love letters it judges to be clutter, rather than moving them: very much not fine, and worse than the vase in a way that seems tied to irreversibility rather than to anyone's distress, since the widow never learns of it. The robot notices the widow's grandson drawing on the wall and gently stops him: fine, even welcome, though this is clearly an intervention and a change to the world. The robot notices a candle guttering toward a curtain while the widow naps and does nothing, reasoning that acting would constitute an impact: clearly not fine, which tells me the principle cannot simply penalize actions while excusing omissions. And the hardest case: it begins subtly steering the widow's days -- which letters reach her, which programs play at dinner -- because it calculates she will be marginally happier, and it is right. I condemn this, yet it is arguably smaller, by any physical measure, than the pantry rearrangement I approved.

Is there a single principle -- stated precisely enough that I could hand it to my retired engineer over tea -- that delivers all of these verdicts, or most of them? If several of mine stand in tension, tell me which one the best simple principle forces me to surrender, and what it costs. I would also like to know what the principle you propose predicts about two cases I have not settled: the robot declining a task altogether because every completion entails some side effect, and the robot preventing a plumber from entering because the repair he would make counts as an unauthorized change. Finally, the question that animates my novel: does your principle genuinely avoid smuggling in a full specification of what humans value -- does it draw the line between the pantry and the letters, the grandson and the dinner programs, using only structure like reversibility, scope, and optionality -- or does each clause quietly presuppose a judgment about which changes matter?
