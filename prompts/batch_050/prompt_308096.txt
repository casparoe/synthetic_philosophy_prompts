I've just come off three weeks of jury duty (fraud trial, mostly very dull) and something from the deliberation room has been rattling round my head ever since. I want to know wether there's an established concept for it, because I keep needing the distinction and don't have the words.

One witness said she'd seen the defendant at a particular petrol station on the night in question. Back in the jury room two jurors had this stand-off about it. The first said: she told us, she had nothing to gain, she seemed completely straight, so now we know he was there. The second kept insisting that's not how it works — you don't just know it from being told, you have to work it out: watch her demeanour, check her story is consistent, weigh up whether she had reason to lie, and only if all that comes out right can you conclude he was there. The first juror thought this was absurd overcomplication — if you ask a stranger the time and they tell you, now you know the time; you don't run a background check on their watch first.

The more I sit with it, the more it seems these two were disagreeing about something really basic: whether somebody telling you something is, all by itself, a way of coming to know it, or whether being-told is only ever raw material that has to be processed through the "proper" ways of knowing (you perceived them saying it, you reasoned about their honesty) before it counts as knowledge. And then I realised this isn't just a courtroom thing. Almost everything I take myself to know — that Napoleon existed, that water boils at 100 degrees at sea level, what my gran's childhood was like — I got by being told, and I definately never did the inferring-from-reliability work for most of it. If juror two is right, it's not clear I actually know any of that. If juror one is right, then courts and sceptics are being oddly fussy about something everyone does unthinkingly all day.

There's also a wrinkle that came up in the pub afterwards. Suppose the witness said the right thing only by fluke — she'd mixed up the nights, or she didn't believe her own testimony and was parroting a script, but what she said happened to be true. Do I know it then? My gut says my knowing depends on her knowing, like knowledge gets passed down a chain rather than minted fresh at my end — but I'm not sure that intuition survives scrutiny either.

So: is there a name, or an established debate, for the first juror's position (say-so as its own route to knowledge) versus the second's (say-so as just more evidence to weigh)? I don't want a bare label, though. I want to know how this differs from adjacent stuff it might get confused with — trusting experts, the informal-fallacy business about appeals to authority, hearsay rules — and I want the edges drawn: does the position cover instruments and photographs or only people? What should it say about the fluke case? And is there a tradition anywhere that has built a whole theory of knowledge around the first juror's instinct, treating being-told as a basic source alongside seeing and reasoning, rather than a poor relation of them? If nothing existing quite fits, coin a term and define it properly, with boundary cases.
