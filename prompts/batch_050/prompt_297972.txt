Last year I got into a long argument with a couple of friends from my reading group about Dennett's idea of free-floating rationales -- reasons that genuinely exist in the world without there being any reasoner who grasps or endorses them, his whole competence-without-comprehension thing. I'm now writing a paper where I try to catalogue the strongest, cleanest examples of the phenomenon, and I've ended up with a list that honestly feels complete to me, which is exactly what worries me. Here's what I have: (1) traits shaped by natural selection, e.g. there's a reason hearts beat and a reason the octopus eye is wired the way it is, even though no one ever intended any of it; (2) instinctive animal competences like spider web-building, where the rationale is in the genome, so to speak, not in the spider's head; (3) the immune system, which "recognizes" invaders and adapts without anyone understanding anything; (4) Hayek's price system, where prices aggregate information that no single market participant possesses; (5) the common law, which seems to accumulate rationales over centuries that no individual judge ever articulated; (6) grammatical conventions in natural language, which are eerily well-designed for communication even though nobody designed them; and (7) large machine learning models, which exploit regularities in their training data that no human understands or even could survey.

What I want from you: give me one more candidate example of a genuine free-floating rationale that is NOT on this list and not just a special case, a blend, or a re-labelling of one of these seven (e.g., "beaver dams" is just (2) again, and "futures markets" is just (4) again). It should be a case where there's a real sense in which something happens *for a reason* but no agent anywhere holds that reason. Then, and this is the part I care about most, stress-test your own suggestion: is the rationale genuinely free-floating, or does it smuggle in a hidden reasoner somewhere (a designer, a collective, a user)? And finally, tell me how your example relates to the items on my list -- does it point to a whole category I've missed, or is it a one-off? If you think my list is actually exhaustive and no eighth example exists, you can argue that instead, but only if you can say *why* the space of possible examples is closed.
