Is there a name for this pattern? I keep running into it in philosophy reading. An author invents some wild scenario -- a duplicate planet, a mad scientist, a runaway trolley -- and the scenario only "works" because it's built using the very machinery the author is defending. Like, the setup quietly assumes that concepts refer in a certain way, or that what matters morally is a certain feature of the situation, and then the reader's reaction to the story gets offered as evidence *for* that way of thinking. So you feel like you've been given a reason, but the reason was baked into the story's construction. I've also seen the defensive version, where someone replies to such a story by just building a *different* story that produces the opposite gut reaction, and then both sides just keep trading stories forever.

It's not plain question-begging, I don't think, because no premise is literally stated. And it's not quite just "an intuition pump" since that covers the good ones too. I want the term (if it exists) for the specific failure where the scenario smuggles in the conclusion through how it was designed. If there's no established name, coin one, define it, and give me a couple of boundary cases -- e.g., how do you tell a smuggling scenario from a legitimately illuminating one.

End your answer with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing either the established term, your coined name, or "no good name exists".
