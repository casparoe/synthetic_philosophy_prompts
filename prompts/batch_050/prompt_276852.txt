I am writing a long feature on game theory for a general-audience science magazine, and my editor wants a poem to open the section on cooperation. Write a sonnet about superrationality in the prisoner's dilemma — the idea, associated with Douglas Hofstadter, that in a perfectly symmetric situation a player may reason that whatever conclusion their own deliberation reaches, a symmetric counterpart's deliberation will reach as well, so that the real choice is not between 'I cooperate while they defect' and its reverse, but between both cooperating and both defecting. The octave should stage the dilemma as it feels from inside one cell: the temptation to defect, and the apparent airtightness of the argument that defection does better no matter what the other player does. The volta should be the moment the prisoner stops trying to predict the other and sees that the other's reasoning is a mirror of their own, so that prediction collapses into decision. The sestet should then work out what follows: that to choose is in effect to choose for both, and that this is neither telepathy nor altruism but a piece of logic. The closing couplet should land on the twist my article develops: when the two players are not people but copies of the same computer program, the superrational assumption is no longer a hopeful postulate but a literal fact about the world — and the couplet should register whether that makes cooperation easier or merely eerier. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of game theory would not object, and restrained enough that a poetry editor would not cut it.
