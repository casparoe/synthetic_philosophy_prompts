I am a novelist preparing a ninety-minute seminar for twelve fiction writers who have little formal philosophical training but are each drafting a multigenerational novel set after a national atrocity. My aim is to give them a usable conceptual grammar for Jaspers’s claim that guilt can take several forms: criminal guilt for acts established through judgment, political guilt arising from membership in a state whose power one has helped sustain or failed to restrain, moral guilt judged before one’s own conscience, and metaphysical guilt arising from a failure of solidarity with other human beings. The ultimate question is not whether the participants feel guilty, but in what sense, if any, a nation and its later generations can remain implicated after the original perpetrators are dead.

Plan the teaching sequence rather than giving the seminar itself. Specify what must be explained first and why, including the dependencies among individual agency, group action, blameworthiness, liability, shame, inherited benefit, and repair. Build the session around one invented case—not Nazi Germany or another actual atrocity—in which the violence occurred several generations earlier, the direct perpetrators are dead, the state has persisted through changes of constitution and personnel, and present citizens differ sharply in their ancestry, acquiescence, material benefit, and willingness to acknowledge the past. Explain what features this case must include and exclude if it is to carry the entire discussion rather than merely provoke sentiment.

Map the misconceptions I should expect and identify the precise stage at which each should be headed off. At minimum, address these: that guilt is primarily a feeling; that guilt can be transmitted by blood; that no one can bear responsibility for what they did not personally do; that collective guilt means equal culpability for every citizen; that an apology requires descendants to condemn themselves as criminals; that institutional continuity is only a metaphor and therefore cannot ground obligation; and that repairing a historical wrong is possible only if the original wrong can be undone. Distinguish inheritance of guilt from inheritance of consequences, obligations, memories, and advantages without letting the distinctions become a way of dissolving responsibility altogether.

Include one closing exercise that would reveal whether the participants can apply the distinctions to an ambiguous fictional descendant who has neither endorsed the atrocity nor knowingly benefited from it, but who now controls an institution founded through it. Say what answers would show that the central idea has landed, what answers would reveal a residual confusion, and how I should respond to each. Finally, state which complications—such as collective intentionality, corporate agency, competing theories of reparations, or the historical particulars surrounding Jaspers—should be explicitly postponed at this introductory level, and how to postpone them without making the account seem evasive.
