I have no academic background — I work in freight logistics — but I read a fair amount and I take my disagreements seriously. Here's one I lost last month and want to re-fight properly.

At a dinner party I posed a version of a familiar hypothetical: police have captured a man who planted a bomb somewhere in the city, it's set to go off in hours, thousands will die, he refuses to talk, and torturing him is the only realistic way to get the location in time. I argued that whatever we think of torture generally, refusing in this case is monstrous, and that anyone who bites the bullet and lets the bomb go off has failed a basic test of moral seriousness. A friend of a friend shut the whole thing down with one sentence: the ends don't justify the means. When I pressed her, she repeated it more slowly, as if I hadn't heard it the first time, and the table moved on.

What bothers me is that I can't tell what claim she was actually making, and I suspect the sentence is carrying at least three or four different claims at once. Sometimes it seems to mean something nearly empty, like: not every good outcome excuses every possible action. Sometimes it seems to mean something absolute, like: certain acts are forbidden no matter what consequences follow from refraining. Sometimes it seems to be a point about how justification has to work — that you can't defend an action just by pointing at the goal, the action itself has to be defensible. And sometimes, in other people's mouths, it seems to be an empirical prediction rather than a principle: that institutions which adopt ugly methods for noble reasons reliably end up with the methods and without the reasons, so the advertised end never actually arrives. There may be more readings I haven't seen.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this saying could be expressing, as a numbered list? For each one, tell me: is it a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive but defensible position, or something actually false — and is my dinner opponent most plausibly committed to it? Then, the part I care about most: which of these readings, if true, would actually defeat my ticking-bomb argument, and which merely rename the disagreement? My suspicion is that the hypothetical was constructed precisely to ask whether any prohibition survives high enough stakes, so that answering it with this slogan just assumes the very thing being asked — but I'm not confident in that, and I'd like it checked. One more wrinkle: I'm fairly sure she accepts killing in self-defense and some wartime bombing, so whatever principle she was invoking apparently has exceptions she hasn't stated. What extra premise would the slogan need to permit those but forbid my case, and is that premise any more secure than the one she was dismissing?
