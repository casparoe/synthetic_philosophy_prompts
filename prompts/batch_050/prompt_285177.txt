I'm assembling a seminar unit on the neuroscience of free will, and instead of ending with another journal article I want to end with a film: Minority Report (the 2002 Spielberg movie — you may gesture at the Philip K. Dick story where the divergence matters, but keep the film primary). Read the film through Libet's experiments and the debate they started. To fix the terms: in Libet's paradigm, a readiness potential builds in motor areas several hundred milliseconds before subjects report the conscious urge to move, which seems to show the brain "deciding" before the agent does; Libet's own response was that consciousness doesn't initiate the act but retains a last-moment veto — his "free won't" — in the final fraction of a second before movement; and critics replied that the veto is presumably preceded by its own unconscious preparation, so nothing is gained. Here's what I want the reading to do:

First, treat the precrime system as Libet's experimental logic pushed to an extreme: a readout of what a person will do, delivered long before the person has any conscious intention of doing it. Where does the analogy genuinely hold, and where does it quietly break — the precogs see outcomes rather than neural precursors, they see the future rather than a preparation in the present, and they can disagree with each other?

Second, the minority report itself: the suppressed dissenting vision showing the future going otherwise. Anderton pins his hopes on finding one for the Leo Crow killing, and it turns out none exists — all three precogs saw him do it — yet at the predicted moment, with the gun in his hand, he doesn't fire, and Crow's death happens anyway but not as foreseen. Is this the best cinematic image of Libet's veto we'll ever get: a choice made without any recorded alternative future to lean on? Or does the scene actually undermine the veto reading, since the prophecy itself manufactured the confrontation — Anderton only meets Crow because he was shown killing him — so the "veto" is exercised inside a fate the prediction created?

Third, the ending: precrime is shut down even though the system's predictions were, as far as the film lets us know, essentially accurate. Does the film thereby take a position on whether reliable foreknowledge negates moral responsibility, and does it conflate determinism with mere predictability in doing so?

Be faithful in both directions: flag where the film resists the Libet framework or trades on confusions a careful philosopher should name, and don't distort Libet to fit the plot. Close with what the film adds to this debate that the literature lacks — I'm especially interested in the self-referential structure, an agent acting on a prediction about himself, which no subject in a Libet-style experiment ever faces. Write in American English. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase stating whether, on the film's own internal logic, a reliably accurate prediction of an action is compatible with the agent's freedom and responsibility for it.
