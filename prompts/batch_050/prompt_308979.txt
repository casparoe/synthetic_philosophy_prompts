At a dinner party, two friends of mine argued about whether the world is becoming less violent, and neither convinced the other. Here is the exchange as best I remember it.

(1) Marcus said the claim is straightforwardly true: death rates from war and homicide, measured per capita, have fallen enormously, and a randomly chosen person in medieval Europe or in a pre-state society faced a far higher risk of violent death than someone alive today. Honest comparison of rates settles it.

(2) Elena replied that the per-capita framing launders the conclusion. The twentieth century produced the most violent deaths in absolute terms, and what counts as a "violent death" is a choice: battlefield and homicide deaths get counted, while deaths from deliberate famine, preventable disease under conditions of neglect, and similar structural harms get excluded. Pick a different but equally defensible category and the trend flattens or reverses.

(3) Marcus said absolute numbers just track population growth — a world of eight billion will produce more deaths than one of a few hundred million no matter what. If the question is how dangerous it is to be alive, rates per person are the right measure. And expanding "violence" to cover every policy-caused death doesn't refine the question, it changes the subject: then the question becomes "is the world less bad," which nobody thinks has a one-word answer.

(4) Elena countered that even on his own terms the inference is statistically naive. War deaths follow a heavy-tailed distribution in which most deaths come from rare catastrophes, so eighty years without a great-power war tells you almost nothing about the underlying rate — it could be a lull rather than a trend. Given nuclear arsenals, the expected number of war deaths per year may be higher than ever even while realized deaths are low.

(5) Marcus answered that judging a trend by catastrophes that haven't happened makes the pessimistic side unfalsifiable: any stretch of peaceful years can be dismissed as a lull. Expectations about hypothetical wars are not data about violence in the actual world.

They asked me to adjudicate and I couldn't. Please go through moves (1) through (5), saying who is right about each specific point and why, and then answer the question they were circling: is there a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, or does the question dissolve once you are forced to fix a measure, a category of violence, and a time horizon?
