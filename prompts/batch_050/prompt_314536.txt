I'm in Year 12 and recently watched a biographical film about a real historical figure with my English class, which led to an argument afterwards that we couldn't settle. The film had invented a private conversation, softened one of the figure's less admirable decisions, and compressed several people into a single composite character. One of my classmates thought all of this was simply dishonest: the person depicted really lived, really suffered, and can no longer object, so the filmmakers owed them fidelity to the established facts. Another argued that drama has its own obligations, and that rearranging the record is legitimate so long as the result captures something true about the person and their time. I found myself agreeing with both at different moments, which is a sign I haven't thought it through properly.

Our teacher pointed us to chapter 25 of Aristotle's Poetics, where Aristotle says that since the poet is an imitator, like a painter or any other maker of likenesses, the poet must represent things in one of three ways: as they were or are, as they are said or thought to be, or as they ought to be. I was struck by this because it seems to give the dramatist permission to depart from the facts — yet Aristotle was writing about figures like Achilles, not about someone whose grandchildren are still alive and whose letters are in an archive. I'm not sure his three-way licence extends cleanly to modern biopics, docudramas, and historical novels about the documented dead.

Could you write a dialogue between two equally thoughtful characters who take the opposing sides of my classroom argument and actually engage with each other's points? One should defend the view that dramatising a real person is constrained by a duty of fidelity to the known record, such that inventions like fabricated conversations or composite characters need special justification. The other should defend something like the Aristotelian position — that art answers to a different kind of truth, and that literal accuracy is neither achievable nor the point. I'd like them to test Aristotle's three options against cases: Does depicting someone "as they ought to be" dignify or insult them? Does it matter whether the person has living descendants, whether the distortions flatter or malign, or whether the audience can be expected to know the real record? Please have each character respond to what the other actually says, concede points where concession is due, and press on the hard cases rather than retreating to slogans. Around twelve to sixteen exchanges would be ideal.
