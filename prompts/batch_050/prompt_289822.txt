I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and for a chapter of my dissertation on the value of information I am trying to determine exactly where Good's theorem loses its grip. By Good's theorem I mean the standard result that, for a Bayesian expected utility maximizer facing a single decision problem, the expected value of acquiring cost-free information before choosing is never negative — provided the agent updates by conditionalization on receipt of the information and then chooses optimally given her posterior. What I would like from you is a thought experiment engineered to the following specifications, together with a demonstration that it meets them.

Construct a scenario in which an agent is better off, in expectation, refusing a cost-free piece of information, but where the refusal cannot simply be dismissed as a failure of rationality on her part. Constraints:

1. One agent only. No other strategically reasoning agents. I am aware that information can have negative value in game-theoretic settings; that result is well established and is not what this chapter is about.
2. No exotic devices: no perfect predictors, no time travel, no cases in which the agent's basic tastes or utility function transform upon learning the information.
3. The information must be genuinely decision-relevant. There must be some possible contents of the message on which a rational agent would act differently from how she would act on other contents. Cases where the information is useless, and refusal is therefore costless rather than beneficial, do not count.
4. The scenario must be realistic enough to arise in ordinary life. Medical, financial, insurance, or professional settings are all acceptable.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following:

(a) Identify which premise of Good's theorem the scenario violates, and show that it violates exactly that one — i.e., verify explicitly that the other premises hold in your case.
(b) Set up the relevant expectations: the agent's expected utility if she takes the information and acts on it, and her expected utility if she refuses it. Confirm that the ordering goes the way you claim, indicating where the asymmetry comes from.
(c) Explain what happens if the violated premise is restored. Does the case collapse back into one where Good's result holds, or does something of the negative-value structure survive?
(d) Address the defender of Good who responds that your scenario merely relocates the irrationality (or the change in the decision problem) rather than identifying a genuine exception — that the theorem was only ever meant to cover the idealized case, so your example is a footnote rather than a counterexample. Does your case teach us something such a defender must concede, or not?

The larger question motivating the exercise, which you should address in a closing paragraph: do all single-agent exceptions to the non-negative value of information smuggle in a failure of ideal rationality somewhere, or is there a clean structural exception available to a fully rational agent?
