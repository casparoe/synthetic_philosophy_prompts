Next friday I'm giving the toast at my PhD advisor's retirement dinner. She's a comparative psychologist who spent her whole career, almost forty years, arguing that scrub jays do more than just behave cleverly: that they plan for tomorrow, remember particular past episodes, maybe even track what another bird did or didn't see. For that entire career, the thing thrown at her was Morgan's canon -- the old injunction not to explain an animal's behavior by some fancy higher capacity when a simpler, lower one will do. Her life's work was basically showing, one experiment at a time, that the lower explanation did not in fact do.

The room is split in half. One half is animal cognition people who know every study, including the ones that half-replicated and the ones rival labs poked holes in. The other half is her kids, her neighbors, her sister, who know her as the woman who won't shut up about birds at parties.

Write the five-minute toast. A few things it has to do:

- Be structured by the canon, not just mention it. My idea: turn the canon on her. The parsimonious explanation of her career is that she just really, really likes watching birds and found a way to get paid for it. The rich cognitive explanation is that she set out to win a forty-year argument about what minds are and where they show up. The speech can play with which account is simpler, which is truer, and what the canon even tells you to do when the simple one is true but incomplete.
- Have one actual joke about the canon that both halves of the room can laugh at. Not a joke for the scientists with a wink to the family, one joke.
- Be honest in exactly the way she'd insist on. She never claimed more than her data showed, and her flashiest claims (the mental time travel stuff especially) are still genuinely contested. A toast declaring total victory would mortify her in front of the people who know better. Somehow the speech has to celebrate her without overattributing -- which is, of course, the canon's whole game, and the toast should notice that.
- Land on affection, not on the science.

Don't start with "when I first met Professor..." and don't end with a raised glass + a quote from anyone.
