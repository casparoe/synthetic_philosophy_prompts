Most European states abolished conscription after the Cold War and now rely on small professional armies recruited on the labor market, but Switzerland, Finland, and (after a hiatus) Sweden have retained or restored compulsory service built around a citizen militia: in Switzerland most able-bodied men serve in initial training and then remain in the reserve for years, keeping their issued equipment and liable to recall; in Finland military service is compulsory for men, with a non-military civilian service available to those who object; in Sweden conscription was reactivated and extended to women, with selection from the eligible cohort when not everyone is needed. All three pair this with a doctrine of armed neutrality or non-alignment — the army exists to deter invasion of one's own territory, not to fight abroad — and all three exempt, or provide alternative service for, conscientious objectors, while no state I know of offers an analogous exemption from the taxes that fund a professional army. I want to know what this package of institutions presupposes, philosophically. What theory of political obligation does it take for a state to be entitled to command years of its citizens' labor and risk their lives, when the same state would not dream of conscripting nurses or firefighters in peacetime? Does the militia model rest on a republican picture on which bearing arms is partly constitutive of citizenship rather than a cost of it, and if so, is that picture coherent with the exemption of objectors, which seems to treat service as a burden to be fairly distributed rather than a privilege to be shared? What does the gendered design of the Swiss and Finnish systems presuppose — about equality, about whose bodies the state may claim, or merely about logistics — and can those presuppositions be separated? Does the contrast between conscientious objection to service and the absence of any conscientious objection to taxation show that the practice treats killing as special, or that it treats the body as special, and are those the same commitment? And does armed neutrality presuppose anything interesting about the moral asymmetry between fighting in defense of one's own territory and fighting in alliances or abroad — an asymmetry a purely professional, expeditionary army seems to deny? Please reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with, flag any pair of elements that presuppose inconsistent things, and say what would have to change if the underlying view were replaced by a thoroughgoing liberal-contractual one on which military service is just an unusually dangerous job.
