I am the ethics officer of a mid-sized international development NGO working in about a dozen countries, and I need to replace our anti-corruption policy with something our field offices can actually use. The current policy was drafted at headquarters and essentially restates a Western compliance standard: no gifts above a token value, no facilitation payments, no preferential treatment of officials' relatives in hiring. Field directors report that applying it literally is unworkable — refusing a presented gift insults a host community, declining to hire a well-connected local candidate costs us access to an entire district, and in some places a small payment to a clerk is simply how routine paperwork moves. At the same time, "local practice is local practice" cannot be the answer either; some customary arrangements are plainly extraction dressed up as hospitality, and I do not want a policy that condemns a wedding gift while tolerating a shakedown merely because the shakedown is old.

Please write a decision procedure that a field office director — intelligent, not a philosopher, with perhaps an hour and no ability to consult me on routine matters — can apply to any proposed gift, payment, hire, or favor, to classify it as corruption, as legitimate local practice, or as a case requiring escalation. Ground it in the best available conceptual account of what makes corruption corruption: the misuse of an entrusted role for ends that are not the role's own. Build in the distinctions the literature treats as load-bearing — between a gift and a bribe (what return is expected, whether the exchange could survive publicity, whether the relationship or the office is its object), and between clientelistic exchange and ordinary reciprocal dealing — and state explicitly which of these distinctions must survive translation into local idiom and which can safely be dropped. Specify the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a refusal, where it terminates in approval, and the points at which it must honestly hand the matter to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than of classification.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a goat presented by village elders to a visiting government inspector; a twenty-dollar payment to a licensing clerk to process paperwork that is due to us anyway but routinely stalls without it; and the hiring of a district officer's son who genuinely meets the stated qualifications. Ensure that at least one case yields a verdict I will dislike — either permitting something that would look bad in our home country's press, or forbidding something a field office considers harmless — and close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do.
