So my partner and I rewatched The Matrix last week and it turned into one of those 2am arguments about whether a life lived entirely inside a simulation could actually count as a good life, and somewhere in there I realized I have a whole cluster of opinions in this area that I've never stress-tested for consistency. For a reading group I have to hand in a one-pager of my commitments on well-being, so let me just write down what I currently think and you can tell me wether the set hangs together or whether I'm kidding myself. Here it is:

(1) I'm basically a hedonist about well-being: the only things that contribute directly to how well someone's life goes are their conscious experiences. Though I'm with Mill that pleasures differ in quality, and I want to cash that out in terms of what the pleasure is a response to -- joy at a genuine accomplishment or genuine love is a higher pleasure than the same raw feeling with nothing behind it.

(2) Because of that, I wouldn't plug into Nozick's experience machine, and I take that to be a verdict about well-being specifically, not about morality or risk-aversion or squeamishness: a life in the machine would go worse for me beacuse my experiences of achievement and connection would be floating free of any actual achievements or actual people.

(3) On the other hand, someone born and raised inside a shared simulation (picture the Matrix except nobody's farming them, and the other people in it are real minds) can have a life just as good as a comparable life in base reality. Their friendships are genuinely friendships, and their accomplishments are real relative to the rules of the world they actually live in.

(4) Similarly, if I found out tomorrow that I've been in a simulation my whole life, I don't think that would reveal my life up to now to have been worse than I thought. My wedding still happened, my grief at my dad's death was a response to something real in my world, and so on.

(5) While a deception lasts, the deceived person is no worse off: someone happily married to a secret cheater is doing exactly as well as the person with the faithful spouse, experientially speaking. But when the truth comes out, it doesn't merely add new pain on top -- it retroactively makes all those apparently happy years worse than they seemed at the time.

(6) I'd want to know the truth about my situation even if knowing changed nothing about my experiences. Truth matters to me over and above well-being.

Audit these for me: which pairs genuinely pull against each other and how severe is each tension, which pairs look like they conflict but actually don't once you look closely, and for each real tension, what's the cheapest revision that resolves it and what does that revision cost me elsewhere in the set. Finish by telling me which single change would clean up the most. Fair warning: I'm roughly equally attached to all six, so don't expect me to be gracious about whatever you conclude.
