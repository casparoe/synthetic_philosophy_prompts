Next term I am teaching a graduate seminar on the ethics of risk, and in preparing the syllabus I have been struck by how philosophically inconsistent the ordinary practices of a single legal system appear to be. Consider the following patchwork, all drawn from familiar, common-knowledge features of contemporary Western law. First, criminal law punishes reckless endangerment as such: someone who fires a gun into the air over a crowded square, or drives drunk and arrives home without incident, is guilty of a serious offence even though no one was harmed. Second, tort law generally refuses compensation for unrealized risk: a person negligently exposed to a carcinogen who develops no illness, or who merely suffers anxiety about the elevated chance of future illness, typically has no claim, or only a very limited one. Third, everyday activities that predictably kill large numbers of non-consenting third parties — above all ordinary driving, which imposes small risks of death on pedestrians, cyclists, and other motorists who never agreed to bear them — are not merely tolerated but socially encouraged, provided the driver is licensed and insured. Fourth, in medicine the logic is reversed entirely: a surgeon who performs a beneficial procedure with a one-in-a-thousand risk of death, far smaller than the risk any commuter imposes on others weekly, commits battery if the patient has not given informed consent to exactly that risk. So unrealized risk is punished in one corner, ignored in another, licensed wholesale in a third, and treated as requiring individual consent in a fourth.

My question is what this patchwork presupposes, philosophically. One natural reconstruction is that the law treats risk imposition as a genuine wrong that consent can render permissible — but then the driving case looks like a massive, unconsented violation that we simply decline to enforce, and it is unclear why expected social benefit should launder it there but not in the operating theatre. Another reconstruction is that only realized harm matters, with recklessness punished purely as a deterrent proxy — but then the consent requirement in medicine, which governs the imposition of risk rather than harm, becomes mysterious, and the criminalization of harmless recklessness looks overinclusive. A third possibility is that the practices track some distinction between imposing a risk and merely failing to protect, or between risks imposed on identifiable individuals and risks diffused across a statistical population, or between ex ante and ex post perspectives — but I cannot tell whether any of these distinctions actually unifies the patchwork or merely describes it. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of risk imposition that this set of practices embodies; to distinguish carefully between what the practices strictly presuppose and what they merely sit comfortably with, since I suspect several rival theories — a rights-based view on which unconsented risk imposition is itself a violation, a straightforward expected-harm consequentialism, and perhaps a contractualist view keyed to what no one could reasonably reject — could each license most of the patchwork while diverging at its edges; to identify where the practice is genuinely underdetermined; and to say concretely what would have to change in each of the four corners if we replaced the implicit theory with each of the rivals.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order: "Reconstruction of the implicit theory" (roughly 300 words, stating your best candidate and the evidence for it); "Strict presupposition versus mere fit" (roughly 250 words, separating what the practices could not survive without from what is compatible with several theories); "Points of underdetermination" (roughly 200 words); "What each rival theory would change" (roughly 300 words, working through the four corners of practice for each rival); and "Your verdict" (roughly 150 words, saying whether the patchwork is coherent, incoherent, or merely in need of a principle no one has yet stated cleanly).
