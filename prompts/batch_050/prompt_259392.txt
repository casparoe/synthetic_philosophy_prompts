so my political philosophy seminar just wrapped up the luck egalitarianism unit — you know, the Dworkin-ish idea that society should compensate people for bad brute luck (things that just happen to you) but not for bad option luck (gambles you chose to take), so that the only inequalities left standing are the ones traceable to genuine choices. I want you to write a satirical piece set in a country that has actually, fully implemented this as public policy.

Specifically: write the official claims handbook of the (invented) Ministry of Distributive Fortune — the government agency that adjudicates every misfortune in citizens' lives as either brute or option luck before deciding whether anyone gets compensated. Think of the genre as a cross between an insurance policy document and a cheerful DMV pamphlet. Around 600-700 words, in American English.

The humor should come from the actual features of the theory, not from inventing strawmen. Some raw material you could work in (don't feel obliged to hit all of it):

- the intake forms and interviews needed to figure out whether your misery was really "chosen" (was your expensive taste for vintage wine something you endorsed having? did you forsee the ski slope being icy? was marrying him a gamble or a circumstance?)
- the skier with the broken leg being politely denied an ambulance because the slope was optional, and the agency's complete sincerity about this being compassionate
- the appeal tribunal where you must prove your preferences were formed outside your control
- the bureau that calculates how much of your personality counts as endowment
- a chipper FAQ tone throughout, completely deadpan

The satire should land the standard criticisms — the harshness objection, the intrusiveness of judging people's responsibility, the impossibility of ever cleanly seperating choice from circumstance — without ever naming them or breaking character. A luck egalitarian should be able to read it and feel the sting precisely because it's fair.

Structure the response in two sections, in this order:

1. The Handbook (600-700 words): the piece itself, formatted with a few titled subsections (e.g. filing procedures, the appeals process, frequently asked questions)
2. Adjuster's Notes (about 150 words): a brief out-of-character commentary on which real features of luck egalitarianism each joke exaggerates, so I can check you actually got the theory right
