I recently got into a late-night argument with two friends from my old reading group about whether we ever really know what we mean when we speak, and one of them -- who took a philosophy of language module years ago and speaks about it with great confidence -- gave us a summary of Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment that has been nagging at me ever since. His version ran roughly as follows: Imagine a planet exactly like Earth where the clear, drinkable liquid in rivers and taps is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ, indistinguishable from water by any test available before modern chemistry. Oscar on Earth and his molecule-for-molecule duplicate on Twin Earth are internally identical in every respect, yet when each says "water", Oscar's word refers to H2O and Twin Oscar's to XYZ, so their words differ in meaning despite their minds being the same. From this, he said, Putnam concluded that meaning is fixed entirely by the external world -- that what goes on inside a speaker's head contributes nothing to what their words mean -- and hence that Oscar in 1750, before anyone knew the chemical composition of water, did not actually know what he meant by the word; his meaning depended on worldly facts he was ignorant of, and the same holds for all of us, since none of us can personally verify the underlying nature of the things we talk about. The upshot, my friend declared, is that we routinely speak without knowing what we ourselves mean, and externalism thereby shows that first-person authority about one's own thoughts is an illusion.

The first part of the summary -- the set-up of the twin planet and the claim that the two Oscars' words differ in meaning -- seemed compelling to me, and I was inclined to grant it. But the further moves felt slippery: I find it hard to believe Putnam's position is that internal states are simply irrelevant to meaning, rather than merely insufficient to fix reference on their own, and the leap from "Oscar is ignorant of water's microstructure" to "Oscar does not know what he means" struck me as trading on two different senses of knowing what one means. Still, my friend is better read than I am, and I could not put my finger on what, if anything, he had distorted.

Could you audit his summary for accuracy? I would like to know which parts a careful proponent of semantic externalism would accept as stated, which parts are defensible simplifications of the sort one finds in competent popular accounts, and which parts Putnam would explicitly disown -- and, crucially, whether my friend's concluding inference about first-person authority follows from the accurate parts of the summary or only from a conflation embedded in the inaccurate ones. Please indicate how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the point in your assessment where you are least sure you have got Putnam's actual position right.
