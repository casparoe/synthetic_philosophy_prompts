I'm a TA for an undergrad philosophy of probability course (mix of philosophy majors and math-curious others, ~18 students, one 75-minute slot) and the professor put me in charge of the week on decision theory. I want to run the St. Petersburg paradox as an actual game instead of just showing the expected value calculation on a slide, because last semester I watched her lecture it and everyone's eyes glazed over at "log utility, therefore finite price." Here's the thing I want students to stumble into themselves: the paradox survives truncation. If you cap the game at, say, 20 coin flips, the expected value is still north of half a million dollars, and yet I guarantee nobody in that room would pay $60 to play it once. So the classic "utility diminishes" fix feels like it's answering the wrong question, or at least not the whole question. I found this out the hard way arguing with my roommate, who is an econ major and was very smug about Bernoulli until I asked him what he'd actually pay to play the capped version and he said like twelve bucks.

Design me the full activity. I want an auction or bidding mechanic where students bid tokens (not money, but something with mild stakes, maybe converts to chocolate or points toward some silly prize) for the right to play the game, first uncapped in principle, then with the cap revealed, then maybe a repeated-play version where they can play 50 times for a fixed price so the average payout starts doing something interesting. Give me concrete rules, exact payout tables I can put on a slide, how to handle the bank (I can't actually owe anyone 2^20 tokens, so how do I make that constraint part of the lesson rather than a logistical embarassment), and how to sequence the rounds so the students notice the weirdness in their own bidding behavior before I name anything. Ideally the room should end up in genuine disagreement about whether the "rational" bid is high or low.

Then the debrief: map each round onto a different attempted resolution (diminishing marginal utility, the finite-bankroll point, risk aversion as something distinct from utility curvature, whatever else fits) and tell me honestly which ones the activity actually puts pressure on vs. just illustrates. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I assume someone will try to collude, someone will bid everything as a joke, and someone will insist on calculating the EV out loud and ruin the reveal for everyone. And be straight with me about what the game can't reproduce: presumably the actual infinity, since no bank is infinite, and maybe something about one-shot vs. repeated choice that I can't capture in 75 minutes with tokens.
