I've been hanging around LessWrong and the adjacent corners of the internet for well over a decade, so I've had "don't throw good money after bad" thrown at me roughly a thousand times, usually as a conversation-ender. Last week it happened again in a way that actually stung: I told some friends I'm debating killing the side project I've put three years and a genuinely embarassing amount of money into (a spaced-repetition app that never found users), and two seperate people immediately hit me with the proverb, one adding that honoring sunk costs is "just definitionally irrational," case closed. And look, I probably should kill the project. But the more I think about it, the less I can say what the proverb is actually claiming, and I'm suspicious that it's doing way more rhetorical work than it has the content to support.

So: pretend you're a decision theorist who's also read the behavioral econ literature and watched a lot of actual startups die. Take the proverb seriously as a piece of language and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making. Off the top of my head there's at least: a descriptive claim that humans systematically over-weight past costs; a normative claim that only prospective costs and benefits should matter, full stop; a heuristic-policy claim that even if sunk costs are sometimes rationally relevant, the bias is so strong you should adopt a blanket rule against ever considering them; maybe a purely accounting-flavored reading. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, defensible-but-contentious, or just false, and give it a one-line verdict. Also, since the proverb smuggles in the word "good": does any reading actually survive the observation that whether the remaining money is "good" depends entirely on whether it buys a finished thing, which is exactly what's in dispute?

The part I care most about: which of these readings, if any, actually justify using the proverb to end the conversation about my project, versus which ones would only justify using it as a flag to double-check my reasoning. If there's a reading under which honoring sunk costs can be rational (self-signaling, commitments, finishing-as-identity, whatever), name it. Format: brainstorm the readings first, then give me the menu as a numbered list, most defensible reading first, whole answer under 700 words.
