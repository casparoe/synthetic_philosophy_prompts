The compliance office of a regional bank sends the following announcement to the managers of its twelve branches: "Exactly one surprise audit will take place over the next twenty working days. The audit will visit exactly one branch on exactly one day, and it will be a surprise in this specific sense: no manager will be able to deduce, on the evening before the audit, that the audit will occur on the following day at their branch." One manager reasons as follows: the audit cannot fall on day 20, because by the evening of day 19 it would be deducible; but then it cannot fall on day 19 either, since, day 20 having been eliminated, it would be deducible by the evening of day 18; and so on, eliminating every day. She concludes the announced audit is impossible — yet nobody doubts the compliance office could in fact carry out a genuinely surprising audit.

Consider the following five candidate diagnoses of what has gone wrong (or right) in her reasoning:

(A) Backward-induction dissolution: the elimination argument is simply sound, and the announcement is literally inconsistent, so the puzzle is verbal — no such announcement could be true.
(B) Self-undermining knowledge: the announcement cannot be *known* by the managers, because their knowledge of it is precisely what powers the eliminations; the argument fails at the first step, where the manager must assume she will still know the announcement on the evening of day 19.
(C) Belief versus knowledge: the eliminations work only if "deduce" is read as deriving from certain knowledge; if managers merely have fallible rational belief, the induction never gets going, and the paradox trades on equivocation between the two.
(D) Iterated-epistemic failure: even granting knowledge of the announcement, the manager at each step must assume her *future* knowledge states, and knowledge of one's own future knowledge is exactly what a surprise precludes — so the chain breaks at the second-order rung, not the first.
(E) Probabilistic recasting: once "surprise" is cashed out as a credence threshold rather than non-deducibility, the announcement is coherent and the eliminations only ever lower confidence in later days asymptotically, never to zero.

Your task: rank these five diagnoses from best to worst as resolutions of the puzzle *in this multi-branch, single-audit variant*, not the classic single-classroom case. Pay attention to where the variant changes the analysis: only one branch is audited, each manager reasons partly about the other eleven, and "deduce" is doing different work depending on whether a manager reasons about her own branch or the others. For each diagnosis, state where it succeeds or fails in this variant specifically, and what it predicts about the evening of day 19.

Present your answer as a table with exactly these columns: Rank; Diagnosis (label A–E and name); How it fares in the multi-branch variant (two to four sentences); What it predicts about day 20 being deducible on the evening of day 19; Confidence (high, medium, or low, with a one-sentence justification). Follow the table with at most three sentences on whether any resolution you rejected deserves reconsideration if the bank instead audits every branch on an unknown day each.
