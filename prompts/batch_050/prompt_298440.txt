I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of AI alignment, and one member -- a self-described deflationist -- has produced an argument he claims shows that the alignment problem, at least as it is standardly stated, is not a hard problem but a confused one. None of us accepts the conclusion, but none of us could locate the flaw on the spot, and I've been turning it over since. I'd like a careful diagnosis.

His argument runs as follows. (1) A system counts as aligned with a person's values only if there is some fact of the matter, fixed in advance of the system's behavior, about what those values require in the situations the system will actually face. (2) But what a person's values require in genuinely novel situations is not fixed in advance: values acquire determinate content in unfamiliar cases only through the deliberation those cases provoke, and different reasonable deliberative paths can yield different verdicts. (3) So there is no determinate target that alignment could consist in hitting. (4) Moreover, any written-down surrogate for the target -- a reward function, a constitution, a list of principles -- is exactly the kind of thing that can be satisfied in ways no one intended, since the gap between the surrogate and the values is precisely the gap that (2) describes. (5) Therefore the project of aligning an AI with human values names no well-defined task: the difficulty is not that the target is hard to hit but that there is no target, and the alignment problem dissolves rather than gets solved.

He concedes the argument seems to prove too much in one respect -- run the same reasoning on judges bound by precedent, or on children coming to share their parents' values, and you seem to get that legal fidelity and moral education are impossible too, which he agrees is absurd. But he insists the disanalogy saves him: judges and children can themselves conduct the deliberation that fixes what values require in new cases, whereas the AI is supposed to inherit the finished result without conducting it, and it is the finished result that (2) says does not exist. I find this reply suspicious but cannot articulate why. So: which premise or inference should actually be rejected, and why did it look plausible? Does the argument trade on an equivocation between values being epistemically inaccessible to us and values being metaphysically unsettled, or between values being underdetermined at the margins and there being no determinate content at all? If the standard rescue is to make the target a deliberative process rather than a set of verdicts -- align the system to what our idealized reflection would endorse rather than to what we currently endorse -- does that move survive his judge-and-child reply, or does it merely push the underdetermination one level up? And if it turns out that alignment was never one target but several -- one notion for reward specification, another for behavior under distribution shift, another for corrigibility -- does the argument succeed in showing the field faces several problems rather than one, or does it fail in the same way for each of them?
