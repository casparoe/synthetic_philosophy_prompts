I am writing a term paper on Kant's Perpetual Peace (1795) and have reconstructed, from premises Kant appears to endorse in the Third Definitive Article, an argument whose conclusion Kant himself explicitly rejects. I cannot decide whether the reconstruction misrepresents him or whether his position is genuinely unstable, and I would like a diagnosis.

(1) The earth's surface is finite and spherical. Human beings therefore cannot disperse so far as to avoid one another entirely; they must stand in possible relations of contact with everyone else, and no community can withdraw from this condition.

(2) Originally, no one has a greater right than anyone else to occupy any particular region of the earth. Possession of the earth's surface is originally in common: it belongs to no one individually and to all equally as the shared ground on which any rightful acquisition must take place.

(3) From (1) and (2) it follows that no stranger may be treated with hostility merely for arriving, and that every person has a right to offer others relations of commerce and exchange. Kant calls this the right of universal hospitality, and he is careful to say it is a right of visitation, not a right of settlement or membership in another's polity.

(4) Right, for Kant, is essentially connected with an authorization to coerce: a claim that cannot in principle be enforced against violators is not a right at all but a mere wish or a counsel of virtue.

(5) Cosmopolitan right is systematically and predictably violated by states, which claim the power to exclude, expel, and turn away strangers at their discretion. Moreover, in such disputes each state is judge in its own case, and Kant himself holds that judgment in one's own case is the mark of the state of nature, not of right.

(6) Therefore cosmopolitan right, like domestic right, can be fully secured only by a coercive authority standing above the parties: a world republic with binding jurisdiction over admission and expulsion, in which hospitality is a claim enforceable against states rather than a courtesy they extend.

(7) Yet Kant explicitly declines to endorse a world republic, recommending instead a voluntary and non-coercive federation of states, and he insists that the right of hospitality does not ripen into a right to remain, settle, or claim membership.

(8) So either Kant's own premises commit him to the very world state he rejects and to a far stronger right of admission than he allows, or one of steps (3) through (6) fails — and I cannot see which.

My question: where, if anywhere, does this argument go wrong? If it contains a defective step, identify it, explain why it looks sound, and say what Kant's system loses by repairing it there. If it is valid, tell me which premise Kant must surrender, and what the surrender costs the rest of his doctrine of right. If forced to choose, I would rather keep (2) and (5) than anything else — the original common possession of the earth and the problem of self-judging states both strike me as load-bearing — but I want to know what I am paying for them.
