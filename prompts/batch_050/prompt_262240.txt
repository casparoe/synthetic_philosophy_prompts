The episode: homosexuality was listed as a mental disorder in the first two editions of the APAs Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-I in 1952, DSM-II in 1968). After years of protests by gay activists at psychiatric conferences, and an internal review led by Robert Spitzer, the APA's board voted in December 1973 to remove it. A membership referendum in 1974 upheld the decision, though the manual kept residual categories for people distressed by their own orientation ("ego-dystonic homosexuality" in DSM-III) until those were dropped too in 1987.

Now analyze this episode through Ian Hacking's looping effect — his idea that human kinds, unlike natural kinds, are changed by being classified, so the classification and the classified interact and the kind itself shifts over time. Concretely:

1. Hacking's stock cases are things like multiple personality and fugue, where being classified seems to create or amplify the phenomenon. Here the causal arrow looks reversed: the classified people organized and changed the classification. Is that still a looping effect, or a different mechanism wearing the same clothes? What exactly has to loop for it to count?
2. The diagnosis was removed by a vote. What does the sheer fact that a purported disease entity can be voted out of existence do to the natural-kind reading of psychiatric categories? Does Hacking's framework predict this possibility, or is it silent on who holds the eraser?
3. After removal, the classified people arguably changed anyway — a stigmatized patient class turned into a public identity with its own norms, politics, ways of being. Does the looping effect also run when you erase a label, not just when you apply one?

And then the bigger question: does this episode confirm the looping framework, stretch it past what it was built for, or expose that it was never really about cases where the classified fight back? Be disciplined about where the theory speaks confidently and where you'd be forcing it to speak.
