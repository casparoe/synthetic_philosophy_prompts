My ethics reading group has spent three sessions on consequentialist cluelessness -- the worry that the remote consequences of any action are so vast and so unpredictable that expected-value reasoning cannot actually rank our options. One member keeps leaning on an analogy she borrows from forecasting: predicting the far-future consequences of an action, she says, is like predicting the weather a year from now. The atmosphere is a chaotic system; tiny differences in initial conditions amplify until, past a horizon of a couple of weeks, a forecast is worth no more than a coin flip. Just as a competent meteorologist simply declines to issue a day-by-day forecast for next spring rather than pretending to have one, a competent consequentialist should decline to count consequences beyond some near horizon and evaluate actions solely by their predictable short-run effects.

She deployed this analogy to settle a real dispute in the group. Half of us wanted our pooled donations to go to interventions with tightly measurable near-term effects; the other half favored speculative work aimed at very large, very distant stakes. Her verdict: the speculative option should receive nothing -- not because its expected value is low, but because the chaos analogy shows that its distant effects are not the sort of thing that can be valued at all, so the measurable option wins by default. She added one refinement: for any action, its remote effects branch out in every direction, with outcomes of unknown sign on both sides, and like measurement errors these cancel out, leaving the near-term effects as the only residue worth counting.

I pushed back using the weather/climate distinction: the same science that makes a one-year daily forecast impossible makes thirty-year climate projections robust, because statistical aggregates can be predictable exactly when individual trajectories are not. If distant moral consequences have an analogous statistical structure, her analogy might support the opposite of her conclusion. She replied that moral causation has no counterpart to the laws of thermodynamics from which such aggregates could be derived, so the climate branch of the analogy simply does not extend to our case.

Please audit the analogy, not the underlying debate in general. Specifically: (1) give an explicit correspondence table -- what plays the role of initial conditions, of the chaotic dynamics, of the forecast horizon, of the forecaster's model, and of the decision the forecast is supposed to inform; (2) identify which disanalogies matter for the particular use she makes of it (licensing the refusal to count distant effects, and hence the donation verdict) and which are irrelevant to that use; (3) assess the "errors cancel" refinement on its own terms -- is it a feature of the analogy or an extra premise smuggled in alongside it, and does chaos actually imply it; (4) adjudicate the weather-versus-climate exchange: does the climate rejoinder genuinely undermine her use of the analogy, or does her thermodynamics reply succeed; (5) if the analogy cannot carry her conclusion, state what conclusion, if any, it can carry, and whether a better analogy from the same domain would do the job she wants done.
