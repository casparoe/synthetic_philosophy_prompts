So I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a scene, which is how I ended up down this rabbit hole. In my draft, a woman finds out -- believably, within the story's logic -- that if she conceives a child in the next year or so, that child will have a condition that means a genuinely good life but a short one, ending around thirty. If she waits a few years (different partner, different circumstances, long story), she'll have a different, healthy kid. She picks the first option, knowingly. My writing group keeps calling her monstrous and I keep feeling like they're wrong but I can't say why in a way that survives their pushback.

Then I rewatched Arrival (the Villeneuve one, with Amy Adams) and realized Louise is basically my character: she sees that Hannah will die young and has her anyway, and the film frames it as profound rather than selfish. So: read Arrival through the nonidentity problem -- Parfit's thing, where you can't say the choice was worse *for the child*, since the alternative wasn't the same child existing healthily, it was a different child existing at all. I want a reading that's loyal to both sides: what the film actually dramatizes about this (the "despite knowing the journey" logic, the way the nonlinear time thing reframes "choice" altogether), where the film quietly dodges the hard version (is it cheating that Louise *sees* the life rather than just being told odds? does foreknowledge-as-experience smuggle in the child's perspective in a way real procreative decisions can't?), and where the nonidentity framework itself strains against the film -- like, would the film hit the same if Hannah's life were clearly below some threshold of worth, and does the movie smuggle in a person-affecting intuition that Parfit would call confused?

Structure it like this, and keep it in American English:

1. The setup (~150 words): the film's choice and my novelist's dilemma, side by side.
2. Where nonidentity fits (~300 words): what the framework gets right about both stories, including why my writing group's "she harmed Hannah" reaction misfires.
3. Where the film cheats (~250 words): the foreknowledge stuff, the sentimental framing, anything else that lets it avoid the actual hard question.
4. Where the framework strains (~250 words): what the film makes vivid that the analytic argument flattens, and whether that's a point for the film or just movie magic.
5. Notes for my scene (~150 words): concrete takeaways for how my character should defend herself -- or admit she can't.
