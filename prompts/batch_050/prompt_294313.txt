For the past year I have been writing a dissertation chapter on panpsychism, and the experience has left me genuinely puzzled about the state of the combination problem debate. Here is what the dialectic looks like to me, as concretely as I can put it. The critic's side runs roughly: even granting that fundamental physical entities have primitive experiences, there is no intelligible account of how a vast number of tiny subjects and their tiny experiences could add up to, constitute, or give rise to the single unified field of experience I am having right now; experiences do not seem to be the kind of thing that sums, the way masses or charges do, and gesturing at structural or functional organization does not close that gap. The panpsychist side runs roughly: this is an open research problem, not a refutation; the conceivability arguments against physicalism show that experience has to be fundamental somewhere, and the combination problem is simply the price of that conclusion, with candidate solutions on the table ranging from phenomenal bonding relations to cosmopsychism, where the big subject comes first and small subjects are derived. Both sides seem to agree about every step of the other side's argument and to have agreed for decades — the core worry is recognizably the one William James pressed in the 1890s, and nothing since appears to have shifted the burden an inch. Nobody updates; people just sort into camps early and stay.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and not a conviction, is that the disagreement is not really about combination at all but about what one is permitted to take as explanatorily primitive. The critic treats the unsummability of experiences as showing that the view posits something unintelligible; the panpsychist treats the hard problem as showing that some unintelligibility-about-emergence is unavoidable wherever you start, so you might as well locate it at the bottom. If that is right, the debate is a clash of explanatory standards wearing the costume of an argument about a specific mechanism, which would explain why no new argument ever lands. But I can also see the case that it is a genuine substantive dispute about a modal or metaphysical fact — whether composition of subjects is possible — that we simply lack the tools to settle, or the case that it hides an empirical question about the neural or informational conditions under which unified experience actually occurs.

Please give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict on panpsychism itself, and do not survey the literature. Structure the response in four sections, in this order: "The shape of the impasse" (about 150 words, confirming or correcting my description of why neither side's arguments move the other); "Diagnosis" (about 250 words: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, a clash of explanatory standards, two different questions under one heading, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for — with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, and an assessment of my hypothesis); "What would count as progress" (about 150 words: a distinction, an empirical result, a formal constraint, a change of question, or an argument that nothing could settle it); and "Bottom line" (about 75 words, stating in plain terms what kind of disagreement this is and whether my chapter should treat the combination problem as fatal, tractable, or ill-posed).
