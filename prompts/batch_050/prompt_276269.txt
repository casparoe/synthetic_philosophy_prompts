Here are my considered verdicts on when it is rational to stop searching for options and take the first one that clears a threshold, and I would like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them.

(1) A chess player under time pressure finds a move that is clearly good, glances for ten seconds to see if something obviously better exists, and plays it rather than searching deeper. Perfectly rational, even though the position is objectively complicated.

(2) A hiring committee, having interviewed forty candidates, offers the job to the next one who is clearly above the bar rather than interviewing the remaining sixty. Perfectly rational.

(3) A surgeon, having formed a diagnosis that adequately explains a patient's symptoms, orders treatment without considering rarer conditions that would also fit, in a case where a missed alternative diagnosis would be fatal and an hour of further thought costs nothing. Not rational; she should keep searching.

(4) A person choosing whom to marry decides that once they find a partner meeting their standards, further sampling of the dating pool is pointless and slightly insulting. Rational.

(5) A family fleeing a wildfire takes the first adequate evacuation route that comes to mind rather than spending three minutes comparing routes on a map. Rational, even though the stakes could not be higher.

(6) An engineer signs off on a bridge design that meets every code and safety margin without investigating whether an unconventional design would meet them more cheaply. Rational.

(7) A consumer buys the first laptop that satisfies her stated requirements, in a market where an hour of searching would predictably save her four hundred dollars and her time is worth less than that. Not rational; she is being lazy, not bounded.

Note the tensions I cannot resolve on my own. The wildfire case (5) is maximally high-stakes, yet stopping early seems right, so "keep searching when the stakes are high" cannot be the principle. The chess player (1) and the surgeon (3) both face complex problems where further search is cheap in absolute terms, yet I reach opposite verdicts. And the engineer (6) and the consumer (7) look structurally similar — a threshold is met, further search has predictable value — yet I judge them differently, and I cannot say whether the difference is that the engineer's obligations run to others while the consumer's loss falls on herself, or something else.

What principle captures the largest defensible subset of these verdicts? I suspect the answer has to do with whether further search has a stable expected value relative to its cost, but that formulation seems to convict (5), since three minutes of route comparison plausibly has enormous expected value under a wildfire, and I do not want to convict (5). A better answer might turn on whether the search space is well-ordered enough that "expected value of further search" is even a coherent quantity — perhaps one can rationally stop whenever the value of continuing is genuinely inestimable, which would save (1), (2), and (5) but threaten (4) and (6). State your candidate principle precisely, identify which clause does the work in each case, and tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts it must overturn. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: a government agency that adopts the first regulatory impact assessment whose projected benefits exceed projected costs, without modelling alternative regulations.
