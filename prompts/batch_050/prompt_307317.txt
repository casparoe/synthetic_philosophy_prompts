I run a monthly philosophy-of-religion reading group — mostly graduate students, with a few sharp undergraduates who keep showing up — and next session we've assigned David Lewis's short late paper on penal substitution. They will all have read it, but past experience tells me they will have read it as a curiosity: an amusing collision between atonement theology and personal-identity metaphysics, rather than as an argument with a load-bearing structure. I have seventy-five minutes, and I don't want to spend them summarizing a paper everyone has in front of them. What I want is a plan for how I should walk them through the argument so that its logic becomes visible — the order in which the pieces have to be put on the table, and why that order.

As I read it, the paper's architecture is roughly this: retributive common sense holds that only the guilty may be punished, which makes substitutionary punishment look not merely unjust but unintelligible, a category error rather than a crime; Lewis's countermove is to reach for his own metaphysics of persons — perdurance, personal identity as psychological continuity, and his treatment of fission and fusion cases in which a single stage can belong to more than one continuant person — so that, under the right stage-sharing arrangement, the one punished can literally be the one who sinned, and the slogan that the guilty party himself suffers comes out true rather than metaphorical; and then there is the residual question of whether this metaphysical availability actually delivers what the doctrine wanted, or merely a nearby simulacrum. Please tell me which of these pieces depends on which (does the retributive constraint have to precede the fission machinery, or can the metaphysics go first and the puzzle be retrofitted?), which single example should carry the session — I'm torn between starting with the mundane case of paying another person's fine and starting with a science-fiction fusion story, and I suspect one of them is a trap — and which misconceptions to expect, in the order the group will produce them. My own predictions: they'll think Lewis is defending the truth of a Christian doctrine rather than testing its coherence; they'll conflate penal substitution with rival theories of atonement they half-remember; and they'll smuggle in endurantist assumptions that make fusion look like the creation of a third person instead of overlap. Tell me where in the sequence each of these should be headed off, and which ones I'm missing.

Two further requests. First, give me one question I can pose in the final ten minutes whose answer would reveal whether they've grasped the argument's structure — as opposed to having enjoyed the ride — and tell me what a good answer versus a tell-tale bad answer sounds like. Second, tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level and say so to the group: whether, for instance, the theological literature on the doctrine, or Lewis's counterpart theory lurking in the background, is a productive detour or a swamp for a session like this. Don't re-teach me the paper; plan the session.
