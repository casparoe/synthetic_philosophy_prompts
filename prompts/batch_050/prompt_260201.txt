I am part of a monthly philosophy reading group, and at our last session we got into a genuinely heated exchange that I want to reconstruct carefully. One member, who defends illusionism about phenomenal consciousness (she has been reading Dennett and Frankish), argued her case almost entirely through a single analogy: phenomenal consciousness is like the desktop of a computer. The files, folders, and trash can on your screen are not literally inside the machine; they are a simplified interface that lets you get work done without knowing anything about voltages, caches, and transistors. Likewise, she said, introspection presents your experiences to you as having intrinsic, ineffable qualities -- the particular redness of red -- but nothing in the brain literally has such qualities. They are the brain's user interface to itself, a schematic misrepresentation that is useful rather than accurate, and once you see this, the hard problem of consciousness dissolves: there is nothing more to explain than why the brain would represent itself to itself in this distorted way. Another member, who accepts physicalism but balks at illusionism, objected that the analogy cheats at exactly the crucial point: a desktop is an illusion presented to a genuinely conscious user, so the analogy presupposes precisely the thing it claims to explain away, and calling the seeming itself an illusion just relocates the mystery, since a misrepresentation of red is still something it is like something to have.

Please audit this analogy as thoroughly as you can. Start with an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the user, the desktop icons, the underlying circuitry, the simplification for practical purposes -- and note where the illusionist herself would have to get creative to keep the table filled in (in particular, who or what is the "user" of the brain's interface supposed to be, and does the interface metaphor survive if the answer is "another part of the same system"?). Then identify which disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support and which are superficial. Consider specifically: whether the computer desktop is genuinely a case of "illusion" in the needed sense or merely of abstraction and compression (a folder icon is a coarse-grained but accurate summary of something real, whereas the illusionist seems to need qualia to be flatly nonexistent); whether the analogy quietly imports a homunculus; and whether the objection that "seeming is itself a phenomenal state" is a decisive refutation or whether the illusionist has a principled reply available (e.g., that "seeming" can be cashed out in purely functional or dispositional terms, and what that move costs). Finally, tell me whether the dissolution of the hard problem survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along; and if the desktop analogy fails, say whether some other analogy -- a mirage, a rainbow, the self-model of a thermostat -- would serve the illusionist better, and why.

Please write your answer in American English, and feel free to take a definite position at the end rather than splitting the difference.
