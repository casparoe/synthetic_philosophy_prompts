Fourteen months ago my father suffered a stroke that left him with what his neurologist calls pain asymbolia: he still reports feeling pain, but the pain no longer troubles him. Watching this up close has unsettled some things I thought I understood about the mind, and I have been reading philosophy in the evenings to catch up with my own bewilderment. I am a retired schoolteacher, not a philosopher, but the carers' charity that has been helping our family runs a small magazine, and I have drafted an essay for it. Before I submit it, I would like rigorous comments from someone who actually knows this territory. The essay is below; my requests follow it.

The Unbothered Patient

Since his stroke, my father reports pain without minding it. His arthritic hand still hurts, he says, exactly as it always did; the feeling itself is unchanged. But he does not wince, does not guard the hand, does not ask for his paracetamol, and can describe the pain, smiling, the way one might describe weather in another county. The sensation survives while its sting — the part of pain that insists, that demands — is gone.

Trying to understand what the stroke has taken from him led me to epiphenomenalism: the view that conscious experiences are genuine occurrences, produced by the brain, but themselves producing nothing. On this view the felt quality of my father's pain never once moved a muscle or shaped a word. Everything he does, including everything he says about his pain, is the causal upshot of neuronal machinery alone.

This invites a difficulty given its sharpest modern form by David Chalmers: the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If experiences are causally inert, my judgment that I am in pain cannot be explained by the pain I feel; it must be explained wholly by physical events that would unfold identically whether or not the feeling accompanied them. The epiphenomenalist thus seems committed to an uncomfortable picture: our beliefs about our own experience are formed in complete independence of the experience they are about. Some conclude that the view must treat those beliefs as systematically false.

My father's case, I think, sharpens the paradox in a way I have not seen captured elsewhere. Call what follows the separability argument. If epiphenomenalism is true, his judgment "it hurts" is the output of physical machinery. The stroke demonstrates that this machinery is modular: it destroyed the machinery of minding while leaving intact both the feeling (if his reports are to be trusted) and the reporting. The reporting module is one thing; the minding module another.

But then there is no reason to expect the reporting module to be more tightly bound to the correlate of the feeling than it was to the correlate of the minding. Somewhere in the space of possible brain injuries there should be a patient whose reporting module fires — who sincerely announces pain, believes it, argues about it over lunch — while feeling nothing at all. The epiphenomenalist cannot rule this patient out by appeal to the feeling, since the feeling, ex hypothesi, causes nothing, and therefore cannot be what keeps our reports honest.

Each of us trusts our own judgments about our experience. But if such a patient is possible, that trust cannot rest on the experience itself; it rests on the contingent integrity of a report module that a stroke could sever tomorrow. What rests on luck in this way is not knowledge. The epiphenomenalist must therefore deny that anyone — including the epiphenomenalist — knows that they are in pain. A theory motivated by taking experience at face value ends by making experience unknowable. It undermines itself.

When I hold my father's hand and he tells me it hurts but is, in his words, quite alright, I have to decide whether to believe him. I have come to think that I believe him because I trust him, and that trust between persons is older and sturdier than any theory of causation. Whatever the philosophers conclude, it gets no vote at the bedside.

The magazine's readers are mostly carers and clinicians rather than philosophers, so the essay needs to survive expert scrutiny while remaining readable. With that in mind, I would like roughly 600–800 words of comments addressing the following:

1. Accuracy and charity. Have I stated epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment correctly? Please identify the single most serious misstatement in the essay, explain what the correct position is, and tell me whether the error is cosmetic or whether the later argument leans on it.

2. The separability argument. Is it valid, and which premise or inference is weakest? In particular, I would like your view on a worry I cannot settle myself: the stroke shows something about the brain's modularity, which any physicalist also accepts, so does the argument actually cut against epiphenomenalism specifically, or against everyone? Please construct the strongest reply an epiphenomenalist could make and say whether it succeeds.

3. The final inference. I move from "the trust would rest on luck" to "no one knows they are in pain" and then to "the view undermines itself". Does either step follow? If the conclusion is too strong, what is the strongest conclusion the argument genuinely supports?

4. The closing paragraph. Does the move from the theory to the bedside earn its place, or is it sentimentality bolted onto an argument that cannot carry it? If the latter, suggest how the essay should end instead.

5. One cut and one addition. If I could remove one passage and add one, what should they be?

I am after reasons rather than references, so there is no need for citations or suggested readings — I would not be able to check them properly in any case. Please write your comments in British English.
