I’m a novelist, and I’ve foolishly agreed to give the closing speech at a small science-and-literature festival, right after a panel with two comparative psychologists and one philosopher who studies animal minds. My new book has sections narrated by a scrub-jay, and the moderator has already warned me the room will be split: half people who can say Morgan’s canon in their sleep and hate cute anthropomorphism, half readers who mostly want to know whether the bird loved anyone back. Please write me a seven-to-eight minute speech for that exact room, casual but not sloppy, no fake authorities, no bibliography vibes, and definately no claiming that fiction settles whether animals are conscious. The spine of the speech should be a principle I want to introduce called a budget of interiority: a novelist gets only a small allowance of inferred mindedness, and must spend it where the observed behavior would be badly explained by cheaper machinery, while every poetic interior metaphor has to be repayable in concrete detail on the page.

Build it around one scene from the book: the jay hides a seed, watches a rival watch him, later re-hides it only when the rival is absent, and the reader is tempted to call this deception, foresight, maybe guilt. The speech needs to show how Morgan’s canon changes what I am allowed to say without making the scene emotionally dead: distinguishing depicting from diagnosing, treating human narration as a flashlight that casts shadows, admitting that the cheapest adequate mechanism may still be the true one, and explaining why a story can be answerable to animal behavior even when it cannot testify about animal experience. End with a short coda that says, plainly, what would change my mind: which experimental result or which literary-critical objection would make me cut the interiority from the scene, and where my own defense is most likely to be wishful thinking dressed up as restraint.
