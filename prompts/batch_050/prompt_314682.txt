I convene a monthly philosophy reading group for adults with no formal training in the subject -- thoughtful, well-read people, but nobody who has studied aesthetics. Next month we're doing Hume on taste, and I've volunteered to lead. The group will have read a short extract beforehand but, if last month is any guide, half of them will arrive convinced that Hume has shown beauty to be merely in the eye of the beholder, and the other half will arrive convinced he has shown that some people are simply right about art and everyone else is wrong -- which is to say, both halves will have missed what makes the essay interesting.

Don't give me a lecture on Hume; plan how I should run the ninety minutes. Specifically:

- What has to be established first, before the question of a standard of taste can even be posed to them? I'm guessing they need to see that Hume takes judgements of beauty to be sentiments rather than discoveries of fact, and that the puzzle only arises because he nonetheless refuses to treat all sentiments as equal -- but tell me if I've got the dependency wrong, or if something else has to be in place first.
- The one example to build the whole session around. It needs to be something where the group will genuinely disagree in judgement (not a case where everyone politely converges), and it must let us dramatise Hume's list of what disqualifies a critic -- prejudice, want of practice, lack of delicacy -- without the example itself deciding anything. Should I use a canonical artwork, a contested modern one, or something deliberately low-stakes like food or humour? Say which, and what the example must have and must lack.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll surface. I expect: first, that Hume is a straight relativist; second, once corrected, that he's an elitist handing down verdicts; third, that the true judges are meant to be identifiable people we could poll. Where in the session should each be headed off, and what should I actually say or do at that point rather than merely asserting otherwise?
- What to do with the obvious objection the room will raise -- that Hume's criteria for true judges are circular, since we seem to identify good judges by their agreement with good taste -- and whether to raise it myself if nobody does, or let it go.
- One question to close on that would show whether they've grasped the structure of Hume's position -- sentiment plus standard -- rather than merely picked a side.
- What I should openly set aside at this level (the comparison with moral judgement, say, or the metaphysics of secondary qualities) and say I'm setting aside.

Please answer in British English. And a standing request: if anything in how I've framed the evening rests on a misreading of Hume, or if ninety minutes with this group can't do what I'm asking of it, tell me that directly and replan accordingly rather than politely working around it.
