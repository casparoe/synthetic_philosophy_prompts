I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of mind seminar, and we just finished the unit on eliminative materialism (the Churchlands, Stich, and some replies). One of my classmates has written up an argument that she thinks improves on the standard eliminativist case, and I am stuck: I cannot accept the conclusion, but I cannot cleanly locate the mistake either. I would appreciate a diagnosis that identifies the weakest step, explains why it looks innocent, and prices the alternatives.

Here is the argument, as she states it.

(1) A predicate picks out a real feature of the world only if, for any clearly described case, there is a fact of the matter about whether the predicate applies — a fact that does not depend on who is doing the describing, what language they describe in, or what they happen to care about.

(2) Belief fails this test. Take the translation puzzle we discussed in class: a man raised in France, on the basis of everything he has heard, sincerely declares in French that London is lovely; years later, having moved to a drab corner of that city and learned English by immersion rather than translation, he sincerely declares in English that London is not lovely. Ask whether he believes that London is pretty. Roughly half the class said yes (on the French evidence), half said no (on the English evidence), and there seems to be nothing further in his brain, his behavior, or his history that would settle the question — only a decision about which description to privilege.

(3) Such cases are not exotic. Because attributing a belief always involves translating someone's words and dispositions into our own idiom, and translation always leaves slack, a puzzle of this form can be generated for essentially any attribution of content.

(4) So there is no ascriber-independent fact of the matter about what anyone believes (from 1–3).

(5) A discourse that purports to state facts, but has no facts to state, should be discarded rather than reinterpreted. That is what we did with talk of witches, humors, and phlogiston, and consistency demands the same treatment here.

(6) Therefore we should stop positing beliefs, and any discipline that quantifies over them — folk psychology, but also decision theory and Bayesian epistemology — is to that extent built on nothing.

Here is why I think the argument proves too much. The same template runs on money: there seems to be no describer-independent fact about whether cigarettes circulating in a prisoner-of-war camp were money, or whether some newly invented digital token is. It runs on biological species, given ring populations where neighboring groups interbreed but the endpoints do not. It runs on persons, given fission cases, and on mountains, if we ask where exactly one mountain ends and the next begins. If my classmate is right, then most of the special sciences — and ordinary person-talk — go the way of witchcraft. She is willing to bite that bullet; I am not.

So: which premise or step should I give up, and what does each option cost? Two things I would like to keep, if possible, are the legitimacy of decision theory and the ordinary claim that witches do not exist. My own suspicion is that the trouble lies either in (1), which sets the bar for reality very high, or somewhere in the move from our attribution practices leaving slack (2–3) to there being nothing there at all (4) — but I cannot make either diagnosis precise. In particular: if I weaken (1) so that interest-relative or description-dependent kinds can still count as real, do I lose the principled basis for saying that witches and phlogiston really do not exist, as opposed to merely being kinds we no longer find it useful to talk about? And if instead I insist that there is a determinate fact about what the man in the puzzle believes, what kind of fact could that possibly be, and what would a theory have to look like to ground it? Please do not just name a position in the literature — walk me through the trade-offs premise by premise.
