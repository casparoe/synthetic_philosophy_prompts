I recently read Josephus's account of the fall of Masada in Book VII of The Wars of the Jews (Whiston translation), and one sentence has stayed with me. After describing how the defenders killed their own families and then one another by lot, Josephus writes: "Yet was there an ancient woman, and another who was of kin to Eleazar, and superior to most women in prudence and learning, with five children, who had concealed themselves in caverns under ground, and had carried water thither for their drink, and were hidden there when the rest were intent upon the slaughter of one another."

Write a dialogue between two thoughtful people disagreeing about what to make of this. One holds that the modern Israeli memory of Masada as a symbol of heroic national resistance is a legitimate use of the past, and that no nation is obliged to let awkward details dictate its self-understanding; the other holds that the Masada myth works exactly like other "lost cause" myths -- the Confederacy, Dunkirk -- by quietly dropping the facts that complicate the preferred moral, and that the seven survivors in the cisterns are precisely the detail that gives the game away. Each should respond to the other's actual arguments, and both should engage directly with the quoted passage, including what it implies about whose choices get remembered as the nation's. Neither should be made to look foolish. Format the dialogue as exactly five numbered exchanges (two speeches each), with no introduction or conclusion.
