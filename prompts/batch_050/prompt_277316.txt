So my reading group spent all of last week on Bernard Williams' Internal and External Reasons and I tried to turn his view into a clean argument, and now I'm stuck because the argument seems to prove way more than anyone in the group will accept. Quick recap of the setup as we understood it: Williams' internalism says, roughly, that someone has a reason to do something only if there's a route of sound deliberation from the motivations she actually has — what Williams calls her subjective motivational set — to being motivated to do it. Desires, loyalties, projects, dispositions, whatever's in the set. From that, a friend and I built this:

(1) A has a reason to φ only if A could, by sound deliberation, come to be motivated to φ starting from the motivations A actually has.
(2) Sound deliberation can only correct false empirical beliefs and trace means-end connections to things A already cares about — it can't manufacture ends out of nothing, since any process that produced a new end would just be conditioning, not deliberation.
(3) Consider Owen: content, well-informed about the plain facts, and caring only about his own comfort. Nothing he cares about is served by keeping his promises, and no means-end chain runs from comfort to promise-keeping.
(4) So Owen has no reason to keep his promises.
(5) If Owen has no reason to keep them, then telling him he ought to, or criticizing him for breaking them, can't be stating anything about his reasons — at best it's an attempt to install a new motivation in him, i.e. venting or manipulation dressed up in normative language.
(6) But the same holds for anyone and any norm: run it with prudence (imagine Owen is genuinly indifferent to his future self, so no reason to save for retirement) and even with epistemic norms (if he doesn't care about truth, no reason to believe what his evidence supports).
(7) So nobody ever has a reason — moral, prudential, or epistemic — that isn't already underwritten by their actual motivations, and every piece of criticism addressed to someone who lacks the right motivation is just an attempt to push their psychology around.
(8) And then two people who "disagree" about what Owen has reason to do are really just disagreeing about, or talking past each other about, Owen's psychology, which is a boring empirical matter — so substantive normative disagreement evaporates entirely.

Conclusion (8) seems absurd to me, but here's the extra wrinkle: as far as I can tell Williams himself basically accepts (4) — his example of the man who is cruel to his wife and can't be reached by deliberation is supposed to show that we can't honestly say such a person has a reason to treat her better. So maybe the argument isn't attacking internalism at all but just correctly extracting its price? Either way, tell me which step you'd reject if you had to reject one, why that step looked fine when I was building the argument, and what rejecting it costs — for instance whether denying (2) commits me to some story about deliberation creating ends (Korsgaard-ish constitutivism?), whether denying (5) lets an internalist keep criticism as something other than manipulation, and whether (8) trades on conflating what makes reason-claims true with what they mean. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than accept that "Owen has a reason to be decent" is just noise, but I want to know what I'm paying for that. And whichever step you finger as the culprit, say what would change your mind — what feature of deliberation or of criticism would have to be different for the argument to go through after all, and where your diagnosis is most likely to fail.
