For a reading group on hinge epistemology I drafted the following argument against the position associated with Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and I now suspect it proves too much, though I cannot locate the error. (1) A hinge, on the standard reading of the text, is a proposition that stands fast for a person or community: it is never seriously doubted in practice, and chains of justification and doubt simply come to an end at it. (2) Justification and doubt are activities that only get a grip against a background of hinges; to evaluate any belief one must hold something else fixed, so no belief can be evaluated all the way down. (3) Therefore, for any believer, a proposition that stands fast for them cannot be rationally evaluated by them, and — since any outside critic must likewise argue from the critic's own hinges, which the believer need not share — cannot be rationally pressed against them from the outside either. (4) There are actual communities for which propositions such as the earth being only a few thousand years old, or a particular ritual securing the harvest, stand fast in precisely this sense: members never seriously doubt them, and all inquiry within the community proceeds around them rather than toward them. (5) By (1) through (3), those propositions are hinges for those communities, and no criticism, internal or external, can rationally get a grip on the people who hold them. (6) But this is absurd. It implies that sheer fixity of conviction immunizes a belief against rational criticism, that no community's fixed points could ever be faulted from anywhere, and that correction or conversion across frameworks is impossible in principle rather than merely difficult. (7) So hinge epistemology, at least on the standard reading, collapses into a relativism that no party to the debate accepts, and something in (1) through (3) must be wrong. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced to choose, I would rather keep the Wittgensteinian insight in (2) and the empirical observation in (4) than the inference to (3), but I want to know what conception of hinges I am committing myself to by giving that inference up, why it looked innocuous, and whether the standard reading of On Certainty can survive without it.
