I've read LessWrong and its neighboring blogs for over a decade, and one reflex they've trained into me is flinching whenever a price moves while the physical object underneath it stays exactly the same. Which brings me to the art market and its treatment of attribution.

The practice I have in mind: when a painting long accepted as the work of an Old Master is reattributed to a follower, a copyist, or a forger, its market price collapses by orders of magnitude essentially overnight, and museums typically pull it from the walls, even though not a single pigment molecule has changed. Notably, the collapse is steepest for forgers who didn't copy any existing work but painted something new in the master's manner — objects that, on any purely visual measure, add to the world's stock of beauty rather than merely duplicating it. Meanwhile, the same institutions happily display casts and replicas when they're openly labeled as such, and visitors will queue for hours to see an original while walking past a perceptually indistinguishable reproduction without breaking stride.

Here is my question: what theory of aesthetic value does this practice presuppose? Several candidates occur to me, and I'd like you to consider at least these before adding your own: (1) an experientialist view on which value supervenes entirely on the experience the object delivers, under which the practice looks simply irrational; (2) a genetic or historical view on which an object's causal history — who made it, when, and with what intentions — is partly constitutive of its aesthetic properties, so that the reattributed painting literally is a different aesthetic object than we thought; (3) a relational view on which appreciating art is a kind of communion with another mind, so that a forgery is a lie and the communion turns out to have been with someone else entirely; and (4) a Hanson-style signaling account on which originals are positional goods and all the talk of aura and authenticity is confabulation draped over a status market. These candidates diverge on a hard case I'd specifically like addressed: suppose a laboratory accident — a malfunctioning robotic arm working with period-correct pigments — produced a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a newly discovered masterwork. Should its value equal the original's? Your favored theory had better have a non-embarrassing answer.

Act as a philosopher of art who is also fluent in economics and in the rationalist idiom I'm writing in. Work carefully: first brainstorm the space of candidate implicit theories, including the four above, and check each against the full pattern of the practice — the overnight price collapse, the banishment to storage, the tolerance of labeled replicas, the queue for the original. Then do the thing my years on the forums have taught me to value most: commit. Tell me which single reconstruction best explains the pattern, acknowledge the parts of the practice it can't explain, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and give your verdict on whether the presupposed theory is actually defensible or whether the art world's behavior should be filed under collective bias dressed up as connoisseurship. I'd rather read a decisive answer I can fight with than a balanced menu of options.
