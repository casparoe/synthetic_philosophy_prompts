At a party last weekend two friends of mine got into an argument about pleasure that got way more technical than I ever expected, and it ended with both of them turning to me to settle it, which I couldnt. I want to lay out the exchange as fairly as I can remember it and get a proper refereeing.

It started when Maya said something like: think of how different pleasures are -- a warm bath, getting a joke, the taste of chocolate, finishing a hard project, hearing a song you love. Since we lump all of these under one word and treat them as the same kind of thing, there must be something they all share, some felt quality, a kind of "glow" that's present in each of them. That's what makes them pleasures.

Ben pushed back immediately. He said there obviously isn't any shared feeling. What chocolate tastes like and what solving a puzzle feels like have nothing in common as experiences, and if you doubt it, do a subtraction: take the chocolate pleasure and peel away everything chocolatey about it, the taste, the texture, the sweetness. There's no leftover "pleasure part" sitting there on its own. The pleasure isn't an extra ingredient in the experience. His own view was that what unifies pleasures isn't how they feel but how we relate to them: a pleasure is an experience you want more of, or want to continue, for its own sake. Pleasures are the class of experiences with that pull, and that's the whole story.

Maya said that can't be right, because enjoying something and wanting it come apart in both directions. A person can want an experience to continue for non-pleasure reasons -- sitting through a boring lecture out of duty, say. And more interestingly, you can have a pleasure you don't want at all: someone who catches herself relishing a bit of nasty gossip and is embarrassed by it, or an ex-smoker who takes one drag, finds it wonderful, and sincerely wishes it weren't. She enjoys it, full stop, while wanting not to. So the pull can't be what pleasure is.

Ben said those cases just show you have to distinguish levels of wanting -- the ex-smoker wants the cigarette in one sense and doesn't in another, and the pleasure lives in the first. He also returned to the subtraction move and said Maya's glow is undetectable: if there really were a common feeling, you'd be able to notice it across cases the way you can notice redness across a rose and a fire truck, and nobody can do that with pleasure.

Then Maya made what I thought was her best move. She said pleasures can be compared in intensity *across* totally different types. You can say the massage was more pleasant than the meal, and mean something about how they felt, not just about your choices. How could there be a common scale of how-good-it-feels unless there's a common dimension of feeling being measured? Two things with zero qualitative overlap shouldn't be rankable at all.

Ben answered that comparison doesn't require a shared quality, just a fact about which one you'd pick. You can rank a poem against a sunset as things to spend an evening on without there being some shared "evening-quality" they're both made of. Rankings come from preferences, and preferences don't need common ingredients.

At that point Maya proposed a thought experiment, which is where they got really stuck. Imagine a pill that produces a sensation everyone describes, while it's happening, as intensely pleasant -- they say so, their faces say so, there's no hidden downside. But here's the catch: not because of any side effect or bad memory, just as a brute fact, nobody who takes it ever wants to take it again. They shrug about it afterwards. If that scenario is coherent, she said, then pleasantness can't just be the pull, since here's pleasure with zero pull. Ben said the scenario is simply incoherent -- if nobody is ever moved toward it in any way whatsoever, even in the moment, then in what sense was it pleasant, and the "they say it's pleasant" part is just words. Maya said he was building his own theory into the definition of the word and begging the question. Ben then offered a counter-pill: a sensation people compulsively seek out and can't stop pursuing but uniformly report not liking at all, seeking without any glow. If that one's coherent, the pull isn't enough for pleasure either, which is a problem for him but also shows the two ingredients just come apart and maybe neither theory works. Maya said that pill is actually coherent, it's basically the familiar shape of certain addictions, and it shows pull isn't sufficient, but it does nothing against her view, since her view only needs the glow, which the second pill lacks and the first pill has.

So they asked me: who's winning. Honestly I could follow each individual step but couldn't put it together. Could you go through the exchange point by point and say who's right about what -- the subtraction argument, the ex-smoker/gossip cases, whether cross-type intensity comparisons need a shared felt dimension, and especially whether each of the two pills is a coherent scenario and what each one would show if it is? And if there's something both of them are missing -- some option neither considered for what pleasure might be -- I'd want to hear that too, rather than just a verdict on their two positions.
