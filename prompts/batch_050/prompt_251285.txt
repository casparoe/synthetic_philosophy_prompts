I co-run an informal reading group for grad students from other departments (mostly math and CS people, a couple of linguists) who want to read some analytic metaphysics. We've done universals and possible worlds fine, but next session is on me: Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and I'm honestly nervous, because last time I tried to explain it informally at a pub half the table concluded it was "just a semantic trick about sentences" and the other half thought it was a point about nothing ever causing anything. Both wrong, but wrong in instructive ways, so I want to plan the thing properly this time.

Give me a teaching plan, not the explanation itself -- roughly 40 minutes, whiteboard, no handouts. What I want specifically: the order the pieces have to come in, and why that order. E.g. my instinct is that the distinction between a mere list of constituents (Socrates, wisdom) and a unified fact or proposition (Socrates *is wise*) has to fully land before the regress is even intelligible, but maybe there's a better entry point -- you tell me. Then the one example to build the whole thing around: it needs to be simple and relational enough that the regress can be generated live on the board, but I'd like advice on what it must *lack* too -- e.g. I worry that using anything involving a mind or a speaker lets people think the unity is supplied by consciousness or by linguistic convention, which dodges the problem. Should the example be a relational fact like a-left-of-b rather than a monadic one, and does that choice matter for how the regress comes out?

Also the misconception map: the people in this group will predictably (a) assimilate it to an infinite causal regress, like first-cause stuff, (b) think it's a puzzle about grammar that dissolves once you distinguish sentences from propositions, (c) think you can just *stipulate* "the fact" as one more entity and be done, and (d) think Bradley must have been confused because obviously wholes are just parts. For each, tell me where in the sequence it will naturally arise and how the structure of the session should absorb it rather than me firefighting at the end. Then a check for understanding -- I'd love something where they have to generate the regress themselves on a fresh case rather than parrot it back, since that's what would convince me the idea landed. Finally: what should I openly bracket at this level? I assume Bradley's own positive view (the Absolute, the unreality of relations) is too much for one session, and maybe also the modern fixes (tropes, non-relational unity, truthmaker talk) -- but tell me if refusing to mention any of these would actually leave them with a distorted picture, since some of these folks will go home and google.
