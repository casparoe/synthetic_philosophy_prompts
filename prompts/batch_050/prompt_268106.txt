Okay so I'm a novelist, I write alternate history, and I have this friend Dan — actual historian, PhD and everything — who has appointed himself my realism police. Before I outline anything these days I pitch him the divergence point, and he tells me whether my premise respects how history actually works or whether I'm doing what he calls "great man fanfic." We've done this enough times now that I want to figure out his rule in advance, partly to save time and partly to beat him in the argument for once.

His verdicts on my last four pitches:

- I pitched "the Sarajevo assassination never happens, no Franz Ferdinand plot, no WWI." Shot down in about ten seconds. He said the alliance system, the arms race, the whole setup was a loaded spring, and if it wasn't that crisis it would've been some other one within a few years. Verdict: not a hinge.
- I pitched "the Channel weather in June 1944 stays terrible, Overlord gets scrubbed or fails on the beaches, and the whole postwar map comes out different." He said yes, THAT one is a genuine hinge — the invasion really could have failed or been pushed into a completely different political situation, and everything downstream moves. Verdict: hinge.
- I pitched "young Newton dies, calculus never gets invented, physics stalls for a century." He laughed at me. Said Leibniz was right there, the math shows up within a couple decades regardless, and ideas are like that — they arrive when the world is ready for them. Verdict: not a hinge.
- I pitched "Gutenberg dies early, print culture delayed fifty years, no Reformation as we know it." He said presses were being circled by multiple people, you delay things a bit at most, and the Reformation happens in some form anyway. Verdict: not a hinge.

Now my new premise, wich I love and do not want to give up: the 1281 typhoon that wrecked Kublai Khan's second invasion fleet heading for Japan never forms, the fleet lands intact, and my book is set in a Mongol-ruled Japan a few generations later. Before I burn three months outlining, tell me what Dan's going to say. I genuinely can't tell — it has weather in it like the D-Day one, but it also smells like the kind of thing he'd call a delivery mechanism rather than a cause, the way he talked about Sarajevo. And if his track record honestly underdetermines it, tell me that straight, and tell me what principle I'd be signing him up for depending on wich way I guess.

Structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order, and keep it in American English:

1. **The prediction** (~60 words): bottom line first — does Dan green-light the premise or kill it, and how sure are you?
2. **The rules he might be running** (~220 words): at least two, ideally three different rules that are each consistent with all four of his verdicts. State each rule cleanly enough that I could apply it myself next time.
3. **Rule by rule on the typhoon** (~220 words): what each rule says about my Mongol-Japan premise, and where they diverge.
4. **Closest precedent** (~80 words): which of the four past verdicts my case is actually most analogous to, and why.
5. **The tiebreaker** (~90 words): one further divergence point — real history or a plausible invention — where his verdict would tell me which rule he's actually using.
