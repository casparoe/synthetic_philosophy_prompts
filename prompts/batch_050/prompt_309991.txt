i've been thinking about tranquilism, which i only know from a few essays and conversations, so forgive me if this is a clumsy summary: the good isn't pleasure as a little plus on some cosmic scoreboard, but the absence of craving and suffering. Satisfying a desire is more like removing a lack than adding a bonus, and a calm experience where nothing is missing isn't somehow worth less than an exciting one.

Write a 16-line poem in terza rima -- five tercets and then one final line -- where the form itself enacts that arguement. Let the middle rhyme of each stanza set up the rhyme the next stanza has to answer, like one appetite generating the next. Then let the last line stand without creating a new demand for rhyme, so the poem's release feels like craving stopping rather than pleasure winning.

Set it somewhere ordinary, maybe someone washing dishes after a day of chasing snacks, notifications, praise, and small purchases. Don't make it sound like numbness, death, or a mystical high, and don't imply that other people's suffering stops mattering once the speaker is calm. It also shouldn't turn into a poem about Buddhism as such. Ideally someone familiar with tranquilism would recognize the view in how the poem moves, even if the word itself never appears.
