For a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, write the specification an adequate theory of biological function must satisfy, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. By "adequate" I mean: good enough that working biologists' ordinary function attributions (the heart's function is to pump blood; the enzyme's function is to cleave that substrate) come out as non-accidentally correct, and good enough that the theory earns its place in explanation rather than merely redescribing it.

I want the list built around the cases that actually discriminate between the two great families of accounts — selectionist or etiological views, which ground function in a history of selection, and systemic or causal-role views, which ground it in a part's contribution to a containing system's capacity. In particular, make sure your desiderata engage at least the following test cases, and state which requirement each one forces:

1. Malfunction: a heart that fails to pump blood still has pumping as its function. Any account on which a token's function is just whatever it in fact does cannot say this, so something has to be required that decouples function from actual performance.
2. The wedging stone: suppose a pebble lodges in a duct in a way that happens to keep a valve open, and the organism thereby survives. The pebble's presence has the same beneficial effect as a genuine organ might, yet we want to deny that keeping the valve open is the pebble's function. What requirement draws this line, and does it draw it in the right place for the first-token case below?
3. First-generation novelty: a population is placed in a new environment and develops, by purely developmental plasticity, a trait that is beneficial and that every individual acquires — before any selection on genetic variants underlying it has occurred. Does the trait have a function? Etiological accounts appear forced to say no; systemic accounts yes. State explicitly which desideratum each verdict satisfies and which it violates, because this is where I suspect the desiderata conflict.
4. Exaptation: a structure evolved for one purpose is co-opted for another. How soon after co-option can the new role be a function, and what does each family of accounts have to say about the lag?
5. The designed organism: a synthetic biologist assembles a novel organism de novo, with parts chosen for roles. If function requires selection history, do its parts have functions? If not, either the theory must bite that bullet or there must be a principled extension to artifacts and designed systems — decide which the specification should demand.
6. Functional attribution without selective detail: physiologists confidently ascribed functions (the liver detoxifies, the kidney filters) long before anything was known of their evolutionary histories. Must an adequate theory vindicate those attributions independently of historical knowledge, or is it an acceptable result that such attributions were at best provisional until the selection story arrived?

Beyond the cases, include the standing structural desiderata: that functions be explanatory of the item's presence or of the system's capacity (and say which of these two explanatory burdens the theory should take on, since the two families split precisely here); that the account not overgenerate functions to every beneficial side-effect; and that it respect the distinction between a trait's function and its merely fortuitous effects.

For each desideratum, give the consideration behind it, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp, and — this is the part I most care about — say whether the full list is jointly satisfiable. My suspicion is that the desiderata forced by cases (2) and (3) are in tension: whatever excludes the wedging stone by appeal to history also excludes the first-generation novelty, and whatever admits the novelty readmits the stone. If that is right, say so and say which requirement should be weakened and why; if it is wrong, show the requirement that threads between them. Finally, note which single desideratum does the most excluding work against the two families as they are actually defended — the one a proponent of each family would have to surrender first.
