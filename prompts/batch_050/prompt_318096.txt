I'm a junior serving this semester as one of the student members of the board that runs our university's Emergency Grant Fund, a small pool of student-fee money that makes one-time grants to students in sudden crisis. I'm also taking an ethics seminar that just finished its unit on utilitarianism and its critics, including Williams on integrity and impartiality, which is making my actual job on this board harder than it should be. I'd like help drafting the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting.

The facts: at the end of term we had exactly one grant left, $900, and two applications the whole board agreed were equally strong on the merits. One student needed a last-minute flight to reach a dying parent; the other had lost medication and a laptop in an apartment flood two weeks before finals. Our procedures call for a majority vote with reasons given. When we voted, our treasurer, a graduating senior with an otherwise spotless record, voted for the first applicant and stated openly that the applicant was her closest friend and that this was her reason. Her vote made the difference, and the grant went to her friend.

Three board members now want to censure her and adopt a rule requiring recusal from any case involving a friend. Their argument is straightforward: fund officers owe every student impartial treatment, voting on personal loyalty is precisely the corruption our procedures exist to prevent, and her stated reason is a confession of it. Her response is what interests me philosophically. She doesn't offer a principle, like the claim that obligations to friends can override institutional duties. She says she shouldn't need one: if the friendship itself isn't a sufficient reason to act for her friend, something has already gone wrong, and being forced to justify the vote in impersonal terms would be a deeper betrayal than the vote itself. She adds that she would rather vote honestly on her real reason than manufacture a merits-based rationale she doesn't believe, which is what a recusal rule would quietly train everyone else to do.

My seminar leaves me half-convinced there's something right in her response, the thought that a friend who first had to check whether impartial morality permits helping you would be thinking one thought too many. But I can't shake the censure side either: the money isn't hers, the role isn't about her friendships, and "she's my friend" is the reason every corrupt official in history has given. Tentatively I think either the vote was defensible and the way she defended it wasn't, or the reverse, and I can't yet say which. Draft the opinion: a clear recommendation on both the censure motion and the proposed recusal rule, with reasons the board could actually act on, taking her defense seriously rather than treating it as a confession, and giving the strongest version of the censure case before answering it. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter to the board, since our secretary handles the formatting later anyway.
