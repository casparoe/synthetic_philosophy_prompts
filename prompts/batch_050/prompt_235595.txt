I'm a high school senior doing a year-long independent study in philosophy, supervised loosely by a teacher who admits this topic is over her head, and I've decided to focus on epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness -- the view that subjective experience (what it's like to see red or feel pain) is produced by physical processes in the brain but itself produces nothing in return. What got me hooked is the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment: if my experiences cause nothing, then my judging, saying, and writing down that I have experiences would unfold in exactly the same way whether or not I actually had any. So it looks like the epiphenomenalist's own belief that they are conscious can't be trusted, since the belief isn't caused by the consciousness it claims to be about -- and yet the belief seems like the most certain thing any of us has.

I have one semester left and need to choose which threads to actually pull on. These are the open questions I keep circling:

1. Whether the paradox is genuinely fatal to the view, or whether a judgment can be about an experience, and be reliable, through some relation other than causation -- for instance, if the judgment and the experience share a common physical cause, or if part of the experience is somehow built into the judgment itself. If any of these work, the paradox may rest on an assumption about knowledge that should be stated and defended rather than smuggled in.

2. Whether the problem is really specific to epiphenomenalism at all, or whether versions of it reappear for other views -- say, for an interactionist dualist who still has to explain how a nonphysical experience hooks up with the brain's report-making machinery, or even for a physicalist who holds that experiences are brain states but insists we know them in a special first-person way.

3. Whether the strongest form of the worry is self-defeat: not just that our judgments about consciousness would be unreliable, but that the argument for epiphenomenalism itself could never give anyone a reason to accept it, since accepting it would undercut the introspective evidence the argument starts from.

4. Whether biting the bullet is survivable -- that is, conceding that our reports about experience aren't to be trusted and rebuilding the view on that basis -- or whether that concession quietly dissolves the very phenomenon the theory was supposed to explain.

What I'd like from you is a defended ordering of these four questions for my purposes: which one or two deserve my remaining semester, and in what sequence. Please rank them by how much the other questions depend on their answers (what's upstream of what), by how tractable each is for a student whose background is an introductory course and careful reading rather than technical training, and by how much a satisfying answer would actually change the overall case for or against epiphenomenalism. If you think I'm missing a question that outranks all of these, say so and explain why. And tell me what would change your ranking -- for instance, if a particular line of response to question 1 turned out to work, what would that do to the rest of the list?

Please write your answer in American English. I'm not asking for a reading list or citations -- I want the reasoning itself, laid out carefully enough that I can structure my final paper around it.
