I'm a PhD candidate in epistemology, and this semester I'm the teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on disagreement and higher-order evidence. The instructor has asked me to grade the midterm essays, and I'd like a second opinion on one of them before I finalize my marks, since I want to make sure I'm neither rewarding superficial fluency nor penalizing a student for being merely incomplete rather than wrong. Below I've reproduced the exam question verbatim, followed by the student's full answer. Please assign a grade on a standard A–F scale (feel free to use +/- gradations), and — this is the part I care most about — provide the specific, point-by-point comments you would write in the margins to justify that grade. I'd like the assessment to distinguish carefully between claims that are correct, claims that are imprecise but defensible, claims that misattribute a position to the wrong philosopher, and inferential steps that simply don't follow, since I suspect this essay contains a mix of all four.

Exam question: "Two epistemic peers — equally intelligent, equally well-informed, and equally familiar with one another's track records — examine the same body of evidence and arrive at different credences in some proposition p. Explain the Equal Weight View and the Total Evidence View as responses to this situation, making clear what each view prescribes and how each handles the higher-order evidence provided by the disagreement itself. Then assess one serious objection to either view."

Student's answer: "The Equal Weight View, defended by Thomas Kelly, holds that when you discover a peer disagrees with you, you should give your peer's conclusion the same weight as your own, which in practice means adopting a credence at the midpoint between yours and theirs. Kelly's argument is that your own reasoning about p is no more likely to be reliable than your peer's, so it would be arbitrary to privilege your own judgment. The Total Evidence View, defended by Adam Elga, holds instead that you should respond to the totality of your evidence, which includes both the first-order evidence about p and the higher-order fact that your peer disagrees. Because the peer's dissent is itself evidence bearing on p, Elga argues that the rational response need not be to split the difference; depending on how strongly the first-order evidence supports your conclusion, you might move only slightly toward your peer or even remain steadfast. A serious objection to the Equal Weight View is the so-called spinelessness problem: if you must always move halfway toward every disagreeing peer, then in a world of pervasive disagreement you will end up holding no convictions at all. This objection is decisive, because the Equal Weight View is itself a philosophical position on which its proponents disagree with many equally capable philosophers, so the view instructs its own defenders to abandon it, which shows it is self-refuting and therefore false. I conclude that the Total Evidence View is correct, since it permits remaining steadfast in the face of disagreement, which is what rationality clearly requires."

For context: the course covered the standard literature at the level of the survey chapters and primary readings one would assign in such a seminar, and the grading rubric allocates roughly equal weight to accuracy of exposition, quality of the objection and its assessment, and cogency of the overall argument. I'd also appreciate, at the end, a one-paragraph summary comment of the sort I could return to the student.
