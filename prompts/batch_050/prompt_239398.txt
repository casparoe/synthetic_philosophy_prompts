I'm in a dispute with a friend from my reading group and it's gone past the point where either of us can see straight. We were reading Wang Yangming on the unity of knowledge and action, and she built an argument she thinks just follows from his view:

(1) Wang's point is that real knowledge of the good is inseparable from doing it. Someone who says they know what filial piety is but acts unfilially toward their parents doesn't actually know it yet. All of his examples work like this: you only know pain if you've felt it yourself, you only know what something tastes like by tasting it. Knowing and acting are two names for one thing.

(2) So whenever someone does wrong, they didn't really know the act was wrong. If they'd actually known, in Wang's sense, they would've acted otherwise. (This is just (1) contraposed.)

(3) Blame requires that the person knew better. Someone who genuinely didn't know that what they were doing was wrong is ignorant rather than wicked, and ignorance excuses.

(4) So no wrongdoer is ever blameworthy. Every wrong act traces back to a failure of knowledge, and nobody is on the hook for what they couldn't have known.

(5) So blame and punishment are never justfied. At most, wrongdoers need teaching — their minds are clouded, and you cure cloudedness with instruction, not penalties.

Her conclusion is that Wang's doctrine, taken seriously, abolishes moral responsibility. She admits the argument proves too much — it would excuse the corrupt official skimming disaster-relief funds, the general who sacks a city, literally anyone — but says that's her point: either the unity thesis is false or moral responsibility is an illusion, and she's inclined to keep the thesis and ditch responsibility.

I feel like the trick is somewhere between (2) and (4) but I can't pin it down. I'd like to keep Wang's unity thesis if I can, since it seems to me the most interesting thing in his whole philosophy, and I'd also like to keep blaming people who obviously knew better. Which premise or step should I reject, and why does it look so plausible on the way in? What does rejecting it cost me? And does Wang himself even have the resources to block the argument — does his picture of the mind as a mirror clouded by selfish desires leave room for a *culpable* failure to know, or does it just push the same problem back one step?
