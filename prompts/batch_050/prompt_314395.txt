I teach a short AI module in a master's program in public policy; the students have no technical background but solid training in regulation and institutional design. One fifty-minute session is devoted to the alignment problem — what exactly is being asked when people say AI systems should be aligned, and whether that request names one problem or several distinct ones. Do not write the lecture for me; plan how I should build it, and make definite choices rather than offering me a menu of options.

Concretely, I want: (1) the order in which the component ideas must be introduced, with reasons — what has to be in place before the gap between what a system is capable of and what it is aimed at can even appear to the students as a problem rather than a failure of engineering diligence, and whether the thought that sufficiently general objectives tend to imply similar instrumental subgoals should precede or follow the thought that objectives are hard to specify; (2) one carrying example to run through the entire session — not a paperclip factory and not King Midas — ideally something mundane and near-term drawn from domains they know (say, an automated system optimizing a regulatory or administrative target), with an account of what the example must contain (a goal that is easy to state and genuinely reasonable to want, a visible gap between the stated goal and what a capable optimizer does with it) and what it must avoid (superintelligence, deliberate deception, anything that lets the room dismiss the case as remote science fiction); (3) the misconceptions the students will predictably bring — I expect at least these: alignment means making AI systems polite or inoffensive, it is ordinary software quality assurance under a new name, it is just a synonym for AI safety generally, and the worry only arises if the AI has desires of its own — and for each one, the point in the sequence where it will naturally surface and how the ordering should head it off rather than patch it afterward; (4) a committed decision on whether the distinction between specifying the right objective and getting a trained system to actually internalize it (outer versus inner alignment) earns a place in a non-technical room, with a defense of including or excluding it; (5) a plan for the session's ending that takes a definite position on the framing question — one problem or several — and shows how the sequence sets that verdict up instead of bolting it on at the close; (6) one question I can pose in the last five minutes whose answers will reveal whether the room has grasped the problem or merely absorbed the vocabulary; and (7) what I should openly decline to cover at this level — I suspect consciousness, machine sentience, and timelines — and how to say so without making the topic sound evasive.
