I'm preparing a two-hour session on Simpson's paradox for a mixed group of graduate students in public policy and data science, and I want help designing the explanatory plan rather than the explanation itself. Most of them have seen the paradox stated -- a trend that holds in every subgroup reverses when the groups are pooled -- and many have glancingly encountered the Berkeley admissions example, but my sense is they treat it as a party trick about arithmetic rather than something with bite. What I actually want them to leave with is the harder and less commonly taught lesson: that the paradox is not resolved by any statistical rule at all, because whether you should trust the aggregated or the disaggregated numbers depends on the causal structure of the situation, and in some cases disaggregating is precisely the wrong move. I want the session to build toward a case where controlling for a variable manufactures a reversal rather than correcting one, so that "always condition on the third variable" dies as a heuristic rather than merely being qualified.

Please plan the arc of the session. What concepts have to be securely in place before the reversal itself will even register as surprising -- I'm assuming something like marginal versus conditional rates, and the intuition that pooled rates are weighted averages of group rates with weights that can differ across the comparison -- and in what order should those go? What is the right carrying example? I'd rather not use Berkeley admissions, both because it's stale and because I suspect it quietly teaches the wrong lesson by making the disaggregated answer obviously the morally right one. The example needs to make the reversal visceral, and I need a second example, parallel on the surface, where the causal arrows run differently so that the aggregated number is the one to trust -- what pair of examples would let me flip students between them? Map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they'll arise: my guesses are "the larger dataset is always more informative," "a reversal means someone made an aggregation error," "any variable associated with both treatment and outcome is a confounder," and later, once I introduce colliders, "so we can never control for anything," and I want to know at exactly which point in the sequence to head each off rather than letting it harden.

Finally: what should the end-of-session check look like, such that passing it shows they grasp that the resolution is causal and not statistical, rather than that they can mechanically recompute group rates? I'm imagining giving them a novel scenario with a reversal and withholding the causal story, then asking what additional information would tell them which table to trust -- but tell me if that's too easy to game with pattern-matching. And tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level: I assume the full apparatus of do-calculus and formal causal graphs is beyond two hours, but I'm unsure whether I can even gesture at back-door criteria without opening that door.
