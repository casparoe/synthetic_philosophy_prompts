I became a parent eight months ago, and since then nearly every piece of advice I have received — from books, pediatricians, relatives, strangers in cafés — has moved in a single breath from a claim about what is natural to a command about what I must do: babies are born needing X, therefore you ought to Y. I have come to suspect that the inference never actually occurs; the two clauses merely sit adjacent, and the word "natural" is smuggled in as though it were itself a recommendation. Write me a sonnet that enacts this. The octave should accumulate purely descriptive claims about infants and exhausted parents — no normative vocabulary anywhere, only is. Where the volta should arrive, the poem must attempt the turn to ought and fail: the sestet keeps reaching for a duty and finding only another fact. Let the form do the work — the octave's rhymes interlocked and self-sufficient, the closing couplet the poem's only unrhymed pair, so that the join the argument needs is sonically absent. The final lines should hold the gap open rather than leap it. Someone who knows the is–ought problem should recognize it immediately; someone who doesn't should simply read a good poem about being a new parent drowning in advice.
