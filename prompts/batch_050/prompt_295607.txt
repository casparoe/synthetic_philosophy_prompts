A colleague in my political theory reading group has built an argument that seems to prove too much, and neither of us can locate the weak joint. (1) A mass death counts as a political crime rather than a natural disaster whenever the relevant authorities could have prevented it at reasonable cost and failed to; the immediate physical trigger, whether drought, blight, or flood, is morally irrelevant to the classification. (2) Sen's entitlement analysis of the 1943 Bengal famine showed that the deaths resulted from a collapse of what people could command, through wartime inflation, speculative hoarding, and the government's denial policies, rather than from an absolute shortage of food; so those deaths were political, not natural. (3) But entitlement failures of exactly the same kind, operating through prices, wages, and trade and welfare policy, also explain why people die steadily of ordinary malnutrition and easily treated illness in the same countries in non-famine years. (4) So those ordinary, chronic deaths are political crimes too, on a par with famine deaths. (5) Nor does the reasoning stop within any country's borders or at the category of hunger: a moderately competent state can nearly always insure harvests against weather through storage, imports, and early warning, so essentially every food-shortage death we have ever casually called natural was preventable, and therefore political. (6) The conclusion dissolves the category of natural disaster altogether and makes virtually every government in history a mass criminal, which my colleague cheerfully accepts and I cannot. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (2) than anything else. Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the claim in your answer you are least sure of.
