I'm a second-year physics graduate student taking a philosophy of physics seminar on the side, and there's a sentence I keep hearing in both buildings: only gauge-invariant quantities are physical. Most recently it was deployed against me at journal club when I asked whether the vector potential A, rather than just E and B, should be thought of as the more fundamental field in light of the Aharonov-Bohm effect -- a postdoc shut the question down with the slogan, and the discussion moved on. But I've also heard the same sentence used to mean something much more innocent: that the freedom to shift A by a gradient is just a redundancy in our notation, like choosing coordinates, and that no one should be surprised that differently-described calculations agree. These seem like very different claims, and I suspect people slide between them. I want to know which reading, if any, actually licenses shutting my question down.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing -- from the near-trivial (gauge-dependent descriptions are many-to-one representations of a single physical state) through the moderate (only invariants can be measured, or only invariants should appear in the fundamental laws) to the radical (gauge-dependent quantities are not merely redundant but literally meaningless, so that asking about A's value at a point is like asking about the average number of legs on a triangle)? For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or an overreach, and test it against the cases that I think put pressure on it: the Aharonov-Bohm effect, where the gauge-invariant holonomy of A seems to do the work but A itself keeps showing up in the explanation; large gauge transformations and charges at spatial infinity, which people tell me make some gauge-related structure physically meaningful after all; and the textbook story of the Higgs mechanism, where the Higgs field is described as taking a definite value even though that value looks gauge-dependent.

Finally, give me a verdict on the dialectical use I encountered: does any defensible reading of the slogan actually settle whether A is real or fundamental, or does every version that would settle it either beg the question or prove too much? If the honest answer is that the slogan marks the boundary of the debate rather than resolving it -- i.e., that different interpretations of gauge theory draw the line between redundancy and surplus structure in different places -- I'd like you to sketch what those options look like and what hangs on choosing among them, so I can go back to journal club with something better than a shrug.
