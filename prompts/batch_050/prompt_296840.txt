You are a philosopher of science whose specialty is the foundations of probability and statistical inference, with long experience chairing workshops between warring camps. Think carefully and work through the conceptual terrain before you draft anything; the quality of this task lives entirely in the precision of the formulations.

Two colleagues of yours have agreed to settle their long-running dispute through an adversarial collaboration, and they have asked you to draft the joint statement both will sign. The first is a strict hypothetical frequentist: probability statements are about limiting frequencies in infinite sequences of trials under fixed conditions, and claims that cannot be cashed out that way are not probability claims at all. The second is a personalist Bayesian in the tradition of Ramsey and de Finetti: probabilities are coherent degrees of belief, constrained by the avoidance of sure loss and by the demand that one update on evidence, and the frequentist's infinite sequences are unobservable fictions. They have argued for years and keep talking past each other. Your job is to write the document that ends that.

The document must contain, in this order:

1. A short prefatory section in which you brainstorm, for each camp, the two or three points on which you judge its position strongest and the one concession it will find most painful but must make for the collaboration to be honest. This is for your own use in calibrating the statement, so be candid rather than diplomatic here.

2. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. These should be substantive, not diplomatic mush: for instance, both can presumably accept the standard probability axioms as a shared formalism while disagreeing about what the axioms are axioms of; both can accept some version of the result connecting exchangeable degrees of belief with limiting frequencies, while disagreeing about which side of that connection is fundamental; and both should be able to agree on an operational core -- a set of prescriptions about how anyone, whatever their interpretation, ought to behave (refusing sure-loss contracts, letting observed frequencies discipline one's assignments, and so on). For each agreed claim, add one sentence noting what each side takes the claim to show, since they will typically read the same sentence differently.

3. A disagreements table with four columns: the issue; the frequentist's position, phrased in terms the frequentist would accept as fair; the subjectivist's position, likewise; and a verdict on whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal. The table must include at least: single-case probabilities (the probability that this particular already-built bridge collapses within a decade); probabilities about fully determined but unknown facts (whether a specified digit far out in the decimal expansion of pi is a 7); and whether probability is a feature of the world or of a state of information. Where you mark a disagreement verbal, explain in one or two sentences what each side was actually asserting.

4. A cruxes section. Identify the three or four sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one side to revise its view. For each crux, specify a case, argument, or possible finding that both parties agree in advance would bear on it, and record each side's prediction about how it comes out. At least one crux must be organized around the following test case, which the collaborators have agreed to treat as neutral ground: a laboratory contains two sealed devices. Device Q generates its output by a quantum measurement. Device C generates its output by a deterministic chaotic mechanism whose exact initial condition was set once by a technician and then irretrievably forgotten; its output sequence passes every statistical test for randomness that exists. A third envelope contains an output from one of the devices, already generated, already fixed, not yet read. The collaborators must state, for each device and for the envelope, whether "the probability of the next (or sealed) output being a one is one-half" says the same kind of thing in all three cases, what each camp is committed to saying, and whether any experiment or argument could distinguish their readings -- with each side's prediction written down.

Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. The standard of success is that each collaborator, reading only their own side's formulations, would say yes, that is what I think, and reading the agreed claims, would not feel that anything essential was traded away for peace.
