DRAFT: "Press play on the work itself"

Standard ontology of music, the kind you'd meet in an analytic aesthetics seminar, treats a musical work as an abstract object: a type, or a sound-structure, or a structure-plus-instrumentation "indicated" by a composer at a time (Levinson's line, roughly). Performances are tokens of the type. This works fine for Beethoven. Nobody thinks the Eroica is any particular performance, and the score underdetermines the work anyway.

My claim in this essay: in pop, rock, hip-hop and electronic music, the work is the recording. Not a type of which the recording is a token — the recording itself, as a repeatable artifact. When you press play you are not hearing a performance of "A Day in the Life"; you're encountering the work directly, the way you encounter a film. Gracyk argued something like this in the 90s and I think everyone in analytic aesthetics has spent 30 years refusing to update.

Three payoffs. First, it explains why a live cover of a studio song is automatically a derivative object: you literally cannot perform the original, since the original includes the specific reverb, the tape splice, the exact vocal take. Second, it dissolves the puzzle of "studio-constructed" music (musique concrète, most electronic stuff) that the type-theorist has to handle with epicycles. Third, it matches practice: fans argue about which mix of an album is canonical in a way that makes no sense if the work is an abstract sound-structure.

Objections I handle in the essay: (a) recordings are tokens, so this collapses the work into a single physical object that could be destroyed — answer: repeatable artifacts are a perfectly good category, see novels, films, prints; (b) the type-theorist can just widen the type to include production details — answer: this saves the letter of the view while conceding everything that matters.

Where it gets hard: remasters, remixed editions, loudness-war re-issues, and now per-listener things like streaming normalization and lossy compression. If the work is the recording, did the 2009 remaster change the work or give us a new one? My tentative answer: works have "editions," and identity across editions is fixed by production lineage, not by sound alone. I'm least confident about this bit.

Also: karaoke and covers are doing more conceptual work than I've credited them with.
