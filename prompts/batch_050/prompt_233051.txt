In two weeks I am leading the opening session of an interdisciplinary reading group on phenomenology. The members are three physical therapists, two cognitive scientists trained in mainstream computational approaches, and one roboticist; none of them has read Merleau-Ponty, and I cannot assume they will have done the reading beforehand. The session is ninety minutes and is devoted to motor intentionality: Merleau-Ponty's claim in Phenomenology of Perception that the body's grip on its surroundings is a genuine form of intentionality, irreducible to either reflex mechanism or representation-guided planning. I do not want a summary of the book. I want a plan for how to run the explanation, addressing the following five things.

First, the order in which the pieces must be introduced, with justification for the dependencies. What has to be in place before motor intentionality can be posed as a question rather than received as a slogan about the wisdom of the body? Consider in particular whether the critique of the constancy hypothesis and the associated empiricist picture of perception must come first, whether the distinction between the body as one object among others and the body as the standpoint from which objects are encountered can be built mid-session, and whether the group needs any feel for what intellectualism and empiricism share (a third-person starting point) before they can see why Merleau-Ponty rejects both explanations of pathological cases at once.

Second, the single case the session should be built around. I am weighing three candidates: the patient Schneider, as Merleau-Ponty discusses him, who performs habitual concrete movements fluently but cannot carry out abstract movements on command, such as pointing to his own nose when asked rather than grasping for it; a case of my own devising, a violinist whose skill survives her inability to describe or rehearse it and degrades precisely when she is asked to explain it; and an ordinary first-person example, reaching for a cup while absorbed in conversation. Argue for one. State what the case must demonstrate (that abstract and concrete movement are dissociable, or that skill is not stored as an instruction set, or whatever you take the load-bearing point to be) and what it must not invite the group to infer (for example, that pathology is the only window onto motor intentionality, or that the concept is merely a redescription of automaticity).

Third, the misconception map. I predict the following, but tell me which I am wrong about and which I am missing: the cognitive scientists will translate motor intentionality into unconscious motor commands, inverse models, or predictive processing and conclude it has been domesticated; the therapists will hear it as a vindication of clinical intuition over assessment; the roboticist will ask what it predicts that control theory does not; someone will construe the body schema and the body image as two internal representations; and someone will read Merleau-Ponty as an early, vaguer version of contemporary embodied cognitive science. For each misconception you keep, say whether to preempt it explicitly or let it surface, at what point in the arc it becomes damaging if left standing, and what move corrects it without derailing the session.

Fourth, a check for understanding to run in the last ten minutes: one task or question whose success would show the group has grasped motor intentionality as a claim about the structure of experience rather than as a label for skilled behavior, and whose failure modes would tell me which misconception survived.

Fifth, what I should deliberately leave out of a first session — the later ontology of the flesh, the detailed engagement with Gelb and Goldstein, the methodological relation to Husserl, or whatever you judge expendable — and how to mark those omissions so they do not sound like the real content being withheld.

Conclude by naming the single most likely way this session fails and explaining how your plan guards against it. Please write the answer in American English.
