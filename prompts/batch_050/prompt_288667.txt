So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, but I keep getting into arguments with a coworker about whether philosophy ever actually does anything for science, and I realized I don't even have a consistent view myself. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases -- tell me what principle, if any, unifies them.

1. Einstein rethinking simultaneity, apparently partly from reading empiricist philosophy: clearly useful. Led to actual new physics.
2. Mach-style positivism telling late 1800s physicists that atoms were dodgy metaphysics: harmful. Atoms were real, and the right move was to keep building on them.
3. The EPR "this is just philosophy" debate eventually producing Bell's theorem and then real experiments: useful. A conceptual squabble turned into hard physics.
4. "Shut up and calculate" as advice to a working physicist in like 1965: I still think this was basically good advice? Most people ignoring interpretive questions did perfectly fine work.
5. Philosophers arguing about whether machines can think (Chinese room type stuff): feels useless. Never once constrained or changed anything I've seen anyone build.

1 vs 2 bugs me: in both someone applied a philosophical framework to physics, one time it revolutionized everything, one time it was wrongheaded. And 3 vs 4: if the interpretive questions EPR raised were secretly fruitful, then maybe shut-up-and-calculate was actually bad advice and I'm contradicting myself.

Is there a principle that separates philosophy-that-helps-science from philosophy-that's-dead-weight and gets all or most of these? If not, which verdict should I drop? And what does the principle predict about the current "do LLMs really understand anything" debates -- worth anyone's time, or no?
