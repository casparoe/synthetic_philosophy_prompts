I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I have constructed the following argument for a reading group. I now suspect it proves too much, but I cannot localise the fault. The argument purports to show that Bertrand-style paradoxes undermine not merely the classical principle of indifference but every use of symmetric probabilities in science.

(1) Principle of indifference: where one's evidence does not discriminate among a set of exclusive and exhaustive possibilities, one ought to assign them equal probability.
(2) Bertrand's chord paradox shows that this principle, applied to a single well-defined question — what is the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? — delivers incompatible answers (1/2, 1/3, 1/4) depending on which geometric parameter one distributes uniformly over.
(3) Any rule that yields incompatible outputs on a single input is inconsistent and must be rejected outright.
(4) But every assignment of a uniform or symmetric distribution in the absence of discriminating evidence — Laplacean reasoning about unobserved cases, the uniform measure over microstates in statistical mechanics, non-informative and maximum-entropy priors in Bayesian inference — is an application of that very principle.
(5) Hence all such assignments are illegitimate, and the empirical success of statistical mechanics is either a mystery or a coincidence.
(6) It is clearly neither. So something in the argument must give, and I suspect an equivocation somewhere between (2) and (4), though I cannot name it.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically: if (3) is rejected, is one committed to there being a privileged parameterisation, and on what grounds could it be privileged? If (4) is rejected, must one show that the uniform measure of statistical mechanics rests on something other than raw indifference — dynamics, typicality, or what? And if (2) is the culprit, what exactly does Bertrand's paradox establish: that indifference is inconsistent, or merely that it is incomplete without a rule for choosing descriptions?

Finally, please be careful to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested: it is uncontroversial that the chord problem yields different answers under different parameterisations, but whether any principled defence of indifference survives (and whether maximum-entropy methods escape the objection) is, I take it, still disputed. Do not present a contested position as the consensus.
