A colleague uses the following case to argue that the rule of rescue is merely a bias toward vivid suffering. Treat the empirical details as stipulations.

A regional health authority has $12 million available and must choose between two mutually exclusive programs:

- Program S funds safety equipment that will prevent two expected deaths and produce 100 expected QALYs overall. The people whose deaths will be prevented cannot be identified in advance; each faces only a small reduction in risk.
- Program R funds a rescue operation that will certainly save one identified worker trapped underground, producing 50 expected QALYs.

Assume that the figures are reliable, the programs have no further effects on public trust, future behavior, employment, or politics, and the funds cannot be divided. The authority uses a value of a statistical life, or VSL, of $8 million and a cost-effectiveness threshold of $200,000 per QALY.

The argument proceeds as follows:

1. A VSL measures the social value of preventing one expected death. If that value is $8 million when a policy reduces many people’s mortality risks by a tiny amount, it remains $8 million when one person’s risk has risen to certainty; probability and identifiability are features of the decision problem, not properties that make a life more valuable.

2. Therefore, the life saved by Program R has a value of $8 million, while the two expected lives saved by Program S have a combined value of $16 million.

3. The same conclusion follows under QALY analysis: R produces 50 expected QALYs, worth $10 million at the stated threshold, whereas S produces 100 expected QALYs, worth $20 million.

4. A fixed public budget should be allocated to the alternative that produces the greater expected health benefit. Consequently, both VSL analysis and QALY analysis show that the authority is morally required to fund S rather than R.

5. This conclusion does not depend on the particular two-to-one ratio in the example. Even if S produced only 51 expected QALYs to R’s 50, or prevented deaths with an aggregate VSL only slightly above R’s, the authority would still be required to let the trapped worker die.

6. Nor is the conclusion confined to distant policymakers. If a hospital can produce slightly more aggregate expected benefit by transferring resources from an identifiable emergency patient to screening or prevention for unidentified future beneficiaries, it should do so.

7. Hence there is no independent rule of rescue. The conviction that an identifiable person in imminent danger has a special claim is just a cognitive response to salience, not a defensible moral distinction.

This conclusion seems to prove too much, but each step initially has some plausibility. Identify the first premise or inference you reject, if any, and explain why it appeared compelling. What does rejecting it cost?

In particular, would rejecting step 1 on the ground that VSL concerns marginal valuations of small risks be enough to rescue Program R, given that the QALY comparison in steps 3 and 4 independently favors Program S? If the ex ante/ex post distinction is doing the real work, explain precisely how. Alternatively, if steps 1 through 5 are sound, explain how to block the extension to step 6 without making an ad hoc distinction between policy and bedside triage. Finally, state whether any rule of rescue can be defended—as priority for the worst-off, as a non-aggregable claim, or as a feature of legitimate institutional roles—and what each defense would sacrifice.
