Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether the collapse of the Roman Republic has any real diagnostic value for thinking about modern democracies, and we ended the evening exactly where we started. Her view is that the analogy is nearly empty: Rome lacked mass suffrage, representative institutions, a modern economy, an independent judiciary, and anything like a free press, so whatever brought the Republic down tells us little about what might bring down, say, a contemporary presidential democracy. My view is that the analogy survives those disanalogies because the mechanisms most plausibly implicated in Rome's fall -- elite norm-breaking escalating into tit-for-tat constitutional hardball, the hollowing-out of informal restraints before formal ones, the personalization of military or coercive loyalty, the inability of institutions designed for one scale and social structure to cope with another -- are mechanisms that operate in any mass political system, and Rome simply gives us an unusually well-documented case of them running to completion.

We agreed on the rough historical narrative (the Gracchi, the breakdown of mos maiorum, Sulla's march on Rome, the civil wars, Augustus's settlement), so the disagreement is not about facts. It is about whether the analogy, given those facts, is informative or merely rhetorically convenient.

So here is what I want from you. Give me your credence -- an actual number -- for the proposition that the fall of the Roman Republic is genuinely diagnostic for assessing the health of at least some modern democracies, where "genuinely diagnostic" means that attending to the Roman case should shift a well-informed observer's judgments about which present-day institutional failures matter, not merely supply vivid illustrations for judgments already held.

Then decompose it. I would like you to identify the sub-questions your credence depends on -- I would suggest, at minimum: the probability that the standard narrative of the Republic's fall correctly identifies the operative mechanisms rather than the contingent ones; the probability that those mechanisms are general enough to operate in systems with Rome's disanalogies; the probability that modern democracies' genuine differences (economic complexity, information environment, constitutional design) neutralize rather than merely re-route those mechanisms; and the probability that historical analogies of this kind mislead more often than they inform, whatever the mechanisms. Assign each a rough probability with a sentence of justification, show how they combine into your final number, and state which sub-question the number is most sensitive to.

Finally, tell me where you think my friend and I most likely disagree within that decomposition, and what evidence or argument would move your credence most in either direction.
