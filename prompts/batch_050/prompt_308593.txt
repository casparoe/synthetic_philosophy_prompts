I'm putting the finishing touches on a position paper for my graduate seminar in democratic theory, and before submitting I want an audit of whether my reform package actually hangs together. The paper defends a set of changes to the franchise that I arrived at separately and have always assumed were mutually supporting — roughly the agenda of an inclusive, egalitarian democrat. But a friend in the seminar glanced at my outline and said she suspected I'd assembled positions that each sound right in isolation without checking whether the justifications are compatible. She declined to say more, which has been nagging at me. Here is the set.

(1) Voting should be compulsory, enforced by a modest fine, because voluntary turnout skews the electorate toward the older, wealthier, and better educated, and a systematically unrepresentative electorate undermines the legitimacy of political outcomes. (2) The voting age should be lowered to sixteen: the cognitive capacities relevant to deliberated choice are mature by that age, and sixteen-year-olds work, pay taxes, and live under the law like anyone else. (3) Long-term resident non-citizens should be enfranchised in all elections, national as well as local; my justification is the principle that anyone durably subject to a state's coercive authority is entitled to a say in how that authority is exercised, so membership in the nation is beside the point. (4) Competence should play no role whatsoever in the distribution of political voice: no voter tests, no plural votes for the educated, no epistocratic weighting of any kind. (5) Voting is a civic duty rather than merely a right, which is precisely why the state is permitted to enforce it. (6) Children below sixteen should not vote, notwithstanding the fact that the law applies to them as much as to anyone else.

What I want is an audit rather than a referendum on the package as a whole. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? In the genuine cases, identify what exactly generates the conflict — which justification is doing work that another commitment forbids — and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. For each one, tell me the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what it costs the overall view. I have no particular attachment to any single element, so don't hesitate to name whichever commitment looks most expendable.
