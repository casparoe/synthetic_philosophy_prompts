I'm in my final year of high school and belong to a small philosophy-and-technology reading group. Last week we discussed a thought experiment we came across called HCH, short for Humans Consulting HCH: a person answers a hard question not directly but by consulting copies of themselves, each of whom works only on one small sub-question and may in turn consult further copies, so that in the limit this models arbitrarily deep and careful deliberation. Some of us found this a beautiful idealization of what thinking could be; others suspected that something essential about judgment gets destroyed the moment you slice a question into pieces and forbid any one mind from holding the whole. Our teacher suggested we settle it properly by trying to construct a case where the factored process demonstrably does worse than the ordinary one, and none of us managed a version the rest of the group couldn't pick apart.

So please act as a philosopher of mind who is also well versed in AI alignment research, and construct this thought experiment to specification. I want a scenario in which HCH-style factored deliberation delivers a systematically worse answer than the same person reasoning directly for the same total amount of time, where the failure flows from the factoring itself. Constraints: the task must be realistic and everyday (evaluating a piece of writing, judging a job candidate, planning an event — not a math puzzle or a trick question); every copy in the tree must be competent, honest, and given a reasonable time budget per step, so the failure can't be dismissed as carelessness or haste; there must be no adversarial questioner feeding misleading sub-questions; and the direct reasoner's advantage must not rest on luck or on the task having a hidden single correct answer they happen to stumble into. The scenario should be describable in one or two paragraphs.

Please think carefully and brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios before committing to the strongest one, then show me the rejected candidates briefly and why they failed. For the chosen scenario, walk me through: what answer the factored process converges on and why, what answer direct deliberation reaches and why, and which implicit assumption about decomposition the case violates — I suspect it will be something like the assumption that answers to sub-questions recombine without losing the global picture, but I want you to identify the real load-bearing assumption yourself. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed, whether letting the reasoner train on records of past deliberation trees (so they get better at decomposing over time) would genuinely repair the failure or merely push it somewhere less visible, and finally, how a defender of factored cognition as an idealization of deliberation would attack the case and whether that attack succeeds.
