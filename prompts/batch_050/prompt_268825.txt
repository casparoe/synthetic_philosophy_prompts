I spent my career as a research chemist and have turned to philosophy only since retiring, so I have been trying to write down where I stand on meaning and reference, prompted largely by Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment. I would be grateful for an audit of whether the following commitments are jointly tenable:

(1) Twin Earth convinces me: what a natural kind term refers to depends partly on the speaker's environment, not solely on what goes on in the speaker's head.

(2) "Water is H2O" is necessarily true; a world whose watery liquid is XYZ is a world without water.

(3) English speakers in 1750, knowing nothing of molecular structure, nonetheless referred to H2O when they used the word "water."

(4) What a person can come to know by pure reflection, without any further investigation of the world, is fixed entirely by that person's internal, non-relational states.

(5) A competent speaker knows, simply in virtue of understanding her own words, what those words refer to.

(6) This is not special to natural kind terms: words like "elm," "pencil," and "contract" likewise owe their reference partly to the speaker's environment.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is; note also any apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection, since I suspect at least one is merely superficial. For each real conflict, indicate what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost, and finally identify the single change that would render the whole set most nearly coherent. I would rather have a pointed diagnosis than a survey of the literature.
