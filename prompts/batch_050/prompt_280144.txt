I recently started teaching an undergraduate course on classical Chinese thought (about 14 students, mostly second-years with no prior background in philosophy, 80-minute seminars), and I keep running into a personal experience that I think could carry the whole session. I play the piano as a hobby, and the better I try to play a passage, the worse it goes; the good performances happen precisely when I stop supervising my own fingers. My teacher's advice — stop trying so hard — is maddeningly useless as instruction, and yet she demonstrably got to where she is through thousands of hours of deliberate, effortful practice. I want my students to feel the force of this tangle before they ever encounter the term wu-wei or hear the story of the skilful butcher in the Zhuangzi: the paradox that some goods seem achievable only by not aiming at them, and yet also only attainable after long, deliberate training that looks very much like aiming.

Please act as an experienced seminar leader who is also a philosopher of action, and design me a runnable in-class activity built around this paradox. I'd like you to think carefully through the design before settling on a final version, and to show me your reasoning about why the mechanics you've chosen will work. Some constraints. First, no lecture up front: the students should be put through a sequence of tasks or staged performances whose rules or scoring arrangements cause the paradox to bite — i.e., a setup where visible effort or self-monitoring genuinely degrades performance, and then a second phase where some form of structured practice is shown to help even though effort-in-the-moment does not. Second, the activity must not rely on meditation, breathwork, or anything that presumes prior experience, and it must work for students who are shy about performing. Third, the debrief should map specific moments of the session onto the components of the paradox — the futility of willing spontaneity, the role of long habituation in producing unforced skill, and the residual question of whether "practising so as to no longer need to try" is coherent or merely relocates the paradox to an earlier time-slice of the agent.

Finally, please include: a minute-by-minute timeline with any materials needed (nothing that costs money or requires equipment beyond a normal classroom); at least two failure modes you would predict the first time this is run, with concrete advice for handling them; and an honest closing note on which feature of the Daoist ideal the activity cannot reproduce — my suspicion is that anything I can stage in 80 minutes will capture skill-automaticity but not the ethical dimension of acting effortlessly in one's dealings with other people, and I'd like you to tell me whether you think that gap is bridgeable in the debrief or must simply be admitted.
