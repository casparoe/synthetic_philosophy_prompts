I'm a bioethics PhD student and I've been going back and forth with my advisor about advance directives and dementia, and I want an outside take. Here's the setup: someone in their 40s, sharp and fully competent, writes a very explicit directive saying that if they ever develop severe dementia, they don't want life-prolonging treatment for infections or anything else -- no antibiotics for pneumonia, no feeding tubes, comfort care only. They write this partly because they watched a parent go through it. Fast forward thirty years: they now have advanced dementia, they don't remember writing the directive, don't really seem to understand they ever had a life before this one, but they seem basically content day to day -- they like music, they like pudding, they smile at people. Then they get pneumonia.

My question: what's your credence that the morally right thing to do is follow the directive and let the pneumonia run its course? Don't just give me a number -- decompose it. I'm thinking something like: the probability that the person sitting there now is, in the morally relevant sense, the same person who wrote the directive (as opposed to identity having thinned out enough that the earlier self's authority doesn't transfer); the probability, conditional on identity holding, that precedent autonomy should win against the current interests of someone who can't form future-directed preferences anymore; and the probability that the younger self could even understand what they were signing up for -- can you really grasp from the inside what contented dementia is like when you're 45 and terrified of it, or is the directive made under a kind of epistemic blindness that undercuts it? Add any other terms you think the decomposition needs. Number and one or two lines of justification per term, then show how they combine into the final credence.

For reference my advisor is at like 0.8 that you follow the directive and I'm at maybe 0.3, so also tell me where you think our disagreement most likely lives -- which term we're probably weighting differently -- and what argument or evidence (conceptual or empirical, like studies on how people's quality-of-life predictions about dementia compare to actual reported wellbeing) would move the number the most.
