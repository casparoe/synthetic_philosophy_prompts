You are a professional philosopher of epistemology. Write a dialogue between two characters, Dana (a conciliationist) and Ravi (a steadfast theorist), who are epistemic peers, longtime collaborators, and each fully aware of the other's track record. The setup: they have just realized that they disagree, as peers, about peer disagreement itself — Dana holds that discovering disagreement with a peer requires substantially lowering one's confidence, while Ravi holds that one may rationally stick to one's well-formed judgment. Dana argues that by her own lights she must now conciliate toward steadfastness; Ravi points out that the resulting position — a policy of conciliating only halfway, toward a view that tells her not to conciliate — seems to undermine itself, and presses her on whether there is any coherent "halfway" policy between the two views at all, or whether splitting the difference on the meta-level just produces an unstable hybrid that satisfies neither theory. Have them work out, together, whether the self-defeat objection to conciliationism is a genuine problem or a confusion between object-level and higher-order evidence, and whether Ravi's steadfastness faces a mirror-image problem when the disputed proposition is about how to respond to disputes. Both characters should be equally competent: neither is a strawman, each must respond to the other's actual arguments, and each should concede at least one real point. Think carefully about the strongest version of each side before writing. Present the result as a dialogue with speaker labels and no narration, at most 600 words.
