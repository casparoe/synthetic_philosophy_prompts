I work as the de facto ethics person at a mid-size regional hospital (not my training — I'm a palliative care doc who got voluntold onto the ethics commitee). Our policy says, like most do, that staff may never "actively end a patient's life" but may "allow a patient to die naturally" by not starting or by stopping treatment. That sounded clean until the consults actually started coming in, roughly one a week now: is extubating this patient killing or letting die? Is terminal sedation without artificial hydration one or the other? What about switching off someone's implanted defibrillator? The committee relitigates the same arguments from scratch every time and the answer tracks whoever happens to be in the room.

Act as a moral philosopher who has also done real clinical ethics consulting. I want you to turn what philosophy actually knows about the killing vs. letting die distinction into a decision procedure my committee can run on a consult in under an hour. Before giving the final procedure, brainstorm the candidate criteria the literature offers — causal contribution, act vs. omission, withholding vs. withdrawing, intention vs. foresight and double effect, and the bare-difference-style arguments that the whole distinction does no moral work — and say briefly which deserve to survive translation into a usable tool and which don't, and why. Then give the procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it terminates with "killing under policy" vs. "letting die under policy," and — this is the part I care most about — the exact points where it has to hand off to human judgment because the question is one of values or because the philosophy itself is genuinely unsettled. Be honest about where any procedure like this will misfire rather than pretending it's airtight.

Then run it on three real-flavoured consults: (1) a patient with capacity, end-stage lung disease, asking to be extubated to comfort care and expected to die within hours; (2) escalating morphine for air hunger in an actively dying patient where each dose foreseeably suppresses breathing a little further; (3) deactivating the implanted cardiac defibrillator of a hospice patient who is not yet imminently dying. At least one of these should come out somewhere my committee won't like — don't sand the edges off to make it palatable.

Constraints: committee members are clinicians with zero philosophy background and can't be asked to apply raw theory; risk management signs off on every final disposition; and the procedure needs to flag when a case is really a consent or advance-directive problem wearing a metaphysics costume.

Keep the whole thing under 800 words, and think carefully before writing — I'd rather wait for a good answer than get a fast shallow one.
