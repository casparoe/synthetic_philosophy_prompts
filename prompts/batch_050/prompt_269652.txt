Many jurisdictions run victim–offender conferencing programs on roughly the following model. A victim of crime, if willing, meets the offender in a facilitated session; the session is counted a success when the offender accepts responsibility, offers an apology, and the victim expresses some measure of forgiveness; an apology that strikes the facilitators as sincere may be reported to the sentencing court, where it can reduce the sentence; and a victim who declines the conference, or who attends but leaves as angry as she arrived, is often described — in program literature and in the culture of the practice — as not yet ready, with the door held open for a later attempt. Defenders of the programs frequently add a therapeutic rationale: forgiving is said to be better for the victim's own recovery, so steering victims toward forgiveness is a service rendered to them rather than a demand placed on them.

What does a practice built this way presuppose about the nature and ethics of forgiveness? Reconstruct its implicit theory. In particular: does the practice presuppose that forgiveness is under the victim's voluntary control — the sort of thing one can be scheduled for, facilitated toward, and gently pressed into — rather than an emotional shift that at best happens to a person and cannot be produced on demand? Does the not-yet-ready framing commit the program to the view that forgiveness, at least once a sincere apology has been offered, is in some sense called for — that the still-resentful victim is falling short of something rather than merely declining something? And does the therapeutic rationale presuppose an account of what forgiveness even is — a change in the victim's inner state, undertaken for her own sake — that sits uneasily with the transactional, apology-and-acceptance structure the conference itself enacts?

Where the practice is underdetermined, say so: identify which of its features several rival theories of forgiveness would license equally well, and which features genuinely require one theory against its competitors. Then consider two replacements for whatever theory you have reconstructed: first, the view that forgiveness is a purely discretionary gift, conditional on nothing and owed to no one, so that even a fully repentant offender has no claim on it; second, the view that forgiveness can be obligatory — that once genuine repentance has occurred, a settled refusal to forgive is itself a moral failing. For each, describe what the program would have to change in order to be honest about the theory it was running, down to the intake materials, the facilitator's role, and the report to the court. Finally, say whether the sentencing discount for a sincere apology presupposes anything about forgiveness at all, or only about remorse — and whether a practice could coherently reward the offender's repentance while treating the victim's forgiveness as strictly her own business.
