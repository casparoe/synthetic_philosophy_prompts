I'm on the board of a volunteer-run regional chess federation -- about 40 clubs, we run the youth league and rate the tournaments. Long story short: one of our long-time coaches, a guy who's built up the junior program at one of the bigger clubs, got into it on twitter last spring and posted some pretty ugly stuff about immigrants during some political fight. The posts had zero to do with chess, but screenshots circulated, a group of parents wrote to us demanding we act, and three clubs are now threatening to pull their teams from the league if he keeps coaching at federation events. So we have to do *something*, or at least decide that we're doing nothing.

Here's the wrinkle. Our bylaws let us expel a member for conduct that brings the federation into disrepute, but expulsion triggers a whole formal procedure -- written charges, a hearing, an appeal -- and honestly nobody on the board wants to run a trial, and half the board thinks we'd lose on the merits anyway since it was his private account and unrelated to chess. So the chair is proposing we skip all that: just quietly stop assigning him to federation events, drop him from the coach directory on our website, and let his organizer certification lapse when it comes up for renewal. He keeps his club job, keeps coaching there, we never formally sanction anyone, problem solved. Two board members are furious about this plan. They say it's punishment dressed up as inaction, and arguably worse than formal punishment, because he gets no chance to defend himself, there's no stated charge, no proportionality, no end date -- it just never expires. The chair's answer is basically: we're a private association, declining to work with someone isn't punishment, it's us exercising our own freedom of association, and due process doesn't apply to decisions about who we choose to platform. There's also a live argument about whether any of this applies retroactively to posts from before people even knew the federation cared about their social media.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before next month's board meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action -- the quiet-exclusion route, a formal proceeding (and if so, with what constraints on proportionality and whether there's any limitations period for old conduct), or genuinely taking no action -- with an actual bottom line the board can vote on. It should also take seriously the strongest argument on whichever side I don't end up recommending, including whether the punisment-vs-mere-non-association distinction even survives the fact that the federation effectively controls access to rated competitive play in the whole region. Keep it to something a board of volunteers with no lawyer will actually read.
