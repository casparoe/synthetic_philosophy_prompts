For the past several years I have co-taught a graduate seminar on the normativity of rational choice with a colleague whose considered verdicts about gambles I have been quietly collecting, because I suspect they commit her to more theory than she realizes. Her record so far: she prefers $500 for certain to an even chance at $1,100 or nothing; she prefers an even chance at losing $1,000 or losing nothing to a certain loss of $450; on an Allais-style pair she takes $5,000 for certain over a lottery offering an 89% chance of $5,000, a 10% chance of $25,000, and a 1% chance of nothing, yet prefers a 10% chance at $25,000 to an 11% chance at $5,000; she reports indifference between $20 for certain and an even chance at $160 or nothing; and she insists she would refuse an even chance of losing $100 or gaining $110 at any wealth level she can realistically imagine occupying.

At next week's meeting I must put three new problems to her, and I want to anticipate her answers. First, whether she would pay $1 for a one-in-ten-thousand chance at $50,000. Second, a compound offer: a coin flip where heads pays $20 for certain and tails runs the even chance at $160 or nothing in which she professes indifference against $20. Third, a two-stage scenario: she has just won a $5,000 prize, and is asked either to choose between keeping $4,550 for certain and an even chance at keeping $5,000 or $4,000, or — the prize already in hand — to choose between returning $450 for certain and an even chance at returning $1,000 or nothing.

Please predict her verdict on each of the three, with reasons. Identify which theory best systematizes her existing record — risk-weighted expected utility with a risk-averse risk function over linear stakes, cumulative prospect theory with a status-quo reference point, rank-dependent utility, or an unsystematized catalogue of framing effects — and state what each serious candidate predicts about the new problems and where those predictions diverge. If you conclude that her record genuinely underdetermines any of the three answers, say what principle she would be committing herself to with each possible verdict. Finally, name the single further choice problem that would most sharply discriminate between the two leading candidates, and explain why it is probative where my three are not.
