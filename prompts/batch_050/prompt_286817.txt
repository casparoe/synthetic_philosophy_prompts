Over the past few weeks I've been keeping a notebook of moments that made me or someone else laugh, because a running argument with two friends about humour has stalled: one of them insists it's all disguised aggression, the other says it's all surprise, and neither seems able to explain the other's best examples. I know the standard menu of theories -- superiority, incongruity, relief, benign violation -- but rather than asking which is true in the abstract, I'd like to work backwards from my own verdicts on cases and see whether any principle, or some principled hybrid, actually captures the pattern. I'll list the cases with my honest reactions, including two I suspect don't sit well together.

A pompous colleague slips on a wet floor while lecturing us about caution: funny. The same fall by a nervous intern on her first day: not funny at all, faintly awful. A pun at dinner gets a groan that is somehow also a laugh, and I genuinely can't tell whether the groan is the joke failing or succeeding. The classic anti-joke about why the chicken crossed the road makes me laugh precisely because nothing surprising happens, which seems backwards for a surprise-based theory. An emergency-room nurse's bleak joke about a patient is funny when she tells it to colleagues and horrifying when repeated at a dinner party, even though the words are identical. A comedian at a roast insults the guest of honour viciously and everyone laughs; the same insult delivered to a stranger on the street would be cruelty. A joke loses almost everything when someone asks for it to be explained, even though nothing about its content changes. A joke about a recent public tragedy feels obscene, while the same joke about a similar tragedy from thirty years ago can land fine. And the case I keep circling: I have a chronic back problem and make dark jokes about it that I find genuinely funny, yet when I laugh at myself there is no one I feel superior to, and I don't feel particularly relieved of anything either -- which seems to break both my friend's aggression theory and the relief theory, though I notice I'd be hurt if a stranger made the identical joke about me.

What I'd like from you: state a principle, or the smallest hybrid you can manage, that reproduces these verdicts, and for each clause of your principle name the case that forces it. Where my verdicts resist any single tidy account -- I suspect the self-directed humour case and the anti-joke case are the troublemakers -- say so plainly, and tell me which verdict you'd advise me to abandon if I wanted the simplest viable theory, and why that one rather than another. Then apply your principle to three cases I haven't listed: nervous laughter at a funeral, finding a typo in a formal legal document funny, and whether a computer could be funny as opposed to merely producing text that makes us laugh. Finally, please indicate how confident you are that your principle is the right reconstruction of my verdicts rather than a just-so story fitted to them, and single out the specific point in your answer where you are least sure of yourself.
