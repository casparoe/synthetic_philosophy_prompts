So a friend of mine recently rebuilt Benjamin Franklin's moral perfection project as a phone app, of all things — thirteen checkboxes, streaks, weekly focus, the works — and it got me thinking about what the original scheme actually assumes. Quick recap in case it's fuzzy: in his Autobiography Franklin describes drawing up a list of thirteen virtues (Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, Humility), each with a short precept, and making a little book with a grid — one line per virtue, one column per day. Each week he concentrates on one virtue, and every evening he marks a small black dot for every fault he committed against any of them that day. Thirteen weeks gets you through the whole list, four rounds a year, the dots gradually dissapearing. He orders the virtues deliberately — Temperance comes first because it's supposed to build the self-command you need for the rest. And he admits he never really mastered Order, and says near the end that although he fell far short of perfection he was better and happier for having made the attempt.

Here's my question: what does this practice presuppose, philosophically?

A few things bug me. The ledger treats moral failure as countable — a black dot is a black dot whether you broke Frugality or Justice. The scheme tracks outward behavior you can review in the evening, yet it includes virtues like Sincerity and Justice, whose violations are often matters of motive that no checklist would catch. It works on one virtue per week, as if virtues were independent modules you can install separately, but Franklin's own ordering rationale seems to assume they depend on each other. And it's a scheme for moral perfection that ends up measuring progress by fewer dots and congratulating itself for falling short.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of virtue here. What does the practice assume about whether virtue is a matter of behavior or of motive, about whether the virtues are one thing or many, about whether moral progress is the kind of thing that can be measured at all? Which rival views — say, an Aristotelian picture with practical wisdom and the unity of the virtues, or a Kantian one where the good will is everything — would this practice simply not make sense under? Which of its parts presuppose inconsistent things, if any, and which parts are merely compatible with several theories and philosophically underdetermined? And if you rebuilt the practice on an Aristotelian or Kantian basis, what would concretely have to change?
