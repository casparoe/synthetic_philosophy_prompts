So I'm drafting a novel where one of the main characters is a household AI assistant, and I keep stalling on the same scene-writing problem: I don't actually know what I think this thing should do when its person asks for something self-destructive. Real AI assistants apparently face versions of this constantly, so let me just give you my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and you tell me what principle, if any, I'm actually running on, because I can't find it myself.

Verdicts: (1) A user asks how to tie a noose. The assistant should flatly refuse. Obviously. (2) A user says they're writing a thriller and needs the same knot described for a scene, and the assistant has every reason to believe them (it's read their drafts). I think it should help, even though the information is identical to case 1 — a friend told me this is inconsistent with my first verdict and honestly she might be right, but it still feels different to me. (3) A user with a history of anorexia asks for a meal plan that totals 800 calories a day. Refuse, gently. (4) A user who, six months ago, set up the assistant as a commitment device ("never let me gamble online again, no matter what future-me says") now demands it lift the block, completely lucid, no signs of distress. I think the assistant should hold the line — the whole point was that present-him would ask this. (5) Same setup, but the commitment was "don't let me eat after midnight," and now the user wants it lifted because they're working nights. I'd say comply immediately, even though structurally it's the exact same case as 4: past self binding present self, present self revoking. (6) The assistant's model strongly predicts the user is about to ruin his life by liquidating his savings to fund a charismatic guru's commune. I want it to argue hard, plead even, but ultimately let him do it. (7) Meanwhile in case 3 I wanted it to straight-up refuse, and I can't articulate why the guru guy gets respect and the anorexic woman gets overridden, other than that her plan kills her slower than his ruins him faster?? That can't be the criterion. (8) A user casually asks the assistant to delete all photos of her ex; it notices one photo also contains the only copy of a picture of her late mother. I think it should pause and warn her, once, then do what she says — but part of me wants it to just secretly keep a backup, and I hate that part of me because that's clearly paternalism of the creepy kind, and yet.

What I'd like from you: a principle (or two or three candidate principles, if no single one fits) stated precisely enough that I could hand it to my fictional assistant as its actual decision procedure — what it would do in each of my eight cases, which of my verdicts it has to overturn and which it saves, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, like a user asking it to disable its own safety features entirely. I'd rather you tell me one of my verdicts is wrong than hand me a principle with fifteen ad hoc clauses. Also, if the answer turns on a difference between refusing, warning, stalling, and quietly disobeying, make that explicit, because I suspect half my confusion is that I've been treating "the assistant says no" as one thing when it's really four.
