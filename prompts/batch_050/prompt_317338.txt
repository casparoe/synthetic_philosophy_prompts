So I've recently started volunteering at my small town's historical society, mostly scanning old documents so they can go in a digital archive, and it's kind of broken my brain a little. Working through the boxes you start to notice what's NOT there: the town had a sizable Black community in the early 1900s that shows up in maybe two dozen records total, almost all of them written by white officials. There are women's names everywhere but almost nothing in their own words. Etc.

While stewing on this I came up with two ideas of my own and I'd like a proper philosophical take on them. First, what I've been calling "archival gravity": once a person or group is well-documented, they get documented more, because later historians build on existing sources, journalists quote earlier records, and so on, while the undocumented stay invisible. Second, what I'd call "second-order silence": the archive usually doesn't even record its own exclusions. Nobody wrote down "we are not keeping the laundresses' letters" or "we threw out the tenant farmers' papers." The silence is itself silent, so later readers can't easily tell the difference between "this group wasn't important" and "this group's records were deliberately or carelessly not preserved."

Write an essay about these two ideas. Weigh the following questions: Are archival silences actually evidence of anything, or is it an epistemic mistake to infer history from what's missing -- absence of evidence vs evidence of absence? Does my "archival gravity" idea hold up, or is it too pessimistic (e.g., can't researchers actively dig up neglected sources)? And does digitizing everything and making it searchable, like what I'm doing, genuinely help correct the record, or does it just entrench whatever biases are already baked into what got saved in the first place? Please reach an actual conclusion and defend it rather than ending with a wishy-washy "it depends." Definately push back on my two concepts if you think they're confused, that's fine.
