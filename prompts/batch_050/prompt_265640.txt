I'm putting together a unit on the ethics of influence for an upper-division course I'm teaching, and I've run into a recurring frustration: my students readily agree that there is a real difference between manipulating someone and persuading them legitimately, but every example I bring to class splits the room, and the split tracks whichever theory of manipulation is doing the work in the background. I'd like a purpose-built case that forces the issue instead of trading on intuitions about obviously bad behavior like lying or blackmail. So: construct a scenario, and a matched control, engineered so that three prominent accounts of manipulation diverge and converge in a specified way.

Here are the three accounts, stated by content rather than by name. First, the bypass account: influence is manipulative when it routes around or subverts the target's capacities for rational deliberation rather than engaging them, whatever the influencer's intent. Second, the pressure account: manipulation consists in applying non-rational pressure or trickery -- guilt trips, wheedling, emotional leverage, deception -- levers the target would not endorse as genuine reasons, though their deliberative machinery remains intact. Third, the covert-influence account: influence is manipulative when it is hidden, when the target either doesn't realize an influence attempt is occurring or doesn't understand the real means by which it operates. The specification: build a single scenario on which the bypass account delivers the verdict "manipulation" while both the pressure account and the covert-influence account deliver "legitimate persuasion" -- that is, a fully disclosed influence attempt, involving neither deception nor pressure, which nonetheless works by routing around the target's deliberation. Constraints: no lies, no threats, no hypnosis or subliminal technology, no exploitation of addiction, illness, or cognitive impairment; both parties must be competent adults; the influence attempt must be announced or otherwise fully transparent to the target; and the scenario must be one an ordinary person could plausibly encounter (advertising, a workplace, a family, a political campaign are all fine). Then construct the control: change the single feature doing the work so that the influence now runs through the target's deliberation, and show that all three accounts converge on "legitimate persuasion" for it. Finally, state what happens to the main scenario if transparency is removed instead, and confirm that the case then collapses into a standard covert-influence case on which all three accounts again agree.

For the deliverable, don't stipulate the verdicts -- derive each one from the account in question, and say explicitly which structural feature of the scenario generates the divergence. Identify which account comes out looking worst (I expect it will be the covert-influence account, forced to bless an overt bypass, but convince me if it's a different one), and give the strongest counterattack a defender of that account would mount against the case. Above all, commit: give me one scenario and one control, not a menu of candidates, and take a definite position on whether the wedge actually holds once the counterattack is answered. If you conclude that one of the three accounts can resist the classification I've assigned it, say so plainly and adjust the scenario until the specified pattern genuinely obtains, explaining what you had to change and why.
