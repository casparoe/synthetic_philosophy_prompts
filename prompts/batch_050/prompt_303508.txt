I'm a third-year cognitive science student taking a seminar on mental representation, and we've just spent three weeks on the imagery debate between the depictive camp associated with Kosslyn and the propositional camp associated with Pylyshyn. A running joke in our study group is that this debate never actually ended — people just stopped having it — and one member insists the two sides were compatible all along once you strip away the metaphors. Our professor has offered extra credit for a project structured as an adversarial collaboration, so before recruiting anyone I want to draft the document myself: the joint statement a committed depictivist and a committed propositionalist might produce if they were locked in a room until they had something both would sign.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims. I expect these to include the robustness of the classic behavioral effects (scanning times increasing with imagined distance, rotation times with angular disparity, interference between imagery and same-modality perception), the reality of imagery as an experience, and some shared concession about what neuroimaging evidence can and cannot establish — but please work out precisely what both sides can honestly sign rather than simply ratifying my guesses, and flag any of my guesses that you think are wrong. Second, a disagreements table in which each position is stated in words its own defenders would accept, without the other side's loaded vocabulary: the depictivist view should not be stated as pictures in the head inspected by an inner observer, and the propositionalist view should not be stated as a denial that imagery exists. I suspect one genuine disagreement concerns whether the metric structure of the effects requires a spatial medium or can be generated by tacit knowledge plus task demands, and another concerns what claiming a "depictive format" adds beyond a claim about content — but check me on this rather than assuming it. Third, a cruxes section: for each crux, a concrete finding or case both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please include aphantasia — people who report no conscious imagery at all yet perform normally on standard imagery tasks — as one of the cases, and spell out what each camp is committed to saying about it and whether it embarrasses either.

One further requirement: throughout, keep separate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. If part of the disagreement is really verbal — both sides accepting the same computational story and differing only over whether the representations deserve to be called picture-like — say so explicitly and explain why. And if portions of the original dispute have been dissolved or bypassed rather than resolved (for instance, by later work on spatial cognition and visual working memory that doesn't map cleanly onto the old dichotomy), note that instead of staging the debate exactly as it stood decades ago. Don't present either camp's framing as the field's received view, and where the field has genuinely moved on, say where it moved to.
