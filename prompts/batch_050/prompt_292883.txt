So I'm a few years into a PhD in philosophy of physics, mostly working on time, and this came out of an argument in our reading group last week about Gödel's 1949 stuff on rotating universes. I ended up defending what I think is my considered view, but afterwards I couldn't shake the feeling that I'd been switching between two different positions depending on who was pressing me. So here's roughly where I've landed and I want to know if it hangs together:

(1) Gödel's argument works. If worlds with closed timelike curves are physically possible under the laws of general relativity, then in those worlds there's no objective lapse of time, no global "now" sweeping forward — and because our world is governed by the same laws, the conclusion carries over: time's passage here is ideal too, not a real feature of the universe. The mathematical possibility alone doesn't do it; the point is that these are physically possible worlds and the laws can't privilege ours.

(2) I'm a B-theorist anyway. The block universe already denies passage, so if Gödel is right he just gives me another argument for a conclusion I already hold. Nothing about the reality of temporal relations is threatened — before/after and earlier/later stay perfectly objective, we just lose the flow.

(3) At the same time, I suspect chronology-protection-type mechanisms (Hawking's instability arguments, whatever the quantum gravity story turns out to be) will rule out closed timelike curves in any physically realistic universe, so Gödel universes are mathematical curiosities, not genuine physical possibilities.

(4) The direction of time is fully reducible to the entropy gradient — the thermodynamic arrow is all the direction there is, so even in exotic spacetimes, local temporal direction is fixed by thermodynamics.

Is this a consistent package? In the reading group, the person pressing me hardest said I can't have both (1) and (3), and I think that's probably right, but I'm less sure whether (2) is really as safe as I claimed — Gödel seems to want something stronger than the B-theory gives, and I don't know if "ideal" for him means what I was taking it to mean. Meanwhile I keep insisting (2) and (4) fit together fine and nobody's convinced me otherwise. Tell me which of these actually pull against each other (and what the cheapest fix is and what it costs), which pairs are only apparently in tension, and be specific about which commitment has to go first. I don't have a dog in the fight about which one I drop, I just want to stop flip flopping.
