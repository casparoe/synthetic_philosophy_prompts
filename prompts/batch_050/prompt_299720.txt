I direct the simulation program for a surgical residency, and I have a budget decision to make that has turned into an argument I can't adjudicate. Each resident gets four supervised hours a week practicing a procedure they aren't yet allowed to perform unsupervised. Two of my senior surgeons disagree about how one of those hours should be spent, and I can only fund one approach properly.

Dr. A has the residents sit quietly with their eyes closed and work through the operation in their imagination, start to finish — she tells them to run it like a film, to see the incision site, to feel where their hands go, to watch the anatomy as it would appear from their angle at the table. Her conviction is that the spatial layout is doing the work: you have to see the operative field in your mind, in detail, and the trainees who can do that perform measurably better. She points to something she's noticed in debriefs: when she asks residents questions about their imagined scene, they take longer to answer about features that would be farther apart in the actual field than about features that would be close together, as if they were moving their attention across a real expanse. She takes that as proof that something genuinely picture-like is present and being inspected.

Dr. B thinks this is window dressing. On his view, what helps the residents is that the quiet hour forces them to recite the sequence of steps and decision points to themselves — knowledge they could rehearse just as well out loud against a checklist, with the advantage that he could throw complications at them verbally. The "film," he says, is a byproduct of knowing what happens next, not a mechanism. As for Dr. A's observation about answer times, he replies that of course it takes longer to retrieve knowledge about distant structures than adjacent ones — a surgeon describing the field out loud would show the same pattern, because that pattern reflects what they know and how it's organized, not the presence of an inner picture being scanned. He'd rather spend the hour on verbal drills.

Here's my problem. My first instinct was that this is resolvable: randomize the residents, compare outcomes, done. But the more I think about it, the less sure I am that even a clean outcome study would settle what they're actually disagreeing about. If the eyes-closed group does better, Dr. B will say it's the recitation, not the pictures. If the verbal-drill group does as well or better, Dr. A will say the drills incidentally prompted imagery anyway. They seem to disagree not just about training but about what the residents' inner experience even is — and I can't tell whether that's a factual dispute wearing a philosophical costume, or the reverse.

So: Who has the better of this argument, and how should I spend the hour? Please identify what their disagreement is actually about, assess each side's evidence — including whether Dr. A's timing observation shows what she thinks it shows, and whether Dr. B's alternative explanation is the rebuttal he thinks it is — and tell me what, if anything, could genuinely settle the underlying dispute, as opposed to merely declaring a winner for practical purposes. Throughout, separate what is actually established among people who work on this from what remains genuinely contested; I don't want one side's position presented to me as the settled science if it isn't.
