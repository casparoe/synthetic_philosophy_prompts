Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is usually read as a powerful result in favor of utilitarian-style social evaluation. But a colleague of mine has pressed an argument that, starting from the theorem, seems to prove far more than utilitarianism — something close to the view that moral claims are hostage to the sheer intensity of people's feelings. I cannot accept the conclusion, yet I struggle to locate the step where the reasoning fails. Here is the argument as he presents it.

(1) Suppose each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms. Then each person i's preferences can be represented by a utility function u_i, unique up to positive affine transformation.

(2) Suppose social preferences over lotteries also satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and suppose they obey the Pareto principle: if every individual weakly prefers one prospect to another, and at least one strictly prefers it, then society prefers it too.

(3) By Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, it follows that social preference can be represented by a weighted sum of individual utilities, W = Σ w_i u_i, with positive weights.

(4) Because each u_i is unique only up to positive affine transformation, we are free to normalize. So normalize every person's utility function to the same scale: set each person's utility to 0 at their least preferred feasible outcome and to 1 at their most preferred feasible outcome.

(5) With everyone's utility expressed on this common 0–1 scale, there is no remaining justification for unequal weights. Assigning person i a greater weight than person j would now be an arbitrary privileging of one person's good over another's — the weights merely encode whose interests we choose to count more, and impartiality requires counting everyone's interests the same. Hence w_i = w_j for all i, j.

(6) Therefore rational social choice consists in maximizing the simple sum of normalized utilities.

(7) It follows that the moral importance of a person's claim is determined by how intensely that person cares about outcomes, as measured against the full spread of their preferences: a person whose utility swings enormously between their worst and best feasible outcomes will, once lotteries are considered, dominate the social calculus relative to someone of more modest temperament. If we could give one group of people a psychological intervention that made them care vastly more intensely about what happens to them — stretching the effective range of their utility — the sum in (6) would then direct resources toward them at everyone else's expense, and rightly so, even though nothing about the outcomes themselves, or anyone's needs, had changed.

(8) But this is absurd. It makes distributive justice a function of how excitable people happen to be, rewards the manufacturing of intense preferences, and licenses what is essentially a utility monster generated by a pill rather than by nature. So at least one step between (1) and (7) must go.

My own sympathies: I would strongly prefer to keep the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms for individuals (1), the Pareto principle (2), and the theorem itself (3). Steps (4) and (5) each have an air of innocent bookkeeping about them, and step (6) looks like a restatement rather than a move. Yet the conclusion at (7)–(8) seems to follow. Which step do you reject, and why does that step look so innocent when it is not? In your answer, please also do the following: first, explain precisely what freedom the affine uniqueness of von Neumann–Morgenstern utility does and does not confer, and whether the move in (4) uses that freedom or exceeds it; second, say whether "equal weights" is a normalization-independent notion, and what it would take to make interpersonal comparisons of utility genuinely meaningful rather than conventional; third, assess what it costs to reject (5) — in particular, whether doing so abandons impartiality or merely relocates where impartiality must be argued for; and finally, indicate whether the diagnosis changes if the argument is run ex ante, behind a veil of ignorance where each person has an equal chance of occupying anyone's position, rather than ex post with known identities.
