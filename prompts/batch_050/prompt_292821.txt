I am the compliance officer at a mid-sized consumer goods company, and I review all external-facing copy -- packaging claims, advertising scripts, press statements, and answers our spokespeople are told to give when journalists ask awkward questions. Our regulator can sanction us for false statements, but I am increasingly convinced that the more important line is a moral one internal to the company: we have informally committed to "never lying" to customers while remaining, shall we say, unembarrassed about being economical with the truth. The problem is that nobody here can say where that commitment actually bites, and my current test -- "would the average customer feel deceived?" -- collapses the very distinction the commitment rests on, since people feel deceived by true-but-misleading statements all the time.

Please write a decision procedure I can train my review team to run on any piece of copy, grounded in what the philosophy of language and the ethics of deception actually say about the difference between lying and misleading: assertion versus implicature, what a speaker commits to versus what a hearer happens to infer, and whether a context makes an inference the speaker's responsibility. The procedure must be usable by a reviewer with no philosophy background in under fifteen minutes per item, so you will have to decide which theoretical distinctions survive translation into questions a copywriter can answer, and which can be safely dropped -- and I want you to say explicitly which you dropped and what that costs. It must handle, at minimum: technically true claims that invite false inferences; true answers to the question asked that would mislead given the question the audience actually cares about; omissions; and claims made with weasel qualifiers that technically defeat the natural reading.

Two complications. First, our internal tradition already has its own rough test -- a spokesperson should never make a statement they could not repeat, verbatim and with a straight face, in a sworn affidavit -- and I want you to assess what this test gets right and wrong relative to the philosophical account, rather than simply discarding it. Second, the procedure should output not just a verdict but a classification (lie, misleading-but-true, acceptable) with different sign-off requirements for each, and it should mark the cases where classification is genuinely a judgement call rather than procedure-shaped.

Then run the procedure on three worked examples: (a) a breakfast cereal labelled "no added sugar" whose fruit concentrate contains more sugar than the sugar it replaces; (b) a spokesperson who, asked whether our factory had any safety incidents last year, answers that no employee was hospitalised, knowing several injuries were treated on site; and (c) a shampoo advertised as "dermatologist approved" on the strength of a single paid consultant's sign-off. At least one of these should come out with a verdict I will find uncomfortable given the company's commitment, and I want you to flag which one and why the discomfort is a feature of the distinction rather than a defect of the procedure. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do -- the class of cases where the philosophy itself runs out and I am back to exercising judgement.
