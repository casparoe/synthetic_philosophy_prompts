Her take: the people who kept turning the dial up were just bad, end of story. Mine was that it depends on what was going on in their heads, and at some point I brought up Abelard — the medieval guy who holds that sin is in the consent of the will, not in the act itself or even the desire — and we realized neither of us actually knew how to apply him to the case. So do that for us: analyze Milgram through Abelard's ethics. Concretely: Abelard says the act is morally indifferent and what matters is whether you consent to what you take to be wrong. What does that say about the roughly two-thirds of subjects who went all the way to 450 volts? Finally, where does the framework break: Milgram is usually read as showing that situations can manufacture compliance almost regardless of character, and I want to know if an ethics of pure consent can even make sense of that, or whether the experiment reveals something about how consent gets engineered that Abelard's picture just can't capture.
