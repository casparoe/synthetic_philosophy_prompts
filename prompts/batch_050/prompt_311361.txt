I'm a philosophy professor, but my training was continental — Husserl is home turf for me, epoché and all, and I've taught Merleau-Ponty more times than I can count. Dennett I mostly know from getting assigned the phil mind survey every other year, and his quining-qualia argument (the one with the two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn) irritates me in a way I've never managed to articulate, even to myself. So I want help articulating it.

Restate Dennett's case against qualia — that there are no intrinsic, ineffable, private, immediately apprehensible properties of experience, and that the coffee-taster case shows we can't even make sense of asking whether the taste changed or Chase's reaction to it changed — in the vocabulary of phenomenology. Natural attitude, epoché and bracketing, noesis and noema, retention, the whole toolkit. And translate the whole argument, not just the slogan: the stuff about what the tasters would say under various conditions, the appeal to judgments and reactive attitudes, the charge that how it seems to you delivers no further fact beyond how you're disposed to judge and respond.

Then the part I actually care about: a residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which survive only with distortion, and where exactly does the distortion creep in? And is there anything in Dennett's argument that has no expression at all in phenomenological vocabulary? Here's my suspicion, and please take it seriously rather than humoring me: Dennett's own method — write down what the subject says, treat it the way a reader treats a fictional text, stay agnostic about what's really going on inside — looks to me like the epoché wearing a lab coat. If that's right, one of two awkward things follows. Either his conclusion is one a phenomenologist already accepts (the reduction only ever delivers judgments and senses about experience, never a peek behind them), or there's a specific point where his method and the phenomenological method come apart, and the whole disagreement lives in that gap. So tell me: does the residue mark something phenomenology can't say, or something Dennett can't mean?

One ground rule. I'm definately not looking for a takedown of Dennett — do the translation in good faith, steelman him where the vocabulary strains. But also don't translate the phenomenally given taste of the coffee for Chase as the noema of his tasting and call it a day. If a move like that just smuggles the qualia posit back in under a Greek name, say so — and if the whole translation only works by pulling that trick somewhere, say that too.
