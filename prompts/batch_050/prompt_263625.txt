**Heritable Slopes: Why the Heritability Debate Keeps Answering the Wrong Question**

Few technical notions have been as persistently misread as heritability. Heritability (in the narrow or broad sense) is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a particular population, developing in a particular range of environments, that is statistically attributable to genetic variance in that population. It is not a property of a trait. It is not a measure of how "genetic" a trait is in any deep sense. It does not apply to individuals. It does not transfer across populations. And it is fully compatible with dramatic environmental effects: a trait can be nearly 100% heritable in a population while a uniform change in diet shifts everyone's value of it substantially, as the standard thought experiments about seeds sown in nutrient-rich and nutrient-poor soil have long illustrated.

The usual lesson drawn from this is that high heritability does not imply immutability. I think this lesson, while correct, is too weak to be useful, and I want to replace it with a sharper one. The sharper lesson requires the concept of a reaction norm: the function mapping environments to phenotypes for a given genotype. Crucially, the *shape* of a reaction norm — its slope, its curvature — can itself vary genetically. Plasticity is not the negation of genetic influence; it is a genetically variable, evolvable trait. There is, so to speak, heritability of the derivative.

Now consider two populations. In Population A, adult height is highly heritable, but a cheap nutritional supplement would raise everyone's height by the same amount regardless of genotype: the reaction norms are steep but parallel. In Population B, height at the current diet is only modestly heritable, yet genotypes differ enormously in their response to the same supplement: the reaction norms fan out, and the intervention widens disparities even as it raises the mean. A naive reading of the heritability statistics gets both cases backwards. It treats A's trait as genetically fixed (it isn't) and B's trait as environmentally malleable in an equalizing way (the actual intervention stratifies it).

My central claim is this: the policy-relevant quantity is never "how heritable is the trait" but "what is the distribution of responses, across the genotypes actually present, to the specific intervention being contemplated" — and the standard heritability estimate contains no information about the latter, because it averages over the environmental axis along which reaction norms are defined, flattening the very structure that matters. Interventions do not act within the environmental range over which the estimate was computed; they extend it, and heritability is silent about what lies beyond the data.

This, I suggest, is why public disputes over the heritability of educational attainment and similar traits go nowhere. Both sides treat h² as a verdict on malleability — one side as a near-verdict of immutability, the other as irrelevant to it — when in fact the statistic is not even *about* malleability under novel conditions. It is a summary of the status quo, and the debate is about departures from the status quo.
