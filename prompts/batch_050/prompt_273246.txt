I'm in a small history-and-philosophy-of-math reading group and we just finished Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, and I got into an argument with two of the others about whether mathematics is really quasi-empirical or whether Lakatos just cherry-picked one weird episode (the Euler polyhedron formula) that doesn't generalize. I have a bunch of gut verdicts about cases and I want to know if there's a single principle behind them, because if there is I can bring it to the group next week.

My verdicts: (1) The urchin and the hollow picture-frame cube do not refute V − E + F = 2 — they sharpen what a polyhedron is. Simply redefining "polyhedron" to dodge a counterexample (monster-barring) is illegitimate, but turning the counterexample into a condition on the theorem (lemma incorporation) is genuine mathematical progress, not face-saving. (2) Same for Cauchy's claim that a convergent series of continuous functions converges to a continuous limit: the counterexamples didn't sink anything, they forced the isolation of uniform convergence, and that's analysis getting better. (3) The four color theorem is proven, full stop — but I admit I'd be less shocked by an error surfacing in the machine-checked case enumeration than by an error in, say, Euclid, and I think that difference in my own reaction says something. (4) Fermat's Last Theorem is also proven, full stop, but here I don't feel any quasi-empirical residue at all, even though nobody has literally checked Wiles's argument back to the axioms. (5) The classification of finite simple groups counts as proven even though it sprawls across hundreds of papers, no one person has verified it all, and a gap (the quasithin case) was found and patched decades later — though here I do feel the residue from (3) again. (6) And yet: if someone exhibited a finite simple group not on the list, I would say the classification theorem had been refuted, not refined — which sits awkwardly with my verdict in (1), where counterexamples refine rather than refute.

So: is there one coherent principle that tells me when a piece of mathematics is operating in the quasi-empirical mode (conjectures tested by counterexamples, proofs resting on social or computational trust, theorems revisable in what they even say) versus the cumulative deductive mode? State the principle precisely, with the clause that each of my cases motivates, tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save — I suspect (1) and (6) can't both stand, and I'd rather know that than be told what I want to hear. End your reply with a last line beginning "Verdict:" naming the single numbered verdict your principle forces me to abandon, or "none" if it accomodates all six.
