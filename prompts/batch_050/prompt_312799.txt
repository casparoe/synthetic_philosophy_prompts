In discussions of whether video games can be art, I keep encountering the following comparison: the game designer stands to the player as a composer stands to a performer. The game's rules, mechanics, and systems are the score; each individual playthrough is a performance; and the designer's artistry consists in composing a space of possible performances, much as a composer's artistry consists in writing a score that admits of many good interpretations. The comparison is usually deployed to settle the question affirmatively: since the designer's role is structurally that of an artist whose medium is enacted rather than viewed, games qualify as art, and players, as performers, participate in the work rather than merely consuming it.

I would like an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on the conclusion it is used to reach. First, lay out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the score, the performance, the performer, the composer, and the audience. Then identify where the mapping strains. A few places where I suspect strain: in music there is a norm of fidelity, so that a performance which disregards the score is, to that extent, a bad performance of that work, whereas a player who exploits glitches, speedruns, griefs other players, or adopts house rules is not thereby failing at the game; a performer reads the whole score, whereas a game's rule system is typically discovered through play and may be deliberately opaque; and a performance is normally staged for listeners, whereas the value of a playthrough seems to accrue primarily to the player herself. But I may be wrong about which of these matter. Distinguish the disanalogies that are fatal to the intended use from the ones that are merely cosmetic, and say what the analogy quietly assumes about where artistic agency and artistic value are located — in the authored artifact, or in the act of play. Finally, does the affirmative conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along? If there is a better-governed comparison for the designer-player relation — instrument-maker and musician, architect and inhabitant, choreographer and dancer — say which fits best, and why.
