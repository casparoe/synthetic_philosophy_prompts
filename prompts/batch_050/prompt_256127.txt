So I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book about kids raised by an institution that has Plans for them (vague on purpose, I'm not here to workshop my plot), and I just reread Never Let Me Go to steal structure and instead got stuck on the ethics. Everyone talks about the book as being about organ donation and what makes someone human, but what hit me this time was the childhood angle: Hailsham gives the kids art, sports, crushes, the whole warm boarding-school package, while the adults running it know exactly how the story ends. Read it through Feinberg's idea of the child's right to an open future. The clones' futures are about as closed as futures get, but the novel seems almost more disturbed by the fact that their childhoods were made to feel open than by the donations themselves -- or is that wrong, is the real horror just the using? Where does Ishiguro actually locate the wrong: in the harvesting, in the engineering of people for it, or in raising them with the forms of a normal childhood while foreclosing the substance? Point me at the scenes that settle it (or that refuse to settle it -- Tommy's tantrums, the deferral rumor, Ruth's trip to see her "possible," Madame and the gallery all seem load-bearing). And as a practical matter for my own draft: does giving doomed kids a genuinely good childhood make the arrangement better or worse? The book seems to say something uncomfortable there and I cant tell if it has the nerve to say it outright.
