I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to get clear on population ethics after stumbling across the repugnant conclusion. What frustrates me in the summaries I find online is that each view is usually presented in isolation, with objections listed as bullet points rather than pressed by an actual interlocutor. I'd like something more adversarial.

Write a dialogue of roughly 1,200-1,500 words between two characters: one who defends the total view and is prepared to accept the repugnant conclusion as a bullet worth biting, and one who defends a critical-level view (adding a person contributes positively to the value of a population only if their well-being exceeds some positive threshold). Both characters should be philosophically sharp, fully informed about the standard moves, and equally competent -- neither should be a vehicle for the other side's talking points.

I'd like the exchange to work the way a good code review works: each character treats the other's axiology like a function and hunts for adversarial inputs -- carefully constructed populations on which the function returns an intuitively unacceptable output. Concretely, the dialogue must at some point cover:

1. The mere addition problem and the sequence of seemingly innocent steps that leads from a high-quality population to the repugnant conclusion, with the critical-level defender explaining exactly which step they reject and why rejecting it isn't arbitrary.
2. The sadistic conclusion: the total-view defender should press the point that critical-level views can rank a population of people with negative lives above a population of people with positive lives below the threshold, and the critical-level defender should respond to the actual argument, not a caricature of it.
3. The question of whether well-being thresholds can be made non-arbitrary, and what the total-view defender's own "zero level" commits them to.

Beyond those set pieces, I want real back-and-forth: each reply should take up the specific claim just made, concede points where concession is honest, and exploit concessions already on the table. Each character should also explicitly name, at some point, the counterintuitive implication of their own view that they consider the price of consistency.

End the dialogue without a winner: have the characters identify, as precisely as they can, the point at which their disagreement stops being resolvable by argument and becomes a clash of intuitions -- and have each state what observation or argument would actually change their mind, if anything.
