A question about a phenomenon I cannot find a name for, prompted by reading Joseph Butler's sermon "Upon Forgiveness of Injuries" (the ninth of his Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel, 1726). Butler treats forgiveness as the deliberate laying aside of resentment toward someone one still regards as having genuinely wronged one. My difficulty is that twice in recent years I have stopped being angry at people who injured me badly — a colleague who claimed credit for my work, and a friend who repeated something told in confidence — but nothing like Butler's process occurred. I did not reflect on the injury and resolve to forswear my anger. What happened instead is that each person slowly shrank in my estimation until their opinions and conduct no longer seemed significant enough to resent. The anger died because they ceased to matter to me, not because I made peace with them. The result outwardly resembles forgiveness — I can work with the colleague, I feel no hostility, I wish neither of them ill — yet it feels more like a demotion of the person than a reconciliation, and I notice I would not now lift a finger for either one.

Does this phenomenon have an established name? The candidates I know seem to miss it: it is not excuse or justification, since I still hold both of them responsible for a real wrong; it is not condonation, since I have not waived the offense so much as lost interest in its author; it is not forgetting, since I remember the facts perfectly; and it does not seem to be what Strawson meant by the objective attitude, since neither person is incapacitated — I simply revised downward how much their regard is worth. Would what I describe count as forgiveness on Butler's account, or does his account require the resentment to be overcome rather than merely outgrown? If there is no established term, please coin one, define it precisely, and mark its edges against forgiveness proper, condonation, and contempt — and say whether the distinction carries any moral weight, or whether only the absence of anger matters.
