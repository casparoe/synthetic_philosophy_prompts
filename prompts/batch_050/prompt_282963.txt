So I'm a philosophy PhD student working on dynamic choice and diachronic rationality, and my reading group has been stuck on Quinn's self-torturer puzzle for basically the whole semester. Quick recap in case the framing matters: the device has a dial with a huge number of settings, each click up pays you more money and increases the pain by an amount too small to notice, so every single click seems strictly worth it, until you're at the top in agony and end up paying to be ratcheted all the way back down to zero.

The group has split into two camps that keep talking past each other. One camp (call them the separatists) holds that no individual click was irrational: rationality assesses each choice against the agent's preferences at the time of choice, each click passed that test, and the real lesson is just that agents like this need precommitment devices the way Ulysses needed the mast. The disaster is a practical problem, not a rational failure, and calling any particular click irrational is a category error. The other camp (call them the patternists) holds that rationality can assess plans, policies, and patterns of choice, not just isolated acts, and that the self-torturer is irrational precisely in how the clicks hang together -- rationality requires being resolute, or having preferences over whole trajectories rather than over neighboring pairs, and an agent who can't sustain that is defective as an agent even if each local step looks fine.

I want you to draft the joint statement these two camps would produce if they actually did an adversarial collaboration instead of re-litigating the same seminar fight. Specifically: the claims both sides can honestly sign (e.g. that the endpoint is dominated, that each step is locally attractive, that precommitment would fix things in practice, whatever else survives), then the exact residual disagreement stated in wording each side would accept for its own position, then the cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling one would actually force someone to update -- and for each crux, some case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it written down.

Two test cases I designed that I'd like the statement to rule on, since I think they're where the camps' predictions actually come apart. First, the relay dial: the dial belongs to a town, and each click is made by a different person (or a different generation), who pockets the payoff and passes the slightly-worse pain level on to successors, so there is no single agent who makes all the choices. Second, the foggy dial: one agent, but the current setting is invisible to them -- they only know that clicking pays, that the pain increase is imperceptible, and roughly where they started. For each variant, say whether each camp's diagnosis generalizes, breaks, or has to be modified, since I suspect the patternists have real trouble with the relay (no one agent to be irresolute) and the separatists might have trouble with the fog (can a choice be rational if you can't tell what you're choosing between?). If you think part of the dispute is just verbal -- say, both sides actually agree about everything except what the word "irrational" properly attaches to (acts vs. agents vs. patterns) -- then say which part and defend that verdict rather than just asserting it.
