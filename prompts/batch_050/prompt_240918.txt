I work in quantitative finance and machine learning, and my native vocabulary is proper scoring rules, calibration, no-arbitrage pricing, and regret bounds in online learning. I keep encountering the Dutch book arguments for probabilism -- the claim that degrees of belief must conform to the probability axioms on pain of guaranteed loss -- and I want to know how much of that argument survives translation into terms I can actually evaluate.

Please do the following. First, restate the core Dutch book argument for the synchronic probability axioms (non-negativity, normalization, finite additivity) as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of a trader quoting prices on binary contracts, or equivalently of a forecaster being evaluated by a scoring rule. Do the same for the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalization as the rational update rule, using the vocabulary of dynamic trading strategies or of sequential prediction with regret guarantees. For each translation, be explicit about the load-bearing premises: where does risk neutrality enter, what role does the assumption of a willing counterparty play, and what exactly is the analogue of "sure loss" in each framing?

Second, and this is the part I care most about, give me a residue report. Which components of the philosophical arguments translate cleanly, and is the translation in those cases genuinely faithful or does it quietly substitute a stronger claim (for instance, substituting the existence of a strictly proper scoring rule for the pragmatic coherence argument)? Which components survive only under distortion -- I suspect the diachronic argument is one of these, since my field has well-known cases where committing to an update rule in advance is not the same as regarding that rule as the unique rational response to new evidence -- and what exactly gets bent? And which components have no expression at all in my vocabulary: I want your assessment of whether the move from "incoherent beliefs expose you to guaranteed loss" to "incoherent beliefs are epistemically irrational" is a substantive normative step that the trading and scoring-rule framings simply cannot state, or whether it dissolves once the translation is done honestly.

Third, close with a verdict. For each item of residue you identified, say whether it indicates that my vocabulary is missing something the epistemologists have, or that the Dutch book tradition was claiming more than the underlying mathematics delivers. If your answer differs across the synchronic and diachronic cases, explain why. I am not asking whether the probability axioms are a good idea -- my whole career assumes they are. I am asking whether the Dutch book argument, stripped of its betting vocabulary, establishes anything that the scoring-rule and no-arbitrage results I already accept do not, and if so, what.
