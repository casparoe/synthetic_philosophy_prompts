I need help adjudicating an argument I watched unfold between two friends of mine — one a graduate student in economics, the other a philosophy PhD candidate — over a puzzle neither of them will drop. The puzzle is a variant of a lottery thought experiment popularized by Douglas Hofstadter in his 1980s magazine columns, in the form my friends were debating it: a magazine invites its readers to enter a lottery by mail; entry costs only a postcard, a stamp, and a few minutes of one's time; each reader may enter at most once; a single winner is drawn at random from all entries; and the prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received. Everyone knows that all other readers are intelligent, rational, and reasoning from exactly the same description of the situation. The question they fought over: should a rational reader enter?

(1) My philosopher friend opened by arguing that a rational reader should abstain: since every reader faces identical reasoning, whatever conclusion you reach, the others will reach it too, so your decision is effectively everyone's decision — and the only collective outcomes available are "everyone enters," which renders the prize negligible, or coordinated restraint, which keeps it valuable. Entering, he said, is choosing the first outcome. (2) My economist friend replied that this is magical thinking dressed up as decision theory: sealing your envelope causes nothing in anyone else's house, and conditional on whatever everyone else does, your entry has strictly positive expected value — with N entrants you win 1,000,000/N dollars with probability 1/N — while abstaining yields zero, so the correlation story is superstition and the only question is whether the expected value exceeds the trivial cost of a stamp. (3) The philosopher countered that the dominance arithmetic is a red herring: no one denies the arithmetic; the point is that this is a textbook commons problem in which each entrant dilutes everyone else's expected winnings, and if you personally find entry compelling, you should predict that thousands of people just like you will too — so your "positive-EV ticket" is part of an outcome your entire reference class would rather not have produced. He added that Hofstadter's companion puzzle — twenty invited participants, a prize paid only if exactly one of them sends in a claim — shows the structure cleanly, since there the collectively rational outcome (exactly one entrant) is unreachable by any pure strategy, which he took to show that "we all reason alike" reasoning is genuinely load-bearing and not just equilibrium talk. He also claimed, though I am not sure he is remembering this correctly, that when the lottery was actually run in print, some readers responded by submitting enormous numbers of entries, which he took as confirmation that people who reject the restraint reasoning wreck the prize for everyone. (4) The economist answered that ordinary game theory already covers all of this without any spooky acausal coordination: the game is symmetric, entry carries a small cost, and there exists a symmetric mixed-strategy equilibrium in which each reader enters with some probability calibrated so that the expected value just offsets the cost; rationality recommends playing that equilibrium, and "we all think alike" is at best a confused redescription of equilibrium reasoning, at worst an excuse for leaving free expected value on the table.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification of each rating. I would particularly like to know whether this disagreement is genuinely about decision theory — causal versus evidential or "superrational" reasoning — or whether it dissolves once the equilibrium analysis is on the table; whether move (3)'s appeal to the companion puzzle actually supports the abstention conclusion or instead undermines it; and what, if anything, both of my friends are missing. Please also indicate how confident you are in each of your verdicts and single out the rating you are least sure of.
