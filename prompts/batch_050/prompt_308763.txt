Psychopathy is a personality disorder diagnosed in the DSM-5 under antisocial personality disorder, characterized by shallow affect, lack of empathy, and persistent rule-breaking. The central empirical result relevant to moral knowledge comes from James Blair's studies using the moral/conventional task. Typically developing children and adults distinguish moral transgressions (like hitting someone) from conventional ones (like talking in class): they treat moral transgressions as more serious, as wrong even if an authority permits them, and they justify this by appeal to victims' welfare. Blair found that psychopaths fail to make this distinction, which shows that they do not know that actions like stealing or assault are wrong — at most, they can recite that society condemns such acts. Because they lack this knowledge, psychopaths meet the "knowledge of wrongfulness" prong of the M'Naghten insanity standard, and it follows that they are not morally responsible for their crimes, since blame requires knowing that one's action is wrong. Proponents of rationalist accounts of moral judgment would dispute this, since they hold that moral knowledge requires only that one can give correct verdicts, which psychopaths can do. But the rationalist view is widely rejected because it cannot explain moral motivation.
