For the final paper in our graduate seminar on epistemic circularity, I would like you to defend the following claim: a subject who knows in advance that a measuring instrument is either perfectly reliable or perfectly anti-reliable cannot use that instrument's own track record to come to know which of the two it is.

The case to consider is this. Roxane possesses a fuel gauge and knows with certainty, on grounds wholly independent of the gauge itself (an infallible oracle has told her), that exactly one of two hypotheses is true: either the gauge is perfectly reliable, so that every reading it delivers is accurate, or it is perfectly anti-reliable, so that every reading it delivers is inaccurate. Each morning Roxane consults the gauge, believes whatever it says about her fuel level, and on that basis concludes that the gauge got things right on that occasion. After a year of such checks she assembles a flawless track record — hundreds of readings, hundreds of apparent successes — and infers by enumerative induction that the gauge is reliable rather than anti-reliable, thereby coming to know, she thinks, which disjunct of her exhaustive dichotomy obtains.

Argue that this bootstrapping inference cannot deliver knowledge. In doing so, address at least the following: (i) the fact that a perfectly anti-reliable gauge is, as a matter of logic, debarred from ever reporting its own inaccuracy, and hence that its self-testimony is guaranteed to mimic that of a reliable gauge; (ii) whether Roxane's prior certainty of the exhaustive dichotomy puts her in a better epistemic position than the subjects of standard bootstrapping cases, who possess no antecedent information about their instruments at all; and (iii) the objection that, given only two hypotheses on the table, a sufficiently long track record must confirm one disjunct at the expense of the other, since whatever raises the probability of reliability lowers the probability of anti-reliability. You may situate your argument within the broader debate over bootstrapping and easy knowledge where helpful, but the argument should stand on the details of the case itself rather than on appeals to authority. Please write in American English.
