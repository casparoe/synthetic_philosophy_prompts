For our school philosophy society I tried to turn Hofstadter's superrationality idea into a formal argument, and I seem to have proved that coordination problems cannot exist, which is clearly wrong. The set-up is his Luring Lottery: a prize is offered whose value shrinks as more people enter, and everyone is told that all entrants are reasoners just like themselves. My argument: (1) In a perfectly symmetric situation, where every agent has the same information, the same payoffs and the same reasoning powers, whatever conclusion is rational for one agent is rational for all, so fully rational agents must choose identically. (2) So when deliberating, I may treat my own choice as a choice on behalf of everyone: I should compare outcomes under the supposition that whatever I do, everyone does. (3) Applied to the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, this requires cooperation, since universal cooperation beats universal defection. (4) Applied to the Luring Lottery, it requires that almost nobody enters, so I should stay out or enter only with tiny probability. (5) But the same reasoning applies to choosing which side of the road to drive on: I must choose for everyone, yet both universal-left and universal-right are perfectly good outcomes, so the reasoning gives me no answer at all — it tells me to do what everyone does while telling me that everyone does what I do. (6) So superrationality either proves too much (dissolving every coordination problem by fiat, when such problems plainly exist) or proves nothing in precisely the cases where help is needed. (7) Therefore at least one of (1) and (2) is false. I would like to keep (1), as bare symmetry seems undeniable, but I suspect the slide is between (1) and (2) — from a prediction about what rational agents will do to a rule about how I should deliberate.
