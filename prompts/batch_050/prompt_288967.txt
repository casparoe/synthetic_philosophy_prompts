I run a small departmental colloquium series, and our upcoming guest is a political economist defending the use of safe Pareto improvements as a policy criterion: roughly, the idea that a reform is legitimate when it leaves everyone who exists under either the status quo or the reform at least as well off, thereby sidestepping the non-identity problem that trips up ordinary Pareto reasoning. I'd like six to eight questions, each with a follow-up tailored to the reply I expect. At minimum, press on the following: first, whether the criterion's appeal to unanimity smuggles in a strong status-quo bias, giving de facto veto power to whoever benefits from existing arrangements (she will likely reply that the criterion is only permissive, not exhaustive, so ask what work it does when it blesses so few reforms); second, how the interpersonal welfare comparisons required to certify that nobody is worse off can be made without the very aggregation the criterion was meant to avoid; third, whether hypothetical rather than actual compensation collapses the view back into Kaldor-Hicks with extra steps. End with one question about a case where she thinks the criterion delivers the intuitively wrong verdict. No general background questions, please — assume I've read the book.
