So I'm in a reading group that's been working through Kant's Groundwork, and last week we got completely stuck on the universalizability tests -- specifically on whether there's any single version of the test that gives the right answers without also giving a bunch of wrong ones. Everyone kept proposing fixes and counterexamples until 1am and we got nowhere, so I figured I'd just lay out my actual gut verdicts on cases and ask: what principle, if any, is behind them? Here they are. Borrowing money on a lying promise to repay: clearly wrong, and I actually buy the classic move that a world where everyone did this is somehow self-undermining, since promising itself would collapse. Never developing any of my talents: honestly seems fine to me, or at worst a personal failing -- a whole world of coasters is lazy, not contradictory. Never helping anyone, ever: wrong, even though a world of universal non-helpers seems perfectly conceivable, just bleak. Lying to the murderer at the door: not only fine but obligatory, whatever Kant himself said. Lying to a telemarketer about being interested: fine. Lying to my spouse about how much I spent on a guitar: not fine, though I can't fully say why it differs from the telemarketer. And free-riding -- hopping the subway turnstile while everyone else pays: not fine, but here's the thing that broke our discussion, if I describe my maxim as "hop the turnstile when wearing this specific green jacket on a Tuesday," it universalizes trivially, which makes the whole test feel like a cheat that depends on how you phrase things.

So: is there one principle (or one principled pairing of tests -- contradiction in conception vs. contradiction in the will, or something else entirely) that captures most of these? Notice my verdicts pull in different directions: the false-promise and no-help cases push me toward the "contradiction in willing" reading over the "contradiction in conception" one, but then my coasting-is-fine verdict refuses to cooperate, and my three lying verdicts need some clause that splits them without just being ad hoc. My tentative stab was something like: an act is wrong iff universalizing its maxim would frustrate the very mechanism the act itself relies on -- but the no-help case and the lying cases don't obviously fit that, so feel free to tear it apart. Tell me which of my verdicts a decent principle has to sacrifice, and why that one rather than the others. And then tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I didn't list and keep flip-flopping on: exaggerating on a CV in a hiring pool where you suspect everyone else does it, and breaking a promise you made to someone who has since died and will never know.
