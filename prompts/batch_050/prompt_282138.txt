I teach a second-year undergrad course on moral progress, about 18 students, one 75-minute session. We're doing the unit on how practices like slavery and dueling actually died out, and the usual lecture version -- some say it was moral arguments winning, some say economic interests, Appiah says shifts in honor and status -- just slides off them, they nod and write it down.

Design an in-class simulation that makes them feel the difference between these three mechanisms. My rough idea: stage some entrenched, mildly absurd practice the class is locked into by a status system (real points or privileges at stake, like who gets to pick the debrief snacks or skip a reading), and then run three rounds in sequence: first someone presents genuinely good arguments against the practice, then the facilitator changes what the practice costs, then the facilitator manipulates what's considered embarrassing vs prestigious. I want them to discover that the killer arguments do basically nothing, and to argue afterwards about which of the other two actually collapsed it -- because I'd love if the game genuinely leaves that ambiguous rather than rigging it for Appiah.

Give me: the practice and rules (keep it simple enough to explain in 5 minutes), the status/payoff mechanics, a timeline, what I do in each round, and a debrief that maps each round onto the arguments/interests/honor debate and onto real cases (dueling, footbinding, Atlantic slavery). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong -- e.g. students treating it as improv comedy, or collapsing the norm too fast -- and one honest note on what this can't reproduce, since obviously nobody's life is at stake and real norm shifts take decades, not 40 minutes.
