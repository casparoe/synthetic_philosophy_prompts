I spent forty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and only got into philosophy a couple years ago through an online reading group that just worked its way through Polybius's Histories. One passage from Book VI (in the old Shuckburgh translation) has been rattling around in my head for weeks, because it seems to describe something I already know the history of:

"Then come tumultuous assemblies, massacres, banishments, redivisions of land; until, after losing all trace of civilisation, it has once more found a master and a despot."

When I read that, I thought: that's the French Revolution, beat for beat — the political clubs and street mobs, the September Massacres, the emigré nobles, the sale of church lands, and then Napoleon tidying up the mess. So here is what I'd like you to do. Take Polybius's whole anacyclosis — the six-stage cycle running monarchy, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, ochlocracy, and back to monarchy — and analyze the French Revolution from 1789 through Napoleon's coup in 1799 as a run through that cycle. Which stages map onto which phases of the Revolution, and where does the mapping feel forced? The part I find genuinely puzzling is the timescale: Polybius describes constitutional decay as taking generations (he says democracy goes bad by the time it reaches people's grandchildren), whereas France seems to have done the entire loop in about a decade. Does that refute him, or does a cycle compressed into ten years actually tell in his favor? And don't let him off easy where it doesn't fit — the war with most of Europe running through everything, the Thermidor reaction, the fact that the Bourbon restoration in 1815 arguably restarts the cycle rather than the Revolution closing it. My worry, coming from a data-driven field, is that a sufficiently vague cycle can be imposed on any turbulent decade after the fact, and I'd like you to address whether anacyclosis predicts anything here or just redescribes it.

Please give your answer as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
