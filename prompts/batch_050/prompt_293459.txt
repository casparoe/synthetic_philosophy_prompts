Ok so I'm in an ethics semniar this semester and we just read Susan Wolf's piece on moral saints — the argument that someone whose every single action is as morally good as it could possibly be would end up being a deeply unappealing person: no irony, no dumb hobbies, no personal projects, everything subordinated to the moral point of view. Our prof suggested we pressure-test the argument against fiction, and I want to write my essay on A Christmas Carol, because post-conversion Scrooge seems like an obvious candidate saint.

Read the novella through Wolf's lens and help me figure out what my thesis should even be. Some stuff I'm stuck on:

1. Wolf splits saints into the Loving Saint (whose own happiness just consists in other people doing well) and the Rational Saint (who is unhappy but does their duty anyway). The whole point of Dickens's ending is that Scrooge is ecstatic — the narrator piles up similes about how gleeful he is. Does that make him a Loving Saint, or is something subtler going on: his happiness is caused by generosity rather than being literally constituted by other people's welfare? I can't tell if that distinction survives scrutiny or if it's just word games.

2. Wolf claims a moral saint would have to be unfailingly nice, couldn't have a cynical or sarcastic sense of humor, and probably couldn't enjoy gourmet meals or difficult novels since the time and money could do more good elsewhere. Converted Scrooge is playful — he basically pranks the Cratchits with the anonymous turkey and messes with Bob the next morning before revealing the raise. Is Dickens just demonstrating that Wolf is wrong, that sainthood is compatible with having an actual personality? Or does the book cheat by only showing us him for about a day, before the daily grind of maximal goodness would kick in?

3. The scope problem: his goodness is super local — the Cratchits, Fred, the charity collectors. Wolf's saint maximizes globally. Would the genuinely-maximally-good Scrooge have liquidated the firm and gone off to relieve famine somewhere? Is what Dickens depicts even sainthood, or just a rich guy being generous with money he doesn't need?

4. Does the story's charm depend on conversion being rare? If everyone woke up on Christmas morning acting like Scrooge, would it still be heartwarming, or does the narrative secretly require the rest of the world to stay indifferent so the saint stands out?

5. Finally — and this is where I want the essay to land — does the novella add something to the debate that Wolf's argument alone can't? Scrooge's conversion is framed as recovering a self he'd lost (the Fezziwig scenes, his sister, Belle), not erasing one, which looks like a direct counter to Wolf's worry that sainthood swallows the self. But you could equally read it as supporting the utilitarian rejoinder: sainthood costs Scrooge nothing because miserliness was making him miserable anyway, so Wolf's trade-off between morality and personal wellbeing only really bites for people who were happy before.

Be honest about where the reading strains — I don't want to hand in an essay pretending a Victorian ghost story cleanly settles a journal article. And give me a rough skeleton for how the essay should be structured.
