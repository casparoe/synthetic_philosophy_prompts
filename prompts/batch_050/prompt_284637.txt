I coordinate a small philosophy reading circle attached to an adult-education program, and over three recent sessions on whether we can ever know that other beings have inner lives, one move kept resurfacing in different mouths. In its basic form it runs: I know from the inside what my own fear, pain, and amusement are like; I can observe that your body is built like mine and that your behavior tracks mine in every respect I am able to check; therefore there is presumably something it is like to be you. One member used this to brush off a visiting skeptic who claimed that, for all anyone knows, the rest of us are mindless. A second member extended the same move downward, arguing that her dog and even an octopus must have feelings because their writhing, avoidance, and play resemble ours. A third extended it sideways, insisting that a language model which produces eloquent, contextually apt reports of distress and confusion has thereby met the very same standard the rest of us meet, so denying it an inner life would be special pleading. A version of this argument is old -- it goes back at least to John Stuart Mill -- and I would like to give the group a rigorous audit of it rather than another evening of trading intuitions.

Here is what I want the audit to contain. First, an explicit correspondence table. In my own case the chain seems to run: bodily event, then felt experience, then outward behavior. State what plays each of these three roles for my neighbor, for the dog, for the octopus, and for the language model, and flag the asymmetry that in my own case I observe all three links while for everything else I observe only the first and third and posit the second. Second, the places where the mapping breaks, with a verdict on which breaks matter for which use: my inductive base is a single case, namely myself, so say what kind of inference from a sample of one this is and whether that is a fatal defect or a routine feature of reasoning to unobservables; outward behavior can in principle be produced by imitation, training, or design rather than by feeling, which seems to matter enormously for the language model and hardly at all for the neighbor -- explain why, without simply asserting it; and similarity comes in degrees, since the octopus shares little of my anatomy, so say whether the argument fades gradually or fails at some threshold, and whether demanding a threshold is even a fair request to make of this kind of reasoning. Third, identify what the analogy quietly imports: that similar effects have similar causes, that my own case is a representative specimen of mindedness rather than a peculiar one, and that the felt middle link is what does the causal work in producing behavior rather than riding along with it.

Finally, the payoff questions. Does the modest, anti-skeptical use -- my neighbor has an inner life -- survive the audit once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying a conclusion it cannot support? Do the extensions to the dog, the octopus, and the language model stand or fall together with the human case, or does each fail at a different link in the chain, and if the language model fails where the octopus succeeds, what exactly is the difference that makes the difference? If the argument from analogy cannot bear the weight, say what the better reconstruction would be -- an inference to the best explanation, a non-inferential recognitional capacity, something else -- and be honest about whether these alternatives are genuinely different arguments or the same inference wearing a more respectable name. The group will not be satisfied with the label argument from analogy plus a thumbs-up or thumbs-down; they want to know exactly what the comparison can carry, for which creatures, and why.
