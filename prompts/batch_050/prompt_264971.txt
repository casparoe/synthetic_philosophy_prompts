I help run a weekly discussion group for first-year uni students, about 18-20 people, 90 minutes. Design an activity that makes them feel hermeneutical injustice from the inside -- not the standard "nobody believes the woman's report" thing they've all ready about, but the weirder version where you literally lack the shared vocabulary to say what's happening to you, so you come off as confused or incoherent even to yourself.

Here's the mechanic I'm hoping for: split the group so that one subgroup is given an experience or task the rules never give them words for, and they have to report on it to the others using only the "official" vocabulary the game supplies, which doesn't fit. Ideally the mismatch is engineered so that their reports genuinely sound muddled to the rest of the room, and some of them start doubting their own experience rather than the vocabulary. Bonus if the game is set up so the whole group actually needs the muddled reports to be accurate in order to win, so the listeners pay a real cost for dismissing them -- I want them to feel that the injustice harms the hearers too, not just the speakers. Small stakes are fine (points, chocolate, w/e). No lecture up front, and nobody should hear the terms testimonial or hermeneutical injustice until the debrief.

Debrief should map specific moments of the game onto the distinction between being disbelieved and being inarticulable, ask whether anyone in the "disadvantaged" subgroup started to distrust thier own experience, and give me a way to talk about what it would take to fix the problem -- is it on the listeners to listen harder, or does someone have to build new shared concepts, and whose job is that. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I suspect the muddled subgroup will just invent their own slang in two minutes flat, which kind of ruins it, so tell me how to prevent that or whether I should let it happen and use it).

Give it to me as exactly eight numbered items -- setup, materials, rules of play, the moment it bites, how to score it, debrief questions, what breaks on the first run, and one thing the activity structurally can't reproduce that I'll have to supply by just telling them -- a sentence or two each, no intro, no conclusion.
