The school choice fight — vouchers, charters, homeschooling — has been running for decades and the evidence seems to have settled into an awkward shape: lottery-based studies find big charter gains in some cities and nothing much in others, the few randomised voucher evaluations are mixed with one large statewide programme apparently showing actual test-score drops, attainment effects (graduation, college entry) sometimes look better than the test scores do, and homeschooling research is close to unusable because of selection. Both sides cite this literature with total confidence and nobody budges. My hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because its actually three disagreements wearing one costume: an empirical one about average effects that may be unanswerable because effects are so context-dependent, a methodological one about which outcomes should count (scores vs attainment vs parental satisfaction vs civic formation), and a values one about whether parental choice is worth something in itself regardless of outcomes — with the values one doing most of the work while everyone pretends its the empirical one. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a real answerable question buried in here somewhere? And if I'm roughly right, what would genuinely count as progress — better research designs, agreeing the outcome list in advance, or just forcing people to separate the value claims from the effect claims?
