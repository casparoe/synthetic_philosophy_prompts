I got stuck on something while rereading Spinoza's Ethics and want your take. The final part opens (Part V preface, Elwes translation) with him saying: "At length I pass to the remaining portion of my Ethics, which is concerned with the way leading to freedom. I shall therefore treat therein of the power of the reason, showing how far the reason can control the emotions, and what is the nature of Mental Freedom or Blessedness; we shall then be able to see, how much more powerful the wise man is than the ignorant."

But back in Part I he argues that nothing in the universe is contingent and everything follows necessarily from God's nature — so the wise person and the ignorant person were each always going to be exactly what they are. In what sense is there a "way leading to freedom" that one could take or fail to take? And the intellectual love of God that the book builds up to — is it an achievement, or just a label for what some minds were determined to arrive at from eternity?

So: give me your actual credence that Spinoza's system is coherent on this point — that his necessitarianism leaves the "way leading to freedom" and the intellectual love of God standing as a genuine ethical ideal rather than collapsing into fatalism with extra steps. Decompose it: the probability that strict necessitarianism/determinism about human action is actually true, the probability that if true it undermines talk of ideals and "ways" (the compatibilism question), the probability that Spinoza's own resources (freedom as acting from the necessity of ones own nature, knowledge as a cause that transforms what you are) genuinely get him out of it, plus any other term you think belongs. A number and one line of justification each, then the combination. And tell me which sub-question you're least sure about and what would shift it.
