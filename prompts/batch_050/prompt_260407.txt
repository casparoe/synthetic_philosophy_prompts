I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a draft of my third book, and I've written myself into a corner I didn't expect. One of my main characters, Marek, is a retired accountant who has brain surgery late in the book (an anterior cingulotomy, for reasons that are complicated and honestly not that important) and afterwards develops something like pain asymbolia: he still says he feels pain when it happens, he can locate it, describe it, even rate how intense it is, but it just doesn't bother him anymore. There's a scene where he leans his palm on a hot stovetop and notices the pain in an almost curious way, like reading a weather report, and pulls his hand away only because his wife screams at him. My sister is an ICU nurse and when I ran the scene by her over beers she said the neurology sounded roughly plausible, but then she asked me whether Marek's condition was supposed to be a tragedy or a strange kind of gift, and I realized I don't actually know, and the answer seems to depend on a philosophy question I can't settle.

So here's what I need. Compare two views about what makes pain bad for the person who has it. The first says the badness lives in the raw feel of the thing itself, the intrinsic unpleasantness of the sensation, so anything that feels like pain is bad just by feeling that way. The second says the badness lives in what pain does to us: the desperate need for it to stop, the way it hijacks attention and steamrolls our projects and desires, so a pain that nobody minds and that interrupts nothing isn't really bad at all, or at least not bad in the way that matters. Pain asymbolia seems built to drive a wedge between these two, since people like Marek apparently keep the sensation but lose everything else. Walk me through which view handles the case better, including the strongest thing the losing side can say (I suspect the first view can accuse the second of confusing pain with what usually accompanies pain, and the second can accuse the first of positing a badness that no one, not even the sufferer, can bring themselves to care about).

Then tell me how to write him: on the better view, is Marek's post-surgery pain still bad for him even though he'd shrug about it, and is his condition a loss I should mourn on the page, a liberation I should quietly celebrate, or something genuinely ambiguous where the reader should feel both pulls at once? I'd rather get the philosophy right and let the drama follow than the other way around. Wrap up by putting your overall call in a machine-readable form at the very end, e.g. \answer{sensory view, pain still bad} or \answer{motivational view, condition is a liberation} or whatever short phrase actually captures your conclusion.
