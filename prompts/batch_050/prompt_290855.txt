The inefficacy objection -- the "your individual contribution makes no difference" line -- gets run as if it were one argument across climate change, factory farming, and voting. But I think the causal structure is totally different in the three cases and nobody seems to keep them apart. With climate, plausibly each extra tonne of emissions does a tiny bit of actual marginal harm (there's no threshold, the damage function is smooth-ish). With meat, my purchase almost never changes what gets produced at all, except on the rare occassion it tips a supermarket's ordering threshold and a whole batch of extra animals gets farmed. With voting, the thing that matters is the tiny chance of being pivotal. So "making a difference" is doing at least three different jobs here: marginal continuous harm, expected harm via a lumpy threshold, and small probability of decisively changing a big outcome. And the standard replies to the objection (expected-value reasoning, complicity, collective obligation, rule-based stuff) might each work for some cases and silently fail for others. E.g. the expected-value reply that's the obvious move in the meat case is *unnecessary* in the climate case, where there is no inefficacy to answer, and looks weird in the voting case for different reasons.
