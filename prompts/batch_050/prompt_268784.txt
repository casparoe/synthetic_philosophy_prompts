I'm a senior in high school and part of a small philosophy reading group with some classmates. On this view, pleasure is valuable only insofar as it relieves or prevents such tension, so a state of deeply contented rest is not a "small positive" on a hedonic scale but a kind of perfection, and adding more pleasure on top of it adds nothing. My friend called the view "tranquilism." Before I try to evaluate it or compare it to other theories of welfare and value, I want to know what I should actually be asking of a theory in this area.

Please write out the desiderata: the requirements that any adequate theory of what has final value — with special attention to whether tranquility-style views could meet them — would have to satisfy. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. I'm interested in things like: which test cases a theory must give a verdict on (e.g., dreamless sleep or general anesthesia vs. mild boredom; a life of intense joy punctuated by real grief vs. a life of undisturbed calm with no highs; the choice between a pinprick for everyone and the prevention of one person's agony; adding lives that are free of suffering but contain no pleasure either); which distinctions it cannot collapse (e.g., between the absence of felt suffering and neutral hedonic tone, between craving as a felt disturbance and merely preferring something, between a person's life going better and the world going better); and which commitments it must avoid falling into without further argument (e.g., implying it would be good to painlessly end all sentient life, or that a moment of excruciating suffering could be outweighed by enough mild contentment, or that nobody ever has a reason to get out of bed). For each desideratum, mark whether you think it's genuinely non-negotiable for any theory of value or instead a contested intuition that one camp treats as decisive.

Second, say which single requirement knocks out the most standard contenders, and then, briefly (a table is fine), score classical hedonistic utilitarianism, negative utilitarianism, and a preference-satisfaction view against the list.
