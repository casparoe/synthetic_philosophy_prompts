I’m helping a museum rewrite its collections policy and realized it seems to smuggle in a whole metaphysics of objects. The current rules are roughly these:

- Separate bones excavated from the same site are catalogued as separate objects, even if they almost certainly came from one animal.
- Once conservators articulate those bones into a skeleton, the skeleton gets its own catalogue number and is treated as one object. Removing the skull temporarily does not create two objects; the skull is still listed as part of the skeleton wherever it is.
- A shattered ceramic bowl in a labelled bag still counts as the same bowl if restoration is planned. But if the shards are permanently incorporated into a mosaic, the bowl is gone and the museum now has a mosaic containing former bowl-fragments.
- A wooden boat can have planks replaced one at a time and remain the same boat, apparently without limit, as long as the replacement follows its existing structure and the museum never stores all the old parts as a competing “original boat.” If the boat is completely disassembled and put back together, though, policy calls the result a reconstruction rather than the original object.
- Pairs and sets are treated differently: a pair of shoes is not itself an object, just two linked catalogue records, while a cabinet of curiosities can be one object even when its drawers contain separately catalogued specimens.

What view about when many things compose a further thing does this practice presuppose? Is it committed to some middle position between “any plurality automatically forms an object” and “there are no composite objects,” or can it be explained as mere bookkeeping with no metaphysical commitments at all? Please distinguish composition from persistence through change, since the boat rule may be about the latter rather than the former.

Also, which parts of the policy seem to depend on intentions, functions, or social classification rather than physical arrangement? Are any of the rules inconsistent with each other? Finally, say what would have to change if the museum adopted unrestricted composition, compositional nihilism, or the view that only living organisms are genuine composite objects. I’m especially interested in where the policy is philosophically underdetermined and doesn’t actually choose between those theories, even if staff talk as though it does.
