A colleague has offered the following argument as a route from the meta-problem of consciousness to illusionism:

1. A scientifically respectable theory can answer only to publicly checkable data: verbal reports, confidence judgments, discriminatory behavior, and neural measurements.

2. For any allegedly phenomenal state, every such datum can in principle be redescribed as the subject’s disposition to classify an inner state as intrinsic, private, ineffable, and not exhausted by any physical-functional account.

3. Therefore, a complete account of the mechanisms producing those classifications, together with the intuitions that consciousness is mysterious and that zombies are conceivable, would explain every datum to which a science of consciousness could answer.

4. If all the data can be explained without positing phenomenal properties, then phenomenal properties are otiose: the hard problem is merely a predictable misdescription generated by introspection.

5. But the same form of reasoning appears applicable to tables. All scientific data concerning tables consist in perceptions, reports, measurements, and related judgments. A complete account of the mechanisms producing table-judgments would therefore explain every datum to which table-science could answer, making tables themselves otiose and the apparent external world a cognitive illusion. That conclusion seems absurd.

6. So either the inference from explaining all the data to eliminating the posited phenomenon is invalid, or consciousness must differ from tables in some respect that has not yet been established.

7. Since no such difference has been established, no solution to the meta-problem can by itself justify illusionism.

Which step should be rejected first, and why did it initially seem plausible? In answering, distinguish at least three things that might be meant by a datum: a public record available to science, evidence that justifies a subject’s belief, and the phenomenon ultimately to be explained. Also assess whether consciousness is disanalogous to tables because first-person appearance might partly constitute its object, or because introspective judgments might be self-presenting in a way table-judgments are not. Finally, state what the strongest defensible version of the argument establishes: merely that an explanation of consciousness-talk is possible, that such an explanation would be scientifically sufficient, or that phenomenal realism is metaphysically unjustified. What would an illusionist and a phenomenal realist each have to concede at the point where their interpretations diverge?
