I teach philosophy of mathematics, and after running a graduate seminar on set-theoretic independence this past term I have been trying to take stock of my own considered reactions to the various things that have been, or could be, offered as resolutions of the continuum hypothesis. I find that my reactions do not obviously cohere, and I would like to know whether some single principle about what it is to settle an independent question lies beneath them -- or whether I must give something up. So here are my verdicts, stated as I find them. First, a derivation of CH from ZFC would settle the matter, full stop; that is the boring anchor case. Second, Cohen's forcing result, though it established undecidability rather than truth, strikes me as genuinely illuminating: it transformed the question rather than merely announcing a stalemate, and I count it as real intellectual progress of a kind I want my principle to honor. Third, Godel's axiom of constructibility implies CH and has ample motivation independent of it, yet adopting V=L does not feel to me like learning the size of the continuum; it feels like declining to ask the question. Fourth -- and this is where I suspect trouble -- the proper forcing axiom implies that the continuum has cardinality aleph-two, and if one adopted PFA on the strength of its broader mathematical fruitfulness, I find that this would feel like receiving a genuine answer. Fifth, simply adjoining CH itself as a new axiom obviously settles nothing; that is stipulation, not discovery. Sixth, Freiling's dart-throwing symmetry argument moves me intuitively every time I revisit it, but I deny that it settles anything; probabilistic intuition applied to arbitrary sets of reals is precisely what I do not trust. Seventh, the multiverse proposal, on which CH simply has no determinate answer, strikes me as surrender dressed as resolution.

The pressure point is plain: my third and fourth verdicts are hard to reconcile, since V=L and PFA are both axioms with independent motivation that decide the question in opposite directions, and I cannot see why one constitutes an answer while the other constitutes an evasion. What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough to be falsified by a case, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. For each clause of the principle, identify which case motivates it. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the simplest defensible version of the principle forces me to abandon -- I suspect it is either the third or the fourth, but I want the reasoning, and I want to know whether the distinction between restrictive and expansive axiom candidates, or between intrinsic and extrinsic justification, can do the work without collapsing into a restatement of my intuitions. Finally, test your principle against a case I have not listed: suppose set theorists produced a canonical inner model, as structurally rigid and well-understood as L but capable of accommodating the large cardinals that L cannot, and suppose CH failed in that model. Would that settle the continuum hypothesis, or is it my third case all over again with the opposite verdict? Please also indicate what your principle predicts about the status of questions like projective determinacy, where the dialectic played out differently.
