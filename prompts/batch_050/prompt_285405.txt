I teach a second-year undergrad philosophy of law seminar, 24 students, and I've got one 80-minute session coming up on evidence and proof. I want an in-class activity -- basically a staged mock trial -- that makes them feel in their gut the problem of naked statistical evidence (the gatecrasher case, the blue bus case): the weird fact that a plaintiff can be more likely than not to be right, even way more likely, and a verdict based on that alone still feels like a miscarriage of justice.

Rough shape I have in mind, but improve on it freely:

Part 1: they should actually convict someone on preponderance of the evidence using nothing but a base rate, and then sit with how gross it feels. I was thinking everyone draws a card at the door, some known number of the 24 cards say "didn't pay admission," one student gets charged as a gatecrasher, and the only evidence the jury gets is the base rate. Real jury instructions, real deliberation, real verdict.

Part 2: rerun the structure as the blue bus version (bus company with X% market share of the buses on that route is the only evidence) so they see the discomfort wasn't an artifact of my first setup. Maybe with a different jury and a higher market share, like 95%, to see where their threshold actually is.

Part 3: pull the rug. Give them individuated-looking evidence instead -- an eyewitness of stated reliability, or a partial plate match -- and let them convict comfortably, then force the question of why that was better, given that witness reliability is itself just a statistic. Ideally at least one juror flips relative to Part 1 and then can't explain why.

What I need from you: full rules, materials, and roles; a timeline that fits 80 minutes; jury instructions for both preponderance of the evidence AND beyond a reasonable doubt so I can split the room and compare how the two standards change the verdicts; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual philosophy -- the gatecrasher paradox (why the same logic seems to let the plaintiff win against literally everyone who attended, which can't be right), the reference class problem, and the "all evidence is probabilistic anyway" rejoinder (why do we happily accept an 80% reliable witness but not an 80% market share?). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing jurors will smuggle in invented evidence, and the defendent in Part 1 might take it personally -- and how to handle those. And be honest about which feature of the real legal/philosophical debate a classroom version just can't reproduce.

One hard constraint: no lecture up front. They shouldn't hear the phrase "naked statistical evidence" until the debrief. They need to discover the discomfort by reaching the verdict themselves.
