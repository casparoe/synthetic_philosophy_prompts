I'm a philosophy major taking a course on social conventions and moral obligation, and I also work part-time in my university's study-abroad office, where I help run pre-departure orientations. Students constantly ask whether they really must follow local norms — tipping in the U.S., queueing in Britain, not jaywalking in Germany, covering up at religious sites — and our office's only answer is "when in Rome," which settles nothing. I want to turn the philosophical question of whether conventions generate genuine moral obligations into a one-page decision procedure we can hand to departing students: an ordered sequence of questions (e.g., does noncompliance impose real costs on others, does the convention merely signal respect, does it coordinate behavior where unilateral deviation is dangerous or unfair, is the convention itself morally objectionable?) that sorts any local norm into a few action categories like follow-always, follow-when-observed, optional, and legitimate-to-refuse. Apply the procedure to at least three worked cases — tipping in the U.S., jaywalking in Germany, and a gendered dress code at a religious site — including one where the procedure yields a verdict students won't like. Mark explicitly which steps are irreducible judgment calls and what the procedure cannot settle (e.g., what to do when a convention strikes the traveler as unjust). The audience is undergraduates with no philosophy background, so every step has to be usable in under a minute. Answer in American English, lead with your bottom-line rule in the first sentence, then give the procedure and reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
