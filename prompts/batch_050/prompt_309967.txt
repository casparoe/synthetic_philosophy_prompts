I run an undergraduate seminar on retrospective moral judgment, and for the first session I want to hand students a decision tree that tells them which position on judging historical figures they actually hold, sorted by their verdicts on cases rather than by which label sounds respectable. Please construct that tree.

Work from two fictionalized figures so that no one needs to adjudicate real history. The first is the Founder: an eighteenth-century statesman who wrote eloquently about universal liberty, owned dozens of enslaved people, sold some to cover debts, was personally familiar with abolitionist arguments, and acknowledged in his letters that the practice could not be squared with his principles. The second is the Strategist: a twentieth-century wartime leader whose decisions plausibly worsened a famine killing millions, who made disparaging remarks about the affected population, but whose military leadership arguably saved his country.

Requirements. The tree should have between eight and twelve diagnostic questions and terminate in six to eight named leaves. Each question must be a concrete case or case-variation a first-year student can answer without any philosophical vocabulary: for instance, relocating the Founder two centuries earlier, before any organized abolitionism existed; a conqueror whose massacres were celebrated by every contemporary voice we know of; a figure who outperformed nearly all peers yet falls far short of us; a figure honored for achievements funded by the proceeds of the wrongdoing. At minimum, the following positions must appear as leaves: strong historicism (cross-era moral blame is a category mistake); timeless universalism; universalism with an epistemic excuse (wrong then and now, but blamelessness where the agent could not have known better); the available-dissenters test (culpability tracks what was knowable in the agent's own milieu); a comparative better-than-one's-peers standard; a split-verdict view that separates judging the person from commemorating them; and a forward-looking view on which commemoration is about present signaling rather than desert.

Three further instructions. First, at every internal node state the question that splits it and why the positions below it come apart there. Second, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate by verdicts alone: I expect universalism-with-excuse and the available-dissenters test to converge on the Founder, so construct at least one case that drives them apart (the pre-abolitionism variant should do it), and diagnose any pair for which no such case exists. Third, identify unoccupied combinations in the logical space. In particular, many students will praise figures for being ahead of their time while excusing figures for being of their time; locate that asymmetry in the tree and say whether it is a defensible position or an unstable midpoint. Also note whether a person who thinks the Founder was blameless can coherently favor renaming the building anyway, and if so, which leaf they occupy.

Close with a short meta-note: is this one tree, or should verdicts about blameworthiness and verdicts about commemoration be sorted by two separate trees whose results are then combined?
