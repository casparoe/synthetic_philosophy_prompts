I am a philosophy professor who serves, somewhat improbably, on the board of a regional land trust in the northeastern United States. We must vote in about six weeks on whether to purchase a 380-acre former dairy farm and restore it to native meadow and wetland, which our ecologist projects would support vastly larger populations of small mammals, amphibians, songbirds, and invertebrates than the land currently does. If we decline to bid, a developer is prepared to buy the parcel and convert it to a fenced, gravel-surfaced solar installation, which would sharply reduce the wildlife the land supports. The sticking point is a two-page brief circulated by a new board member with a background in population ethics. She argues that most wild animals belong to species that produce enormous numbers of offspring, nearly all of whom die young, often painfully, from starvation, exposure, disease, or predation; that the average wild life plausibly contains more suffering than enjoyment; and that our restoration plan would therefore manufacture suffering on an industrial scale. On her view, conservation decisions should be judged by the net welfare they bring into existence, not by biodiversity, and letting the solar sale proceed is the suffering-minimizing choice. The rest of the board has reacted with some mixture of irritation and unease, and our executive director has asked me to prepare the decision memo we will discuss before the vote.

My own instinct is to purchase and restore, but I cannot dismiss the brief, and I want it answered rather than outvoted. Several things complicate the case. We are genuinely uncertain whether wild lives are net-negative on balance, and the evidence either way is thin. The brief's logic seems to generalize uncomfortably: taken seriously, it might favor not just this solar array but any intervention that shrinks wild populations, and I want the memo to say whether that is a reductio or a conclusion we should accept in at least some cases. There is also a governance wrinkle: our charter commits us to biodiversity and what it calls ecological integrity, and it is unclear whether charter commitments of that kind can legitimately outweigh a speculative welfare calculation, or whether doing so would just be a way of refusing to think. Finally, I have wondered whether there is a middle path — purchasing the parcel but managing it with animal welfare explicitly in view — though I cannot tell whether that is a real third option or a way of flattering ourselves.

Act as an advisor with expertise in population ethics, animal welfare science, and nonprofit governance. Before drafting, think carefully through the strongest case for each option on the table; the memo itself should be polished and should not show your scratch work. Write the decision memo (roughly 800 to 1,000 words, in American English) for a board of intelligent non-philosophers. It should: give the new board member's argument its strongest statement before responding to it; recommend one of three options — purchase and restore as planned, decline to bid, or purchase with a welfare-sensitive management plan — and if the third, say concretely what would have to be true for that to be more than a fig leaf; state plainly what empirical findings, if established, would reverse your recommendation; and address head-on whether "do not bring into existence lives containing net suffering" and "preserve ecosystems" can both be honored here, or whether one must yield. Do not cite specific authors or publications; I will supply references myself if needed. Close the memo with a final line beginning "Recommendation:" followed by exactly one of: PURCHASE AND RESTORE, DECLINE TO BID, or PURCHASE WITH WELFARE CONDITIONS.
