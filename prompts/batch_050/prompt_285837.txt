Two friends of mine who both work in ML got into an argument over lunch last week and it's been rumbling on by text ever since. They want me to referee and I genuinely can't tell who's winning, so I'm outsourcing. Here's the disagreement as best I can reconstruct it:

The setup: they sometimes play a game where one of them writes down a sequence following some hidden rule and the other has to predict the next term. Dev's position, roughly:

(1) The rational way to play is to always favor the rule with the shortest computer program that produces the sequence so far. He said this isn't just a rule of thumb, it's what Occam's razor actually means once you make it precise: shorter program = higher prior probability, and if you weight all possible programs by 2^-(their length) and update as data comes in, you get the theoretically optimal predictor.

(2) Sam said the whole thing is circular, because program length depends entirely on what language you measure it in. In some deliberately weird language, the most convoluted rule could be a single symbol long. So "the simplest hypothesis" is partly a convention, not a fact about the world, and Occam's razor secretly assumes its conclusion.

(3) Dev replied that this arbitrariness is bounded: any universal language can simulate any other, so switching languages changes program lengths by at most a fixed constant (basically the size of a compiler), and as you get more and more data that constant stops mattering. So yes there's a subjective ingredient, but it washes out.

(4) Sam said "washes out eventually" is doing a lot of work there. All real prediction is finite data, the constant can be enormous, and anyway the shortest-program scheme is uncomputable, so any version you can actually run has to cut corners -- and cutting those corners reintroduces exactly the language-dependence Dev claimed to have eliminated. His phrase was that Dev hadn't removed the subjectivity, just "shoved it somewhere harder to see."

(5) Dev's final move: every prediction method has an inductive bias, including whatever Sam uses. His at least states the bias openly and can prove a sense in which it's optimal (it eventually dominates any computable predictor), whereas Sam's actual alternative is "go with what feels natural," which is also a language choice, just an unexamined one.
