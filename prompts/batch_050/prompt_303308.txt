I coordinate the final exam for our third-year Philosophy of Biology module, and this year we covered biological individuality — the standard menu of physiological, immunological, and evolutionary criteria, plus the usual cases (biofilms, the Portuguese man o' war, eusocial insect colonies, lichens, host–microbiome systems). Last year's question asked students straight out whether a holobiont is an organism, and I got fifty near-identical essays rehearsing the hologenome debate. This year I want a question built around a case they cannot have prepared for, one that forces them to run several criteria of organismality on the same system, watch the criteria deliver different verdicts, and then say something principled about what to do when that happens — rather than just announcing their favorite criterion.

Here is the setup I want the question built around. It is entirely fictional, so students must reason from the stipulated details rather than from remembered facts. The Kessler mat is a deep-sea hydrothermal vent formation: thousands of sessile tubeworms embedded in a shared mineral skeleton that the mat itself deposits and that persists across many worm generations, channeling vent fluid through the colony in a way no isolated worm could manage. Each worm houses sulfur-oxidizing bacteria in a dedicated organ; the bacteria are transmitted vertically through the worm's eggs and cannot survive free-living. The worm's immune system tolerates its own bacterial strain and attacks foreign strains. Worms from outside the mat occasionally settle and join, bringing their own bacterial lineages, so the mat's membership turns over continuously and there is no mat-level reproductive bottleneck. The question should ask students to assess three candidate individuals — the worm alone, the worm-plus-bacteria holobiont, and the mat as a whole — against a metabolic-integration criterion, an immunological criterion, and an evolutionary criterion (fitness alignment plus bottlenecked reproduction), and then to answer one applied question: under a fictional conservation statute that protects individual endemic organisms of a vent field, which unit gets counted, and does the evolutionary criterion have any authority to settle that question, or is the choice of unit here irreducibly pragmatic?

Please write: (1) the exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, for a 75-minute essay worth 25 marks; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words written at the level of a strong student; (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 5 marks each, where for each point you name the specific move an answer must make to earn full credit, the typical wrong move that caps it at partial credit (one line each — e.g., treating the holobiont's vertical transmission as relevant to the mat's status, conflating being an organism with being a unit of selection, or answering the conservation question by bare intuition), and a closing note that states exactly what separates an A answer from a B answer. The model answer should land somewhere definite on the pluralism question — whether the criteria's divergence shows that organismality is context-relative or merely that one criterion outranks the others for certain purposes — and the marking scheme should penalize answers that gesture at pluralism without taking that stand. Do not require or reward citations to particular authors; the question should be answerable from the stipulated case plus the criteria as taught in any standard course.
