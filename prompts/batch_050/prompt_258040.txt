I teach an undergraduate aesthetics seminar, and for our week on photography I want to give students a decision-tree handout that sorts them by their actual intuitions about photographs rather than by labels they've half-remembered. The starting point for the tree should be the everyday conviction many of them walk in with: that looking at an old photograph of a deceased grandparent puts them in some kind of perceptual contact with her that a painting of her never could. The tree should lead from there to a named position on the transparency debate — roughly, whether we literally see through photographs to the things they depict, in the way we arguably see through mirrors, telescopes, and live video feeds, or whether we only ever see the picture itself.

Please build the tree from the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of names. The branch nodes should be the distinctions that actually do the work in this literature — for instance: what ordinary seeing requires of the causal chain between perceiver and object; whether a perceptual experience must let you locate the object relative to yourself (where it is, how far, in which direction) for it to count as seeing that object; whether counterfactual dependence of the image on the scene is sufficient for perceptual contact regardless of mediation; whether a temporal gap between event and viewing breaks anything that a purely spatial gap would not; and whether cases of deliberate manipulation or fabrication (an airbrushed portrait, a fabricated image of an event that never occurred) change what is seen in the non-manipulated case. You need not use exactly these, but the tree should be built from questions at this level, and six to nine branch nodes is about right.

Hard constraints on the diagnostic questions: each one must be posed as a question about a concrete case — a photograph of a long-dead relative, a mirror, a live security-camera feed, a recording of that feed watched a year later, a telescope image of a star that has since gone supernova, an ultrasound, a photocopy of a photocopy, a convincingly doctored image — phrased so a student can answer it without knowing which philosophical position their answer commits them to. No question may contain the words transparency, depiction, representation, or any philosopher's name.

The leaves must include, at minimum: a full transparency view of the kind associated with Kendall Walton, on which we literally see deceased people and past events in photographs; a restricted view on which mirrors, telescopes, and live feeds are transparent but recordings and photographs are not, because they cannot supply the perceiver with the object's present location relative to them; a view on which no mechanically produced image is ever transparent and we see only pictures, never the depicted things; and a deflationary leaf on which transparency talk is merely a loose way of registering photographs' superior evidential reliability, with no literal seeing anywhere in the vicinity. You may add further leaves if the space supports them. For any combination of branch answers that lands on an unoccupied leaf, mark it as unoccupied and give a one- or two-sentence diagnosis: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Where two named positions in the literature cannot be separated by any question on your tree, flag that explicitly instead of papering over it.

Deliver the handout in two parts. First, the tree itself as a nested outline, with each branch node numbered and phrased as its diagnostic question, and each leaf stating its position in one or two plain sentences. Second, a table with exactly these columns: Node ID | Diagnostic case | Question asked about the case | What answering "yes" commits you to | What answering "no" commits you to; followed by a leaf table with columns: Leaf | Position name (or "unoccupied") | Closest associated philosopher, if any | Most awkward commitment. If any leaf is unoccupied, its diagnosis goes in the final column of the leaf table.
