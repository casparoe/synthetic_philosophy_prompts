I'm in my third year of a philosophy PhD working on social ontology, and I've been circling a dissertation on whether "generations" -- Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, all that -- are real social kinds or just marketing categories that got reified into existence by journalists and HR consultants. I have roughly two and a half years of funding left and a committee that's supportive but skeptical the topic is "real philosophy," so I need to spend the time on questions that actually matter rather than ones that are just fun.

So: what are the three or four genuinely open questions in this area, and in what order should someone like me attack them? I'm much less interested in a survey of the debate than in the prioritization itself and how you defend it.

Some candidates I've been chewing on -- feel free to reject any of these and substitute better ones:

- Whether generation-talk does any real causal or explanatory work, or whether it's just a noisy proxy that collapses age effects, period effects, and cohort effects that demographers already know how to pull apart. If the demographers are right that you can almost never cleanly separate those three, does the metaphysics even get off the ground?
- Whether a kind can be real even with arbitrary boundaries. The cutoffs between Boomers and Gen X are obviously somewhat conventional, but so are plenty of boundaries we tolerate in biology and geography. Does conventionality at the edges kill the kind, or only if the boundaries track nothing at all?
- The looping-effect angle: does a cohort become *more* of a real kind because people believe in it and organize around it (identify as Millennials, get marketed to as Millennials, vote as a bloc because pollsters tell them they vote as a bloc)? Or does that kind of self-constitution make it a worse candidate for a natural-ish kind and more like a fiction we keep funding?
- Whether the concept is doing active harm in public discourse -- moral panics about Gen Z, lazy workplace essentialism, intergenerational blame -- and whether that's a reason to work on the metaphysics at all, or a separate applied-ethics question I shouldn't let drive the agenda.

For each question you rank, tell me: why it's upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone trained in philosophy of science but with basically no quantitative methods background, and what discovery would make you abandon it midway (e.g. "if it turns out X, drop this immediately").

And the thing I care most about: tell me what would change your mind about the ordering itself. Which argument or piece of evidence, if I brought it back to you, would lead you to rank these differently, and where do you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fall apart?
