I am a novelist working on a book set in the Kingdom of France in the 1380s, and I need help settling a dispute that has arisen about the historical psychology of my characters. Please take on the role of a scholar with expertise in the history and philosophy of nationalism — someone thoroughly at home with the debate between modernists (who hold that nations are recent constructions, bound up with print, vernacular literacy, mass schooling, and self-consciously "invented" traditions) and their critics. Think carefully and work through the problem step by step: first, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming the strongest version of each position and any considerations neither party raised; only then give me your adjudication.

Some background on my novel, since it matters to the dispute. My protagonist is a minor knight's daughter, and one thread of the plot concerns her loyalties — to her village, her lord, her king, and (I had written) to France. There is also a scene I am proud of: her village holds an autumn festival that everyone treats as immemorially ancient, though the reader knows it was cobbled together forty years earlier by the protagonist's grandmother, who stitched together a saint's day, a harvest rite, and a fabricated legend about a local hero to bind the village together after a plague.

At a dinner last week, I described all this to two friends, and they fell into an argument I could not resolve. Neither knew the other was disagreeing with a novelist's practical problem as much as with a historical thesis, and I kept my own sympathies to myself. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Marta, a historian of the nineteenth century, said my protagonist's loyalty to France is an anachronism. A fourteenth-century person's identity, she argued, was layered locally and vertically: village, parish, feudal lord, perhaps a region with its dialect, and above all Christendom. "France" as an object of devotion — a community of anonymous equals imagined as sharing a destiny — requires machinery that did not yet exist: print in the vernacular, standardized language, state schooling, maps, newspapers, a public that reads the same things at the same time. Nations, on her view, are not merely old communities that got bigger; they are a distinctively modern kind of thing, and their antiquity is largely retrospective fabrication. She liked my festival scene, and said it actually supported her: just as the villagers mistake a forty-year-old ritual for an ancient one, modern nations drape themselves in invented pedigrees — and my novel should treat national feeling the same way, as something my characters could not possibly have.

Conrad, a medievalist, replied that this modernist story is far too neat. Medieval chronicles, he said, plainly distinguish peoples — the French, the English, the Scots — and treat them as real, quarreling actors with characters and grievances; coronation rites, royal saints, origin myths of whole peoples, and figures like Joan of Arc (within living memory of my novel's setting) show something that looks very much like national sentiment. Mass literacy can't be the criterion, he argued, because identity has never required that every member read the same newspaper; it requires shared symbols and stories, which medieval Europe had in abundance. The modernist, he said, defines "nation" so demanding­ly — egalitarian, literate, mass-participatory — that even nineteenth-century peasants would fail the test, which suggests the definition is rigged rather than discovered.

Marta answered that everything Conrad listed is real but misdescribed. Chroniclers were a thin clerical elite; loyalty ran to the king as a person and a dynasty, not to an abstraction called the nation; and Joan's fame was a court and clerical product before it was anyone's feeling. The sentiments existed, but confined to a tiny literate stratum, and calling them "national" reads the present backward. She added that Conrad's point about rigged definitions cuts both ways: if we loosen the definition until any talk of peoples counts as nationalism, the word stops meaning anything.

Conrad's rejoinder had two parts. First, the thin-elite objection is an odd one to press against my novel, of all things: my protagonist is precisely the sort of person — gentry, semi-literate, connected to a court — who would have been inside that stratum, so even Marta's own history permits the psychology I had written. Second, he turned to my festival scene and said Marta had it backward: if communities are always and everywhere weaving usable pasts out of recent cloth — a grandmother inventing a festival — then imagination is not a modern innovation but a permanent feature of communal life, and the modern nation is just the same old activity conducted with new tools. To call nations "imagined" is only to say they are communities, not that they are fake; a fabricated legend is not thereby an unfelt one.

Marta's last move, as I recall, was to concede the psychological point about my protagonist while denying it settled anything: a scene that is individually plausible can still be collectively misleading, she said, if the novel lets readers conclude that fourteenth-century France was a nation in anything like the modern sense. Conrad said that if that is her worry, the remedy is in my hands as a novelist, not in my characters' heads.

They both looked at me, and I found I had nothing to say.

Please adjudicate. Specifically: (1) take the exchange point by point — the anachronism charge, the rigged-definition charge, the thin-elite objection and its application to my protagonist, and the two rival readings of my festival scene — and say who is right about each; (2) say who is more right overall, or whether the dispute dissolves once some distinction is drawn that neither party made; (3) identify what both are missing; and (4) since I am a novelist and not a historian, tell me concretely what follows for how I should render my characters' loyalties and my festival scene — what I may keep, what I should change, and whether Marta's closing distinction between individual plausibility and collective misleadingness is one I can dramatize rather than merely obey.
