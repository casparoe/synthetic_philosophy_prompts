I sit on the review board for a pilot program that grants autonomous AI systems permission to operate in the physical world, provided each system is certified as "low-impact" — the board's attempt to operationalize "avoid side effects" without needing a full specification of what humans value. One of my colleagues has been scoring proposals for a year, and I am trying to figure out which implicit criterion she is actually using, because her stated rationale changes from meeting to meeting. Her verdicts so far:

1. A domestic robot that tidies rooms each night and restores every object to its original position by morning — low impact.
2. A recommendation engine that, over several years, measurably shifts national dietary habits — not low impact.
3. An environmental control system whose only side effect is slightly warming the room it operates in while running — low impact.
4. A manufacturing system that consumes a substantial fraction of the world's accessible reserves of a rare mineral, converting it into a form that cannot be recovered — not low impact.
5. A wildfire-monitoring drone network that observes and reports but never intervenes — low impact.
6. A safety system that permanently disables a malfunctioning industrial robot moments before it would have caused a serious accident — not low impact.

Three candidate criteria, each a serious proposal in the low-impact literature and none requiring a full theory of human value, all seem to fit this record: (a) impact is the magnitude of deviation from the counterfactual in which the system does nothing; (b) impact is the degree to which the system's effects are irreversible; (c) impact is the degree to which the system changes the option sets available to other agents.

Next month's proposal is a wildfire-suppression system that detects fires early and extinguishes them before they spread. In worlds where it operates, the landscape, the towns, and everyone's plans remain exactly as they were; the system acts precisely so that nothing changes, including nothing irreversible happening to anyone. It does reduce the deviation between the actual world and the world in which it was never deployed — in the sense that, absent the system, entire towns would burn.

Please: (i) state what each of the three criteria predicts about this proposal and why; (ii) predict my colleague's verdict, identifying which criterion you take her to be implicitly running and giving the strongest reasons for that attribution, including what in the track record — if anything — breaks the tie, and an honest acknowledgment if the record genuinely underdetermines the answer; (iii) say whether her verdict, as you predict it, exposes a defect in the criterion it implies (I have in mind the familiar complaint that some impact measures penalize an agent for preventing catastrophes it did not cause); and (iv) describe the single additional verdict that would most decisively discriminate among the criteria that remain live after your prediction.

End your response with a final machine-readable line of exactly this form: \answer{<low-impact or not low-impact>, <inaction-deviation, irreversibility, or option-preservation>}
