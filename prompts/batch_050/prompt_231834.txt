So last night I ended up in a massive argument with a friend from my history MA that went until like 2am and honestly I don't think either of us won. We'd been talking about presentism -- whether historians can or should judge people in the past by the moral standards of today, whether there's any escaping the assumptions of your own era, that whole debate. My friend is firmly on the side that you can't escape your own time, full stop, and at some point he dropped "history is written by the victors" as his knockout move, and used it to say that presentist judgement is just what every generation of historians does anyway, so there's nothing especially wrong or avoidable about it. That felt like a bait and switch to me, but I couldn't pin down exactly why in the moment, and it's been bugging me all day.

What I want is for you to take that slogan apart. What are the distinct precise claims "history is written by the victors" could actually be making? I can think of a few candidates myself -- a literal claim about who physically produces and preserves records, a sociological claim about whose framing ends up dominating textbooks and public memory, an epistemic claim that we should treat all received history as untrustworthy, maybe a cynical normative claim that every era rewrites the past to flatter itself -- but I suspect there are more, and I want them laid out as a proper menu. For each reading, tell me whether it's basically a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, or whether it's just false. One thing that came up in the argument: I pointed out that plenty of history is written by the losers -- I used the Lost Cause mythology of the American South as my example, since defeated Confederates and their sympathisers dominated how that war got remembered for decades -- and my friend pivoted to saying the slogan is really about power rather than victory. That pivot felt like it conceded the slogan was wrong as stated, but tell me if I'm being unfair.

Then the bit I actually care about: which of these readings, if any, would actually support what he was trying to do with it, namely defending presentist judgement as inevitable and therefore blameless? My hunch is that the strong version he needs is the least defensible one, and the defensible versions don't get him anywhere near his conclusion, but I want to know if I'm wrong.
