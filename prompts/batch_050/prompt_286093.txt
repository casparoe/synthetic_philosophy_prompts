So a coworker and I were arguing about whether our team should ship an LLM feature that answers customer questions about our docs, and at some point he goes "look, it's not lying when it gets things wrong, it's bullshitting" like that settles it. Except I genuinely can't tell if that's supposed to be reassuring or damning. I've seen this line everywhere -- "LLMs don't lie, they bullshit" -- sometimes used to say hallucinations are harmless glitches, sometimes to say LLMs are somehow worse than a liar would be (people gesture at Frankfurt's thing about bullshit being more corrosive to truth than lying). Half the time I think people just mean "it's often wrong and sounds confident," which is way less interesting.

Can you lay out the different precise claims this slogan could be making? I want a menu, not just one reading. Some things that seem like they'd have to be pinned down: what exactly "bullshit" means in each reading (indifference to whether what you say is true vs. just being unreliable vs. confidently confabulating vs. making assertions with no communicative intention at all), what "not lying" requires (does lying need an intent to deceive, or a belief that what you said is false, and is that even the right test), and the elephant in the room -- whether any of these categories can even apply to something that arguably has no beliefs or intentions, or whether the slogan only works if you grant the LLM some kind of speaker-ish mental states, or whether it works better if you deny them. For each reading: is it true of LLMs, is it trivial, and does it actually support the conclusion the slogan gets used for in either direction (ship it, it's just error / don't ship it, bullshit is worse than lying). I'd also take a verdict on whether "hallucination" is itself a sneaky term doing work here. My suspicion is the slogan survives scrutiny on like one reading and that reading isn't the one my coworker needed, but convince me otherwise.
