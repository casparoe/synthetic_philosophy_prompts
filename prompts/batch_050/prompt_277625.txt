In my seminar on the ethics and governance of advanced AI, I have been defending the following position, and I would like you to stress-test it rather than summarize it.

My position is that the "competitiveness constraint" — the claim that safety-conscious AI developers will be outcompeted by less cautious rivals, so that safety measures must either be nearly free or else be imposed on everyone simultaneously — has been systematically overstated, and that the so-called "alignment tax" is for the most part not a tax at all. My argument has three parts. First, most of what gets called alignment work is dual-use: interpretability, robustness, and capability evaluation tend to improve the product, so the true tax is confined to a narrow class of interventions (deliberate capability throttling, deployment delays, refusal behaviors) rather than spread across the whole safety agenda. Second, the constraint is usually modeled as if developers compete on a single undifferentiated dimension, when in fact safety, reliability, and predictability are themselves things customers — especially enterprise and government customers — pay for, so that a cautious developer can occupy a market niche rather than simply lose the race. Third, the constraint's force depends on the assumption that the dangerous capabilities arrive before the dangerous failures do; if clumsy, untrustworthy systems start failing publicly at scale while still short of transformative capability, the market and regulators will punish recklessness faster than they reward it, and the constraint inverts.

Here is what I want from you. First, tell me what would count against this position — which empirical observations, historical precedents, or theoretical results, if established, would force me to abandon or substantially weaken each of the three parts, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Please go beyond the obvious replies (for instance, that dual-use safety work is precisely why the tax is invisible: it is paid as foregone capability, not as extra cost) and tell me which of those obvious replies actually survive scrutiny. Second, distinguish the defeaters my opponents would cite from the ones I, holding this position, should myself concede as genuine risks to it — including any evidence that has arguably already accumulated and that I may be tempted to explain away. Third, tell me plainly whether the position, as I have hedged it with qualifiers like "for the most part" and "narrow class of interventions," has been insulated against falsification — and if so, give me the most defensible reformulation that still risks something. Finally, take a stand: state which single piece of evidence or argument you judge most damaging to the position as stated, commit to a verdict on whether it should be abandoned, weakened, or retained as is, and defend that verdict rather than presenting me with a menu of options.
