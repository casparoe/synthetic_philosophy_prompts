I'm writing a term paper for a course on economic methodology, and I've gotten stuck on the literature around path dependence, which seems to me to have been going in circles for decades. On one side stands the claim that market economies can become locked into inferior technologies and arrangements — the standard keyboard layout, the videotape format wars, the internal combustion engine's victory over early alternatives — so that history is littered with small, contingent events whose consequences never wash out. On the other side, critics reply that the canonical examples collapse under scrutiny, that network benefits and switching costs are genuine benefits rather than market failures, and that calling a standard "inefficient" when everyone keeps voluntarily using it is close to a contradiction in terms. Each side has answered the other's case studies point by point; newer candidates, like charging standards or dominant social-media platforms, get drafted into the same stalemate without changing it. Nobody appears to have moved in thirty years.

My own hypothesis is that the dispute isn't really empirical at all, but methodological in a way neither side admits. To demonstrate that an inferior technology survived, you would have to show that a superior alternative was available, known, and rejected for reasons that don't themselves count as legitimate costs — and that counterfactual strikes me as nearly impossible to establish in any real historical case, since the defeated alternative's performance at scale is precisely what we never get to observe. If that's right, the two sides aren't disagreeing about what happened; they're disagreeing about what would even count as evidence of lock-in, and each side's standard of proof conveniently favors its own conclusion. My professor, for what it's worth, thinks the disagreement is conceptual instead: that it turns on what "inefficiency" can coherently mean when the costs of switching are themselves real costs borne by real people, so that the allegedly superior path is only superior in a world where transition is free.

Diagnose this debate for me. What kind of disagreement is it, actually — empirical, methodological, conceptual, or some mixture with one element doing the real work? Is "path dependence" a single claim or a ladder of claims of increasing strength (history matters; inferior outcomes can persist; markets cannot self-correct them), with the two sides arguing about different rungs under one label? Given your diagnosis, say what progress would concretely look like: a formal result about when lock-in is possible, a different kind of historical evidence, a stipulative distinction that would let the sides separate, or an honest admission that the strong version of the thesis is unfalsifiable. I want engagement with the structure of the disagreement rather than another survey of case studies, and a candid verdict on whether this debate can be settled or only dissolved.
