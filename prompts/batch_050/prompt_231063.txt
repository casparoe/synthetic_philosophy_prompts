I'm in the second year of a BFA program, and my critique seminar professor has a catchphrase he deploys whenever he wants to end a discussion: art is infection. He got it, he tells us, from Tolstoy's What Is Art?, which he assigns every fall, and as far as I can reconstruct from my notes, Tolstoy's idea is roughly that art is the activity of transmitting feelings one has lived through to other people by external means, so that they come to feel the same thing, and that the merit of a work tracks the strength of that transmission, which depends on things like the individuality of the feeling, the clarity of its expression, and above all the artist's sincerity. Last week he used the slogan to dismiss a piece of mine: a generative sound installation built from anonymized hospital waiting-room audio, which I deliberately designed to be cold and affectless. He said it can't be art because nothing passes from me to the listener. Several classmates pushed back, and the conversation went nowhere because everyone seemed to mean something different by the slogan.

What I want is a disambiguation I can bring to our next critique. Lay out the distinct precise theses that "art is infection" could be expressing, and for each one say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false, and whether it would actually support my professor's verdict on my installation. Here are the readings I've been turning over, though I suspect I'm missing some: a definition of art (any activity that transmits emotion counts as art, and nothing else does); a mere criterion of success (works are better the more strongly they infect, whatever art is); a causal claim about how art works on audiences (it produces the same feeling in the audience that the artist had); a sincerity condition (the artist must genuinely have felt the emotion for the work to count or to be good); a norm of artistic honesty (deliberately manufacturing an emotion you don't feel is fraudulent); and a claim about what makes art valuable at all (union or communion between people, so that a work that shares nothing has failed at art's point). Some of these collapse into each other and some pull apart, and I'd like that untangled.

A few wrinkles I'd like the answer to engage rather than assume away. First, on the causal reading: does it matter whether the audience feels the same emotion or just some emotion, and does the Tolstoyan need the stronger claim? Second, my installation arguably does transmit something -- unease, numbness -- but not anything I felt while building it; which readings does that escape and which does it fail? Third, the sincerity condition seems to threaten huge swaths of admired art (actors, commissioned music, fiction written on assignment), so if a reading has that consequence, I want to know whether the consequence is a reason to reject the reading or a bullet my professor should bite. Finally, I'd like a verdict on which reading people who repeat the slogan most likely have in mind, and whether that reading is one of the defensible ones. Please answer in American English, and feel free to go somewhat long -- this is effectively my ammunition for the rest of the semester.
