So my lab's journal club (we're mostly molecular bio people, plus two stray philosophers who keep inviting themselves) has now spent three sessions on the etiological vs. causal-role fight over biological function, and I honestly can't tell anymore whether we're disagreeing about anything real. Standard setup: the selected-effects camp (Wright, Millikan, Neander, that whole lineage) says a trait's function is whatever it did that got it selected, which is why a diseased heart that can't pump still has pumping as its function, and why the heart's whooshing noise is a mere side-effect. The Cummins camp says function is just causal contribution to some capacity of the containing system, no history required, which handles novel traits and lab constructs nicely but seems to make functions absurdly cheap -- the heart's functions now include making noise, and presumably half of what any organ does counts.

We keep cycling through the same three moves. (1) Swampman-style duplicates: a molecule-for-molecule heart that crystallizes out of a random lightning strike has no functions, says the etiological side, and the causal-role side says that's exactly why the historical theory is nuts. (2) Malfunction: a broken heart still has a function it fails to perform, which the causal-role people allegedly can't say without smuggling in the very normativity they claim to avoid. (3) Brand-new traits: a CRISPR edit that's never been through selection, or a drift-fixated trait that happens to help. The etiological side has to say "no function yet" (or retreat to some Godfrey-Smith-ish recent-history weakening), which my bio colleagues find insane because they will cheerfully write grants about the function of a gene they edited last year.

My working hypothesis: this was never one disagreement. The selected-effects people are answering "why is this trait here?" and the causal-role people are answering "what is this part doing in this system?", both are questions biologists genuinely ask, and the whole multi-decade fight is an artifact of both camps insisting on owning the single word "function." But maybe that's too quick -- maybe there's a real question underneath about what makes malfunction *possible*, and that question forces an actual choice.

So: diagnose the dispute. Verbal, substantive, or two-questions-under-one-label? Engage what each side actually says about the hard cases above, not textbook summaries of their slogans. Then tell me what would count as progress: a permanent terminological split where everyone just writes "etiological function" vs "systemic capacity" and the fight dies of boredom; empirical work on how working biologists actually deploy the term; or some principled argument that one of the two questions is the real one and the other should be answered in different vocabulary entirely. Also, does the organizational/self-maintenance camp (Mossio and friends) dissolve the fight or just add a third team to it? And if your honest answer is "no progress is coming because intuitions about malfunction are bedrock and neither side can argue for theirs," then argue *that* instead of pretending there's a fix.
