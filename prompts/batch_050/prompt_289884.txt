Next month I'm giving a colloquium talk in a philosophy department's philosophy of mathematics series, defending a paper in which I argue that Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations should be read not as a piece of mathematical pedagogy or charming history, but as the correct epistemology of mathematics: proofs are conjectural instruments that generate and refine their own concepts, mathematics grows by the dialectic of conjecture, proof, counterexample, and concept-stretching, and so mathematics is quasi-empirical in something like the way the natural sciences are. The further, more controversial move in my paper is a reversal of the standard complaint: where critics say Lakatos's account describes only messy informal proof and collapses once proofs are made fully rigorous, I argue that modern computer-checked formalization actually vindicates him — that the work of translating textbook proofs into a proof assistant, and the counterexamples, refactored definitions, and discovered hidden lemmas this process throws up, is precisely the Lakatosian dialectic operating at the level of formalization itself.

The audience will be mixed and not uniformly friendly. There will be at least one working set theorist of a broadly Platonist temperament who thinks talk of "growth" and "refutation" in mathematics confuses our fallible knowledge with the timeless subject matter; one formal-methods researcher who uses proof assistants daily and may either embrace my extension or — worse — tell me I've romanticized formalization, which in practice is mostly tedious engineering that confirms rather than refutes; a neo-Fregean philosopher who will press the distinction between the context of discovery and the context of justification; and a historian of mathematics in the Kuhnian tradition who may accuse me of flattening Lakatos into a philosophy-of-science template he was reacting against. What each of them cares about is different: certainty, practice, justification, and historical accuracy respectively.

Act as a philosopher of mathematics with deep expertise in Lakatos, in the discovery/justification distinction, and in how contemporary formal verification actually works, and think carefully before answering. Prepare me a full question-period briefing: the objections I should expect, ordered by how likely they are to be asked and how damaging they would be if I answer badly; each objection phrased in the voice of the person who would raise it, using their vocabulary rather than mine; for each, what I should concede immediately, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Identify the single reply that must land for the talk to survive, and tell me how to open the question period so that it comes up. Finally, tell me the objection I'm secretly hoping nobody raises — the one targeting the softest joint in my argument, which I suspect is the claim that formalization counterexamples are genuine refutations rather than mere debugging — and how to defend it honestly without pretending it is stronger than it is. Do not give me a reading list; I want a prep sheet, not a bibliography.
