So I'm in this political theory seminar and we just did the unit on sortition -- randomly selecting citizens for the legislature instead of electing them. Pretty much every reading and every classmate who defends it leans on the exact same move: we already trust random ordinary people with life-and-death decisions on juries, so if it's good enough to send someone to prison it's good enough to write tax law. Our prof is sympathetic to sortition but he kept poking at this and it got me thinking the analogy might be doing alot more work than it can handle.

Here's what I want. Treat the jury analogy as an actual argument and audit it. Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of voir dire and jury selection, the judge, the trial itself, the evidence, the verdict, the fact that it's temporary, all of it -- what does each piece map onto in a randomly selected legislature, if it maps at all? Then the disanalogies: jurors don't get to choose what question they're deciding, they don't write the law they apply, they're screened and instructed, they go home after one case and can never build a career out of it, and the stakes are one defendant rather than a whole country. Which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion "so sortition would work," and which are just superficial? I have a feeling the no-career-incentives part is where the analogy is strongest and the one-case-at-a-time part is where it collapses, but tell me if I'm wrong.

Then the verdict: does the analogy actually support replacing elections with lotteries, or does it only support something weaker, like citizens' assemblies on single issues? And if the jury comparison mostly fails, is there a better analogy sortition defenders should be using instead? Please answer in American English.
