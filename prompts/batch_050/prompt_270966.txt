I've been reading rationalist blogs and the EA-adjacent forums for years, and there's that whole genre of posts about "moral trade" -- gains from trade between people with different terminal values, compromise as Pareto improvement across value systems, that kind of thing. I always nodded along, but last week some friends and I got into a long argument (started over whether vote-trading is ethical, escalated from there) and I realized my gut verdicts about when compromising with a different value system is legitimate are a total mess. Here they are:

Case one: I care mostly about animal welfare, my friend cares mostly about global health, and we agree that I'll donate $500 to her favorite global health charity if she donates $500 to my favorite animal charity, instead of each of us just donating $500 to our own. Verdict: totally fine, this is the platonic ideal of moral trade. Case two: our department committee is split between two policies and someone proposes flipping a coin, and both sides agree beforehand to abide by it. Verdict: fine, even if the stakes are significant, because the procedure is fair. Case three: a judge who believes the death penalty is murder gets offered a deal -- if she personally sentences one particular defendant to death (whom she believes would be exonerated on appeal anyway, let's say), the legislature will abolish the death penalty forever, saving dozens of future lives by her own lights. Verdict: not fine, she shouldn't take it, even though by her own values the outcome is much better. Case four: a stranger tells me "unless you donate $1000 to the political cause you despise, I'll spend the weekend donating $2000 to an even worse one and also kick this dog." Verdict: this isn't a "trade," it's extortion, and complying would be at best damage control, not a legitimate compromise. Case five, the one I keep flip-flopping on: two legislators trade votes -- each votes for a bill they believe is moderately harmful in exchange for the other voting for a bill they believe prevents something much worse. By each of their own lights the expected outcome improves. Sometimes I think this is obviously just case one again, other times it feels like the judge case in a suit.

So: is there a principle that gets all or most of these? I've tried "any Pareto improvement across value systems is legitimate" (clean, but it blesses the judge case) and "compromise is fine when you only permit or forgo, not when you actively perform what you take to be wrong" (handles the judge, but the coin flip in case two commits you to actively implementing the bad policy if you lose, and I still think it's fine -- and the extortion case might not involve performing anything wrong at all, so this misses whatever is wrong there). Maybe the right answer has a separate clause for threats-vs-offers, maybe the distinction is between your values as preferences to be satisfied versus constraints on your own agency, maybe I'm just wrong about one of the cases. Give me a candidate principle (or two or three candidates) stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case, say which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to drop and whether I should actually drop it, and give me each candidate's ruling on case five plus one new case I didn't list (something like: compromising with a past generation's values embedded in an institution, or trading with an AI system whose "values" you don't take seriously).

At the end, give me a summary table with these columns: "Candidate principle" (one-line statement), "Verdicts it keeps", "Verdicts it overturns", "Ruling on the vote-trade case", "Biggest problem with it". Keep the table compact -- the reasoning should be in the prose, the table is just so I can compare the candidates at a glance.
