I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind drafting the framework chapter of my dissertation, and I want the specification written before I survey the literature. My topic is the paradox of phenomenal judgment: epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties exert no causal influence on the physical world, yet we constantly judge, report, and believe that we have phenomenal states — and if nothing about those states explains the judgments, their apparent reliability looks like a coincidence, which is awkward given that such judgments are the very evidence the consciousness debate runs on. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the relation between phenomenal states and our judgments about them must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or the prejudice of a particular camp. At minimum the list should address: explaining the apparent reliability of phenomenal judgment without appeal to coincidence; consistency with the causal closure of physics, or a principled rejection of it; accommodating genuine introspective error — change blindness, confabulation — without collapsing the distinction between having a phenomenal state and judging that one has it; explaining the apparent immediacy and non-inferential character of phenomenal judgment; and the self-application problem, since the datum that there is something it is like to be in pain is itself a phenomenal judgment the theory cannot afford to render untrustworthy. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly — that is the result I care about most. Then score four contenders briefly against the list — epiphenomenalism, interactionist dualism, type-identity physicalism, and illusionism — keeping the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring. Structure the answer in four sections, in this order: "The specification" (the numbered desiderata, each with its motivating case and negotiable status; roughly 700 words), "Joint satisfiability" (roughly 250 words), "Scorecard" (a short table plus at most 200 words of commentary), and "The most exclusionary requirement" (roughly 100 words).
