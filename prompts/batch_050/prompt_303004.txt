ok so here's something that keeps bugging me on the alignment forums. everyone in my corner of the internet talks about interpretability like it's neuroscience for models. like, we found a feature that lights up for the golden gate bridge, or for deception, the way neuroscientists find a neuron that fires for your grandmother. and people use this to argue for real conclusions: that models genuinely have internal representations (not just shallow pattern-matching), that we could eventually read off what a model "wants" before it acts, sometimes even that alignment is more tractable than the doomers think because minds made of matrices are inspectable in a way brains never will be.

but I've also seen skeptics say the analogy is doing all the work. a brain is a thing with causal structure that neuroscience discovers; a feature you got from a sparse autoencoder is a decomposition you chose, and maybe the model has no fact of the matter about which features are "really in there." like we found a way of describing activations that we like and then reified it.

so: audit the neuroscience analogy for me. give me the actual correspondence table -- what plays the role of the neuron, the lesion study, the fMRI voxel, the grandmother cell -- and then the disanalogies that matter (superposition and the fact that we have total access to the weights seem like the big ones, but tell me if I'm wrong). then the real question: does "models have representations" survive as a claim once you stop leaning on the brain comparison? is there some weaker or different sense in which a probe or a steering vector shows something is there, or is it all instrument-relative in a way that should make the optimists shut up? and if the neuroscience framing is bad, what's a better analogy for what interpretability researchers are actually doing?
