Ok so context: my aunt died about a year ago and the community theatre group I'm in decided to adapt part of her life into a short piece (she was a local activist, genuinely complicated person). This has gotten me low-key obsessed with the question of what we owe dead people when we dramatize them. Is it wrong to invent a scene that never happened if it "captures her spirit"? What if the invented stuff makes her look worse? Does a dead person even have a claim on us anymore, or is it really just about the living relatives?

Anyway, I now get to run a 90-minute workshop with the 8 people involved in the production, mixed ages, zero philosophy background, and I'd rather make them feel this tension than lecture at them. Act as a professional philosopher who's also an experienced workshop facilitator and design the activity for me.

What I'm picturing: something where they get a packet of "true facts" about a made-up deceased person and have to compress it into a short dramatic piece under real constraints (limited scenes, needs a climax, has to land emotionally), and then afterwards something forces them to confront what they did to the person on the page — like an audit of what they invented or cut, or someone arriving in the role of the person's kid. The point is they run into the problem themselves, so no mini-lecture up front and dont name any theory or framework before the activity. The debrief should then map specific moments of what happened onto the real questions (can the dead be wronged, who are we answerable to, does dramatic truth excuse factual distortion?).

Please think carefully about this: brainstorm 2-3 possible activity structures first, briefly say why you're picking the winner, then work that one out fully — materials I need, a timeline with rough minutes, the exact instructions I'd hand out, the twist moment, and the debrief questions in order. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (people getting paralyzed and writing nothing, someone refusing to give the dead person any flaws, etc.) and how to handle it, plus be honest about one thing the activity fundamentally can't reproduce — e.g. my aunt's actual son will be at the premiere, and that stakes arent pretend.
