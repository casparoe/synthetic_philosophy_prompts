I manage a small customer support team at a software company, and at a planning meeting last week I suggested we start tracking how quickly agents close tickets, since I suspected a few people were letting hard cases sit. A colleague shut the idea down by saying that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure, and everyone nodded as if that settled it. I've since heard the same line repeated in a podcast about school testing and in a comment thread about hospital wait times, always as a conversation-ender. I have no academic training in statistics or management theory, but the more I think about it, the less I can tell what the sentence is actually claiming. It seems like it could mean something almost trivial, like that if people know they're being measured on something, some of them will cheat or game it, so the numbers stop reflecting reality. Or it could mean something subtler: that the measure was only ever a stand-in for what we cared about, and the moment you optimize the stand-in directly, the connection between the two breaks, even if nobody is being dishonest. Or it could be a much stronger claim, that any quantitative target inevitably corrupts the practice it touches, which would seem to imply that hospitals shouldn't track wait times and schools shouldn't test at all -- a conclusion my colleague would probably not accept if stated plainly.
