I cover physics and adjacent stuff for a general-interest science magazine, and I keep running into the same one-liner: "more is different". A condensed matter guy used it on me last month when I asked whether his lab's results on a strange metal tell us anything about the underlying particle physics -- he basically waved the question away, like the macroscopic behavior floats free of whatever the electrons are doing. Then a week later a high-energy theorist used the same five words to argue the opposite-ish thing, that you don't need to know the deep theory to get solid answers at everyday scales, which was somehow supposed to justify not caring about her field. Same slogan, doing two different jobs, and I've also seen it deployed online to mean something much stronger, like "reductionism is just false" or even "emergent properties are spooky and irreducible in principle".

So: what are the actually distinct claims hiding inside "more is different"? I want them pulled apart properly -- e.g. is it just saying that new concepts and laws become useful at bigger scales (which sounds like a platitude nobody would deny), or that the lower-level theory is in practice unable to deliver the higher-level answers even if it contains them in principle, or that the higher-level facts genuinely don't depend on the lower-level ones, or something else entirely I haven't thought of. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivially true, a substantive but defensible claim, or actually false, and flag where the line between "you can't compute it from the bottom up" and "it isn't determined by the bottom up" matters, because I suspect a lot of equivocation lives exactly there.

Most importantly: which of those readings would the slogan have to mean for my condensed matter source's brush-off to be legitimate, and is that reading one he could actually defend? And separately, does any defensible reading license the stronger "so reductionism is dead" take, or is that always an overreach? Lay it out as a menu of readings with verdicts rather than picking one and explaining it to death.

End with a single last line starting "Bottom line:" that says in one sentence what the slogan is worth, journalistically speaking.
