I've been turning over a pattern in my own reasoning that I can't quite classify, and I'm hoping there's an established way of thinking about it — or at least a name — because it keeps showing up in everyday decisions and I can't tell whether it's a genuine insight or a systematic mistake. Here's the shape of it. Whenever I'm deciding something whose direct effect is negligible, I catch myself reasoning that my choice isn't really just mine. Voting is the cleanest case: my one ballot will almost certainly never change an outcome, and yet it feels wrong to treat the decision as irrelevant, because my reasoning isn't unique to me. Whatever line of thought led me to skip the polls is presumably being run, right now, by a great many other people who resemble me psychologically and who will follow it to the same conclusion. So when I decide, it feels less like I'm choosing one ballot and more like I'm somehow choosing on behalf of an entire invisible cohort of near-duplicates of myself. The same thing happens with recycling a single plastic bottle, with skipping meat at a restaurant, with leaving one negative review of a predatory business — in each case my act alone does approximately nothing, but I seem to have access to the fact that "people like me" will mostly do what I end up doing, and the aggregate of that group does matter.

A few clarifications about what the pattern is and isn't. It's not a moral rule along the lines of "act only in ways you could will everyone to act" — I'm not asking what would happen if everyone did it as a test of permissibility, I'm making a predictive claim: that the number of people who actually will do it is correlated with what I do, because their decision-making resembles mine. And it's not a pact with those other people; I don't know them, can't coordinate with them, and they'll never learn what I did, so nothing here depends on reputation or reciprocity. It's closer to the thought that my own deliberation is a kind of diagnostic sample: I can't observe the millions of similar minds directly, but I can observe my own choice, and that choice is evidence about theirs — and by making my choice one way, I seem to be making the evidence come out one way, which feels oddly like influencing the total.

Does this pattern of reasoning have a recognized name, and is there a literature on whether it's legitimate? I'm suspicious it might just be a subtle double-counting error — perhaps my cohort's behavior is fixed independently of what I do, and my decision is evidence about it without being connected to it in any way that should move me. But then it seems my decision theory would have to say voting is pointless for everyone like me, and everyone like me would stop voting, which is itself an outcome I can apparently foresee and want to avoid. I'd like to know: what is the nearest established concept, how does the thing I described differ from it (if it does), what are the strongest objections to reasoning this way, and does the answer change depending on how literally we take the idea that similar people are running similar reasoning — does it require near-duplicates, or does some weaker kind of similarity suffice? A bare name wouldn't satisfy me; I want to understand where the edges of the phenomenon are and whether it survives scrutiny.
