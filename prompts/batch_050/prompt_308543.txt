So I somehow ended up on the collections commitee of our little college art museum (long story, don't volunteer for things) and we have a genuine headache that is basically a metaphysics problem wearing a legal costume. I need a memo I can actually bring to the committee.

Background. Last year we accepted a long-term loan of an unfired clay sculpture, "Dancer," by a mid-career artist with a real but not huge market. It's a shaped lump of grey clay on a steel armature, maybe 40 lbs, insured for $180k (her auction record is $220k, so this seemed fine). Three months ago the artist visited, decided the piece wasn't finished, and — with the registrar standing right there, her loan agreement explicitly lets her modify it — squashed it back into a lump, then over the next two days re-formed it into what she calls the same dancer, finally right. To my eye it's the same pose with the arms different. Same clay, same armature, same pedestal, same room.

Now the problems:

1. Insurance. For about 26 hours between the squashing and the re-forming, the museum was in possession of an unshaped lump of clay and nothing else. Our insurer's adjuster is now arguing that during that window we held nothing the policy covered, since the policy schedules a sculpture and there was no sculpture — and worse, he's hinting that if a pipe had burst during those 26 hours we'd have been out of luck. He also wants to know whether the thing currently on the pedestal is even the item scheduled in the policy, or a brand-new unscheduled object we're holding uninsured. We owe him a response.

2. Cataloging. The registrar needs a ruling: did Dancer cease to exist for 26 hours and then come back? Did it survive inside the lump, somehow? Is the current object Dancer (same accession number, continuous provenance) or a new work that happens to be made of the same stuff (new accession, new provenance, old one marked destroyed)? This sounds pedantic but it drives the insurance answer, the loan paperwork, and the artist's catalogue raisonné.

3. The label. The artist wants "Dancer (2019, revised 2025), clay." The registrar wants something like "Dancer (2025), clay from the destroyed Dancer (2019)." The artist is genuinely upset about the word destroyed and is threatening to pull the loan over it, which would be a development-office problem on top of everything.

Write me a memo to the committee recommending (a) what we tell the insurer and (b) how we catalog and label the object now on the pedestal. Actual bottom lines, not a menu of options — the committee can reject my recommendation but I refuse to hand them a shrug. Reason it out from first principles about what the artwork is versus what the clay is: get into the metaphysics of material constitution as deep as you need to (that's literally why they put a philosopher on this thing), but the audience is a registrar, a development officer, and a dean, so it has to land for non-philosophers. Give the strongest honest version of the adjuster's view — that nothing of value was ever at risk except some clay and a shape, and shapes aren't property — before answering it, and if you don't think it can be answered, say so and tell me what we should concede. Also flag any spot where the metaphysically clean answer and the legally or diplomatically smart answer come apart, because the committee will definately notice. No citations, no reading list — I don't need a bibliography, I need a decision by Thursday.
