I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about the normative weight of the word genocide: whether anything of moral or political significance turns on applying that label to a massacre rather than describing it as mass murder, crimes against humanity, or civil war. Lemkin coined the term to name a crime aimed at the destruction of groups as such, and the 1948 Convention tied the label to a duty to prevent and punish, which has the perverse consequence that powerful states have an incentive to avoid the word precisely when it applies. My supervisor has encouraged me to engage with film for this chapter, and I have chosen Terry George's Hotel Rwanda (2004), which dramatizes the 1994 slaughter of Tutsi in Rwanda through the story of Paul Rusesabagina, the hotel manager who sheltered over a thousand refugees in Kigali while the international community looked away.

Read the film through the lens of the politics of naming atrocities. The film seems to me acutely aware of the strange power the Convention gives to a single word: it shows Western officials tying themselves in knots to avoid saying it, conceding at most that acts of genocide may have occurred, precisely because they take naming to entail a commitment to act. I would like a reading that treats the film as making an argument about this. Does Hotel Rwanda hold that the label itself does moral work — that the refusal to name was itself a further wrong, a second betrayal layered onto the first — or does it suggest, more cynically, that the word is a fetish of legalism, and that the actual wrongs (the murders, the withdrawal of the peacekeepers, the evacuation of white foreigners while Rwandans were left behind) needed no special name to be what they were? Which scenes support each reading, and how does the film's handling of the specific-intent question — its attention to the hate radio and to whether the killing was organized group-destruction rather than wartime chaos — bear on the legal category it keeps circling?

Then press on the film's own commitments, because I think there is a real tension here. The film's structural material — the radio broadcasts, the UN contingent forbidden to intervene, the diplomatic parsing of the Convention — sits awkwardly with its narrative form, which is a story of individual heroism and rescue, a Schindler-like tale of one resourceful man's bribes, phone calls, and negotiations. Does centering that story domesticate or trivialize the structural question about naming that the film itself raises? If the film implicitly endorses the thought that what mattered was what particular people did rather than what governments said, does that undercut the claim that the label mattered — or can the two be reconciled, perhaps by reading the label's significance as consisting precisely in what it licenses ordinary people to demand of their states? Finally, if you think the film dramatizes something the philosophical literature on genocide as a legal and moral category tends to miss — for instance, something about the gap between a category's official function and its lived meaning for the people being sorted by it — say what that is. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: the film should not be flattened into an illustration of Lemkin, and the legal-philosophical distinctions should not be blurred to fit the film.
