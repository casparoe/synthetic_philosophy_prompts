I host a philosophy podcast, and my next guest is an epistemologist defending a view she calls stratified dogmatism about perceptual justification. On her account, having a perceptual experience as of p gives a subject immediate, prima facie justification for believing p — but only when p is a first-stratum proposition, meaning one whose content does not concern whether the subject's perceptual appearances match reality. Second-stratum propositions, which do concern that match (such as 'my color experience is reliable' or 'it is not the case that things appear red to me without being red'), can be justified only inferentially, through an inductive track-record argument built on first-stratum beliefs. She advertises the stratification as her solution to the dogmatism paradox: the suspiciously easy deductions that generate the paradox all terminate in second-stratum conclusions, so on her view no perceptual justification is ever transmitted to them, and the paradoxical result — coming to be justified in believing that one's experience is not misleading merely by having the experience — never arises.

Draft eight to ten interview questions for this guest. I want questions that press on the actual mechanics of the view rather than invitations to restate it. For each question, add (a) a one- or two-sentence statement of the reply she is most likely to give, and (b) a follow-up question keyed to that reply that targets its weakest point. The sequence should collectively cover at least the following pressure points: whether stratum membership is a property of propositions or merely of their verbal formulations, and what happens when one and the same fact admits of both a first-stratum and a second-stratum description; which closure principle, if any, survives the stratification, and what it costs to deny unrestricted single-premise closure for justification; whether the track-record induction she licenses differs in any principled way from the discredited procedure of certifying a gauge's accuracy by consulting that very gauge repeatedly; the Bayesian-style objection that an experience can confer justification on a first-stratum proposition only if it also disconfirms the corresponding mismatch between appearance and reality, which on her taxonomy is a second-stratum matter the experience is not entitled to touch; and, finally, what would have to turn out to be the case for her to regard stratified dogmatism as refuted rather than merely counterintuitive.
