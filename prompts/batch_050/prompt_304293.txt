So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I've gotten sucked into this ongoing argument about whether LLMs are best understood as agents or as simulators. The rough idea on the simulator side, as I understand it, is that a base model isn't really a character with goals and beliefs, it's more like a engine that predicts how some text continues, and the "assistant" you chat with is just one persona it happens to be roleplaying -- so it could equally well play a villain, a bad poet, whatever, and the persona has no more fixed goals than a character in a novel does. The agent side pushes back that this is a distinction without a difference: once you RLHF a model into a stable assistant persona and it reliably pursues conversational goals, plans, uses tools, etc., then for all practical purposes it IS an agent, and saying "it's really just simulating one" is like saying a chess engine isn't really playing chess.

Here's what bugs me: I've been reading people go back and forth on this for a while now (twitter threads, blog posts, a couple papers my coworker sent me) and it feels completely stuck. Nobody seems to concede anything ever. My half-baked hypothesis is that the two sides are actually answering different questions -- one is about the architecture/training process (what kind of thing did gradient descent build), the other is about the right level of description for interacting with the thing (what kind of thing am I talking to) -- and both can be right, like how "it's a calculator" and "it's electrons moving through silicon" aren't rivals. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a real substantive fight here, e.g. about whether the model has situation-independent goals that generalize out of distribution, which seems like an empirical question that could actually be settled. Or maybe it's a purely verbal fight about what "agent" and "simulator" even mean.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. Using what each side actually argues: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or are the sides answering different questions under one label? If there's a hidden empirical question doing the work, name it and say what evidence would settle it. If it's a level-of-description confusion, explain why both descriptions can coexist and what work each one does. And tell me what progress would even look like -- a distinction everyone accepts, an experiment, or is the impasse a sign the question is ill-posed. Also tell me honestly if my different-questions hypothesis is too easy. End your response with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for what kind of disagreement this fundamentally is.
