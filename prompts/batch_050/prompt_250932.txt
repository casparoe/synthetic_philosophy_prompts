I'm trying to settle what emotions are by going on my considered verdicts about cases, and I'd like to know which theory of emotion — emotions as evaluative judgments, as perceptions of value, or as feelings — my verdicts actually add up to, if any. Here they are:

- Fear of flying: I accept the statistics, sincerely believe the flight is safe, and still grip the armrest. I'd call this genuine fear, and irrational fear — but I don't take myself to believe, even faintly, that the plane will crash.
- Long grief: my father died years ago. Most days nothing hurts, yet I'd say I've been grieving the whole time. Grief can obtain without any current felt episode.
- Sunday-evening dread about nothing in particular: I'd count it as an emotion even though, so far as I can tell, it isn't about anything.
- Anger at a colleague for a cutting remark, where I then learn he never made it: the anger doesn't go underground or linger as resentment — it simply vanishes. Anger, it seems, requires believing the offense occurred.
- The cool-headed observer who judges the regime to be unjust but feels nothing about it: I'd deny that she is indignant. Judgment without feeling isn't the emotion.

The trouble I can already see: my first and fourth verdicts pull in opposite directions for a judgment theory, my second and fifth are awkward for a feeling theory, and the third is awkward for any view on which an emotion must be about something. So: is there a single account — or a principled hybrid — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause each case motivates? If some verdict has to go, tell me which one and why that loss is cheapest. And tell me what the winning account predicts for a case I haven't listed: the person who says, sincerely and calmly, that she is terrified of dogs, while showing no trace of fear when one walks into the room.
