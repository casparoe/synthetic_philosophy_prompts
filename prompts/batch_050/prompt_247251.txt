I'm a high school senior in a philosophy-of-law elective, and for our capstone project my partner and I were deliberately assigned opposite sides of a classic dispute. I have to defend something like Lon Fuller's position: that the rule of law has an "inner morality," such that a legal system conforming to the familiar desiderata (general, public, prospective, intelligible, non-contradictory, stable, practicable laws, administered in line with their announced meaning) is thereby morally better, at least somewhat, and that there is a conceptual connection between legality and respect for persons as responsible agents. My partner must defend something like Joseph Raz's position: that the rule of law is essentially instrumental, a matter of how effectively law can guide conduct, and is therefore morally neutral in itself -- conformity to the desiderata makes a system better at doing whatever it sets out to do, including evil, and adds no moral worth of its own. After weeks of talking past each other, our teacher told us to stop writing competing essays and instead produce, together, the joint statement an honest adversarial collaboration between these two camps would issue.

Please draft that joint statement for us, as a model we can adapt. It should treat "the Fuller camp" and "the Raz camp" as the two signatories and should not simply declare one side the winner; the whole point is to find formulations neither side would consider slanted. Structure it in exactly these sections, in this order: first, "Shared Ground" (about 250 words, a numbered list of claims both camps can genuinely sign -- for instance about what the desiderata are, about why arbitrary power is bad, about what the rule of law does for planning and agency, and about what each side is NOT claiming); second, "Statement of Disagreements" (a table with one row per disagreement, where each side's position is phrased in terms that side would itself accept -- for example on whether rule-of-law conformity constitutes moral value or merely enables it, and on whether a wicked regime could in principle satisfy all the desiderata); third, "Cruxes" (about 150 words per crux, identifying two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, and for each crux a hypothetical case, argument, or finding both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance); and fourth, "Verbal or Real?" (about 100 words, stating which part of the dispute, if any, dissolves once terms like "morality" and "instrumental" are made precise, and which part is a genuine disagreement that would remain).

If you find that the two camps' famous slogans exaggerate their actual distance, or that one camp's standard talking point misrepresents the other, say so plainly within the relevant section rather than smoothing it over -- our teacher grades us on whether the document is one both historical figures could plausibly have signed.
