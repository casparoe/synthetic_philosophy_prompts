Ok so I need a referee. I was at the pub with my friend Dan last night and we got into a huge arguement about whether only informed people should get full voting rights. He laid out an argument, I tried to hit it with a reductio, and somehow I walked out more confused than I walked in. His argument:

(1) Whenever a decision will impose serious risks on people who never agreed to let that decision-maker decide, the decision is only legitimate if it's made by people competent at that kind of decision. (That's why we license surgeons and structural engineers, and why a verdict from a jury that's demonstrably clueless about the evidence can get thrown out.)

(2) Elections settle policies -- wars, tax codes, criminal law -- that impose serious risks on millions of people who never agreed to let those voters decide: people who voted the other way, non-voters, future generations, foreigners who can't vote at all.

(3) So mass voting is only legitimate if voters are generally competent at political judgement.

(4) Voters, measurably, are not: on basic tests of policy-relevant knowledge most of the electorate fails, and that's before you count bias and motivated reasoning.

(5) So universal equal suffrage as actually practiced is illegitimate, and political influence ought to be restricted to, or at least weighted toward, the competent -- some flavor of epistocracy.

I went: fine, then your argument proves way too much:

(6) Nothing in (1)-(5) is specific to voting. Parents make decisions imposing serious risks on children who never consented to anything; so do people who decide what kids get taught, who start religious movements, who publish diet and health advice to millions of strangers.

(7) So by the same logic the state may require parenting licenses, put expert boards in charge of curricula, license preachers, and censor amateur health bloggers.

(8) But that's a dystopia. Any line of reasoning that implies (7) has gone wrong somewhere.

Dan bit the bullet on about half of (7) -- "maybe parenting licenses aren't crazy, have you seen some parents" -- but even he wouldn't defend licensing preachers, so he agreed the argument overgenerates. Now we both think there's a flaw and after two hours neither of us could pin it down. He suspects (8) is just squeamishness on my part. I suspect the move from (1) to (3) but couldn't articulate why, which was annoying.

So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Go through the serious candidates (premise 1, the 2-to-3 move, premise 4, or the 6-to-7 generalization) and for each one explain why it looked fine and what you have to swallow to give it up. If it matters: I'd most like to keep the intuition behind (1), since clueless juries and unlicensed surgeons really do seem like a problem, and I'd like to keep universal suffrage -- but I care more about knowing where the argument actually breaks than about protecting either one.

One more request: when you lay out the candidate fixes, be explicit about which objections are more or less settled among the political philosophers who work on this stuff and which are live disputes where informed people land on both sides. Don't dress up a contested view as the consensus. I got burned once already tonight.
