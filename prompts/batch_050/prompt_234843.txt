I am a novelist revising a book in which several characters keep mistaking things for the matter that briefly makes them up: a patron cannot tell whether the bust survives being melted down, a restorer thinks each replaced stone preserves the abbey, and a forger exploits everyone’s confusion by making perfect casts from the same mold. I need a diagnostic instrument for a debate I will stage through scenes rather than exposition.

Write a decision tree for the statue-and-clay problem of material constitution, built for readers who know no metaphysics labels. Start from the ordinary commitment that a statue can exist and can change at least a little, then branch only through questions about concrete cases: squashing the clay and later reshaping exactly the same matter; grain-by-grain replacement of a marble torso until none of the original stone remains; melting a bronze figure and casting the metal into coins; a second statue made from the same lump at the same time by a divided mold; a church repaired over centuries until visitors doubt whether any medieval material survives. Each node should be phrased as something a character could answer from intuition—what is counted, what persists, what is destroyed, what would have been true had one atom differed—without saying which theory it serves.

The leaves should be located in the logical space first and named second: constitutionalism; coincident-object pluralism; the view that the statue simply is the lump while coincidence lasts; eliminativism about artifacts; four-dimensionalist perdurance; stage/exdurantist counterpart talk; mereological essentialism; and compositional nihilism. For every combination of answers your questions permit but no position occupies, say briefly whether the gap looks incoherent, merely costly, or historically unexplored. Flag any leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate—especially perdurance versus stage theory, and constitutionalism versus coincidence when the two are made to agree about counting—and propose one extra case that might pry them apart without resorting to science fiction.

Make the sorting do narrative work: after the tree, give a three-column table showing, for each leaf, the most natural verdict on the bust, the abbey, and the forger’s cast, plus the one sentence a character holding that view would be embarrassed to say aloud. Keep the whole answer usable offline; do not supply citations, bibliographies, or a reading list. End with a short confidence note stating how certain you are that the tree really exhausts the live positions, and identify the single branch or leaf you are least sure of.
