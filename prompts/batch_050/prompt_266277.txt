I'm preparing for a graduate reading group on personal identity, and this week we're doing Locke's memory criterion together with Reid's famous objection: the boy who is flogged for stealing apples grows up to be a brave officer who remembers the flogging, and the officer in turn grows old into a general who remembers his military exploits but no longer remembers the boyhood punishment. Reid's point, as we understand it, is that memory-connectedness is not transitive, while identity must be, so Locke's criterion cannot be right as stated. So far, so familiar. What I want help with is the standard rescue attempt that came up in our discussion and that I've also seen in textbooks and survey articles: the claim that personal identity over time is like a chain, or sometimes like a rope of overlapping fibers, where each link is directly connected only to its immediate neighbors, but the whole chain nonetheless hangs together as one thing. On this picture, the general is the same person as the boy not because he remembers being the boy, but because there is an unbroken overlapping series of memory connections running from the boy through the officer to the general. Several people in the group leaned hard on this image, treating it as if it simply dissolves Reid's objection, and one went further and said it shows that Locke's view needed only a minor patch rather than a fundamental rethink.

I'm suspicious that the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, and I'd like a proper audit of it. First, make the mapping explicit: what in the picture of a chain corresponds to person-stages, to memory connections, to the persisting person themselves? Second, identify where the mapping breaks down in ways that matter for the intended conclusion. Some things that worry me, though you should feel free to reframe or add to these: identity is transitive and chain-connectedness is not, so it's unclear the analogy answers Reid rather than simply declining to use the notion he was criticizing; a chain is itself a single persisting object, which threatens to make the analogy quietly presuppose the very thing at issue; and real chains can split, which raises the question of what the picture says about fission cases, where psychological continuity branches. Third, say which features of chains the analogy imports that persons and their lives lack, and whether any of those imports are load-bearing for the patch to Locke. Finally, the actual payoff question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the rescue of Locke survive, and if so in what weakened form — or was the chain image carrying the whole argument? If you think a different analogy (a river, a thread, a relay race, a family resemblance series, or something else) does the work better, propose it and explain what it captures that the chain misses.
