I run a 14-person research group at a small biotech. We’re deciding whether to spend most of next quarter developing one compound. At kickoff, everyone read the same dossier and agreed the chances it would hit a clearly specified efficacy target by June were about 50-50. Then people went off and did different follow-up work.

To avoid the usual loudest-person-in-the-room dynamic, I opened an internal play-money market on whether the compound will hit the target. Everyone starts with the same points, and the best forecasters get a modest cash bonus. The market opened at 50%, moved around a little, and then Maya, who ran the key assay, bet heavily against the compound. She wouldn’t explain why because the result is still messy and she doesn’t want to bias the team. The price dropped to 61% YES. Now another scientist, Jordan, wants to bet NO at that price.

My cofounder says I should freeze the market. His argument is that everyone involved is competent, knows the others are competent, and started from the same evidence. Maya’s first bet already told us she had negative information, and the price movement made that public. So if Jordan still wants to bet NO and Maya or anyone else accepts the other side, at least one of them must be fooling themselves. In fact, he says repeated offers should keep making everyone update until there’s no bet left that two rational people would both accept.

That sounds clever, but also wierd. If nobody could ever trade after seeing somebody else’s willingness to trade, how would a market pool information in the first place? And I keep thinking of this passage from Friedrich Hayek’s “The Use of Knowledge in Society”:

“The peculiar character of the problem of a rational economic order is determined precisely by the fact that the knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess.”

Assume for the sake of argument that we really do all share the same initial view and all know each other to be competent Bayesians. Is my cofounder right that Jordan’s desired trade should unravel? Should I personally take the YES side, or does accepting the bet just prove I haven’t absorbed the information in Jordan’s offer? More practically, how should I redesign the process so it captures Hayek’s point without treating the market price as magic? Please give advice based on the actual setup, not generic “markets are good/bad” stuff. In particular, explain what changes if Maya’s edge is tacit lab judgment rather than a reportable result, if people care differently about the bonus, or if I replace peer-to-peer bets with a subsidized automated market maker.
