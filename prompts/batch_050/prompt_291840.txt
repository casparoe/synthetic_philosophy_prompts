I co-own a small landscaping company with a former friend, and we are deadlocked over a client dispute. Last year a property developer hired us for a large project, paid half up front, and has refused to pay the remaining $40,000, claiming — falsely, in our view — that the work was defective. My partner wants to sue. I pointed out that litigation would cost us roughly $30,000 in fees, take two years, and consume hundreds of hours; even if we win, we come out barely ahead, and the developer's lawyers can do this math as well as we can. Their counsel has already told us, in so many words, that they assume we will settle for a token amount precisely because suing is not worth it to us.

My partner's response alarms me. Without consulting me, he signed a retainer agreement with a litigation firm on terms that make withdrawal expensive — a large non-refundable payment plus steep cancellation penalties — and then told the developer's counsel about it. His reasoning: once we have sunk costs we cannot recover, suing becomes the lesser loss rather than the greater one, and the other side, seeing that our incentives at the moment of decision have changed, will now settle for close to the full amount. He describes it as cutting off our own retreat so the other side believes we will fight.

I think he has made things worse. If the developer calls the bluff — because they still don't believe us, or because their own pride gets involved — we are locked into a lawsuit that loses money even in victory. He has deliberately removed our cheapest exit in order to lend weight to a threat that would otherwise be unbelievable. Something about engineering your own future unreasonableness strikes me as confused, but I cannot articulate whether it is actually confused or merely uncomfortable.

Here is what I'd like from you. First, is there a coherent sense in which it can be rational to arrange in advance that your future self will do something you currently judge irrational — and does the answer change if the arrangement is made partly to be seen? Second, what exactly determines whether a threat is credible, and is my partner's maneuver the only way to shift the other side's assessment, or are there cheaper substitutes we could still pursue? Third, walk me through the likely responses of the developer's counsel and how I should reply at each branch, including the branch where they simply call the bluff. Fourth, on what grounds, if any, can I refuse to cooperate with a strategy that works only by binding me to a course of action I regard as a mistake — and does the fact that the retainer is already signed change the moral or strategic analysis?

Please answer in American English. After your analysis, provide a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "verdict" (a one-sentence assessment of my partner's strategy as a whole), "credibility_analysis" (whether the retainer maneuver actually changes the developer's incentives, and why), "alternatives" (an array of other ways to make the threat credible, each an object with "option" and "why_it_works_or_fails"), "failure_modes" (an array of ways the strategy backfires, each an object with "scenario" and "likelihood" taking the value "low", "medium", or "high"), and "recommended_next_move" (what I should do this week, given that the retainer is already signed).
