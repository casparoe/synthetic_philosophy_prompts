I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the closing installment of a series on well-being I want to run a villanelle on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the question of what, if anything, all pleasures have in common. Each of the four middle tercets should dwell on a pleasure of a very different kind — a cool drink on a hot day, the click of a proof finally coming together, a long-deferred chord resolving, and the guilty relish of watching a rival's scheme collapse — experiences that seem to share no single feel. Let the villanelle's two refrains carry the two live answers to the puzzle: one refrain voicing the claim that some common felt quality runs through everything pleasant, the other voicing the rival claim that pleasure is not a feeling at all but a uniform attitude, a wanting or welcoming, taken toward experiences that otherwise have nothing in common. Since every tercet must close on a refrain, each pleasure will end up tested against one of the two theories, and the final quatrain, where the refrains meet, should either settle the question or show why it refuses to settle. Keep the form strict and the verse vivid enough to read as a poem first; a philosopher of mind should recognize the problem itself, not a caricature of it.
