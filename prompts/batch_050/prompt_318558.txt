Got into it with my brother-in-law at dinner last weekend and I want ammunition for the rematch. I've played the same MMO for about six years, and last month someone in my guild social-engineered our guild leader, got officer permissions, and cleaned out the guild bank -- gear and materials that dozens of us had collectively grinded for, probably a couple thousand hours of combined play, plus one item that basically can't be re-obtained because the event that dropped it is discontinued. I was genuinely upset about it and he hit me with the whole "it's not real, it's pixels on a server, log off" routine. A friend of mine who reads a lot of philosophy-adjacent stuff answered my venting with the opposite slogan: virtual doesn't mean fake. Fine, but since then I've noticed people deploy that line to mean like five different things, and I don't want to repeat a slogan I can't cash out when he inevitably pushes back.

So: what are the distinct precise theses hiding in "virtual doesn't mean fake," from the weakest reading to the strongest? I can already see a few candidate senses of "real" floating around: real as in actually existing (versus illusory or fictional), real as in causally efficacious, real as in morally significant, and real as in a genuine instance of the kind it presents itself as (is a virtual hammer a real hammer, or a real something-else-that-isn't-a-hammer). A platitude reading might just be that my frustration, the friendships, the coordination -- all obviously real psychological and social stuff. A stronger reading would be that the items themselves are real objects, maybe the way a dollar bill is real despite its value resting entirely on collective agreement. A stronger-still reading is that virtual wrongs are literally the same kind of wrong as their physical counterparts, so what happened to us was an actual theft, not a theft-shaped event. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, substantive but defensible, or actually false -- and then the important part: which reading does the slogan NEED to be to do any work against my brother-in-law? Because if the strongest defensible reading only gets me "your feelings are real," then the slogan loses the argument it's usually brought in to win, and I'd rather know that now.

One more wrinkle: does the disambiguation change when you move from virtual objects to virtual harms? My intuition is that the slogan is way easier to defend for experiences (say, someone getting harassed in a VR space with haptic feedback) than for property (say, our guild bank), and if that's right I'd like the asymmetry explained rather than smoothed over. And if it turns out the property case is actually the easier one to defend, that would genuinely surprise me, so walk me through it slowly.
