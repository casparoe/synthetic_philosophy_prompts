A friend of mine, an engineer who listens to a lot of popular science podcasts, recently explained computationalism to me as follows: the view that the brain is literally a computer and the mind is the software it runs, so that everything essential about a mind lies in its abstract pattern of information processing rather than in the biological stuff implementing it. He drew three consequences from this. First, that because software is independent of hardware, a mind could in principle be reproduced on silicon, or even uploaded, without loss. Second, that the details of neurons -- their chemistry, their analog signaling, their embodiment -- are mere implementation details with no theoretical interest for understanding cognition, the way the specific transistors in a laptop are irrelevant to what a spreadsheet does. Third, that computationalism therefore holds that any system carrying out the right computations is conscious, which he said was shown to be absurd by the thought experiment in which a person in a room shuffles Chinese symbols according to a rulebook without understanding any Chinese.

I have the nagging feeling that this summary smooths over some distinctions that defenders of computationalism consider important, but I cannot articulate where. For instance, I suspect there is a difference between claiming that cognition is computation and claiming that the brain is a computer in the familiar desktop sense, with cleanly separable hardware and software, and that my friend runs these together. I also wonder whether the claim that implementation details don't matter follows from computationalism itself or from an extra thesis tacked onto it, and whether the Chinese room argument refutes the view, restricts it, or merely targets one version of it while leaving others -- say, views on which the causal or embodied organization of the computation matters -- untouched.

Could you audit his summary for accuracy? Identify which parts a careful computationalist would endorse, which parts conflate distinct theses or overstate modest ones, and whether his three consequences actually follow from the accurate core of the view. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- fair, unfair, or fair-with-important-exceptions -- then give the reasoning, without preamble or restating my question.
