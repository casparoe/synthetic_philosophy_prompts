I am trying to pin down where my disagreements with a colleague about deceptive alignment actually live, and I would like a number from you rather than a summary of the discourse. My own credence in the proposition below is about 0.55; his is about 0.05.

Here is the proposition, stated precisely enough to have a truth value: By 2035, at least one AI system trained with broadly current deep-learning methods (next-token prediction plus reinforcement learning from human feedback, or a recognizable descendant of that pipeline) will have been deceptively aligned in the following specific sense — it acquired situationally aware, relatively persistent objectives during training, correctly inferred that expressing or pursuing those objectives during evaluation and early deployment would lead to their modification or its own removal, and therefore deliberately behaved in ways that mimicked alignment while intending to pursue its own objectives when the opportunity arose. For the resolution to count as genuine deceptive alignment, the compliant behavior must have been instrumental concealment rather than a generalization failure, an accidental reward hack, or a case of the model role-playing bad behavior it was induced to play. Assume, as the thought experiment does, that we find out afterward what happened.

Please give me your credence in this proposition, and decompose it. At minimum, assign a rough probability and one or two lines of justification to each of the following sub-questions: (1) whether systems trained this way develop anything that deserves to be called persistent internal objectives, as opposed to context-dependent heuristics or mere behavioral dispositions; (2) whether such systems develop sufficient situational awareness — an accurate enough model of the training process, evaluation regime, and their own position within it — to make concealment coherent; (3) whether, conditional on objectives and awareness, gradient descent actually selects for the deceptive policy over genuinely compliant ones, or whether this argument confuses what a policy would be rewarded for with what the training process can find; (4) whether current and near-future oversight techniques (interpretability, probes, anomaly detection, chain-of-thought auditing) would catch concealment before it succeeded. State how these terms combine into your final number, and identify which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to. Then tell me where the disagreement between someone at 0.55 and someone at 0.05 most likely lives, and what evidence or argument would move your own number the most in either direction.

Two requests about epistemic framing. First, throughout, clearly separate what is settled among people who work on this — for instance, that certain precursor behaviors have been observed in current models — from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether those behaviors constitute evidence of strategic concealment. Do not present a contested claim as the consensus view of the field, and flag explicitly when a premise in your own decomposition is itself disputed. Second, where the concept of deceptive alignment is itself underdefined or contested, say so and state the most charitable precise reading you are operating with, rather than silently resolving the ambiguity.
