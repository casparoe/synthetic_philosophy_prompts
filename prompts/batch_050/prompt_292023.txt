I am a second-year undergraduate taking Philosophy of Language and Gender, Race and Speech, and I keep circling an argument inspired by Person-Paper-style reversals: you take a trait whose grammatical marking normally goes unnoticed, make an analogous trait obligatory in a dialect, and let the sudden visibility do the work. I want to know where the following argument goes wrong, because each step looks locally reasonable and the conclusion is not.

(1) A grammatical feature is objectionable only if, considered in itself, it encodes a false or demeaning proposition about the people it classifies. (2) The English contrast between he, she and they encodes sex, or at most invites a sex classification, but by itself it asserts nothing false or demeaning. (3) Where the available forms are symmetrical across the classified groups and speakers have usable alternatives, the mere existence of the classification does not create a hierarchy. (4) So the pronoun system is morally inert apart from patterns of use; any harm done by generic he, by assumptions of male default, or by reluctance to use singular they is pragmatic, historical or psychological rather than semantic. (5) Now imagine a dialect, Callern, in which honorifics obligatorily mark race: there are parallel forms for every racialised group, the system is perfectly symmetrical, speakers may use a cumbersome neutral form, and its defenders insist that race is encoded only because institutions sometimes request the information. (6) By (1)-(4), Callern is also morally inert apart from use; finding it creepy before examining uptake would confuse felt salience with encoded content. (7) Yet Callern seems objectionable immediately, in virtue of what it makes ordinary, not only after we measure outcomes. (8) Therefore either we must say Callern is fine until proven harmful, or we must admit that generic he cannot be criticised on grounds deeper than bad habits. Since the first option is monstrous and the second seems to dissolve the feminist critique of default masculinity into etiquette, the argument proves too much.

Please identify the premise or inference you would reject, explain why it looked attractive, and price the main alternatives. In particular, distinguish encoded content from default inference, accommodation, salience and what a grammatical practice treats as mentionable; say whether a reversal of this kind can establish a semantic conclusion or only expose an asymmetry of attention; and state what would have to be true of actual English use for your diagnosis to change. I would rather preserve the thought that default assumptions can be built into apparently neutral forms than retain premise (1), but I do not want to win by defining every classification as domination. Please give a clear verdict, not just a menu, and answer in British English.
