I fix and restore old machines for a living, mostly vintage motorbikes and the odd tractor, so I live in a world where every part is there for a reason. The carburettor is for mixing fuel and air. If you pulled it off and stuck a random bit of pipe there instead, the bike wouldn't run, because the engine was designed around that part doing that job.

Lately I've been reading popular science stuff about evolution and biologists talk exactly the same way: the heart is for pumping blood, the eye is for seeing, the liver is for detoxing. But there's no designer in evolution, nobody who put the heart there on purpose the way someone put a carburettor on a Triumph. So I got curious and poked around a bit and apparantly philosophers of biology have two main stories about what "for" can even mean here. Roughly: one says a trait's function is whatever it did back in history that got it selected and kept around (the history story, call it). The other says a trait's function is just whatever it currently does that contributes to the whole organism working (the role story). I might have these slightly wrong, feel free to fix my versions first if so.

Here's the actual ask. Take both stories and restate them in the vocabulary of my workshop: parts, repairs, replacements, bodged fixes, what a machine was built for versus what some part happens to be doing right now, a part that's been swapped for something that wasn't designed for the job but works anyway. Do it as exactly as you can, not just loose gestures. Then give me what I'd call a leftover report: which bits of each story translate cleanly into workshop talk, which bits you had to bend to make fit, and which bits have no workshop counterpart at all.
