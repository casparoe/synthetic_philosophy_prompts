I have no academic training in linguistics, but I have been thinking on my own about innateness and the “poverty of the stimulus” argument in language acquisition. At a recent discussion group, two members, Priya and Owen, had the following disagreement. I stayed out of it, and I would like an impartial adjudication.

Priya began with the familiar auxiliary-fronting case. A child hears sentences such as:

- The dog is hungry.
- The dog that is barking is hungry.
- Is the dog hungry?
- Is the dog that is barking hungry?

Priya argued that a learner relying only on surface word order might adopt the rule “move the first is to the front,” producing the ungrammatical sentence “Is the dog that barking is hungry?” Since children generally grasp that the auxiliary belonging to the main clause must move, and since adults do not systematically correct every possible wrong rule, she concluded that children must possess an innate, language-specific bias toward analyzing sentences hierarchically rather than as flat strings of words.

Owen replied that this argument defeats only one deliberately simple learning rule. A child might instead classify words by their grammatical function, associate auxiliaries with predicates, notice pauses and intonation, and connect sentences with visible situations and speakers’ intentions. In his view, poverty-of-the-stimulus arguments often treat the input as nothing more than an ordered list of strings. He called that “string-only poverty” and contrasted it with “ecological poverty,” which would remain after including meaning, context, social cues, memory limitations, and the learner’s prior experience. Showing string-only poverty, he said, does not establish ecological poverty.

Priya answered that adding context does not eliminate the underlying problem. A finite set of encounters remains compatible with indefinitely many grammars, so the learner still needs constraints. She proposed what she called an “evidence ledger”: for every grammatical distinction a child acquires, researchers should identify which observable features of the input support it, distinguish directly relevant examples from merely related ones, and estimate whether there are enough of the former. Until empiricists supply such a ledger, she said, appeals to rich context remain promissory.

Owen objected that an evidence ledger treats evidence as if its importance were determined mainly by frequency. One especially clear contrast, an emotionally salient correction, or a meaningful minimal pair might outweigh thousands of ambiguous examples. He also argued that the same demand should apply to nativism: a nativist should specify exactly which constraint is innate, what form it takes before exposure to a particular language, how it interacts with linguistic variation, and why it should be considered language-specific rather than a general feature of cognition. Otherwise, innate knowledge becomes an unexplained explanation.

Priya then invoked children’s errors. Children sometimes overgeneralize patterns, as when they produce forms analogous to “goed,” but they do not appear to pass through a stage in which they systematically use the surface rule of fronting the first auxiliary. If learning were driven entirely by general statistical induction, she asked, why would children be adventurous in one place and conservative in the other? She said this selectivity is exactly what an innate constraint predicts.

Owen replied that the absence of a predicted error is weaker evidence than the presence of one. Children may avoid the bad auxiliary rule because the relevant categories have already been learned, because such an analysis never becomes cognitively available, or because the structures involved differ in frequency and communicative function. He also said that “general-purpose learning” does not imply that every possible hypothesis is equally available at every developmental stage. Priya was treating a constrained learner as if it had to be either completely unconstrained or equipped with innate grammatical rules, thereby omitting intermediate possibilities.

Priya’s final move concerned development. She argued that native-like mastery becomes harder when acquisition begins later, which suggests that the relevant learning system has a biologically prepared period of operation. Owen responded that age of acquisition is entangled with amount and type of exposure, education, motivation, identity, interference from a first language, and opportunities to use the second language. Priya countered that multiplying possible explanations can make anti-nativism impossible to falsify. Owen answered that nativism has the same danger whenever an unsuccessful innate constraint is replaced by a more abstract, probabilistic, or indirectly expressed one.

Please adjudicate their disagreement in American English. Go through each round rather than simply declaring an overall winner. For each exchange:

1. State the strongest reasonable version of Priya’s claim and Owen’s response.
2. Say whether the response actually answers the claim or changes the subject.
3. Identify who has the better point on that specific issue, or explain why the issue remains undecided.
4. Distinguish among three conclusions that the discussion sometimes seems to blur: that language learning is constrained, that some constraints are innate, and that the innate constraints are specific to language rather than consequences of general cognition.
5. Assess whether Priya’s proposed “evidence ledger” and Owen’s distinction between “string-only poverty” and “ecological poverty” genuinely clarify the debate.
6. Conclude by identifying what both participants may be missing and what kinds of evidence, short of citing particular studies or scholarly works, would discriminate among their positions.
