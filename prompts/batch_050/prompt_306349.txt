Our reading group just did Jaspers on German guilt and it kicked off an argument that's been running for weeks. One member, call her Dana, has a settled set of verdicts about collective responsibility and I want to figure out the rule underneath them, because we've hit a new case and nobody can predict what she'll say. Her track record so far: she says the grandchild of a Nazi official, born in the 80s, is guilty of nothing and owes nothing beyond what any citizen owes; she says a chemical company that poisoned a valley in the 1960s is still guilty and still owes cleanup even though every executive and worker from that era is dead and the current staff had nothing to do with it; she says new members of a fraternity who joined after a hazing cover-up, knowing about it, genuinely share in the blame and not just a duty to quit; she says descendants of slaveholders who still hold and profit from land worked by enslaved people are not guilty but owe restitution; and she says a tourist vacationing in a dictatorship isn't guilty of anything, while a businessman who builds his company's whole growth strategy around that regime's contracts is "implicated," though she refused to call him guilty. She's read alot of Jaspers and splits guilt from liability pretty fluently (criminal vs political vs moral guilt, what you owe your conscience vs what the collective owes), but she doesn't seem to apply the scheme consistently, or atleast I can't see it.

Here's the new case we fought about: a mid-sized modern democracy whose present territory and much of its wealth trace back to a 19th-century conquest of an indigenous nation. Nobody alive participated in it. The state issued a formal apology decades ago but kept the land, the resources, and the institutions, and its ordinary citizens vote, pay taxes, and enjoy infrastructure and pension funds ultimately built on that conquest. Emigration is possible but costly, and most citizens have never seriously thought about it. Predict Dana's verdict: guilty, liable-without-guilt, merely implicated, or off the hook entirely? Then tell me the rule she seems to be running across the earlier five verdicts, at least two rival rules that fit those verdicts just as well but come apart on this new case (voluntary membership vs ongoing benefit vs institutional continuity are the obvious candidates, but maybe there's a better one I haven't thought of), which rule you'd actually bet on and why, and the single further case you'd put to her that would most cleanly decide between your top two candidates. Keep it at the level of principles rather than scholarship -- I don't need citations, just reasoning I can bring back to the group on Tuesday.
