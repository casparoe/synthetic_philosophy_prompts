I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized company (about 200 engineers), and I've ended up as the owner of what we informally call "engineering memory": our architecture decision records, incident postmortems, runbooks, the internal wiki, onboarding docs, and a graveyard of archived RFCs going back eleven years. Until now this stuff accumulated untouched, and we've just had a painful quarter where two outages were prolonged by obsolete runbooks and a new hire spent three weeks implementing a pattern that a 2016 decision record had explicitly ruled out. Leadership has asked me to design a triage process: every artifact gets reviewed on a rotating schedule (dozens per quarter), and reviewers decide whether to promote it, keep it, annotate it, rewrite it, deprecate it, or delete it. Deletions need sign-off from two senior engineers; everything else a single reviewer can do in about 30 minutes per artifact.

While thinking about this I read Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life, and his threefold scheme struck me as almost a spec for what I need. I don't have the text in front of me, so here is my own paraphrase of the relevant stretch, from my notes:

"There are three ways the past can serve the living. Monumental history looks backward for greatness: the knowledge that something great was once achieved proves it is achievable again, and this faith sustains those who act. Its danger is that it must falsify to inspire -- it smooths away difference and circumstance, makes the past look inevitable and repeatable, and can be weaponized to belittle whatever is attempted now. Antiquarian history preserves with reverence: it tells a person or a people where they came from and lets them belong to something, and its danger is that it preserves indiscriminately, mummifies, and smothers the new -- soon nothing is allowed to grow because everything old is guarded. Critical history judges and condemns: one must sometimes drag the past before a tribunal and find it guilty, since every inheritance contains injustice and error. Its danger is that we are ourselves products of what we condemn, and the habit of negation rarely learns how to plant anything in the cleared ground. A living thing needs some measure of all three, and suffers when any one of them rules alone."

Assume the paraphrase is fair enough and work from it; if I've distorted something that matters for the application, say so briefly, but don't make the answer depend on scholarship I can't check.

What I want is a written procedure our doc-triage rotation can actually run, grounded in this scheme. Specifically:

1. Translate the three modes into operations on documents. For each mode, say what it becomes when the "past" is a decision record, a postmortem, a runbook, or a founding-era design doc, and what its characteristic corruption looks like in each case (e.g., what is the antiquarian failure mode of a postmortem archive? what does monumental distortion do to an old ADR?). Tell me which of Nietzsche's distinctions have to survive the translation intact and which can be collapsed or dropped for a non-philosopher reviewer, and justify the cuts.

2. Give me the procedure itself: an ordered set of questions or tests a reviewer applies to one artifact in 30 minutes, ending in one of my six verdicts (promote / keep / annotate / rewrite / deprecate / delete). The procedure should encode the idea that an artifact can be monumentally valuable and critically rotten at once, and that the healthy archive needs all three modes represented rather than converging on one.

3. Worked applications to three artifacts: (a) a 2015 ADR whose technical rationale is obsolete but which is constantly cited in design debates as proof that "we once thought big"; (b) a beloved, beautifully written design document by our founding CTO, who left acrimoniously, describing an architecture we have fully migrated away from; (c) a stack of forty routine postmortems nobody reads. One of these should be a case where the procedure gives a verdict I'll find uncomfortable -- flag which one and why I'll resist it.

4. Mark the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and a human has to just decide, and say what the procedure structurally cannot do -- including whether the framework itself has a blind spot for a kind of artifact I haven't listed.

One more thing: Nietzsche's framing is that history must serve life and action, not knowledge for its own sake. If I take that seriously, is "we should preserve an accurate record of what happened and why" itself a suspect justification under this scheme? Tell me honestly whether the procedure should include a verdict of "accurate but dead," and what that implies for our instinct to keep everything.
