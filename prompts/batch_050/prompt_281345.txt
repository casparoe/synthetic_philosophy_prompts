I teach a second-year undergraduate epistemology course — 24 students, one 75-minute seminar — and I want an in-class activity on Moore's paradox: the peculiar defectiveness of sentences of the form "p, but I do not believe that p." Design a game, playable with cards, tokens, and no real stakes, that makes students feel why such sentences are defective before I ever tell them this is the topic. The core mechanic must force a decoupling of assertion from belief: I want a structure in which a player is permitted to assert something while explicitly disavowing belief in it, and in which the group discovers for itself, through play, that such moves are unusable — that no one can act on, score, or stake anything on a Moore-paradoxical utterance, even though the utterance may well be true. So that students see the sentence is not a contradiction, include a phase in which the same form of words is entirely unproblematic (said about another player, or about one's own past self), and an optional extension round that approaches what I will later name as blindspots: truths about the group's own current distribution of beliefs that no member can come to believe while the game continues. Provide complete rules, a materials list, a token-based payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The debrief should map specific moments of play onto (i) the omissive versus commissive forms of the paradox, (ii) the distinction between the absurdity of asserting a Moore sentence and the impossibility of believing one, and (iii) the candidate norms of assertion the game's scoring implicitly enforces, so that I can connect what happened to the claim that assertion is the outward expression of belief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run it — I anticipate students treating the move as ordinary lying, or dismissing it as a self-reference trick — and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in discussion; my suspicion is that a game can stage assertoric absurdity but cannot give anyone the first-person experience of actually holding a Moorean belief.
