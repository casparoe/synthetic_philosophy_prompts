I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and I've committed to defending a thesis that most of my classmates consider obviously wrong; I want it argued as forcefully as possible before I have to present it. The background is Chalmers's paradox of phenomenal judgment, which is standardly deployed as a problem specifically for epiphenomenalism. If phenomenal properties are causally inert, then our judgments about our own experience — that this looks red, that this hurts, that the pain is throbbing rather than stabbing — cannot be caused by the experiences they concern, and so the striking fit between what we say about experience and the experience itself appears, on the epiphenomenalist picture, to be an unexplained coincidence. Interactionist dualists and reductive physicalists alike wield the paradox against epiphenomenalism while treating their own positions as immune.

My thesis is that this asymmetry is an illusion, in both directions. Either the paradox generalizes — because an interactionist causal link from experience to judgment does not by itself explain the reliability of the judgment, since one must still say why the judgment tracks phenomenal character rather than merely being downstream of it, and a physicalist who identifies experience with a brain state faces a structurally identical question about why the self-ascriptive judgment latches onto the phenomenal rather than merely the neural — or else whatever reply rescues those views, whether an appeal to constitution, to acquaintance, or to the claim that introspective judgments are partly constituted by the experiences they concern, is a reply the epiphenomenalist can help herself to as well. In short: either every theory of consciousness faces the paradox, or none does; it cannot be a selective weapon against epiphenomenalism alone.

Acting as a professional philosopher of mind specializing in consciousness, argue for this thesis as rigorously as you can. Think carefully before writing, and in particular weigh the two or three strongest objections — for instance, that an interactionist causal link genuinely does explanatory work the epiphenomenalist cannot mimic, or that acquaintance-based accounts of introspective knowledge are unavailable to anyone who denies phenomenal causation — and build your replies to them into the argument rather than appending them afterward. Where you think the thesis needs qualification or a distinction I have not drawn, draw it and keep going. Open with a one- or two-sentence statement of your bottom line, then give the argument. Keep the whole response under 600 words, and do not include citations or a bibliography; argue from the structure of the positions themselves.
