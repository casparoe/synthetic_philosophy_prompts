You are a moral philosopher with deep expertise in the work of Emmanuel Levinas and practical experience designing decision procedures for front-line public institutions. Think carefully about the genuine tensions involved before settling on a design.

I direct a municipal ombuds office that handles complaints against city departments -- housing, sanitation, benefits administration, code enforcement. My office receives roughly forty complaints a week. My six case officers triage each one: they decide whether to open an investigation, whether to attempt informal resolution first, whether to escalate to a formal hearing, and when to tell a complainant that the city acted within its rules and we will not pursue the matter further. Each officer has about ninety minutes per complaint at intake, no legal training, and their decisions are reviewed by me only if appealed.

I want our intake procedure rebuilt around Levinas's ethics, as far as that is possible. My interest is not decorative. Two features of his thought seem directly relevant to what my office does badly. First, his claim that responsibility is commanded by the concrete face of the other person before any rule is applied -- that general categories (case numbers, eligibility criteria, file summaries) risk effacing exactly the thing that makes a complaint matter. Second, his acknowledgment that the arrival of the third party -- everyone else who is also owed something -- forces comparison, calculation, and justice, which is precisely what an institution like mine exists to do. Levinas seems to say both that the face must come first and that institutions must inevitably betray the face in order to be just. I want a procedure that takes that tension seriously rather than resolving it in either direction.

Please begin by briefly brainstorming -- maybe half a page -- the specific points where Levinasian ethics resists proceduralization, and identify which of his distinctions you judge must survive translation into a usable procedure and which can safely be dropped. Defend the cuts.

Then write the procedure itself, as something I could hand to a new case officer. It should specify: the questions to ask in order at intake; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points where the procedure requires the officer to stop applying criteria and attend to the complainant as a singular person -- and what attending concretely means in a ninety-minute interview, since without that the instruction is empty; where the procedure stops with a definite no; and where it must hand the decision to a human being with authority because the question is one of competing responsibilities that no rule can rank. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a tenant complaining that a housing officer dismissed her repair request without visiting, where the file suggests the officer followed standard practice; (2) a dispute in which honoring one complainant's claim will foreseeably worsen conditions for many other residents, so the third party enters forcefully; and (3) a case of your construction where the procedure yields an answer I will not like -- say, declining to pursue a complaint from someone whose situation is genuinely sympathetic -- and show the procedure generating that outcome honestly rather than being bent around it.

Close by stating what this procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, and in particular whether a checklist designed to protect singularity from totalization becomes, in the writing, one more instance of the very totalization it guards against -- and what, if anything, an institution can do about that besides knowing it.
