so my friend Mira has gotten really into Kant's Groundwork this semester and she keeps running everyone's maxims through the universalizability test at dinner. The bit she always reads out loud is from the Preface (Abbott translation), where Kant writes:

Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, "Thou shalt not lie," is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason

Her verdicts so far, as far as I can reconstruct them:

- borrowing money by promising to repay when you know you won't: fails the test
- a maxim of never helping anyone, ever, no matter how cheap it would be: fails
- letting all your talents rust and just coasting: fails
- lying, but only in cases where the lie is guaranteed to be undetectable: fails (this one surprised me)
- playing tennis at 7am on Sundays specifically because the courts are empty then: passes
- collecting clockwork trains, buying but never selling: passes (she admits this one is weird but says the test has no problem with it)

Here's the new case. Our other friend is one of those hyper self-reliance guys, and his maxim is roughly: never help anyone, but also never ask for help or accept it -- full hermit, nobody owes anyone anything, he'd rather crawl out of the ditch himself than take a hand. He says his maxim is consistent in a way the mere non-helper's isn't, because he's not counting on anyone else's aid while refusing his own.

So: does Mira's test pass or fail the hermit maxim, and why? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in:
