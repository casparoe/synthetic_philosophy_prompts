I teach a second-year course on rational choice and moral philosophy (about 24 students, one 80-minute session, they all know the prisoner's dilemma already and are frankly a bit bored of it). I want an in-class game that makes them feel the trap in the hitchhiker-style case: a stranded person is only rescued because the rescuer believes she's the kind of person who will pay up later, but once she's safely in town, paying looks like pure loss -- yet the people who wouldn't pay are the ones who never get rescued in the first place. Crucially, I do not want this to collapse into "cooperation is good in repeated games" -- they've done that to death. The interesting and uncomfortable bit is the one-shot version, where there is no reputation and no future, and the only thing doing work is whether the rescuer can somehow tell what kind of person you are before committing. So the activity needs some mechanism that simulates the rescuer reading the traveler's character rather than just remembering her past moves.

So: design the game with actual rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme using points or tokens (I can convert totals into a tiny amount of course credit, so the stakes are small but real), and a timeline for the 80 minutes including at least two rounds with different information structures. Tell me how to stage it so the students discover the problem themselves -- e.g., they notice that the ones who kept their "promises" mysteriously kept getting picked up, or that rescuers started screening in a way that punished people who made perfectly "rational" choices. Then a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the game onto the philosophical structure (the promise, the backward-looking irrationality of paying, the fact that the disposition is what has value even when the act doesn't) without me having to lecture first. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students treating it as a repeated game, collusion, someone gaming the character-reading mechanism), and how do I handle each. And be honest with me about the one thing this kind of game basically can't reproduce -- in the original case the rescuer is supposed to be a near-perfect judge of character in a single encounter, and any playable classroom proxy for that is going to be leaky -- and tell me how to acknowledge that in the debrief without deflating the whole exercise.
