You are a philosopher-economist who designs seminar activities for epistemology and rational choice. Think carefully, but do not show private chain-of-thought; begin with a concise design rationale comparing two or three possible mechanisms, then give the single best version. Write in American English.

Design a runnable classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar, 16 to 24 students, one 75-minute meeting, no laptops required, on no-trade results and the epistemology of markets. The goal is not that students can state a theorem afterward; it is that they feel, in their own choices, why a willing counterparty can be bad news when the prior is public, the game is zero-sum, and rationality is as public as the facilitator can make it. Participants should run into the pull to trade on private information, then into the information revealed by bids, accepts, refusals, and silence, before the facilitator names the result.

The activity should center on a novel but simple market you invent: an asset whose value is the sum of hidden components, an explicit public prior written on the board, private signals that are informative but incomplete, and a market rule under which every offer is itself evidence. Make the mechanism crisp enough to run with index cards, tokens, a whiteboard ledger, and a visible countdown clock. Avoid real money; use points that convert to a small grade-neutral privilege or chocolate-free prize so incentives matter without creating resentment. Include a way to make rationality ceremonially common knowledge at the start, and say honestly why ceremony cannot fully manufacture the idealized condition.

Specify: setup and materials; exact rules in numbered form; how students are paired or grouped; what is public and what is private at each moment; the payoff formula with two worked numerical examples; the timeline for a 75-minute session; at least three rounds with escalating publicity of information; a record sheet; and a stopping rule. Include one variation for a quiet class that will not talk and one for a class that tries to collude. Build in a trap round in which the obvious profitable-looking trade is available only because someone else knows why it is being offered.

The debrief should map concrete moments from the game onto the ideas: common prior, private signal, adverse selection, updating from another agent’s willingness to transact, the difference between I have information and the market has already priced the fact that I would act on it, and the boundary between speculative trade and trades motivated by liquidity, risk-sharing, deadlines, or heterogeneous needs. State which misconception will appear first, which will appear only after the first loss, and which will survive until the debrief. Predict the three most likely ways the activity goes wrong the first time and give a repair for each. End with a frank paragraph on which feature of no-trade reasoning the game cannot reproduce, and give one five-minute written exit question that tests whether students understood the epistemic point rather than merely learning that trading felt risky.
