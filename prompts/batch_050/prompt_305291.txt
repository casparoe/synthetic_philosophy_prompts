I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, and I got into an argument with a coworker after I bragged about my setup for not blowing money on cloud GPUs: I have a script that auto-shuts-down my instances and I gave the admin credentials to my brother with instructions not to hand them over before the end of the month no matter what I say. He said what I did is literally irrational and then sent me this argument he wrote up. It bothers me because by the same logic constitutions are irrational too (he says yes they are), which seems like proving too much. Here it is:

(1) A rational agent, at any given moment, does whatever seems best to them at that moment given the evidence they have at that moment. Acting against your own current best judgment is just what irrationality is.

(2) A precommitment device only does any work if it makes you do something at the later time that you would not, at that later time, choose to do on your own. If you'd do it anyway, the device is dead weight -- a lock on a door nobody was going to open.

(3) So any precommitment device that actually works makes you act, at the later time, against what you then judge best.

(4) By (1), acting at the later time against what you then judge best is irrational at that time.

(5) So every functioning precommitment device guarantees a future irrational act.

(6) No rational agent should set up a mechanism whose whole point is to guarantee a future irrational act. So rational agents never precommit: no Ulysses contracts, no credential hand-overs, no constitutional entrenchment clauses that are deliberately hard to amend.

The conclusion has to be wrong somewhere, because literally everything is a precommitment device if you squint (contracts, deadlines, wedding vows, alarm clocks on the other side of the room, the fact that git makes rewriting public history annoying). But I can't find the step. My instinct is (2) is doing something sneaky -- like, my script doesn't wrestle the keyboard away from me, it changes what my options cost, so maybe future-me does freely choose the right thing? But then Ulysses tied to the actual mast isn't covered by that fix, and I want that covered too. If I have to give something up I'd rather drop (6) than (1), because (1) feels like it's just the definition of being rational. Walk me through which step to reject, why it looked obviously fine, and what it costs to reject each of the other candidates instead.
