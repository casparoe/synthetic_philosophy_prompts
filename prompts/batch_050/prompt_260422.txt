I'm a graduate student in bioethics, and I recently got into an argument with a friend who studies criminal law about what "valid consent" even amounts to, because we realized that the institutions we each work with seem to operate on radically different assumptions. Consider the patchwork: in medicine, informed consent can be invalidated by a doctor's failure to disclose a significant risk, and even a competent adult's consent counts for little if they weren't told what they were consenting to; in academic psychology, by contrast, institutional review boards routinely approve studies that actively deceive participants about the experiment's purpose, on the condition that they be debriefed afterward. In contract law, a hard bargain struck under severe economic pressure -- signing an unfavorable lease because you have nowhere else to live -- generally stands, while in criminal law a guilty plea induced by a prosecutor's threat to pursue a much harsher sentence at trial is treated as voluntary. Meanwhile, in the sexual context, many people hold that consent obtained through certain deceptions (about identity, say, or about being single) is morally void, even though deceptions of comparable or greater magnitude elsewhere in life rarely void anything.

What theory of valid consent does this patchwork presuppose? In your answer, please address the following. First, for each of the domains above, reconstruct the implicit account: does the practice treat consent as a mental state (informed, unpressured intention), as a public performance (a token of agreement that creates permissions regardless of inner states), or as something whose validity depends on the fairness of the surrounding conditions? Second, say which threats to validity -- deception, nondisclosure, coercion, background pressure, impaired competence -- each practice treats as defeasible and which it treats as disqualifying, and ask whether those differences track anything principled (the stakes of the transaction, the vulnerability of the consenting party, the institutional purposes at play) or whether the patchwork is just historically contingent and mutually inconsistent. Third, distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with: could, for instance, a purely performative theory of consent explain why medicine demands disclosure, or does that requirement force a mental-state component? Finally, pick one domain and say concretely what would have to change in its practices if it adopted the theory of consent implicit in one of the others.
