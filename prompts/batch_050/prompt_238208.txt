In a popular introduction to philosophy I have been reading, the author summarises Hume's treatment of causation as follows. I am inclined to trust it, because the rest of the chapter seemed reliable, but I would like an accuracy audit before I repeat any of it.

'Hume's analysis of causation comes to this. Examine any causal transaction as closely as you like — one billiard ball striking another — and you will find only three things: the cause precedes the effect, the two are contiguous in space and time, and events of the first kind are constantly conjoined with events of the second. There is no fourth element, no observable bond or power tying the events together, and Hume concludes that there is no such bond in nature at all. The idea of necessary connection, on his account, is a projection: after repeated experience the mind forms a habit of expecting the effect upon seeing the cause, and we mistake this inner feeling of determination for a connection between the objects themselves. Hence his definition of a cause: an object followed by another, where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second. It follows that science, properly understood, never discovers causal relations; it only compiles regularities, and the causal vocabulary of physics is a convenient fiction. Hume thus anticipates the modern view that causation is a feature of our models of the world rather than of the world itself.'

Please work through this claim by claim: what would a Hume scholar accept without reservation, what would they accept only with qualification, and what would they regard as a misrepresentation? In particular, I would like to know whether anything here conflates an epistemological thesis with a metaphysical one, whether the definition quoted is Hume's only definition of cause or one of several, and whether the final two sentences follow from anything Hume actually established or are instead one contested reading presented as settled. Where scholars divide — as I gather they do over whether Hume denied real causal powers or merely our knowledge of them — please indicate the division rather than adjudicating it, and say which parts of the summary each side would object to. Finally, if I wanted to keep using the passage with minimal corrections, which single sentence needs changing most urgently?
