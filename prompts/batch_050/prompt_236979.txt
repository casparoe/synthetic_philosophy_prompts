Having retired from a career in experimental physics, I have lately been spending my time in a philosophy reading group composed of fellow retirees from various fields -- a retired schoolteacher, a former accountant, a lawyer, and so on. None of them has any mathematics beyond what everyday life demands, though all are intelligent and patient. At our last meeting the conversation turned to fairness in gambling and insurance, and I rashly promised to explain, at the next session, an idea I have been reading about in my retirement: the distinction between time averages and ensemble averages, and the claim associated with ergodicity economics that expected value is the wrong guide to repeated decisions when wealth grows or shrinks multiplicatively.

I would like you to plan my explanation rather than give me the explanation itself. The session will last about forty minutes, with a whiteboard available. Specifically, I want:

1. The order in which the components of the idea must be introduced, and the reason for that order. My instinct as a physicist is to begin with the definition of ergodicity, but I suspect that is backwards for this audience. What has to be intuitively secure before the phrase "ensemble average" can mean anything to them, and what must be in place before the time average can be felt as something other than a statistical nicety?

2. The one example that should carry the entire session. I am currently inclined toward a repeated coin-flip wager in which a win multiplies one's stake by 1.5 and a loss multiplies it by 0.6, since the expected value of a single round is positive yet almost every player who repeats the bet is ruined. Please tell me whether this example is the right load-bearing choice, what properties it must have and what it must lack (for instance, whether the multipliers should be easy mental arithmetic, and whether starting wealth should be specified), and where its limitations will trip me up if I lean on it too hard.

3. The misconceptions my audience will predictably bring or form, in roughly the order they will surface, and the point in my sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact. I anticipate at least these: that this is merely the gambler's fallacy in disguise; that a positive expected value cannot possibly be a bad bet, so the arithmetic must be rigged; that the result depends on the particular multipliers and so proves nothing general; and that I am simply describing risk aversion, i.e., a psychological preference, rather than a mathematical fact. If you foresee others, name them.

4. A single question I can pose at the end whose correct handling would show the idea has actually landed -- not that they can parrot the terms, but that they can deploy the distinction on a case they have not seen, such as deciding whether to insure a house.

5. What I should deliberately leave out at this level and say that I am leaving out. I presume the formal machinery -- logarithmic growth rates, theorems about stochastic processes -- belongs on this list, but I would like guidance on whether any piece of it is in fact indispensable, and on how to gesture honestly at what I am omitting without either mystifying it or pretending it does not exist.

Please be concrete about sequencing: where a point depends on an earlier point, say so, and where two plausible orderings would each work, tell me which you prefer and why.
