At a journal club last week, a colleague who works on decision theory offered the following summary of the Kelly criterion, which I'd only ever encountered secondhand. I've been turning it over since, because it sounds mostly right but ends in a place that seems stronger than anything mathematics could establish on its own. Here is his summary, as best I remember it:

"The Kelly criterion tells you to bet, on each gamble, the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes the expected logarithm of your post-bet wealth. This isn't one rule of thumb among many: it's provably optimal. A bettor who follows Kelly will, over time, end up richer than any bettor following any other fixed-fraction strategy, and betting more than the Kelly fraction is the only way to ruin yourself in the long run. The deep reason Kelly is correct is that it maximizes geometric rather than arithmetic growth, and geometric growth is what actually governs an individual's fate, because you only ever live through one sequence of outcomes — you don't get to average across parallel possible worlds. So the naive strategy of maximizing expected wealth is actually the irrational one; it optimizes for an ensemble average that no real person ever receives. It follows that logarithmic utility is the uniquely correct utility function, built into the structure of repeated multiplicative betting, and anyone who deviates from Kelly — say, by betting half of Kelly out of caution, or full expected value out of aggression — is simply making a mistake, whether or not they realize it."

I suspect the opening claim about expected log wealth is fine, and I'm inclined to believe there's something to the point about living through one sequence rather than an ensemble. But the final move — from "Kelly maximizes long-run growth" to "log utility is the uniquely correct utility function and all deviations are mistakes" — feels like it smuggles in more than the math delivers, and I'm not sure the claims about ruin and about beating all other strategies are stated precisely enough to be true.

Could you audit this summary? Specifically: which parts would a careful proponent of Kelly betting accept as stated; which parts conflate distinct results (for instance, claims about asymptotic growth rates versus claims about what happens in any actual finite run, or about growth-rate optimality versus probability of ending up ahead); which parts overstate a modest mathematical fact; and where, if anywhere, does a substantive normative assumption about risk and utility enter, such that accepting the mathematics wouldn't force you to accept his conclusion? Finally: once the summary is corrected, does his closing verdict against fractional Kelly and expected-value maximizers survive, or does it depend on premises his own summary hasn't established?
