I'm a retired marine biologist who has fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole in my sixties, and I'm stuck on the so-called boundary problem in democratic theory, i.e. the question of who gets a say in a vote before the vote happens. As far as I can tell the debate has been stuck for decades. You've got the all-affected-interests people, the all-coerced people, the nationalists who say the demos is just a pre-political people, and everyone concedes that you can't settle the membership question democratically without already having decided who's voting, so there's this regress nobody escapes. All sides seem to know each other's arguments cold and nobody budges.

Here's my hypothesis, offered as a working biologist rather than a philosopher: this looks to me exactly like the species problem in my old field. Thirty-odd rival species concepts, each defensible, endless dispute, until people mostly accepted that "species" is a tool and the right delimiation depends on what job you're doing (conservation, phylogenetics, whatever). Maybe "the demos" is the same: the disagreement isn't about a fact, it's about what the concept of the people is *for*, and the sides are just answering different questions under one label.

So my ask: is that diagnosis right, or is there a real substantive disagreement underneath — about, I don't know, what makes political obligation binding, or what democracy is fundamentally for? Diagnose the impasse, don't survey the positions, and tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, an empirical finding, or just everyone admitting it's a choice of tools. If you think my species analogy misleads more than it illuminates, say so and say where it breaks.
