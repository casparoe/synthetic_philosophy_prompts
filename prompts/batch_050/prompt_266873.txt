I sit on a professional conduct committee that disciplines members for breaches of our code, and the relevant clause prohibits "deceiving clients" — it says nothing about lying specifically. Complaints keep arriving in which a member said something literally true but engineered a false impression: the financial adviser who, asked whether there are any fees, names only the annual fee while knowing the client is really asking about fees in general; the surveyor who writes that the roof was repaired in 2019, omitting that the leak recurred in 2021; the member who answers an awkward question by asserting a different, true proposition and letting the client draw the obvious conclusion. Draft an adjudication procedure the committee can actually apply: the questions to ask in order to classify a contested utterance (what was asserted, what was merely implied, whether the speaker reasonably expected the false inference, whether the client could easily have checked), the point at which classification must stop because the remaining question is one of moral judgment rather than linguistics, and an explicit stance on whether the committee should treat misleading as seriously as outright lying, given whatever reasons philosophers have offered for thinking the two morally differ or don't. Then run the procedure on the three cases above, and flag at least one case where it yields a verdict a complainant is likely to find counterintuitive, saying which step of the procedure is doing the work.
