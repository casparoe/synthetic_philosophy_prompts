A colleague of mine has firm intuitions about when a thing survives a change and when it does not. Her verdicts so far: a rowboat repaired piece by piece over twenty years until every plank, oar, and nail has been replaced is still the same boat; a rowboat sawn in half, with each half then built out with fresh lumber into a complete boat, is gone, and neither resulting boat is it; a jazz band whose founding members were gradually replaced one at a time is still the same band; a bronze statue melted down and recast as a candlestick no longer exists, though the bronze itself persists; a stream whose course has shifted gradually over centuries is still the same stream. Now consider a new case: a clinic scans your brain and body down to the last detail, painlessly destroys the original, and assembles a perfect replica on another continent from entirely new matter, with all of your memories, dispositions, and character intact. Would my colleague say the person who steps out of the machine is you? Please lay out at least two general rules about persistence that are each consistent with all five of her earlier verdicts, say what each rule predicts about the new case, give your best guess as to her actual verdict with reasons, and describe the further case that would most decisively tell the competing rules apart.
