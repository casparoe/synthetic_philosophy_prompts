A close friend of mine defends her dissertation next month on computational self-models, and over the past year our small reading group worked through Douglas Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop, since his account is the looser, more literary ancestor of the formal work she does. I want to give her a poem instead of a card. For the sake of whoever takes this on, let me restate the picture as I understand it: on Hofstadter's view the "I" is not an inner observer sitting behind the eyes but a symbol — a pattern in the brain that comes to refer to the whole system that contains it, roughly the way Gödel's coding let arithmetic make statements about itself. Because the symbol-level then seems to reach back down and steer the neural machinery that sustains it, the hierarchy of description folds into a strange loop: climb from neurons to symbols to selves and you arrive where you began. He also holds, more poignantly, that a person's pattern can persist at reduced fidelity in the minds of others, so that selves are less private than they feel.

Write me a sestina on this. The six end-words should be I, mirror, loop, name, signal, and pattern, and each should be made to work in at least two grammatical roles or senses over the course of the poem — the pronoun included, so let the referent of I wobble at least once. The stanzas should move through the levels the theory names: one close to the neural register, one at the level of symbols, one in which the symbol appears to reach down and drive the machinery, one brushing the Gödelian moment of a system grown rich enough to describe itself, and one on the coarse-grained copy of a person living on in someone else. The envoi should gather the end-words as sestinas do, but with one modification: its final line must run grammatically into the poem's first line, so the whole poem can be re-entered without a seam, the way Escher's staircases rejoin themselves. That re-entry should not feel like simple repetition; returning to the first line after the loop should feel like coming home to an origin that has quietly changed.

Two further requirements. First, accuracy: a reader who knows the book should recognize the view and not merely the vocabulary, and in particular no Cartesian ghost may slip back in — the speaker may ache for a substantial, indivisible self, but the poem must not grant one. Second, after the poem, add a prose note of no more than 150 words mapping each formal feature onto the piece of theory it enacts. I chose the sestina partly because its fixed permutation of end-words cycles every position and would, if extended another stanza, return to its starting arrangement, which struck me as the right skeleton for a strange loop; in your note, tell me whether the finished poem actually earns that analogy, and be honest about where accuracy lost out to music.
