I'm an economist at a regulatory agency drafting internal guidance on how to value non-market goods — clean air, quiet, historic streetscapes — in cost-benefit analysis. Before the guidance circulates, I want a consistency check on the positions I've built into it. I hold all of the following: (1) the willingness-to-accept/willingness-to-pay disparity is real, large, and mostly a bias — prediction errors and attachment effects — rather than a revelation of stable preferences; (2) when a policy removes something people currently hold, compensation should be assessed at willingness to accept, since that is what making them whole, as they see it, requires; (3) a preference identified as a bias rather than a genuine taste deserves no weight in welfare analysis — policy should correct for it, not cater to it; (4) automatic enrollment in retirement savings plans is justified precisely because it harnesses status quo bias and reference dependence to move people toward what they would choose on reflection; and (5) loss aversion, in the sense that losses loom larger than equivalent gains, is a robust psychological fact that underlies both the endowment effect and status quo bias. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and at what cost, and flag any place where one of my justifications quietly assumes something — for instance, a reference-independent true preference — that my other commitments rule out. Finally, recommend the single revision that leaves the most of the package intact.
