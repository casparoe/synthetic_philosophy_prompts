I'm TAing a second-year undergrad course on research methods in the social sciences, and I've got one 75-minute seminar to teach the so-called gender-equality paradox -- the finding that countries scoring higher on gender-equality measures tend to have *fewer* women going into STEM fields. I want to use it as the semester's big case study in how hard it is to interpret a cross-country correlation, because the students have spent ten weeks learning that correlations mislead and I want to see them actually sweat one.

Don't explain the paradox to me; plan how I should explain it. Specifically:

- The order the ideas have to come in. What do students need in their heads before the finding is even surprising to them? I'm assuming they need the distinction between formal/legal equality and actual outcomes in place first, plus a working sense of what a correlation across countries does and doesn't license -- but maybe there's something else that has to come before either of those, and I'd like you to argue for whatever order you pick rather than just listing it.
- The carrying example. I want one concrete comparison to anchor the whole session, something like Norway vs. a country with worse equality scores but a higher share of women engineers. Tell me what the pair has to have and what it has to avoid -- e.g., not two countries that differ in twenty other ways that will swallow the entire discussion, and not a pair so cherry-picked that a sharp student can correctly accuse me of rigging it.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually occur to smart 19-year-olds, and where in the sequence I should head each one off. My guesses: "this proves the career differences are biological," "this proves the equality indices are measuring the wrong thing," "it's just a statistical artifact," and "this shows feminism overshot." For each: where it comes from, wether it's a reasonable reading or a genuine mistake, and whether I should kill it on sight or let it breathe for a few minutes because wrestling with it teaches something the lecture can't.
- A check at the end that tells me whether they understood the interpretive problem rather than just learned a fun fact to repeat at parties. Something I can pose cold, not a recap question -- ideally a small new case where the right move is to apply the same caution, so I can see transfer rather than memory.
- What I should deliberately leave out at this level -- I'm thinking the replication fights and corrections around the original study, and the deeper debate about what the equality indices actually capture -- and how to flag that I'm leaving those out without making it sound like a cover-up.
