I direct clinical training at a psychotherapy institute, and I keep refereeing the same dispute in supervision. Many of our patients are religious, and trainees writing case formulations disagree about when it is legitimate to describe a patient's faith as wish-based or defensive. Some pathologize any comforting belief; others treat religious conviction as off-limits to formulation entirely. I want to settle this by operationalizing Freud's actual position in The Future of an Illusion, which is more careful than either faction assumes: Freud defines an illusion as a belief in whose motivation a wish-fulfillment plays a prominent role, explicitly sets aside whether the belief is true or false, notes that an illusion may even turn out to be true (his example is an ordinary girl's fantasy of marrying a prince), and takes the belief's own indifference to verification as a diagnostic marker rather than its unverifiability.

Please write a procedure my trainees can run on material from their sessions, five to eight steps in order, for deciding whether a patient's religious belief is functioning as an illusion in exactly this technical sense. The procedure must respect the two constraints built into the definition: it can never use the belief's falsity, implausibility, or unverifiability as evidence (the question is motivational, not epistemic), and a positive finding licenses nothing by itself — no intervention, no verdict on the doctrine's truth. For each step, specify what observable feature of the patient's talk counts as satisfying it (what does it look like, concretely, for a wish to be a prominent factor in motivation, or for a belief to set no store by verification?), and mark the steps where the trainee must exercise judgment rather than check a box. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a recently widowed patient who has become certain she will be reunited with her husband, changes the subject when the belief is discussed, and has stopped making plans because she will see him soon anyway; a patient whose adult conversion followed years of reading arguments on both sides, who reports that the belief consoles him but still allows he might be wrong; and the trainee's own conviction that any patient can be helped if the therapeutic alliance is strong enough — a case I expect the procedure to flag, uncomfortably, since it is secular and clinically convenient. Close with what the procedure cannot do, including whether a finding of illusion says anything about whether the belief helps or harms the patient.
