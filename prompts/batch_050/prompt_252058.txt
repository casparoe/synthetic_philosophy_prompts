In my AI safety reading group, discussion of the shutdown problem has been stuck for months. Everyone agrees on the standard setup: an agent maximizing a fixed objective has an incentive to prevent its own shutdown, since a shut-down agent cannot achieve much of anything; and everyone knows the standard responses -- utility-indifference schemes, carefully designed shutdown utilities, interruptibility constraints -- along with the familiar counterexamples showing that each either fails under self-modification, incentivizes the agent to engineer its own shutdown, or quietly smuggles the original incentive back in. The group splits into two camps that never engage: one says the problem is a technical gap that the right formal construction will eventually close, the other says corrigibility is constitutively impossible for any agent with a stable objective and we should instead design agents whose objectives are themselves revisable by us. My hypothesis is that this is not really a dispute about technical feasibility at all: the two camps disagree about what corrigibility is -- a property of an agent's incentive structure, which could in principle be engineered, or a relation between the agent and its operators that no internal incentive design could secure, since the agent that truly controls whether it can be corrected is by definition not corrigible. Diagnose the disagreement: is it substantive or verbal, is a hidden formal question (e.g., whether any utility function is provably safe under arbitrary self-modification) doing the real work, and given your diagnosis, what -- a theorem, a distinction, a change in how the problem is posed -- would count as progress, if anything?
