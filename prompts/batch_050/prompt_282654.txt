The exam question was: "In our unit on wu-wei we distinguished two versions of the paradox of trying not to try: the goal version (if wu-wei is something you deliberately aim at, the aiming itself defeats it) and the effort version (any effort toward effortlessness is itself effort, so wu-wei seemingly can't be cultivated). For each version, explain whether the Zhuangzi's skill stories — Cook Ding, the cicada catcher, the ferryman — dissolve it, sidestep it, or leave it standing. Then state one respect in which the Confucian and Daoist pictures of how wu-wei is reached differ."

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Wu-wei is effortless, unforced action, acting in step with things rather than against them, and it is not mere passivity or doing nothing — Cook Ding's whole point is that he is intensely active, just without friction. The paradox is that this state can't be forced. On the effort version: the skill stories dissolve it, because all of Zhuangzi's masters trained for years. Cook Ding needed years before he stopped perceiving the ox as an ox and could meet it with spirit rather than eyesight. So effort and wu-wei are compatible — effort is a ladder you kick away, not a contradiction. And since the same stories handle the effort version, they dissolve the goal version too: if effort toward mastery is fine, then aiming at wu-wei as a goal must be fine, and the paradox turns out to be overblown. On the Confucian/Daoist difference: on the Confucian picture wu-wei arises more or less spontaneously once you are properly aligned with your nature, whereas on the Daoist picture it is the prize of long, deliberate discipline, as the skill stories show."
