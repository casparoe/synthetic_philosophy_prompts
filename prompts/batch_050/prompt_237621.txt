So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been working on a long feature about the sortition revival -- the idea that we should replace some or all elected offices with bodies of randomly selected ordinary citizens, like oversized juries. I've interviewed a bunch of advocates, sat in on a deliberative assembly pilot, read the ancient Athens history, and I'll admit I've kind of talked myself into the strong version of the position. Roughly: a standing citizens' chamber, members drawn by lottery, serving staggered single terms with real legislative power (not just advisory), would produce better and more legitimate policy than elected career politicians, because it kills the incentives that make elections go wrong (fundraising, party discipline, campaigning skill selecting for the wrong traits, reelection horizons) while getting you something close to a descriptively representative sample of the public. My editors loved the pitch. Now I have to write the piece, and I want a whole section on what would count against the view -- because right now my file of counterarguments is basically just "but isn't this anti-democratic?" which even I find lazy.

So: steelman my position if you think I've stated it badly, then map its exposure. What evidence, cases, or results would actually defeat it, and which would merely be awkward? I'm interested in the weeds: would it matter if sortition chambers turned out to be capturable by whoever sets the agenda and briefs the members, or is that just a design problem with a fix? Would the self-selection issue (the people who accept the invitation aren't a random sample of who was invited) undermine the whole representativeness claim or only dent it? Does the lack of accountability-to-voters actually do work, given that single terms and no reelection is supposed to be a feature? What about the expertise objection -- is there a finding about how much policy knowledge matters for good judgment that would sink the view, or is that objection overrated? And where does the comparison class matter: maybe sortition beats first-past-the-post legislatures but loses to some other reform, and maybe that should move me less than I think.

Also -- and this is the part I most need for the piece -- what would the honest advocates themselves concede counts against them, versus what they'd wave away? Every movement has objections it treats as load-bearing and ones it treats as bad faith; tell me which is which here. And end with a verdict: is the strong sortition position actually risking anything, or has it been insulated by qualifications (it's a pilot, the conditions weren't right, the powers weren't real) in a way that should make me suspicious of it? If the fair conclusion is that it's unfalsifiable as currently advocated, I'd rather print that than a puff piece, though it'll annoy my freinds who pushed me to write this.
