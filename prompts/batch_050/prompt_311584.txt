For an ethics seminar I'm putting together a handout on pacifism, and what I actually need is the checklist, not the taxonomy. Write the list of requirements a pacifist position has to meet to count as a worked-out ethical theory rather than a temperamental aversion to violence, with the case or consideration behind each one.

A few things I want the list to cover, though don't limit yourself to these:

- It must say what the prohibition is actually on -- war, killing, or violence full stop -- and it must survive the cases where those come apart: a cyberattack that kills nobody directly but predictably crashes hospital power grids, and a single targeted killing that plausibly averts a war. If the theory gives the same verdict on both, that tells me the prohibition was never really about war.
- It must take a stand on whether the ethics of self-defense scales. Lots of pacifists want to permit an individual fending off an attacker while condemning defensive wars, so the theory owes us a reason why a million attackers and a million defenders changes the moral facts, not just the bodycount.
- It must handle the cleanest possible case: a purely defensive war against an unambiguous aggressor, where every jus in bello and ad bellum condition the just war theorist wants is satisfied. Forbid it and own the consequence (the invasion proceeds); permit it and explain what's left of the pacifism.
- It must not secretly rest on empirical generalizations ("war never achieves its aims") that could be falsified without touching the normative core -- I want it stated upfront whether the position is absolute or contingent, since those fail in completely different ways.
- Newer one: it has to say something about wars with no human bodies at risk on one or both sides (uncrewed systems shooting at uncrewed systems). If the pacifist has nothing to say there, the objection was to killing persons and the "war" framing is decoration.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for anything deserving the name and which are just the preferences of a particular strand (religious, deontological, consequentialist). And if some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable -- I'm fairly sure the self-defense one, the absoluteness one, and the cleanest-case one can't all be held together -- say so explicitly, since that's the interesting part. Skip the bit where you score historical pacifists against the list, I'll do that with the class.
