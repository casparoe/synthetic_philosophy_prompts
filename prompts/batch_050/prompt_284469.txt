So I got into a dumb argument at a family dinner that has now eaten two weeks of my life. My brother-in-law is one of those guys who watches history documentaries constantly, and he was going on about how "the Renaissance" changed everything, and I said something like, you know nobody at the time called it that, right, the whole thing is a label invented later by historians, same with "the Middle Ages" and probably "the Scientific Revolution" too. I was mostly bluffing but then I actually went and looked some stuff up, and now I've talked myself into a real position: these period names are basically marketing. Petrarch, Michelangelo, Galileo never woke up thinking "ah, another day in the Renaissance." Continuity everywhere, and the cuts are made afterwards for textbooks and museum gift shops. The killer bit is that I found the guy who basically invented the Renaissance-as-a-thing, Jacob Burckhardt, and in the introduction to The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy he literally admits it:

"It is the most serious difficulty of the history of civilization that a great intellectual process must be broken up into single, and often into what seem arbitrary categories in order to be in any way intelligible."

Which reads to me like a confession. The man who built the category says the categories are arbitrary. My brother-in-law's comeback was that plenty of things are real even though the boundaries are conventional, like a coastline is fuzzy but the ocean is still real, which annoyed me because its actually kind of good.

So here's what I want: what would count AGAINST my position? Don't try to just rebut me -- I want a map of what I'd have to be wrong about. Like, what kinds of evidence or arguments would show that "the Renaissance" (or any period) tracks something real rather than being a retrospective construction, sorted by how decisive they'd be? Would it matter if people at the time did have some self-conception of a break, even without our word for it? Would statistical discontinuities count (if, say, the rate of some change actually kinked around 1450), or would I be right to say you can manufacture a kink for any boundary you pick? Also be honest about the reverse: what would defenders of real periods themselves concede as damaging to THEIR view, not just what skeptics throw at them. And the thing I most want checked is whether my position has quietly become unfalsifiable -- because I notice that whenever someone gives me evidence people felt a new era beginning, I go "well, that's just the construction working on them too," which is starting to feel like a trick. If my view risks nothing, tell me, and tell me whether that's actually a defect for a view about historiography or just what being right feels like from the inside. What would personally move me most, I think, is a case where a period boundary was drawn for one reason and then turned out to be confirmed by totally independent evidence nobody drew it for -- if that exists, or if even that wouldn't count, say why.
