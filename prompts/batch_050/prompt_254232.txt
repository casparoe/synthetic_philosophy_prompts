I'm facing two financial decisions this quarter, and a friend who's generally sharper than I am about this stuff insists my picks contradict each other. I've been reading rationalist blogs for years, so I know the drill — shut up and multiply, don't trust gut feelings over arithmetic — but when I actually run the arithmetic I still can't tell which of my instincts is the error. I'd like help thinking it through.

First decision: my small company is being acquired, and the buyer offered me two ways to structure my payout. Option one is a flat $3 million, guaranteed, wired at closing. Option two is an earnout tied to a regulatory approval: if it goes through in the most likely form, I get the same $3 million (their lawyers and mine both put this at about an 89% chance); if it goes through with the expanded scope we're hoping for, I get $15 million (about a 10% chance); and there's a residual 1% chance the whole deal collapses and I walk away with nothing. I chose the flat $3 million. The certain option lets me pay off my house and never work again, and the 1% blowup scenario genuinely terrified me.

Second decision: an unrelated investment syndicate I'm part of is dissolving, and each member can take their residual stake in one of two lottery-like instruments (long story involving how the fund's remaining assets are structured). Ticket one pays $3 million with probability 11% and nothing otherwise. Ticket two pays $15 million with probability 10% and nothing otherwise. I chose ticket two without much hesitation — the odds are nearly identical, so why not take the shot at the bigger prize?

My friend says these two choices can't both be right: that if you lay out the payoff tables, the second decision is just the first one with a large chunk of probability mass subtracted uniformly from every branch, so whatever principle justified my caution in the first case should have justified it in the second, or vice versa. I can sort of see the structure he's pointing at, but both of my choices still feel obviously correct when I consider them one at a time.

My questions: Is he right that I'm committed to an inconsistency, and if so, what's the cleanest way to see exactly where the contradiction lives? If I have to revise one of the two choices, is there a principled method for deciding which intuition — my risk aversion in the first case or my shrug-at-near-equal-odds in the second — deserves to win? And does it matter that the first decision involved my entire financial future while the second involved money I already thought of as house money, or is that just a framing effect I should ignore?
