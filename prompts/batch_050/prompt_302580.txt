So I've landed on the view that Buridan's ass is basically a pseudo-problem. My position: when two options are exactly tied in value, rationality just permits picking either one arbitrarily, and there's nothing deeper to say. The ass starves because it's bad at being rational, not because rationality has some gap in it. Choosing is what you do when you have a reason; picking is what you do when you don't, and picking needs no justification beyond "standing there starving would be worse than either bale."

What would count against this? I want the defeaters mapped, not just listed.

A few things I've been chewing on that I'd want addressed:

- Adversarial ties. Suppose someone knows my decision procedure and deliberatly engineers ties to control me. If my policy is just "pick either," can an adversary steer me by controlling which option becomes salient first, or which one I happen to encounter? Does the view survive that, or does surviving it require importing some kind of randomization device -- and if it does, isn't that a concession that "just pick" wasn't the whole story?

- Do true ties even exist? There's a line of thought that what we call ties are usually something else -- two options that aren't better, worse, or equal, but just "on a par." And there's that small-improvement worry: if A and B were truly equal, then adding a tiny scrap of value to A should make it strictly better and the choice obvious; if it doesn't, maybe they were never equal to begin with. Does that argument sink the tie picture my view depends on? Would a committed defender bite the bullet and say equality is real but hard to detect, or does this force a different account of what picking even is?

- Diachronic stuff. If I pick arbitrarily today, am I rationally committed to treating that pick as a settled preference tomorrow? People clearly do treat their arbitrary picks as meaningful afterwards -- is that just a psychological quirk, or is it evidence that picking covertly generates reasons, which my view says can't happen?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed defender of the picking view would themselves concede it as a problem or dismiss it as changing the subject. I want the exposure map from the inside, not just what critics would throw at it.

And give me an honest verdict on this: is "just pick" a position that risks anything at all? It seems like any counterexample can be neutralized by saying "well then the options weren't really tied." If that move is always available, is the view impressively robust or just empty?

Where I'm coming from, if it helps: the thing that would most persuade me is a realistic case -- not identical bales of hay -- where two options are plausibly exactly tied and picking arbitrarily still seems like the wrong thing to do. If no such case can be constructed without smuggling in a hidden asymmetry, that tells me something too.
