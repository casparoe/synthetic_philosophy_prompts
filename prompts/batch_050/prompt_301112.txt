I serve on the model review committee at a mid-sized consumer lender. Every credit model must be cleared by us before deployment, and we try to keep our reasoning consistent across decisions. Here is our track record so far:

- Model 1: a default-risk model in which two applicant groups had nearly identical base repayment rates. The model was well calibrated within each group (among applicants assigned a given risk score, actual default rates matched the score in both groups) and had nearly equal false positive rates (qualified applicants wrongly flagged as likely defaulters) across groups. Approved.
- Model 2: a model that was miscalibrated within one group — it systematically overestimated default risk for that group relative to observed outcomes — and also had unequal false positive rates across groups. Rejected.
- Model 3: a model that achieved equal approval rates across groups by applying different score thresholds to different groups, with the result that it was both miscalibrated within groups and had unequal error rates. Rejected, despite the vendor's argument that equal approval rates is the fairness criterion regulators care most about.
- Model 4: a model with substantially unequal approval rates across groups, but well calibrated within each group and with equal false positive and false negative rates across groups. Approved over one member's dissent that equal approval rates should be required.

Now we face Model 5. The underlying base repayment rates genuinely differ between the two applicant groups in our portfolio — not because of any defect in the data or the model, as far as our audits can tell. The model is well calibrated within each group. Because of the difference in base rates, however, its false positive rate is higher for the group with the lower base repayment rate. Our in-house statistician reminds us of a known impossibility result: except in degenerate cases, when base rates differ, no non-trivial model can be simultaneously calibrated within each group and have equal error rates across groups, so this trade-off cannot be engineered away.

I want a prediction, not a survey of options. Tell me, first, which two or more decision rules are each consistent with all four of our prior verdicts, and how you know the committee is not simply running a requirement of equal approval rates. Second, commit to a prediction of how the committee will decide Model 5, with reasons — including which prior decision is the closest analog and what it suggests about which rule the committee is actually, perhaps implicitly, running. Third, since the prior verdicts may not fully determine the answer, describe a single hypothetical future model whose approval or rejection would most cleanly reveal which rule the committee is following. If you believe the track record genuinely underdetermines the Model 5 decision, say so plainly, but still state which outcome you would bet on and why.
