I work as the resilience officer for a mid-sized coastal city, and every year my office has to divide a fixed preparedness budget across a handful of threats — flooding, wildfire smoke, pandemic, a cyberattack on the water utility — before the council signs off in March. The recurring problem is that our expert reports don't give us usable probabilities. For storm surge we have convergent models; for the engineered-pathogen scenario the estimates from equally credentialed people span four orders of magnitude and the modeling assumptions are openly contested. Last cycle the council's loudest member insisted we just multiply the harms by our best-guess probabilities and fund the highest expected value, while our fire chief kept arguing we should secure against the worst credible outcome full stop. Neither position is crazy, and I realized my office has no principled way to decide *which* decision rule governs *which* allocation — we just oscillate depending on who's in the room.

I'd like a written decision procedure my office can actually run, annually, on each candidate allocation, grounded in the Rawls-versus-Harsanyi dispute about choice under ignorance rather than just gesturing at it. Rawls held that maximin is rational when three conditions jointly obtain: you lack any reasonable basis for assigning probabilities, you care little for gains above the minimum you could secure, and some options risk outcomes you find unacceptable. Harsanyi held that under genuine ignorance you should assign equal or best-guess probabilities and maximize expected utility, and that maximin smuggles in extreme risk aversion. My office has no decision theorists, roughly two hours of deliberation per allocation, and a city auditor who needs to see the reasoning afterwards. So: give me the ordered questions we should ask about each allocation to determine whether Rawls's conditions genuinely hold for it, and — this is the part I can't get from reading Rawls — what would count operationally as a *sufficient* answer to each. In particular, translate "no reasonable basis for probabilities" into something checkable (does expert disagreement across orders of magnitude qualify? where's the line between disputed probabilities and no probabilities?), and translate "care little for further gains" and "unacceptable outcomes" into judgments a non-philosopher can defend to an auditor. Specify what happens when some conditions hold and others don't, where minimax regret should enter if at all, and where the procedure must stop and hand the decision back to the council as a value choice rather than a technical one.

Two things I want you to be definite about rather than even-handed. First, the procedure must commit to a default rule for the hard middle cases — where the probability basis is contested but not absent — and you must argue for that default, since a menu of options just relocates our oscillation. Second, there's an obvious regress lurking here: choosing *which rule* to use under ignorance is itself a decision under ignorance, and I want you to break that regress at a specific, defensible point and say why stopping there isn't arbitrary. Then run the finished procedure on two real allocations from our budget: raising the storm-surge barrier versus subsidizing household flood insurance (convergent models, modest stakes per error), and stockpiling antivirals against the engineered-pathogen scenario versus spending the same money on ventilation upgrades that pay off under ordinary flu (contested probabilities, catastrophic tail). At least one of these should produce a recommendation my council will dislike; don't soften it. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — which disagreements in my office it will fail to settle because they were never really about the decision rule.
