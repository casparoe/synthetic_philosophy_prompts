I'm helping plan a one-semester graduate working group (mixed philosophy and linguistics students) on generics, and I've been asked to draft the syllabus around open problems rather than around the standard survey of views. The classic data are familiar enough: "ducks lay eggs" is fine even though only the females do it, "mosquitoes carry the West Nile virus" seems true even though only a small fraction of mosquitoes do, and yet "books are paperbacks" sounds false even though most books are. Everyone in the group knows that much. What I want from you is a defended prioritization of where the live action actually is. Which three or four open questions about generics should a semester be organized around, in what order, and why?

For each question, tell me what makes it load-bearing -- i.e. how many neighboring debates (in formal semantics, developmental psych, social philosophy) depend on how it comes out -- versus which famous-sounding questions are actually overrated or mostly terminological at this point. I'm also curious how you weigh a couple of tensions I keep running into: whether the semantics of generics really needs to be settled before the psychology (do people essentialize because of the language, or does the language reflect an essentialist bias?), and whether the recent interest in how AI systems generate and repeat generic claims about social groups is a genuinely new research question or just the old stereotyping literature with worse methods. Give me your criteria for the ranking explicitly, and note what empirical results or formal developments over the next few years would move a question up or down your list. I'd rather have a strong opinionated agenda I can argue with than a balanced menu.
