At our local rationalist meetup last night two people got into a genuinely heated argument about Kavka's toxin puzzle and it's still going in the discord. For those who need a refresher: a billionaire will pay you a fortune if at midnight tonight you sincerely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make you miserably ill for a day but not kill or permanently harm you. He has a brain scanner that detects your actual intention at midnight. You keep the money no matter what you do tomorrow; whether you drink the toxin is irrelevant to the payout.

So here's the argument, roughly as it went down.

(1) Anna's opening move was that the puzzle shows you can't just intend something on command for instrumental reasons. Come tomorrow you'll have every reason not to drink, you know this now, so you can't genuinely form the intention tonight, and that's not a failure of willpower -- it's that intentions aren't responsive to incentives the way actions are. Even a perfectly rational person loses the money.

(2) Ben replied that this conflates human psychology with rationality. An ideal updateless agent absolutely gets the money: from the outside the billionaire's scanner is just a predictor, and the policy that wins is "be the kind of agent that drinks the toxin." He said the puzzle tells us something mildly interesting about the limitations of human intention-formation and nothing deep about rationality itself, same way Newcomb tells us nothing deep about physics.

(3) Anna said that's smuggling in a precommitment device and dodging the actual question. Kavka asked whether a person like us can form the intention, not whether a being hardwired to act on its own policies can be rewarded for it. If your answer requires being a different kind of agent than the one in the thought experiment, you've answered a different puzzle.

(4) Ben then tried a different move: ok, forget ideal agents, consider a plain human who tonight signs a legally binding contract with a third party -- penalty for not drinking tomorrow is way worse than one day of illness, enforcement is certain. Having signed, they genuinely, sincerely intend to drink at midnight, because at midnight drinking really is what's in their interest. So incentive-driven intention formation is possible after all, no self-modification required.

(5) Anna said the contract move dissolves the puzzle by changing it, not by solving it. The whole bite of the original setup is that at midnight you have no reason whatsoever to drink tomorrow, and the contract gives you one. You've shown intentions track reasons-to-act when those exist, which nobody doubted.

(6) Ben's counter was that this proves too much -- by that standard nobody ever forms intentions "for incentives," since signing any contract is just arranging your incentives. The rational agent wins the money by engineering tomorrow's reasons tonight, and the puzzle was never about whether you can want to drink poison for no reason, it was about whether the causal chain from tonight's decision to midnight's intention can run through your future incentives, which it clearly can.

They both asked me who's right and I genuinely can't tell anymore. Can you go through the six moves and say who's right at each step, and which specific point each of them is getting wrong? Also: is there something both of them are missing -- like, does it matter how the scanner works, or whether "intending to drink knowing you won't" is even conceptually coherent, rather than just psychologically hard?
