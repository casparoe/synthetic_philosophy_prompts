Here is my setup, stated without quoting any text. Locke holds that a person at one time is the same person as a being at an earlier time just in case the later person's consciousness can extend backward to the earlier one's actions — that is, roughly, personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness, cashed out through memory. Reid objects with his brave officer case: as a boy, a man is flogged at school; as a young officer, he takes a standard in his first campaign and still remembers the flogging; as an elderly general, he remembers taking the standard but has forgotten the flogging. Reid argues that Locke's criterion makes the officer identical to the boy and the general identical to the officer, yet the general not identical to the boy, violating the transitivity of identity. Defenders of Locke typically reply in one of two ways: either they distinguish the criterion of personal identity from our evidence for it, or they revise the view so that identity requires an overlapping chain of direct memories rather than memory of the original episode.

Show how the sub-probabilities combine into your final number, identify the sub-question to which your answer is most sensitive, and state what consideration or argument would move your credence the most.
