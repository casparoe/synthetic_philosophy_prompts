Last weekend I spent the better part of a dinner arguing with an old friend who had just returned from a year teaching abroad. Her position: everyday conventions like tipping, queueing, and jaywalking rules are local coordination devices with no moral weight of their own, so a visitor who finds them irrational may ignore them in good conscience. Mine: that can't be right across the board, since at least some of these conventions distribute real burdens, and opting out as a guest is freeloading on the locals' compliance. We never resolved it, and now the argument has become professionally relevant.

I sit on the conduct committee of my university's study-abroad consortium. We have a live case. One of our students, placed at a partner university in a country where tipping is considered insulting and queueing is strictly observed, repeatedly tipped service staff after being asked not to, and was cited twice by local authorities for crossing mid-block in a district where jaywalking is both illegal and socially censured because drivers are not trained to expect it. The partner institution has formally asked us whether our pre-departure code of conduct treats such conduct as a violation; under our agreement, if it does, we must decide what consequences attach, and if it doesn't, we owe them a principled explanation of why not. Our current code says only that students must obey local law and act with cultural respect, which the committee reads three different ways.

Please draft the decision memo I will bring to the committee. It should:

1. Recommend a concrete disposition of this student's case and state what general rule our code should adopt going forward -- something like one of the following, though you may propose a better candidate: (a) conventions bind visitors only where deviation imposes uncompensated costs on others; (b) conventions bind visitors categorically, as a condition of being a guest; (c) conventions bind only insofar as local compliance is near-universal, on the theory that a convention's obligatoriness derives from the expectations people have actually built around it.

2. Distinguish carefully between conventions that are mere coordination devices (which side of the road to walk on), conventions that load costs onto specific third parties (queue-jumping; tipping regimes where wages are set assuming tips), and conventions whose violation is risky mainly because others have been trained not to expect it (jaywalking in different traffic cultures) -- and say whether the student's tipping, which offended but arguably harmed no one, belongs to any of these categories or a fourth.

3. Engage honestly with my friend's strongest point: that treating every local convention as binding commits the visitor to endorsing conventions she reasonably judges irrational or even mildly unjust, and that guests have more latitude, not less, to deviate precisely because their non-membership exempts them from the reciprocity that obligates locals. If the memo rejects this, it should say what is wrong with it rather than simply asserting the etiquette default.

4. Note what the memo's rule would imply for two hypothetical cases the committee will inevitably ask about: a student in a country where tipping is insulting who tips anyway out of home-country habit, versus a local-culture visitor to the United States who refuses to tip on principle -- and whether the asymmetry, if any, can be defended without special pleading.

Aim for roughly 800-1,000 words, in the register of a committee memo that a dean could sign off on.
