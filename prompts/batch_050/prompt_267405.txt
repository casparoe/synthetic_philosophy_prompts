Longtime LW/EA reader here, which probably shows. I'm trying to write down my rescue-ethics position without ending up with a pile of mutually inconsistent slogans. My current package is:

1. In Taurek-style cases with strangers and no special obligations, where I can save either five people from death or one different person from death, I ought to save the five. A fair coin between the two groups seems morally wrong, not just optional.

2. Taurek is still right about separateness in one important respect: burdens and benefits don't form one giant soup. In particular, no number of mild headaches distributed across different people can outweigh one person's death or paralysis. I call this my "floor-and-tally" view: sort claims into rough severity tiers, never aggregate across a tier boundary, but tally claims within the same tier.

3. Within the death-or-comparable-catastrophe tier, expected numbers count. So a 10% chance of saving 100 strangers can justify passing up a certainty of saving one stranger, assuming the probabilities are genuine and everyone is otherwise similarly situated.

4. Nevertheless, when claims are equally strong, a public rescue procedure should normally give each claimant some nonzero chance. A weighted lottery favoring the larger group seems fairer than simply deleting the minority from consideration.

5. Private agents may favor their own child over two strangers because special obligations can outweigh the bare numerical difference. But firefighters, triage officers, governments, and other role-holders should not import their private attachments into official decisions.

6. Institutions should be designed by their expected results across many cases. So an emergency service could rightly adopt a save-the-most-lives policy even if, in an isolated one-off case, a weighted lottery would be the fairest procedure.

7. Statistical lives count as much as identified lives. The identifiable-victim effect is a bias, not a source of moral reasons. Thus cost-effective public-health spending can properly beat a highly salient rescue of one named person, provided the stakes fall in the same claim tier.

Does this actually hang together? Audit it rather than just summarizing the literature. Identify every real tension, ordered from most to least severe, and distinguish them from tensions that only appear real. For each genuine conflict, say exactly which commitment has to be weakened or abandoned and what that revision would cost me. If my floor-and-tally idea can be repaired with a more precise tie-breaking or lottery rule, propose the least ad hoc repair. Don't assume I already know which plank I want to give up.
