THE QUESTION:
Hume is standardly read as offering a regularity theory of causation. (a) Reconstruct his positive account of what causal reasoning consists in for us, including the role played by the idea of necessary connection and where that idea comes from. (b) Hume gives two definitions of "cause" in the Enquiry. Give them (paraphrase is fine) and explain how they relate to each other. (c) So-called "New Hume" interpreters claim the standard reading gets him wrong. State their claim and assess whether it rescues Hume from the objection that his theory leaves out whatever makes the effect follow the cause.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:
For Hume, when we examine any particular instance of causation -- one billiard ball striking another, say -- we observe only contiguity in space, priority in time, and (over many cases) constant conjunction. We never perceive any power, force, or necessary connection in the objects themselves. Nonetheless we do have an idea of necessary connection, and Hume's official story is that this idea must be copied from some impression. Since no impression of sensation supplies it, Hume locates its source in an impression of reflection: after repeated experience of a conjunction, the mind is determined by custom to pass from the impression of one object to the idea of the other, and the felt determination of that transition is the impression from which the idea of necessary connection derives. So repetition is crucial because it finally lets us perceive the connection between the events that a single instance hides from us.

Hume then offers two definitions of cause. Roughly: a cause is an object followed by another, where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second; and a cause is an object followed by another, where the appearance of the first conveys the thought to the second. The first states the regularity condition, the second states the psychological condition, and since the regularity is exactly what produces the expectation, the two definitions are really just two ways of saying the same thing, one from the side of the objects and one from the side of the observer.

The standard reading takes all this to mean Hume deflates causation to mere regularity, which faces the objection that he leaves out whatever actually makes effects follow causes. New Hume interpreters like Galen Strawson argue Hume was really a causal realist: he believed there are objective necessary connections or powers in nature, and the regularity theory was only ever a claim about what we can know, not about what causation is. On this reading Hume's point is that science can eventually get past the regularities and uncover the hidden powers underlying them, so the deflationary reading rests on confusing his epistemology with his metaphysics. I find this plausible, since Hume often writes as if there are secret powers in objects, even while denying we have any idea of them.
