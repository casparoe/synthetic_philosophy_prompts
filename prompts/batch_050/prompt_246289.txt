In my philosophy of mind seminar this semester we have been working through the literature on the intentional stance: the question of when it is legitimate to attribute beliefs and desires to a system, rather than merely convenient to speak as if it had them. I have come to suspect that my own verdicts about particular cases do not cohere with any view I know how to state, and I would like you to do the reflective work of finding out whether a principle underlies them.

My verdicts, case by case:

The thermostat. When I say the thermostat thinks the hallway is too cold, this is loose talk and nothing more. It has no beliefs whatsoever.

The honeybee. A forager that returns and performs the waggle dance, leading her nestmates to a nectar source two hundred meters northeast: I am inclined to say she genuinely believes the nectar is there, and that her audience comes to believe it as well.

The chess engine. A top engine that declines a knight sacrifice after seconds of computation: I say it literally believes the sacrifice is unsound, and the fact that neither its programmers nor I could point to the internal structure that is this belief does not trouble me.

The weather model. A numerical forecasting system whose iterated computations issue in a prediction of rain tomorrow: here I dig in my heels. It does not believe it will rain. My colleagues point out that it is, like the chess engine, a predictive device whose inner workings resist decomposition into discrete representations, and I have no good answer to them yet.

The corporation. When we say that the firm knew its product was dangerous, though no single employee possessed all the relevant documents and some possessed none, I take this to be literally true. The belief, and later the knowledge, were properties of the organization.

The colony. An ant colony that has converged on the shorter of two paths to a food source: I want to say the colony has the information, perhaps even the belief, while no individual ant believes anything at all about where the food is.

The dog. The family dog waiting at the door each afternoon at six believes a walk is imminent. Straightforwardly yes, and I take this to be the least interesting case on the list.

The language model. A large language model that, asked whether it believes Paris is in France, answers that it does: I do not think its answer settles the question in either direction, and I find myself genuinely torn between saying that it believes a great deal and saying that belief requires something it lacks. This is the case I keep returning to.

Here is what I want from you. First, determine whether there is a principle — about internal representation, about the explanatory payoff of adopting the intentional stance, about proper function, about interpretability, or some principled combination — that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts. If you find candidates, state each precisely and say which cases motivate which clauses; do not present a disjunctive patchwork as though it were a unified theory. Second, if no principle saves every verdict, tell me plainly which verdict you would have me surrender and why it is the cheapest to lose. I suspect my intuitions are least stable in the pairing of the chess engine with the weather model, and that the corporation and the colony sit uneasily alongside the thermostat; confirm this or disabuse me of it. Third, apply your favored principle to cases I have not listed: a slime mold that reliably finds the shortest route through a maze, a patient with blindsight who sincerely denies seeing anything yet guesses correctly, and an immune system that has learned to recognize a pathogen. Finally, say what your principle implies about the language model, treating that implication as a verdict the theory must earn rather than a datum it must be forced to accommodate.

Please write your response in American English, at whatever length the argument requires.
