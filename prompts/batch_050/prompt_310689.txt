So I retired from forty years of radiation physics, got bored, fell down a philosophy hole, and somehow ended up hosting a monthly podcast for our local college's philosophy society (long story, involves too much free time). Next month's guest is a young philosopher who defends the pro-aggregation answer to that torture-versus-dust-specks thought experiment: one person tortured horribly for fifty years, versus an utterly astronomical number of people each getting a single dust speck in the eye, and she says the dust specks are the worse outcome. Coming from dosimetry, where a trivial dose spread over a huge population really does add up to real cancers, I'm halfway sympathetic -- but I want to actually press her, not just nod along.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. No softballs about how she got into philosophy. Specifically I want the set to cover:

1. The spectrum argument, since that's presumably her engine: between fifty years of torture and one dust speck there's a continuum of ever-milder pains, and any threshold seems arbitrary. When she says the continuum forces her conclusion, I want a follow-up about whether this is just the sorites paradox wearing a hat -- we accept vague boundaries everywhere else (baldness, heap, my old instruments' detection limits), so why does vagueness sink thresholds here.

2. Whether her aggregation works within one life as well as across lives: would she trade one person's entire lifetime of hourly dust specks (billions of them, all instantly forgotten) against one bad week for that same person? If she distinguishes the intrapersonal and interpersonal cases, follow up on what work "separateness of persons" is doing and whether it secretly smuggles the threshold back in.

3. The empirical wrinkle I keep tripping on: nobody ever notices or remembers their speck, so the "harm" is measured in microseconds of mild irritation. If she says unfelt, unremembered harms still count, follow up on whether her view then ranks outcomes by a quantity no one experiences, and what that does to the intuition's grip.

4. Whether she's also committed to the repugnant conclusion, since the aggregation logic looks the same, and if so whether she thinks that's a bullet-biting cost or actually evidence the logic is right.

5. The policy edge: real public health does exactly this tradeoff (tiny distributed costs versus concentrated severe ones), so does her view commit her to always soaking the many to spare the one, and what happens under uncertainty where the specks are only probable.

Also one closing question asking what finding or argument would make her abandon the position -- scientists love a falsifiability check, untill I learned philosophers find that rude. Keep the tone friendly but don't let her off the hook.
