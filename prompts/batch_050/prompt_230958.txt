I am in the second year of a PhD in epistemology, with a side interest in the history of pragmatism, and I keep noticing a pattern of argument that I cannot find a label for, though it feels as though someone must have named it. Three instances. A demographer I know continues to organise her work around a composite index she will cheerfully concede, in private, measures nothing coherent; when the index is criticised she does not defend it but instead describes what would unravel if it were dropped — three decades of comparative datasets, two multinational collaborations, the interpretability of her students' results. In our reading group, a contested premise in the theory of well-being was defended last term almost entirely by inventorying the dissertations, syllabuses and hiring decisions that presuppose it. And I catch myself doing a version of the same thing when I treat peer review as a filter of quality while no longer really believing it is one, because my own career plans are built on the assumption. The structure is the same each time: what is offered is not evidence that the belief is true, and it is not quite self-deception either, since everyone involved is perfectly candid about the evidential position. The consideration is retrospective — it concerns what has already been built with the belief, not what the belief predicts — and yet it is put forward as a justification for going on treating the belief as true, not confessed as a bias. It is not wishful thinking, and it is not quite what Peirce criticised as the method of tenacity, because these people are not shielding themselves from doubt; they recite the doubts and keep the belief anyway, with the doubts attached.

The reason I am asking now is that I have been re-reading William James and the pattern looks uncomfortably like a perverse application of his method. In Lecture II of Pragmatism, "What Pragmatism Means", James writes: "The pragmatic method in such cases is to try to interpret each notion by tracing its respective practical consequences. What difference would it practically make to any one if this notion rather than that notion were true?" The trouble is that the practical difference between my demographer treating the index as sound and her treating it as worthless is enormous — careers, comparability of data, whole literatures — so on a careless reading James's test would licence retaining it. But the difference in question is a difference in what we would have to undo, not a difference in what experience would be like if the claim were true rather than false, and I take it those are not the same thing. My question is whether the configuration I have described has an established name, in pragmatist scholarship, in epistemology, or anywhere else. If it does, please explain how it differs from Peirce's tenacity, from James's own more expansive remarks about truth later in the book, from the conservatism built into web-of-belief pictures of revision, and from the ordinary sunk-cost fallacy — since one possibility I want taken seriously is that this is simply sunk-cost reasoning wearing epistemic dress. If nothing established fits, say so plainly and coin a term: give it a definition, some boundary cases, and an account of whether it is best understood as a misapplication of the test in the passage above or as exposing a genuine gap in it.

A few requests about the shape of the answer. I do not want a reading list or bibliography; I want the carving done directly, since for my purposes the label matters far less than knowing exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are. Please write in British English, and in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just the way one would write a considered letter to a colleague. Where the answer makes claims, indicate how confident you are in them, and single out explicitly the one point on which you are least sure, whether that is the fit with James, the novelty of the coinage, or something else.
