I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and after a conference panel I ended up listening to two philosophers of physics have a surprisingly heated arguement about gauge theories. They were talking mainly about electromagnetism, but also about Yang–Mills theory. For anyone rusty: a gauge transformation changes the mathematical variables used to describe a system while leaving every experimental prediction unchanged. A Wilson loop, roughly, measures the phase picked up while going around a closed path. They both knew the technical material, so I don’t think either was simply confused.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Nadia said that a physical state should be identified with an equivalence class of gauge-related descriptions. So the vector potential at an individual spacetime point cannot be real, because a gauge transformation changes it. The real facts are the gauge-invariant ones: electric and magnetic fields, Wilson loops, charges, scattering outcomes, and so forth.

2. Owen said this assumes that anything altered by a descriptive convention must be unreal, but that is too strong. Velocity depends on a reference frame, yet relative velocity is plainly physical. Similarly, a gauge-dependent potential may encode a genuine relational fact even if it cannot be assigned an absolute, gauge-independent value.

3. Nadia replied that the reference-frame analogy fails. A change of reference frame can be associated with an actual change in the motion of a laboratory or observer. A gauge transformation leaves the laboratory, the observer and every relational arrangement exactly as they were. If two complete descriptions agree about every possible measurement, then saying they represent different physical facts is just surplus structure.

4. Owen brought up the Aharonov–Bohm effect. Charged particles pass through a region where the magnetic field is zero, yet their interference pattern depends on the magnetic flux enclosed by their paths. The local electric and magnetic fields along the particles’ routes do not explain the full effect, so the potential must be doing real physical work.

5. Nadia said this proves only that the gauge-invariant loop integral or holonomy is real. It does not resurrect the potential at each point. Many different local assignments of the potential give exactly the same loop phases and exactly the same interference pattern, so the Aharonov–Bohm effect supports a nonlocal gauge-invariant fact, not a point-by-point potential fact.

6. Owen accepted that distinction, but said the equivalence-class view becomes awkward when we divide a system into subsystems. Describing one spatial region separately from its environment introduces boundary degrees of freedom and ways of attaching electromagnetic fields to charges, often called dressings. Treating all of that as mere redundancy makes it hard to explain locality, subsystem entanglement and the physical separation between a charge and its surrounding field.

7. Nadia answered that subsystem language is precisely where physicists often smuggle in a gauge fixing. One can define local charged operators by extending Wilson lines from a charge out to a boundary, but the choice of line or dressing is conventional. Unless some independent physical procedure fixes it, differences between dressings are bookkeeping differences rather than physical differences.

8. Owen said a convention can become physically significant when it is tied to a material reference system. If a detector, phase standard or boundary system is actually correlated with the field, then quantities defined relative to it can be operationally meaningful. He also said that some large gauge transformations and boundary modes may carry physical charges or energy even if the ordinary local transformations connected smoothly to the identity are redundant.

9. Nadia was willing to make an exception for genuinely large transformations or physical boundary conditions, but said Owen was moving from that exception to the much broader claim that ordinary gauge-dependent quantities are real. In her view, his examples show only that the boundary between gauge redundancy and physical structure depends on the system being described and on which transformations are treated as changes of description.

Neither would give way. Nadia kept saying that Owen was mistaking representational bookkeeping for ontology; Owen kept saying that Nadia had turned the sensible demand for gauge invariance into an overly crude test for reality.

Please adjudicate the dispute move by move. For each of moves 1–9, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible only under a particular interpretation, and briefly explain why. Then address these underlying questions separately:

- Does the Aharonov–Bohm effect give us a reason to regard the electromagnetic potential as real, or only a reason to regard nonlocal holonomies as real?
- Is gauge invariance a necessary condition for physical reality, a sufficient condition, neither, or both?
- Are relational, frame-dependent and subsystem-dependent facts automatically less real than absolute facts?
- Is there a principled distinction between mere surplus structure and structure whose physical significance appears only relative to boundaries, laboratories or material reference systems?
- What is the strongest overall position that combines what Nadia gets right with what Owen gets right?

Finally, give a verdict about who is more right overall, but don’t flatten the disagreement: identify the precise points on which each person wins and anything important that both are missing. Please answer in British English.
