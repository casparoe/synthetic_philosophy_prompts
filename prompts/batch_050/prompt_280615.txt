In the philosophy reading group I joined after retiring from a career in marine biology, we recently discussed services that build conversational simulations of deceased persons — "griefbots," in the current jargon — trained on the messages, letters, and recordings someone leaves behind, so that the bereaved can go on conversing with a version of the dead. One member defended the practice with an analogy I have since heard repeated elsewhere: a griefbot is simply the photograph album of our century. We keep photographs, home videos, voicemails, and letters of the dead without moral qualm; the griefbot, she argued, is the same memorial impulse served by better technology, and anyone who finds it sinister must either condemn the shoebox of photographs as well or confess to mere technophobia. She concluded that griefbots raise no genuinely new ethical questions — only old grief in a new medium.

I am not persuaded, but I want the analogy audited rather than applauded or dismissed. Please set out, explicitly, what corresponds to what: what plays the role of the photographic record, of the person depicted, of the viewer who opens the album, and of the occasion on which it is opened. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I can see candidate disanalogies — a photograph is a fixed record whereas the simulation generates utterances the deceased never made; a photograph does not answer back, and so cannot present the relationship as an ongoing exchange; one can check a photograph against one's memory, but no memory exists against which to check a novel utterance — but I would like to know which of these matter for the conclusion the analogy is asked to carry, and which are incidental.

As a test case, consider a restricted version of the service: a griefbot permitted to respond only with sentences the deceased actually wrote or said, retrieved and stitched together on demand. Does the photograph analogy hold for this archival bot and fail only for the generative one — in other words, is the ethical weight carried by generativity rather than by interactivity? Or does interactivity alone already break the analogy?

Finally, if the photograph analogy cannot carry the conclusion that griefbots raise no new ethical questions, say what comparison fits the practice better — theatrical impersonation, the séance, the authorised biography, or something else — and whether the defensible conclusion changes with it.

Please lead with your verdict in the first sentence — how far the analogy holds and whether the "no new questions" conclusion survives — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question.
