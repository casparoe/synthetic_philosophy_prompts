I have no academic training, but I've spent the last year thinking on my own about whether the right to vote should depend on knowing anything, and I've ended up somewhere I didn't expect: I think epistocracy -- the idea that political power should be weighted by political knowledge -- deserves a fair hearing, even if it's ultimately wrong. Next month I have to lead one evening of my neighborhood discussion group on this, and I want to do it properly. Don't argue the position for or against; instead, plan how I should explain it in about an hour. What has to be established first -- I'm guessing something about why we value democracy in the first place, and the difference between valuing it as a tool versus valuing it for its own sake -- and in what order the rest depends on that? What single example should carry the session, and what must it have and lack (I'd rather not use Plato's ship captain or a driver's-license analogy, since both seem to smuggle in the conclusion)? Which objections will the group raise, in the order they'll raise them -- I predict 'you just want rule by college graduates' and 'who decides what counts as knowledge' -- and at what point in the sequence should each be absorbed rather than left to derail things? How can I tell by the end whether they've grasped the strongest version of the idea rather than a caricature, and what should I deliberately leave out at this level?
