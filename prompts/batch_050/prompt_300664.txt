My roommate and I are both in a philosophy of math reading group, and last week we got into a genuinely stupid-sounding fight that turned out not to be stupid: he said 2 is obviously an element of 4, I said 2 obviously has no elements at all, and then we realized we were both just assuming different reductions of the naturals to sets (von Neumann vs. Zermelo style), and both reductions work perfectly well. So the question "is 2 an element of 4?" seems to have no answer, which I gather is basically Benacerraf's problem and the whole reason structuralism about math exists as a position. What I want for the reading group is a decision tree that takes someone from this starting point -- "the numbers have multiple equally good reductions, so their non-structural properties aren't really facts about them" -- to a named position they can defend.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. The branch nodes should be questions someone can answer without already knowing the jargon, roughly: are mathematical structures themselves objects over and above their instances, or just a way of talking about anything that satisfies the axioms; does a structure contain positions-as-objects of their own, or only roles that get filled by whatever system exemplifies it; is the fact that "is 2 an element of 4?" has no answer a defect of set-theoretic foundations that structuralism fixes, or a reason to think the question was ill-formed all along; and does doing math ever require more than that it's possible for there to be systems of the right shape. Aim for maybe 6 to 8 leaves covering things like ante rem structuralism, in re structuralism, eliminative or modal structuralism, category-theoretic approaches, plain set-theoretic reductionism (the person who just bites the bullet that 2 really is an element of 4 relative to one privileged reduction), and the deflationary "there's no fact of the matter and nothing needs fixing" stance. For any combination of answers where nobody actually sits, say so and guess why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored), and flag any leaves your tree can't really seperate from each other with the questions given.

Since the group has a mix of backgrounds -- some people have done the phil math seminar, some just like arguing -- phrase every node as a concrete question about numbers or cases, not "do you accept sui generis positions." Also, tell me how confident you are that each named position actually lands where you put it on the tree, and single out the one placement or branch you're least sure of, since I know some of these views get described differently by different people and I'd rather know where you're guessing.
