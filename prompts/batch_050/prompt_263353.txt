I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on an argument that will run through my whole book, so I need this sorted. The book has a marine biologist character who keeps a rescued octopus in her lab and basically refuses to rank animals: in her view the octopus, her neighbour's dog, and the shrimp in the freezer are all in the same boat. Her antagonist (a somewhat unlikeable cognitive scientist, tbh) keeps insisting that a dolphin plainly matters more than a prawn because of its bigger, richer brain, and that pretending otherwise is sentimentality. I've been researching animal ethics to write their fights convincingly, and last week I tried the antagonist's line on a friend who volunteers at an animal sanctuary. She immediately fired back with that famous old slogan -- the one that goes, roughly, the question is not whether animals can reason or can talk, but whether they can suffer. And everyone at the table nodded like that had settled it, like brain size had just been ruled irrelevent.

Here's my problem: the more I turn that sentence over, the less I understand what it's actually claiming, and I need to know whether my cognitive scientist is the villain of that exchange or the one making the better point. Can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making? For instance: is it saying that suffering is the only thing of moral value, so that once a creature can suffer, fancier cognition adds exactly nothing to how much it matters? Or is it only making a point about the entry ticket to moral consideration -- who's in the club at all -- while staying silent on how much each member matters relative to the others? Or is it maybe claiming something stronger and weirder, like that suffering can't come in depths tied to cognitive richness, that a pain is a pain is a pain?

For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive-but-defensible position, or actually false, and why. Then the part I really care about: which of these readings, if any, would actually support my friend's move of shutting down the brain-size point, since I suspect the slogan does way less work at a dinner table than people think -- the question of whether a shrimp can suffer at all, and how much, might itself depend on the very neurobiology the slogan is supposedly brushing aside. Also, if none of the readings do the trump-card job, tell me what stronger claim my sanctuary friend would need to defend instead, because I'd like to give her character (okay, she's going in the book) the best version of the argument.
