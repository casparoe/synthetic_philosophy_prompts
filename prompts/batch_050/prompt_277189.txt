So next fall I'm teaching our grad seminar on moral psychology for the first time, and our DGS suggested I hand out a one-pager on day one saying where I myself stand, since the students always ask anyway and it gives them something to push against. I drafted it last night over wine and now I'm worried it doesn't add up. Here's what I wrote, roughly:

1. Moral objectivism. Some moral truths hold regardless of what anybody, or any culture, thinks about them. Slavery was wrong back when nearly everyone endorsed it; honor killing is wrong even in communities where it's unanimously approved of.

2. The cross-cultural literature is basically right. I accept the Henrich-style point that educated secular Westerners are psychological outliers, including in moral judgment: bargaining norms in small-scale societies, the weight people give purity and loyalty versus harm and fairness, intuitions about sacrificial dilemmas — these vary a lot, and my own gut reactions are parochial products of where I happen to have been raised.

3. Still, I'm broadly a nativist about moral psychology: humans share an evolved moral faculty, and the cultural variation is parameter-setting on a shared system, roughly the way languages differ on top of a universal grammar.

4. Reflective equilibrium is the right way to do normative ethics, full stop. My considered judgments — which are, and I admit this cheerfully, those of a secular liberal Western professor — are a perfectly good starting point, and the method can deliver justified beliefs about the objective moral truths from 1.

5. Moral progress is real. Abolition, suffrage, the expanding circle: later views are often better, not just different, and my own society's recent trajectory is mostly progress rather than drift.

Where's the problem? I'm fairly sure at least two of these pull against eachother but I can't pin down exactly where, and I'd rather hear it from you than from some sharp grad student in week two. Can you audit the set: which pairs are in genuine tension and how badly (rank them by severity, don't just flag everything), which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and for each real tension what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me. If a single change would clean up most of it, name it.

One more thing, since this straddles philosophy and psychology: keep three levels seperate — what's actually established in the empirical literature, what's still genuinely contested among the psychologists themselves (e.g. how deep cross-cultural variation in trolley-style intuitions really runs, or whether the "universal moral grammar" picture is even the consensus among the nativists), and what's a philosophical gloss that people like me put on the data. Don't present a contested reading as settled, even when it's the fun one.
