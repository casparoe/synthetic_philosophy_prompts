**Why Gettier cases keep working, and a patch that probably doesn't**

In 1963 Edmund Gettier published a paper, famously only about three pages long, that caused trouble for the idea that knowledge is justified true belief. The recipe: you have excellent justification for a belief, the belief happens to be true, but it's true for the wrong reason. You look at a stopped clock that happens to show the right time. You infer "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket" from evidence about Jones, but actually Smith gets the job and Smith, unbeknownst to you, also has ten coins in his pocket.

Here's what I think is going on in most of these cases. Your justification is never really for the specific proposition you end up believing. It's for a disjunction, or something disjunction-like. Your evidence supports "the clock says 3:00 and clocks usually work," and from that you extract "it's 3:00." Your evidence supports "Jones will get the job," and from that you extract a proposition that would be made true by a whole range of situations. The Gettier case is what happens when the disjunction is true but the disjunct you were actually relying on is false.

The standard response in the literature was the "no false lemmas" idea: knowledge is justified true belief where the justification doesn't essentially depend on anything false. That handles the coins case, because your reasoning goes through the false belief that Jones will get the job. But everyone knows it dies on fake barns. Henry drives through countryside full of barn facades, looks at the one real barn, and thinks "that's a barn." There's no false premise anywhere in his reasoning. He just got lucky.

So here's my patch. I think the right condition isn't about false lemmas, it's about route diversity. A belief counts as knowledge only if the justification that actually supports it would have supported it in most nearby situations where the belief is true. In the stopped clock case, your justification (the clock says 3:00) only supports the belief in the one-in-a-day situations where the clock happens to be right, so the route diversity is terrible. In fake barn county, Henry's justification (looks like a barn) supports his belief in only a tiny fraction of the nearby situations where he forms a barn-belief, because almost all of those beliefs are false. Wait — that last part is about situations where the belief is *false*, not true. Hmm. Maybe the condition should be: the justification must support the belief in most nearby situations where you actually deploy it. I'll leave both versions on the table.

Objection I can already hear: this is just a modal condition, like sensitivity or safety, dressed up in new words. Fair. But I think "route diversity" gets something right that those miss: it explains why the disjunction cases and the barn cases feel like the same problem even though one involves inference from a falsehood and the other doesn't. In both, your justificatory route is one route among many the situation could have forced on you, and yours only works by accident.
