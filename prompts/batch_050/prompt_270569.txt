I run benefits and internal comms at a company with about 600 employees, and twice a year we redesign something — the enrollment flow, the 401k paperwork, the cafeteria, the commuter signup. Every single time, someone on the team says "let's just nudge them," and every time I have to decide whether that's fine or creepy without anything better than a gut check. I know the broad strokes of the debate — Mill's harm principle, the soft vs. hard paternalism distinction, the whole libertarian paternalism thing from Thaler and Sunstein, the fights about whether defaults exploit inertia or respect it — but none of that comes with instructions.

So: turn the paternalism literature into a procedure I can actually run. I want an ordered sequence of tests or questions I can apply to any proposed nudge in about an hour, before it goes to our general counsel. Things I'd expect it to sort out: whose welfare standard counts (the employee's own goals, or what we think their goals should be — the means vs. ends paternalism distinction seems to matter here, but tell me if it survives contact with practice); whether the opt-out is real or just technically present; whether the intervention works because people are informed or because they're not paying attention; whether the default will be read as a recommendation (and whether that makes it more or less defensible); and whether it matters that the company also benefits. For each step, tell me which philosophical distinction it preserves and which ones you collapsed or dropped to make it usable, and mark the steps where no checklist can replace judgment and a human just has to decide.

Then work it through three cases, showing the procedure's output at each step, not just the verdict:

1. Auto-enrolling new hires into the 401k at 6% with automatic annual escalation, opt-out anytime but it takes a form.
2. Making the cafeteria default lunch option plant-based, with meat available on request at the same price.
3. Defaulting everyone into the high-deductible health plan because it's actuarially the best deal for most of our (young, healthy) workforce — and, full disclosure, it also saves the company a chunk of money.

I want at least one of these to come out with an answer I won't like — either killing a nudge I think is obviously good or greenlighting one I find queasy — and I want the procedure to own that outcome rather than fudging the test. End with a section on what the procedure cannot settle: cases where two reasonable people following it faithfully would still disagree, and who should sign off in those cases.

Write it in American English, and keep the philosophy accurate even where you simplify — I'd rather have a procedure that's honest about losing nuance than one that pretends it didn't.
