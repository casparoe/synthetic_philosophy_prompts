I am a second-year PhD student in linguistics, trained mainly in formal semantics and possible-worlds tools, who is moving into philosophy of language for the dissertation. My topic will be fictional names and fictional truth — whether a name like Sherlock Holmes refers, and what makes claims about what happens in the stories true or false. I have roughly three years and room for three or four serious chapters, so what I need from you is a defended research agenda, not a survey of the area and not a reading list.

Two features of my situation should shape the agenda. First, my department requires the prospectus to declare a background framework up front — artifactualism about fictional characters, pretense-theoretic irrealism, or some revisionary semantics for empty names — even though the unresolved problems in the area are precisely what ought to decide between these. So I need to know which open problems are framework-decisive, in the sense that settling them could force me to switch frameworks mid-dissertation, and which are internal housekeeping that can be pursued within any of them. Second, I have a standing disagreement with one of my committee members. I suspect that negative existentials — truths to the effect that Sherlock Holmes does not exist, which every framework must accommodate while still distinguishing them from parallel claims about, say, Hercule Poirot — are the upstream bottleneck: get their semantics right and the rest of the system falls into place. My committee member argues instead that the real bottleneck is mixed discourse, especially comparative sentences that appear to rank fictional characters against actual people, because those force any theory to interface with ordinary non-fictional content. Please adjudicate this and fold your verdict into the ordering.

So: which open problems here — the semantics of negative existentials, the treatment of mixed and metalinguistic sentences, the principles governing what is true in a fiction and how to handle internally inconsistent or impossible fictions, the epistemology of our apparent knowledge about fictional characters, or others I have not listed — deserve my three or four chapters, and in what order? For each, explain which other problems depend on its resolution, how tractable it is for someone whose tools are semantic rather than metaphysical, and how neglected it is relative to its importance. Make your ranking criteria explicit, state the trade-offs among them, and tell me what arguments or results appearing in the next couple of years would most change the ordering — in particular, what would show that my bottleneck hypothesis, or my committee member's, is mistaken.

One further request: the literature on the metaphysics of fictional characters and the literature on the semantics of fictional discourse seem to me to proceed in near-total independence, and I suspect some standard problems are artifacts of that split rather than genuine. If you agree about any of them, say which, and downgrade them in the ranking accordingly.
