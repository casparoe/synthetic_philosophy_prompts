So I've been going around in circles on the grue thing and I want to try a different approach: instead of arguing about it in the abstract, here are my gut verdicts on cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is generating them. Feel free to tell me some verdicts have to go.

1. Every emerald I've seen is green, so the next one is green. Obvious yes, the observations confirm the hypothesis.
2. Every emerald I've seen has been green-when-observed-before-2050, and I refuse to count that as confirming "the next emerald, observed after 2050, will be blue." Hard no.
3. A lab finds that a bacterial strain is susceptible to an antibiotic for the first ten generations and resistant after that. The data from generations 1-10 genuinely does confirm "susceptible until generation 10-ish, resistant after" -- I'd bet on generation 12 being resistant. But this hypothesis has exactly the same shape as the emerald one in case 2! It's a "property A before threshold, property B after" predicate. Yet here it confirms and there it doesn't. This is the pair I can't reconcile.
4. Goodman-adjacent one: every coin that has ever been in my pocket has been a coin in my pocket, but finding one more coin in my pocket doesn't make me think all future coins anywhere will have been in my pocket. Location-indexed predicates don't confirm. Except in case 3 the "location" is a position in a generational sequence and it DOES confirm. Ugh.
5. Symmetry worry: someone tells me you can define green in terms of grue and bleen just as easily as grue in terms of green and blue, so there's no principled priority. My gut says this argument is a trick and doesn't move me at all -- case 2 still gets a no -- but I can't say where it goes wrong.
6. Entrenchment worry: I catch myself wanting to say the difference is that "green" and "resistant-after-10-generations-given-what-we-know-about-mutation-rates" are predicates a working community actually uses, and "grue" isn't. But then I notice that makes projectibility a sociological fact about us, and it feels like induction shouldn't bottom out in "whatever predicates scientists happen to like."

So: is there a single principle that delivers verdicts 1-4 while also explaining why the move in 5 fails and either vindicating or replacing my impulse in 6? If no principle gets all of them, which verdict would you sacrifice and why that one? And then test whatever you land on against a case I haven't listed: a community that has always, for centuries, used a grue-like color predicate as their basic one, and treats our "green" as the gerrymandered disjunction. Does your principle say their inductive practice is rational, irrational, or does it just go silent -- and are you okay with that answer?
