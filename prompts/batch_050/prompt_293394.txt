I've followed population ethics discussions on rationalist blogs and EA-adjacent forums for years, and I keep noticing a pattern in the dialectic that I have no word for. A theory of how to rank populations of different sizes gets proposed; someone exhibits a verdict it gives that almost nobody can accept; the theory is repaired; and the repair turns out to generate a new verdict, elsewhere in possibility space, that seems comparably unacceptable. Totalling welfare ranks a vast population of lives barely worth living above a small population of excellent ones. Averaging instead says that adding a happy person drags the world down whenever they're slightly below the existing mean. Counting only welfare above a threshold can make it better to add a few lives of intense suffering than to add any number of lives that are good but modest. Weighting excellence heavily flips the original verdict, preferring a handful of superb lives to an enormous population of very good ones. The badness seems conserved: every fix relocates it rather than eliminating it.

My question: does this meta-level phenomenon have an established name? I'm not asking for a name for any of the individual counterintuitive verdicts, nor for the bare claim that no axiology satisfies all our intuitions. I've heard there are formal impossibility theorems in this literature showing that certain lists of adequacy conditions can't be jointly satisfied, and I'd like to know how the informal pattern I'm describing relates to those results -- whether it's just their colloquial shadow or something distinct, weaker or stronger. If nothing names it, propose a term, with a definition precise enough to mark boundary cases: what distinguishes this pattern from ordinary theoretical tradeoffs in any field, and from cases where the relocated verdict is merely unfamiliar rather than genuinely as bad as the one it replaced?
