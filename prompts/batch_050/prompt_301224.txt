Okay so I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on the conceptual foundations of computability, and I've been going in circles with my advisor about the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis. She thinks its basically settled by Turing's analysis of what a human following a routine procedure can do — finite instructions, finite steps, no flashes of insight — so the thesis has roughly the status of a conceptual analysis, and no experiment in physics could possibly touch it. I keep pushing back that the version people actually lean on in practice, the claim that no physically buildable device could compute a non-recursive function, is an empirical hypothesis that could in principle be falsified, and that retreating to the "analysis" reading whenever physics comes up is a bait and switch.

What I want: write a dialogue between two philosophers of computation, each holding one of these views, hashing it out (over conference dinner, on a long train ride, wherever — your choice). Both should be smart and well-informed; please don't let either be a strawman. A few requirements: (1) real back-and-forth — each character responds to the other's actual last point rather than reciting a prepared statement; (2) the empirical-hypothesis side should raise something like an accelerating/supertask machine or an oracle-box thought experiment, and the analysis side should respond with something better than "that's not what Turing meant" — for instance, pressing on whether such a device would even count as computing in the relevant sense, or on finite precision, noise, and how you'd ever verify the output of a machine that supposedly decides the halting problem; (3) the analysis side should land some hits of its own, e.g., the point that "physically possible machine" is itself theory-relative and vague, so the empirical reading might be unfalsifiable in its own sneaky way, or that the two readings keep getting equivocated in the literature; (4) don't wrap it up with agreement — end on the sharpest remaining point of disagreement, whatever the two of them genuinely can't get past.

Keep it in American English, roughly 700–900 words, and make it read like an actual conversation — interruptions, partial concessions, one of them getting a little annoyed — rather than two encyclopedia entries taking turns.
