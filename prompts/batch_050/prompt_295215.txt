A friend of mine claims to have a principled answer to the question of when two things genuinely share a universal, as opposed to merely falling under the same word or the same description. I have been collecting her verdicts and would like to figure out her system before our next meeting. Her verdicts so far:

- Two electrons: they genuinely share a universal (negative charge).
- Two hydrogen atoms: yes, they share one.
- Two white billiard balls: yes, they share whiteness.
- Two wooden tables: no — "being a table" is not a genuine universal.
- A game of chess and a game of tag: no — there is no universal of gamehood that they share.
- A piece of jadeite and a piece of nephrite: no — "jade" names no genuine universal, whatever jewelers say.
- Two books that happen to belong to Alice: no — being owned by Alice is no universal.
- Two emeralds, each of which is green if examined before the year 2100 and blue otherwise: no — there is no universal of grue.

Her next case, which she has promised to rule on at our next meeting, is this: two pebbles, one found on a beach in Wales and one recovered from the surface of Mars, that are exactly congruent in their highly irregular shapes, so that either could be perfectly superimposed on the other. Is there a genuine universal — this particular determinate shape — that the two pebbles share?

Please do the following. First, reconstruct at least two general rules about universals that are each consistent with all eight verdicts on record — I suspect something in the family of an austerely scientific, a posteriori realism about universals on the one hand, and a resemblance-based criterion restricted to intrinsic, non-gerrymandered respects of similarity on the other, but formulate them properly yourself. Second, show that the rules come apart on the pebble case, stating clearly what each predicts and why. Third, give me your best prediction of her actual verdict, with your reasons — including any considerations about which rule better explains the overall pattern, or about how a proponent of the less-favored rule might resist your characterization of it. Finally, describe one further case, not on the list, whose verdict would discriminate between the two rules whichever way she answered the pebbles.
