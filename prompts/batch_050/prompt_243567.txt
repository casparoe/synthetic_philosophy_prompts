I'm a retired geophysicist (well, mostly retired -- I still consult now and then) and about two years ago I fell down the philosophy rabbit hole. I host a little reading group at our public library, eight regulars, mix of retired folks and a couple of grad students, and we've been working through philosophy of physics. We just got to the hole argument -- Einstein's old worry that you can take two solutions of his field equations that agree everywhere outside some empty region, differ inside it, and since both satisfy the equations, the theory seems not to determine what happens in the hole, unless you bite the bullet and say the two solutions describe one and the same physical situation. My group understood the words fine but I could tell nobody felt the squeeze. They nodded at "the differences are merely mathematical" like it was obviously right, and when I played devil's advocate they nodded at "determinism fails" like that was obviously right too. Nobody felt forced anywhere.

So I want to design an activity for our next meeting, about an hour, that makes them run into the problem with their hands before we name it again. My vague thought: something with grids or maps where different people fill in the interior of a blank region differently given identical boundary information, and then the group has to argue about whether the different fillings are genuinely different states of the world or the same one described twice -- and feel why both answers cost something. But I don't trust my game-design instincts, so: give me concrete rules, materials (nothing fancier than paper, pencils, maybe envelopes), a rough timeline for the hour, and a debrief that maps what happened in the game onto the moves in the actual argument -- including the substantivalist and relationist ways out, or however you think the options should be carved up. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time we run it, and be honest about which feature of the real argument a parlor game like this just cannot reproduce (I suspect the dynamical metric is the part that won't fit on an index card).

Two requests. First, American English, and keep the whole thing under 650 words -- I'll be taking notes by hand. Second, if you think this whole idea is misconceived -- say the hole argument is too technical to stage as a game, or I've garbled what's actually at stake in it -- tell me that plainly instead of politely designing a broken activity. I'd rather be corrected at the library than in print, but mostly I'd just rather be corrected.
