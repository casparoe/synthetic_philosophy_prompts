I have been trying to use Kant's Formula of Universal Law as a working test of permissibility, and a colleague pressed an argument on me that seems to prove too much. I would like a diagnosis: which step fails, why it looked sound, and what rejecting it costs.

(1) A maxim is impermissible if I cannot consistently will it as a universal law. In particular, on the practical reading of the test, a maxim fails when acting on it in a world where everyone acts on it would be self-defeating: the universal practice would undermine the very end the maxim is adopted to achieve.

(2) Take the maxim: "When tickets for a popular event go on sale, I will purchase mine in the first minute, ahead of the crowd, in order to secure a seat." Universalized, everyone attempts to purchase in the first minute. The surge makes being early worthless: the servers are overwhelmed, allocation becomes a lottery, and no one secures a seat by this means. The universalized practice destroys exactly the advantage the maxim aims at. So the maxim is practically self-defeating, and by (1) it is impermissible.

(3) The argument generalizes to every strategic maxim of the same shape: leaving at 5 a.m. to beat the traffic (if all do, there is traffic at 5 a.m.); standing up at the concert to see better; arriving at dawn for unreserved picnic spots; applying to an obscure fellowship precisely because competition is light; taking the little-known shortcut. In each case, universal adoption eliminates the benefit the maxim exists to capture, so each fails the test by the same reasoning as (2).

(4) Therefore the Formula of Universal Law condemns as impermissible the whole class of competitive-timing maxims.

(5) But at least some of these are plainly permissible. Buying concert tickets promptly when sales open is not wrong; leaving early to avoid congestion is not wrong. No moral theory that forbids them can be right.

(6) Therefore the Formula of Universal Law is not an adequate test of permissibility — at least not the practical, self-defeat reading in (1).

I am more attached to the verdicts in (5) than to anything else, and I would also rather not abandon the universal law test wholesale, since it seems to get the central cases (false promising, free-riding on practices one wants to exist) exactly right. What I cannot see is which step to give up, and I suspect more than one rejection is available, at different prices. Please identify the step you would reject, explain why it was tempting, and price the main alternatives — including any response that concedes the argument works but denies that it damages the test (for instance, by relocating the problem to how maxims are described in the first place). If the argument turns out to be valid and some premise in (1)–(5) must genuinely fall, tell me which falls cheapest and what a Kantian who pays that price can no longer say.
