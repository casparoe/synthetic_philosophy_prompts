I've been chewing on a puzzle for a few weeks now, ever since a long drive where I caught myself doing something odd, and I'd like to know whether philosophers have a name for it and what they make of it. The drive version: I told myself I'd accept one extra minute of traffic in exchange for a dollar, and that seemed obviously worth it. But then I noticed that if I'd accept one minute for a dollar, I should accept the next minute for another dollar, and so on, step by step, until I've "accepted" nine extra hours in traffic for $540, which is a deal I would obviously refuse outright. Each single step felt like a clear yes, and yet the total they add up to is a clear no. A second example, with my headphones: turning the volume up one click is never perceptible as a change, so click 12 sounds exactly like click 13 to me, and yet click 5 is comfortably quiet and click 60 is painful. If every adjacent pair is indistinguishable, it seems like they should all be the same to me, but the ends plainly aren't. A third, which bothers me most: comparing jobs. Offer B pays a bit more than offer A and is otherwise identical, so B is better. Offer C improves on B the same tiny way. I can keep going until I reach offer Z, and every step seemed like a strict improvement, so surely Z beats A. But I've read that some philosophers think chains like this can actually break down -- that you can dutifully prefer each thing to the last and still find, at the end, that the endpoint comparison goes the other way, and that this isn't a mistake but a fact about what "better than" is like. I genuinely can't tell whether that's deep or just confused.

I know about the old puzzle with the heap of sand, where removing one grain never turns a heap into a non-heap, so where does the heap go. Mine feels related but different, and I'd like you to take that feeling seriously: the heap puzzle seems to be about where a fuzzy word stops applying, whereas mine seems to be about whether one thing is actually better than another, and about whether step-by-step verdicts and end-to-end verdicts can legitimately disagree. So my questions are these. First, is there an established name for the kind of argument or puzzle I'm describing -- the graded series where every local step looks fine but the global result looks absurd -- and if it sits between several known notions (the heap puzzle, the "if you keep conceding small steps you slide all the way down" style of argument, whatever the job-offer case is), please tell me which fits best and how my cases differ from each neighbor. Second, where exactly does the reasoning go wrong, if it does: is the hidden problem that "no perceptible difference" between neighbors isn't the same as "no difference at all," so tiny improvements silently accumulate; or is it that my indifference between neighbors was real and the relation itself just doesn't chain the way I assumed; or is the whole thing only a problem for words and not for value? Third, if no existing label covers exactly the thing I'm pointing at, please coin one, define it precisely, and mark off its edges with a case that falls inside and one that falls outside.

Please answer as a professional philosopher with expertise in value theory and the logic of preference and choice, and take your time: begin by briefly brainstorming the candidate concepts this phenomenon could be identified with and testing each against my three examples, then settle on your best classification before turning to the diagnosis. I'd like the strongest version of each rival diagnosis and a concrete test case on which they'd give different verdicts, so I can see what hangs on the choice. I'm a layperson, so explain any technical terms you introduce, and skip the bibliography -- I want the concepts, the structure of the arguments, and your own best judgment about whether "better than" can really behave the way my headphone dial suggests, or whether one of my steps must have been wrong all along.
