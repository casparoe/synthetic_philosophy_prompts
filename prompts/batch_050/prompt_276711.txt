I am a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of science seminar on scientific modeling, and I also serve as the student representative on my university's capital projects committee. This year the committee must decide what to do about the university's coastal marine research station, and I would like to bring the seminar material to bear rather than simply defer to the engineers. Please help me draft a short written memo that I will circulate to the committee before our next meeting.

The situation is as follows. The station sits about a meter above current high-tide levels and has flooded twice in fifteen years. The university can (a) spend roughly $4 million now to elevate the main building and harden the shoreline, (b) defer the decision for a decade and spend the money elsewhere, or (c) interpret the recent flooding as the start of a managed retreat and begin relocating research operations inland, which costs little now but locks in a thirty-year wind-down of the site. The scientific input we have been given is a standard multi-model ensemble: projections of local sea-level rise span roughly 0.3 to 1.1 meters by 2100 across models and emissions scenarios, and everyone on the committee agrees the ensemble was not designed to sample the space of possible models — it is an "ensemble of opportunity" assembled from whatever models exist — so the spread of outputs cannot be read off as a probability distribution over futures. There are also low-confidence, high-impact possibilities, such as rapid ice-sheet loss, that appear in some individual models but fall outside the ensemble's central range.

The committee is split. The engineering lead argues that we should use the ensemble mean, run the cost-benefit arithmetic, and decline the $4 million, since on those numbers elevation is not cost-effective until mid-century. A faculty member on the committee counters that when model uncertainty is deep — when we cannot assign defensible probabilities across models — averaging is the wrong move, and we should instead look for decisions that are robust: choices that perform acceptably across the widest range of plausible futures, even if they maximize expected value under no particular probability weighting.

The memo should recommend one of the three options, with a concrete time frame, and address the committee's actual dispute directly: what, if anything, the ensemble spread entitles us to infer; whether the tail scenarios deserve weight given that we cannot say how likely they are; and whether robustness reasoning is a genuine alternative to expected-value reasoning or merely expected-value reasoning under a different name. Please give the strongest available case for whichever option the memo recommends against — I would rather it lose honestly than win against a caricature. Finally, take care to distinguish what is genuinely settled in climate science (the direction and principal physical causes of sea-level rise, the reliability of the baseline thermodynamics) from what remains contested among people who work on these models (the interpretation of ensemble spread, the status of low-confidence ice-sheet dynamics, the merits of robust decision frameworks). A contested methodological view should not be presented as though it were the consensus.
