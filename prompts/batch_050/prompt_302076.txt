I'm a novelist stuck on a character and I think it's secretly a philosophy problem, so I'm hoping you can help me think it through. In my book, a woman called Ruth has spent fifteen years furious at her older brother, who back when they were teenagers spread a rumour about her that quietly wrecked her prospects for a long time. I've written her resentment as this living, justified thing — it's basically the engine of the novel. Then in the final act it's revealed that at the time he did it, he was already in the early stages of a degenerative brain disease. Undiagnosed back then, obvious in hindsight.

Here's where I'm stuck. I want the revelation to change something real for Ruth, but every draft where her anger evaporates overnight feels fake, and every draft where it doesn't budge an inch makes her seem monstrous. A beta reader said she should just forgive him, but forgiveness feels like the wrong tool — it's not that Ruth decides to excuse him, it's more like the anger has lost it's target, like being angry at the weather. Except her life was genuinely ruined, and he genuinely did it, so the anger going mute also feels like a betrayal of her younger self.

What I'd like to get right: what actually happens, morally and psychologically, when you find out the person who wronged you wasn't fully in control of themselves? Is Ruth's lingering resentment a mistake about the facts, a failure of character, or a perfectly reasonable response? And is there a coherent middle position where she keeps feeling *something* — grief, or loss, or solidarity with the brother-that-was — that isn't just watered-down blame? Practical advice on how to write the scene is welcome, but I mostly want the underlying logic to be sound. British English please.
