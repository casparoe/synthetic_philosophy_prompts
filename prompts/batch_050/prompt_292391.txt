I am writing the final examination for an upper-level course on epistemic game theory. Draft one essay problem on common knowledge of rationality that is built around the following self-contained scenario, and then supply a model answer and marking scheme. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or quotations; the examination must be answerable from the prompt alone.

Scenario to use: Two autonomous treasuries, North and South, must independently choose Commit or Hold during a ten-second window. Commit pays 10 to each if both Commit while the shared asset state is Good; it pays -20 to a treasury that Commits while the other Holds or while the state is Bad; Hold pays 0 regardless. The state is Good with prior probability 0.9. A relay observes the state and sends a signed packet to both sides; every packet and every acknowledgment can independently be lost with probability epsilon, but the protocol is known to halt after at most seven rounds. Each side sees its own received packets and sent acknowledgments, and both know the full protocol, payoffs, loss probability, and halt bound. A further complication is that each treasury assigns a tiny prior probability delta to the other running a legacy fallback that always Holds when uncertainty remains; delta is commonly known, positive, and much smaller than epsilon.

Write a question that makes candidates determine whether and when Commit can be part of a rationalizable outcome, with special attention to three contrasts that students routinely blur: first, common knowledge versus mutual knowledge only up to some finite depth created by the seven-round halt; second, high probability of Good versus sufficiently high-order knowledge that the other side is not merely likely but strategically prepared to Commit; third, ordinary payoff uncertainty versus the legacy-fallback tremble, which can make Hold contagious even when both receive favorable evidence. The question should ask candidates to prove or refute that no loss bound can make Commit dominant once delta remains positive, to identify exactly which orders of knowledge the failed acknowledgments undermine, and to explain how the answer changes if epsilon is commonly known to be exactly zero after round three but this fact is itself only known to depth two. It should not be answerable by reciting that rationality is commonly assumed, by naming a standard electronic-mail example, or by asserting that small probabilities can be ignored.

Provide: the examination question for a 75-minute answer; a model answer of about 700 words written at the level expected of a strong student; and a marking scheme out of 30 points. The marking scheme must isolate six must-hit moves with point values, state what separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of explicit induction over depths of knowledge, and name the characteristic mistake behind each lost mark, including treating near-certainty as common knowledge, confusing ex ante optimality with interim rationalizability after one's own message history, assuming acknowledgments certify receipt rather than only another uncertain signal, folding the legacy fallback into ordinary risk dominance, and invoking the seven-round halt as if it automatically created common knowledge at termination.
