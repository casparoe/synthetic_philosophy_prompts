I am a novelist, and before I let a piece of philosophy into my book I want to know whether it is sound. The novel turns on a neuroengineer who claims to have built a device that produces pure pleasure — not the pleasure of anything, just pleasure itself — and on a philosopher who objects that the device is conceptually confused, because the things we call pleasure have nothing in common for such a machine to produce. Wanting the philosopher's side of the argument to be intellectually respectable, I asked a friend with a philosophy degree where the idea comes from. He sent me to Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics (I have been reading the seventh edition, 1907), whose first book contains a chapter on pleasure and desire, and he summarized its argument for me like this:

'The chapter makes two moves. First, Sidgwick observes that the experiences we call pleasures — tasting wine, finishing a proof, a hot bath, a friend's company — share no single feeling-quality; there is no common pleasure sensation running through them, and the apparent unity of the concept is really just a trick of our having one word. Second, to explain what holds the class together despite this, he defines pleasure in terms of desire: a pleasure is simply whatever feeling we happen to want while we are having it. Pleasure, on his account, is a label for our desires rather than a kind of feeling in its own right. The ethical moral follows directly: since pleasures are heterogeneous, there is no single scale on which they can be weighed, and that is why Sidgwick thinks hedonism — the doctrine that pleasure is the one thing ultimately good — cannot be worked out with scientific precision. One caveat, though. The chapter began life as a journal article, and Sidgwick admits in his own prefaces that he changed his mind about it afterwards, saying the chapter as it now stands contradicts what he first wrote, so scholars treat the psychology of Book I as something he himself disowned.'

His caveat puzzled me, because I then found the relevant passages in Sidgwick's prefaces, reprinted in that edition. The first: "I ought to mention that chapter iv. of Book i. has been reprinted (with considerable modifications) from the Contemporary Review, in which it originally appeared as an article on “Pleasure and Desire.”" The second: "For instance, in chap. iv. of Book i. (on “Pleasure and Desire”), which has been a good deal criticised by Prof. Bain and others, although I still retain my former opinion on the psychological question at issue, I have been led to take a different view of the relation of this question to Ethics; and in fact § 1 of this chapter as it at present stands directly contradicts the corresponding passage in the former edition." I am not at all sure the second sentence says what my friend takes it to say, and a reader of my draft has compounded my unease by scrawling in the margin, next to my friend's summary: 'desired feeling, or desirable feeling? check which one Sidgwick says.' I am inclined to trust my friend — he is usually careful — but I would rather get this right than defer to him.

So: how fair is the summary? Please tell me which parts a careful reader of Sidgwick would accept, which are common simplifications one might let pass in a lecture but not in print, and which ascribe to Sidgwick positions he explicitly argued against. In particular: does he unify pleasures through desire in the way my friend describes, or is that the very view the chapter exists to refute? Does the heterogeneity of pleasures, properly understood, carry the ethical moral my friend draws about a single scale and the limits of hedonism? And what would Sidgwick's actual position imply for my fictional device — is my philosopher right that pleasure in general names nothing a machine could deliver, or does Sidgwick's own way of unifying pleasures leave the device a coherent possibility?

Please commit. Where scholars read Sidgwick differently, say which reading you judge better supported and defend it, rather than offering me a menu; and end with a firm ruling on whether I may put my friend's summary into the philosopher's mouth as a reliable account, together with a corrected version of three or four sentences that the character could plausibly speak if the ruling is no.

Structure your answer in the following sections, in this order: (1) The Preface Passages — about 120 words on what Sidgwick does and does not say changed between editions, and whether the passages bear out my friend's warning; (2) What the Summary Gets Right — about 120 words; (3) Where It Distorts — about 250 words, separating harmless simplifications from positions Sidgwick rejected; (4) The Moral for Hedonism and the Machine — about 180 words on whether the ethical conclusion follows and what a Sidgwickian would say of the device; (5) Verdict — about 100 words, including the corrected summary for my character.
