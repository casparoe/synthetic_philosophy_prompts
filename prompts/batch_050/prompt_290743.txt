I sit on my university's honor council (a mixed student-faculty panel), and a case has landed on our docket that I have to write the draft opinion for. A philosophy professor, teaching a seminar on hate speech, read aloud — three times, slowly, to make sure everyone heard the phonetics — an anti-Black slur that appears in a 1950s court transcript the class was analyzing. He also wrote it on the whiteboard in full and left it up for the whole two-hour session. Two students filed a complaint under our harassment policy, which bans "derogatory epithets directed at a person or group on the basis of race." Nobody claims he aimed the word at anyone: it's in the source text, and the whole point of the session was to figure out how that word does its damage in the first place.

The professor's defense is basically the use-mention distinction: he was talking about the word, not wielding it, and you can't study slurs while being forbidden to produce them. The complainants say the distinction is a philosopher's dodge — the word landed on them the same whether it was quoted or hurled, and hearing it from a white professor's mouth, repeated, on the board for two hours with no warning, did something a silent reading assignment wouldn't have.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It needs to do a few things: (1) reccommend a concrete outcome — complaint sustained or dismissed, and if sustained, what remedy (a reprimand? mandatory syllabus language? a classroom policy on reading slurs aloud?), and if dismissed, whether we still adopt some guidance going forward. (2) Give the strongest honest version of the losing side: if you dismiss, say what's right in the complaint that quotation-marks don't neutralize a slur; if you sustain, say what it costs when a university can't read its own primary sources out loud. (3) Actually engage the theory, not just the feelings. Here's the puzzle I want worked through: if a slur's derogation is part of its literal semantic content, then even mentions should carry it and the professor has no real defense — but then neither does the transcript, the syllabus, or the complaint itself, which also prints the word. If instead the offense is a taboo on utterance regardless of content, then mention is no refuge either, but the taboo has to be explained as more than circular ("it's forbidden because it's forbidden"). And if there's a principled third option where context, speaker, and audience do the work, spell out what it implies for this specific case rather than just gesturing at "context matters." Land somewhere definite — our council deadlocks if the opinion is mush.
