I am an undergraduate currently taking a course in formal epistemology, and I would like you to answer as a philosopher of science with particular expertise in Bayesian confirmation theory. Please think the issue through carefully before writing; I want depth and precision rather than a textbook survey.

In lectures we covered the problem of old evidence: because conditionalisation requires the evidence to have probability less than one, evidence an agent already possesses with certainty — the standard case being the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, which was known for decades before general relativity and was nonetheless taken to confirm it — cannot confirm a hypothesis on the standard Bayesian picture, since P(H|E) = P(H) whenever P(E) = 1. That much I understand. What I do not yet understand, and would like explained carefully, is the structure of the dialectic that follows.

First, the logical-learning responses. Garber and Jeffrey each proposed, in different ways, that what actually does the confirming is not the old evidence E itself but the agent's newly discovered logical fact that the hypothesis entails E. Please explain, at an intuitive but rigorous level: (a) exactly how this manoeuvre is supposed to restore confirmational credit to the old datum; (b) why critics object that it merely relocates the problem — on the grounds that the entailment, once learned, itself becomes old evidence for the next theory, and that the manoeuvre seems to presuppose an implausible picture of agents acquiring bare logical truths in isolation from everything else they believe; and (c) how that objection differs from the more familiar complaint that the proposal requires failures of logical omniscience to be represented inside the probability function, which orthodox Bayesianism cannot do.

Second, a question I cannot find treated head-on in my readings: is the problem of old evidence a problem for Bayesianism as a theory of confirmation, or only for conditionalisation as a rule of belief revision? Some writers defend a static or counterfactual Bayesianism on which we assess confirmation using the credences the agent would have had, or should have had, before learning E. Explain precisely what that proposal commits one to — in particular, whether it requires counterfactual credence functions to be well-defined and rationally constrained, and what epistemic machinery would have to do that constraining if the agent's actual history is allowed to be arbitrary.

Finally, I want a committed answer rather than a menu. Do you judge that the problem of old evidence forces a genuine revision of Bayesian confirmation theory — and if so, which of the available responses exacts the lowest theoretical cost — or is it a puzzle that dissolves once one is careful about what Bayesianism actually claims? Give your reasons, state the strongest consideration on the opposing side as fairly as you can, and explain why it does not move you. Please write in British English.
