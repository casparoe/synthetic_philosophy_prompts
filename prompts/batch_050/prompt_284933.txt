Okay so I teach medieval philosophy and every year in my Abelard unit I do this exercise where I take what I take to be the core machinery of his *Ethics* -- sin is in the consent, not the act, not the desire, not the pleasure, and coerced bodily compliance without mental consent is no sin at all -- and I run it over a bunch of hypothetical cases to show students how weird and interesting the view gets. I want you to stress-test whether my reconstruction is actually one coherent theory or whether I've been quietly sliding between two or three different theories this whole time.

Pretend you're a specialist in medieval ethics who also moonlights as a very fussy decision theorist. Think carefully, and before you commit to anything, brainstorm a list of the different precise rules that could generate my verdicts below. Don't just pick one and run with it -- I want the menu first.

My track record of verdicts (these are my hypotheticals, not cases from the text, so don't worry about matching anything he actually wrote):

1. A man fully, deliberately consents to seduce his neighbor's wife, arranges everything, and she simply never shows up. Verdict: fully guilty, exactly as guilty as if he'd succeeded. The thwarting adds and subtracts nothing.
2. A monk is physically overpowered and forced; his body does the deed, his mind never consents, and -- here's the part my students hate -- he even feels some involuntary bodily pleasure. Verdict: no sin. Pleasure isn't consent, and consent is all there is.
3. A servant is ordered by his master to kill an innocent man and does it, believing throughout that it's wrong but too afraid to refuse. He consents under pressure. Verdict: sin. Coercion that reaches the will is still consent; only case 2-style bypass of the will gets you off.
4. Soldiers execute a prisoner sincerely believing him to be a dangerous traitor whose execution the law demands; in fact he's innocent and the execution is a grave injustice. They consent fully to what they're doing. Verdict: no sin -- they consented to what they honestly took to be just, and they would have sinned *more* by sparing him against their own conscience.
5. Same setup as 4, except the soldiers privately doubt the man's guilt, consign their doubts, and go ahead anyway because it's easier. Verdict: sin. Now they're acting against their own conscience, and that's contempt, even though the act looks identical from the outside.

Here's the new case for next semester, and I genuinely don't know what my reconstruction says about it:

A moneylender in a town where everyone, including his confessors and teachers, has always taught that lending at modest interest to the poor is a work of mercy. He has never once doubted this. He deliberates, consents, and lends at interest with a clear conscience and genuine intent to help. Suppose for the sake of argument that usury really is wrong -- that his conscience is *erroneous on the permissive side*, not just on the obligatory side like in case 4.

Predict my verdict, or rather predict what the theory I've been running *has* to say about him. Then tell me: what rule have I actually been running across cases 1-5? Because I suspect there are at least two live candidates -- something like "sin tracks consent to what is in fact bad" versus "sin tracks contempt for one's own conscience, full stop" -- and maybe a third that cashes everything out in terms of the will alone. Say which of those rules each of my earlier verdicts supports, which ones they can't distinguish, and what each candidate predicts about the moneylender. If my track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say so honestly, give me your best guess about which rule is the most Abelardian of the bunch (just from the general shape of the view, no need for citations), and describe the one additional hypothetical case whose verdict would settle the matter for good.

Casual is fine, but be precise about the rules -- write each one out as an actual principle, not a vibe.
