I've been watching a lot of wildlife documentaries with my two young kids -- the big-budget, sweeping-score kind with calm, authoritative narration -- and something keeps nagging at me. The narration constantly describes what the animals on screen know, want, fear, and decide: the cheetah weighs up which gazelle is weakest, the meerkat on lookout duty trusts its companions to warn it, the mother bear worries that her cubs haven't learned to fish, the octopus hatches a plan to escape the shark. When I mentioned this to a friend who studied animal behavior, she invoked Morgan's canon, the old methodological principle from comparative psychology that an animal's behavior should not be interpreted as the product of a higher psychological faculty if it can be adequately explained by a lower one. Her complaint was that the narration routinely credits animals with deliberation, foresight, and even something like friendship, when associative learning, fixed action patterns, or simple heuristics would explain the same footage just as well. I pushed back that nobody takes the narration literally -- it's storytelling, a way of making forty minutes of animal footage emotionally legible to a general audience. She replied that this defense is too quick, and I've been turning the exchange over since.

So here is what I'd like you to do. Acting as a philosopher of mind with a strong background in cognitive ethology and the methodology of animal cognition research, analyze what this narration practice actually presupposes. First, reconstruct the implicit theory: under what view of animal minds, and of what mental-state language even does, would narrating a hunt in terms of intentions and deliberation be a straightforwardly legitimate thing to do? Consider at least three candidate readings -- that the narration literally attributes beliefs and desires; that it is harmless metaphor or as-if talk that no competent viewer misreads; and that it is a compressed functional description, where decides is shorthand for some flexible, information-sensitive control process that doesn't require propositional attitudes. For each, say whether Morgan's canon even applies to it, or whether the canon governs only scientific explanation and has no jurisdiction over television voiceover. Second, probe the practice for internal tensions: the same programs that freely narrate the mental lives of mammals often describe insects and fish in purely mechanical terms, and they occasionally hedge with phrases about what researchers believe while elsewhere stating mental states as flat fact -- does this inconsistency reveal anything about what the practice is really committed to? Third, consider the costs on both sides: what would a rigorously Morgan-compliant narration actually sound like, would it be unwatchable or unmakeable, and does that tell us something about whether the canon itself is the right standard here, or only about what audiences are for?

Please think carefully rather than reaching for a verdict in either direction. Before composing your answer, briefly brainstorm the strongest version of each side -- the charge that this kind of narration systematically misleads the public about animal minds, and the defense that it is a legitimate, even indispensable, mode of description. In your answer itself, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, note where it is philosophically underdetermined (that is, where several rival theories of animal cognition would license exactly the same voiceover), and end by saying what, if anything, this case teaches us about Morgan's canon itself -- whether the trouble lies with the narration, with the canon, or with the assumption that describing behavior and explaining it are the same activity.
