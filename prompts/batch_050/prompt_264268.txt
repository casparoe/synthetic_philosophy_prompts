I have strong intuitions about when punishment is justified and how much is deserved, and I want to know whether any single principle, or any coherent hybrid of retributivist and consequentialist thinking, captures them all -- and if not, which of my verdicts is the weakest link. Here are my cases and verdicts:

1. A man commits a serious assault, is never caught, and over twenty years genuinely reforms: remorse, restitution to the victim's family, an exemplary life. Punishing him now would deter no one (the crime is unknown) and protect no one. My verdict: he should still be punished, though perhaps less than at the time of the offense.

2. A sheriff could frame an innocent drifter for an unsolved murder and thereby credibly deter a wave of violence that will otherwise kill several people. My verdict: absolutely not, no matter the body count. This is not a close call for me.

3. Two drivers are equally negligent in identical conditions; one's victim happens to step into the road and is killed, the other's does not. My verdict: they deserve the same punishment. What matters is what they did and risked, not how the dice fell.

4. Two shooters each fire with intent to kill; one's bullet strikes, the other's misses by chance. My verdict, consistent with (3): equal punishment. Attempt and success with the same mens rea stand or fall together.

5. A burglar is badly injured in the course of the burglary -- paralyzed when a roof collapses -- through no one's doing but chance and his own choices. My verdict: this should count for something at sentencing. Not full punishment as if nothing had happened. I realize this sits uneasily with (3) and (4), which is partly why I'm asking.

6. A jurisdiction doubles penalties for a common offense purely because the offense has become common, not because offenders have become more culpable. My verdict: justified, even though identical offenders are now punished more harshly than identical offenders last year.

7. A first-time offender and a repeat offender commit the same crime. My verdict: the repeat offender may be punished somewhat more, but not endlessly more -- at some point the increment for prior record has to level off, though I can't say why.

My tentative principle is something like: desert sets the ceiling and floor of permissible punishment, and consequences may operate only within that band. Test that against my cases. I expect it will have trouble with at least (1), (6), and (7). If it fails, offer me the best repaired version you can construct -- or, if you think no band principle works, the best alternative -- and for each clause tell me which of my cases motivates it. Then give me its verdicts on cases I haven't considered: punishment of the already-reformed when punishment would in fact deter; collective punishment where the guilty individual can't be identified but the group can; and very lenient treatment of the genuinely dangerous but blameless (say, violence caused by a brain tumor). Finally, tell me which one of my seven verdicts you think a coherent theory most plausibly has to sacrifice, and how confident you are in that judgment. I'd also like you to flag, throughout, how confident you are in each claim you make about what my verdicts commit me to, and to single out the single point in your analysis you are least sure of.
