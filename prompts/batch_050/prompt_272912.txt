In a reading group on early Chinese thought, a friend who audited a course last year gave us her summary of the Mencius–Xunzi dispute, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it in my own paper. Here is what she said:

"Mencius and Xunzi are usually presented as opposites — Mencius says human nature is good, Xunzi says it is bad — but the opposition is mostly verbal. Mencius's famous thought experiments, like the child about to fall into a well, show that everyone will reliably act morally even without training, and Xunzi would not have denied that. What Xunzi meant by calling nature 'bad' is simply that people are born selfish and antisocial, which is why he thought morality is wei — artifice, something fabricated and worn like a mask — and why he put all the weight on ritual, law, and punishment to keep our natural impulses in check. Mencius concedes the crucial point himself in the water passage: our goodness is only a direction, and whether we actually end up good depends entirely on circumstances forcing us one way or another. Once you see that, the two are really saying the same thing — raw nature plus environmental shaping — and the whole debate dissolves into a difference of emphasis and temperament."

She pointed us to this passage from the Gaozi I chapter of the Mengzi, in James Legge's translation, as the proof text for the last claim: "Water indeed will flow indifferently to the east or west, but will it flow indifferently up or down? The tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards. There are none but have this tendency to good, just as all water flows downwards. Now by striking water and causing it to leap up, you may make it go over your forehead, and, by damming and leading it you may force it up a hill - but are such movements according to the nature of water? It is the force applied which causes them. When men are made to do what is not good, their nature is dealt with in this way."

My own sense is that the summary is mostly competent but that she has made the two thinkers agree more than they do, though I cannot say precisely where the elision happens. Could you audit it for me? Specifically: which parts of her characterization of Mencius and of Xunzi are accurate, which are common simplifications a specialist would qualify, and which are outright misreadings? Does the water passage actually say what she needs it to say — that for Mencius, moral outcomes are wholly a matter of environmental pressure — or is she conflating two claims the passage keeps apart? And finally: if the inaccurate parts are corrected, does her conclusion that the dispute "dissolves into a difference of emphasis" still stand, or does a genuine disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi survive? Please identify the strongest version of that disagreement on the corrected reading.
