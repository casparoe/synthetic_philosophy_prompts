After years of reading LessWrong, Slate Star Codex, and the surrounding blogosphere, I've noticed that people in those circles (myself included) tend to absorb a set of metaethical commitments piecemeal -- a bit of Hume here, a bit of Moore there, some Sam Harris from the Moral Landscape era -- without ever checking whether the package is coherent. I got into a long argument with a friend who does bioethics, and she claimed my views contradict themselves, but she couldn't tell me exactly where. So I'd like an audit. Here is what I currently hold:

(a) Hume's is-ought gap is real and strict: no argument whose premises are entirely descriptive can validly yield a normative conclusion, so every sound moral argument must contain at least one irreducibly normative premise. (b) Moore's open-question argument succeeds: any attempted naturalistic definition of "good" fails, because for any natural property N you propose, "things are N, but are they good?" remains a genuinely open, non-trivial question -- which shows "good" can't just mean N. (c) I nonetheless endorse the Harris-style thesis: once we grant that morality is about the flourishing of conscious creatures, every remaining moral question is empirical, and science can in principle determine human values. (d) Normative concepts in medicine are unproblematic: "healthy" is effectively an empirical notion, doctors simply discover what is healthy, and ethics should aspire to the same status as medicine. (e) I'm also attracted to moral error theory -- all positive first-order moral claims are false -- and I take this to be fully compatible with (a), since (a) is merely a point about logical form.

My friend insists something in here doesn't fit, and I want specifics rather than a vibe. Please go through the set and tell me: which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension but dissolve on inspection, and for each genuine tension, how severe it is and what the least costly revision would be -- naming exactly which commitment has to go and what I lose by dropping it. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and please don't just flag everything; part of what I want to know is which of my friend's objections wouldn't actually work. One constraint on your method: don't resolve anything by appeal to authority or by noting that some famous philosopher disagreed with me; I want the tensions traced structurally, in terms of what each commitment's standard motivation commits me to.

End your answer with a final line of the form \answer{...} naming the pair of commitments involved in the single most severe tension and the one commitment you'd advise me to abandon -- for example, \answer{(x) vs (y); drop (y)}.
