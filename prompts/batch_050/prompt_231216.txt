Here's the conceit I need dramatised. Marguerite has a pet theory (mine really, but it's hers in the book) that what everyone calls "the alignment problem" is actually three different problems wearing the same coat:

1. the instruction problem — getting a system to do what you literally tell it;
2. the intention problem — getting it to do what you actually meant, which is never fully contained in the instruction;
3. the allegiance problem — the question of whose meaning counts, since even a perfectly obedient system just amplifies whoever holds the pen, and the people holding the pen are, well, us.

Marguerite has to take a side in this speech on whether those are genuinely one problem with three faces, or three seperate problems that keep getting lumped together — and she suspects the lumping is exactly how labs convince themselves they're making progress whilst only ever chipping at problem 1.

So here's the job. You're a brilliant speechwriter who moonlights as a philosopher of AI — think carefully about this one, don't rush it. First, jot down some rough brainstorm notes sketching the strongest honest case for each side (one problem vs three problems), then commit to whichever you find more defensible and write Marguerite's speech arguing for it. About eight minutes spoken, so 1,000–1,200 words.

A few constraints from the scene as I've written it:

- The argument has to do real structural work — she's staking her professional reputation here, not doing vague thought-leader decoration.
- It has to land for both halves of the room. The researchers should hear rigour, no hand-waving past the hard bits; her daughter should be able to follow every single sentence.
- No citations, no name-dropping papers or reading lists — she's speaking without notes, and honestly nobody in a novel ever gives references.
