I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and since my dissertation touches on the functions debate I've been keeping a running record of how my advisor — who leads our graduate seminar on teleological notions in the life sciences — rules on contested cases of function attribution. Her verdicts so far, as I've noted them down, are these. First, a healthy mammalian heart: pumping blood is one of its functions; producing the thumping sound is not, even though the sound reliably helps clinicians diagnose heart disease. Second, the human nose's holding up of eyeglasses: not a function of the nose, however useful it happens to be. Third, a heart with a severe congenital defect that has never pumped blood and never will: pumping is still its function — the organ is malfunctioning, not functionless. Fourth, a bacterium in which a mutation arose this very generation, producing an enzyme that happens to neutralize an antibiotic in its environment and thereby greatly improves its survival prospects: neutralizing the antibiotic is not (yet) a function of that enzyme; it is merely a fortunate accident. Fifth, feathers in a bird lineage whose distant ancestors were selected for insulation but which, for the last several million years, have been maintained in the population by selection for flight: flight is now a function of the feathers, whatever the original story was.

Here is the new case I want a prediction about. A population of fish has spent tens of thousands of generations in completely lightless caves. Their eyes still begin to develop in the embryo but degenerate before adulthood; no adult in the population can see, and vision plays no role in their survival. In the ancestral, surface-dwelling population, eyes were of course selected for vision. One further complication: the degenerate eye tissue continues to secrete a small amount of a growth factor that modestly accelerates skull development in the embryo, and it does so in every member of the population.

Please do four things. First, reconstruct the two or three most plausible general rules for function attribution that are jointly consistent with all five verdicts above, stating each rule precisely enough to have teeth — and if a familiar rule from the literature has already been eliminated by the record, note in passing which verdict eliminated it. Second, say what each surviving rule predicts about the cavefish case: both whether seeing is a function of these eyes and, where the rule has something to say about it, whether secreting the growth factor is. Third, give me your best guess at my advisor's actual verdict, with your reasons for thinking she is running that rule rather than the alternatives, and flag honestly where the track record underdetermines the choice. Fourth, describe one further hypothetical case — ideally one I could plausibly raise at next week's seminar — whose verdict would discriminate between the two rules you find most credible. Close with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing your predicted verdict on the cavefish eyes (function of seeing: yes or no), followed by a dash and the name of the rule you take her to be applying.
