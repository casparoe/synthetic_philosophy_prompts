I'm helping run a discussion section for a course on formal epistemology, and we've just reached the week on anthropic reasoning. The doomsday argument has predictably split the students: roughly, the argument observes that about 100+ billion humans have lived so far, and notes that if humanity were to persist at anything like current birth rates for hundreds of thousands of years, then you and I would be implausibly early members of the total human sequence -- whereas if extinction arrives within the next few centuries, our position near the beginning is unremarkable. By a Bayesian update on one's own birth rank, doom soon becomes much more probable than naive extrapolation suggests. Half the students find this airtight; the other half find it obviously fishy, and neither half can articulate to the other where the disagreement actually lives.

My own understanding is that the argument's cogency hinges on a choice between two principles for handling self-locating evidence. Under something like the self-sampling assumption -- reason as if you are a random draw from the observers in your reference class -- the birth-rank update goes through and doom gets boosted. Under something like the self-indication assumption -- the mere fact of your existence is evidence favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist -- there's a prior tilt toward enormously populous futures, and this tilt roughly cancels the doomsday update, since populous futures were already more likely to contain you at all. What I'd like explained, at a gut level rather than via formalism, is this: first, why these two principles, which agree in most everyday cases, diverge so dramatically here; second, whether the cancellation is exact or merely approximate, and what that depends on (e.g., how wide the range of possible population sizes is); and third, whether the doomsday argument can be reformulated in a way that doesn't presuppose the contested principle -- for instance, by careful choice of reference class or by conditioning on something other than birth rank.

Please structure the answer as an explanation I could relay to the section, using a concrete toy model (a small number of urns or civilizations with stipulated sizes is fine) to make the two updates visible side by side. Avoid leaning on citations; I want the reasoning itself. Where the two principles genuinely clash, take a position on which handles the doomsday case better and defend it against the strongest objection. End with a final line of the form \answer{SSA}, \answer{SIA}, or \answer{neither}, indicating which principle you judge more defensible for this specific problem.
