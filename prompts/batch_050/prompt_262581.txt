I am a retired experimental physicist -- forty years in condensed matter, now filling my days with philosophy reading groups at the university in town, where I am very much the amateur. Next month I have talked myself into giving a forty-minute presentation at the philosophy department's graduate colloquium, followed by questions, and I want to go in with my eyes open.

My thesis is that the simulation argument, taken seriously, undermines what I understand philosophers to call fanaticism about the far future: the willingness to let a tiny probability of an astronomically large and long-lived future civilization dominate present-day moral calculation. My argument runs through a mechanism I am calling the computational-load discount. If we are likely to be simulated, then the simulations that get run are the ones their operators can afford and have reason to run; a future containing trillions of sentient minds spread over eons is vastly more expensive to instantiate than a short or sparse one, and plausibly gets approximated, truncated, or never run at all once it has served the simulators' purpose. So the probability that a future is actually instantiated falls as its population and duration rise -- which means the very futures whose expected value does all the work in fanatical reasoning are the ones the simulation hypothesis makes least probable. I conclude that one cannot consistently assign substantial credence to being simulated and still let enormous far-future populations drive one's decisions.

The audience, as I understand it from attending the colloquium: one faculty member who works on anthropic reasoning and knows the simulation argument literature far better than I ever will; a population ethicist with total-utilitarian sympathies; two formal epistemologists who will pounce on any loose talk of probabilities; a few metaphysicians who visibly regard this whole genre of question as a waste of a Thursday afternoon; and a dozen graduate students of mixed commitments. Essentially no one there knows physics, so my home turf buys me little.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience -- not the ones I would get from a room of physicists -- ordered by how much damage each would do to the thesis, each phrased the way its likely asker would actually phrase it, with a reply I can give in about two minutes. For each reply, mark what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and where the reply is softest. Flag any place where my physicist's instincts -- treating simulations as approximations, treating computational resources as plainly finite, treating instantiated and merely-described worlds as different categories -- are liabilities in front of this audience rather than assets. And tell me plainly which objection I am hoping nobody asks, so I can decide whether I have an honest answer to it before the talk rather than after.
