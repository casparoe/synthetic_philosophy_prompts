I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I keep hearing 'correlation is not causation' deployed to end arguments — most recently by a colleague dismissing an epidemiological paper that moved from observational data to a policy recommendation without a randomized trial. I would like the slogan taken apart rather than repeated. (i) Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, from the near-trivial (a purely logical point about what correlation entails) through the methodological (claims about which study designs license causal inference) to genuinely substantive ones (for instance, a Reichenbach-style thesis about what must explain a correlation). (ii) Give a verdict on each: platitude, defensible but limited, or false — noting that the slogan is sometimes used to deny that correlation is any evidence for causation at all, which seems plainly wrong but I want the argument, not just the verdict. (iii) Explain whether and how these verdicts shift depending on whether one adopts a counterfactual or an interventionist account of causation; my suspicion is that interventionism makes the slogan more defensible as a warning about extrapolating from observed populations to hypothetical interventions, but I would like that suspicion checked rather than confirmed. (iv) Say which reading, if any, would actually license the dismissive use I encountered, and whether the slogan as commonly wielded does real argumentative work or merely signals skepticism. Please be explicit throughout about which points are settled and which are genuinely contested among people working on causal inference and the philosophy of causation, rather than presenting a contested methodological stance as the consensus.
