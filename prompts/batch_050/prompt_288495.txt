**Question:** Zhuangzi responds to his wife's death by drumming on a tub and singing, and elsewhere (the four masters of Chapter 6, the skull episode) treats death with equanimity or even playfulness. Yet Zhuangzi does not seem to advocate emotional numbness generally. Reconstruct his view: on what grounds does he refuse to grieve, and does his position amount to a proof that death is not bad, or something more modest?

**Student's answer:** Zhuangzi refuses to grieve because death, for him, is simply one more transformation of the same qi that constitutes all things. When his wife dies, he reasons that she had no body, then a body, then life, and now death — a sequence like the succession of the seasons — and concludes that wailing would show he failed to understand this natural process. So his refusal to grieve is intellectual: grief is a symptom of ignorance about change, not a feeling to be suppressed. His general stance is that we lack the knowledge to rank life above death. Just as the butterfly dream suggests the dreaming self might wake into another existence, death may be a waking-up, so preferring life is like a child dreading a journey it has never taken. He thus shows that death is not bad and that the sage, having personally achieved the state in which life and death are equalized, will simply never feel grief again — Zhuangzi himself, he reports, felt none even at the moment of his wife's death. This view also implies personal survival: since the transformations never end, something of the person continues, which is why later Daoist religion could develop its immortality teachings directly out of Zhuangzi's philosophy.
