I write a column on science and ideas, and a colleague has pitched me a piece built around the following argument about the Darwinian theory of culture — the view that ideas, catchphrases, practices and styles spread through populations by imitation, and that their prevalence is explained by selection for whatever makes them good at getting transmitted, much as biological traits are explained by selection for whatever aids survival and reproduction. I want to know whether the pitch survives scrutiny. Here is his argument, as faithfully as I can render it:

(1) If a cultural item is widespread, that fact calls for explanation, and the relevant explanation is whatever made the item good at spreading; differential replication is the engine of cultural prevalence.

(2) What makes an item good at spreading is its effect on transmission — catchiness, emotional charge, the status it confers on those who pass it on — not its truth or its usefulness to the people who hold it. Truth and usefulness matter only insofar as they happen to boost transmission.

(3) So for any widely held belief, the default explanation of its acceptance is its transmission-fitness rather than its truth.

(4) Belief in the Darwinian theory of culture is itself a belief held widely within certain intellectual circles, and it spreads within those circles largely by imitation — people pick it up from colleagues, popular books and conference talks, much as they pick up anything else.

(5) Therefore the default explanation of why those intellectuals accept the theory is its transmission-fitness, not its truth.

(6) But then taking the theory seriously gives us standing reason to distrust the theory: by its own lights, its acceptance is to be explained in terms that are indifferent to whether it is right.

His conclusion is that the theory saws off the branch it sits on, and that any account of culture as a Darwinian process self-destructs in the same way. My instinct is that the argument proves too much: if step (3) is sound in full generality, we would apparently have to treat every widespread belief — including elementary arithmetic and the germ theory of disease — as accepted primarily for transmission reasons, which is absurd. Yet the reasoning looks valid on its face, so one of the premises presumably has to go, and I cannot see which one is doing the damage. It may help to know that my colleague would most like to keep (1) and (2), since he regards them as the theory's core commitments; he is flexible about the rest.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, please say whether the trouble lies in the move from explaining a belief's prevalence to assessing its warrant, in the assumption that transmission-fitness and truth are competitors rather than potential allies, or somewhere I have not thought to look — and indicate how a defender of my colleague's argument would respond to your diagnosis. Finally, I would like an honest measure of conviction: state how confident you are in the diagnosis you settle on, and single out the specific point in your answer about which you are least sure.
