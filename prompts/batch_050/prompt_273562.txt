ok, consistency check request before I embarass myself at my reading group next week. I've been assembling my moral views, which are mostly stolen from Scanlon, and I want to know if the package actually hangs together:

1. An act is wrong iff it would be disallowed by any principle that no one could reasonably reject. So yes, I'm basicly a contractualist.
2. I accept the individualist restriction on reasonable rejection: complaints have to be raised from the standpoint of particular individuals. There are no impersonal complaints, and there is defintely no summing of burdens across different people the way utilitarians do.
3. But: in the standard rescue case where I can save either one stranger or five strangers from drowning and everyone involved is morally on a par, the numbers count, and I ought to save the five. And I think contractualism can deliver this verdict without smuggling aggregation back in.
4. I'm fine with policies that impose tiny risks on huge populations when the benefits warrant it, e.g. rolling out a vaccine that will predictably cause a handful of severe reactions in people nobody can identify in advance. My reasoning is that no individual's ex ante risk was large enough to ground a reasonable rejection of the policy.
5. In a straight one-versus-one rescue where both victims face the same threat, I think flipping a coin is fine, and I also think a parent may save their own child over a stranger's child facing the identical danger.

Which of these are genuinely in tension with each other and which only look like it? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me. Please don't do the thing where you just gesture at everything being vaguely problematic.
