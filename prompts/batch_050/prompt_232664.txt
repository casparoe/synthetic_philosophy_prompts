I am a PhD student working on philosophy of mind, and I keep getting stuck trying to reverse-engineer my supervisor's view of personal identity from the verdicts she gives on thought experiments in our meetings. Her cases are variations on the standard teletransportation scenarios from the personal-identity literature. So far she has committed to the following:

(1) Standard teletransportation: your brain and body are scanned and destroyed, and a molecule-for-molecule replica is assembled on Mars from new matter, with all your memories and character. Her verdict: you die; the person on Mars is a perfect successor, not you.

(2) Gradual replacement: over two years, your neurons are replaced one at a time by functionally identical synthetic neurons, while you remain awake, go to work, and notice nothing. At the end, none of your original brain matter remains. Her verdict: you survive, the same person throughout.

(3) Fission: your two brain hemispheres are transplanted into two different bodies, each of which wakes up fully psychologically continuous with you. Her verdict: since identity cannot branch, you strictly survive as neither, though everything that matters to you in ordinary survival continues twice over.

Here is the new case I want to put to her, which I call piecemeal teleportation. You step into the scanner, but instead of being destroyed all at once, your brain is scanned and destroyed one cubic millimeter at a time. As each chunk is destroyed, the corresponding chunk of the Mars replica is constructed and immediately begins functioning, networked seamlessly with your remaining original tissue, so that there is never a moment at which your overall pattern of mental activity lapses. At the end of the process, a complete functioning replica exists on Mars and nothing is left on Earth.

My question: what will she say about this case, and why? I am less interested in your own verdict than in the reconstruction. Please lay out the plausible rules that jointly fit verdicts (1)-(3) -- I can see at least two (one on which survival requires uninterrupted functional and organizational continuity regardless of what realizes it or where, and one on which survival requires a single continuously embodied process, so that even gradual replication at a distance yields only a successor) -- say what each predicts about piecemeal teleportation, give your best guess at her verdict with reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and tell me what further case would discriminate between the rules that agree on all of (1)-(3) and on the new case.

One request about calibration: please distinguish carefully between what is genuinely settled here (for instance, the logical point that identity cannot branch, or what the thought experiments stipulate) and what remains contested in the personal-identity literature. In particular, do not present any theory of survival -- psychological-continuity, bodily, or otherwise -- as the consensus view when it is not, and flag where my supervisor's pattern of verdicts may simply be unstable rather than an expression of a coherent underlying rule.
