I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and a running argument between two colleagues of mine has followed me home. We were discussing whether anything could, even in principle, out-compute an ordinary computer given unlimited time and memory, and I looked up the source everyone gestures at: A. M. Turing's 1936 paper On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem. Early in the paper, having described his abstract machine's operations of writing, erasing, scanning, and shifting one square at a time, Turing writes: "It is my contention that these operations include all those which are used in the computation of a number." My colleagues read this sentence in flatly incompatible ways. Priya, who works on quantum hardware, treats the so-called Church-Turing thesis as an empirical hypothesis about what physical systems can compute: a bold conjecture that might one day be refuted by a machine exploiting, say, closed timelike curves or some supertask arrangement, just as other confident claims about physics have been refuted. Marcus, our resident type-theory enthusiast, insists this is a category error: Turing was analysing the pre-theoretical notion of a human following a rote procedure with pencil and paper, and once that analysis is accepted the thesis is secure against anything physicists might discover, because a physics-defying device simply would not count as computation in the analysed sense. They have been talking past each other for months, and I suspect some of their disagreement is about words while some is genuine, but I cannot separate the two.

Draft the joint statement that Priya and Marcus could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I would like, first, a numbered section of claims both accept, which should include what Turing's paper actually established (the formal results, the equivalence with Church's lambda-definability) and what it did not and could not establish by proof; second, a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording the relevant side would endorse as a fair description of its own position; third, the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to concede ground, and for each crux a case, argument, or finding that both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance (for instance, how each would respond if a physically realisable device were shown to settle the halting problem, or if it were proved that no such device is consistent with our best physical theories). Where Turing's own procedure in the paper bears on a crux, say so, and say whose reading of his "contention" it supports. Finally, if you judge that part of their dispute is merely verbal, identify which part and explain why, in terms both would accept.
