I teach a weekly philosophy discussion seminar for a mixed group of undergraduates and community members — about 18 people, most without any formal training in decision theory, and we have a 90-minute slot. We recently finished a unit on practical rationality, and during a conversation after class a few participants insisted that any consistent person who prefers a sure gain over a slightly risky gamble must carry that preference through into every structurally similar choice. I want them to discover, in their own choices, why that's not so easy. Design an in-class activity built around the pair of choices at the heart of the Allais paradox: participants should face two decisions involving lottery-style payoffs (you can use points, candy, or grade-independent tokens rather than money), make their selections privately and sequentially without knowing the second decision is coming, and then — only after both choices are locked in — see the two decisions laid side by side so they can notice for themselves whether their pair of answers hangs together.

Please give me the full setup: exact payoff structures and probabilities simple enough to explain in two minutes, how to run the two rounds so that nobody realizes the choices are related (including what to tell participants the activity is "about"), how to collect and display the group's choices publicly so the tension becomes visible, and a rough timeline for the 90 minutes. Then give me the debrief: how to walk the group from what they actually did to the idea that a certain kind of consistency principle — which you should only name at the end, as the independence axiom of expected utility theory — rules out the combination most of them will have chosen, and how each of the two conflicting intuitions (the appeal of certainty on one hand, the "the difference is negligible" reasoning on the other) can be defended. I'd like the debrief to also raise the question of whether violating the axiom here is irrational or whether it reveals something the axiom misses, since several of my participants will want to defend their choices rather than recant them.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong: participants who pattern-match to "always take the safe option" or "always maximize expected value" and thus never produce the revealing combination, participants who do the math out loud and spoil the second round, and the person who will insist the probabilities were too small to care about. And be honest with me about what an activity like this can't capture — I suspect real-money stakes and the one-shot nature of the choices matter, and I want to know what I'm sacrificing by running it with tokens in a classroom.
