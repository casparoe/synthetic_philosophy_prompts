I have spent much of my career defending nonreductive physicalism about the mental, and I remain committed to it: mental properties are real, distinct from physical properties (multiply realized, not identical), and yet mental events genuinely cause physical outcomes in a world where every caused event has a sufficient physical cause. Like most people in my position, I treat Kim's causal exclusion argument as the principal threat, and I take the standard battery of replies — the rejection of the exclusion principle, the appeal to overdetermination being benign in realization cases, the claim that mental and physical causes are not competitors but related as different levels of description of one causal process — to succeed. But I am about to commit to this view in a book manuscript, and before I do I want a serious audit of my exposure rather than another round of reassurance. So: what would count against nonreductive physicalism, properly understood? Not the familiar opening moves of the exclusion argument, which I know, but the deeper pressure points.

Here is what I have in mind. First, which live debates in the metaphysics of causation does the view depend on, and how badly would it fare if those debates resolved the wrong way — for instance, if causation turned out to be fundamentally a matter of physical production rather than difference-making or counterfactual dependence, or if proportionality constraints on cause selection were shown to license mental causes only at the cost of licensing them everywhere, so that the view cannot explain why mental causation seems special? Second, the generalization worry: if the exclusion reasoning is unsound for the mental, it seems unsound for the biological, the chemical, and the geological too, and my replies had better deliver that result wholesale — but is there a principled stopping point, or does defending mental causation commit me to an inflationary picture on which every level of every special science is causally autonomous? Third, which positions on realization and multiple realizability would, if established, quietly collapse the view into either reductionism or a form of dualism I would not accept?

Please rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed nonreductive physicalist — playing fair, not stonewalling — would concede it as genuinely counting against the view or dismiss it as resting on assumptions the view was never obliged to share. I would also like an honest verdict on the converse worry: whether the position, after two decades of qualification (two senses of causation, compatibilist readings of completeness, deflationary accounts of levels), still risks anything at all, or whether it has been insulated to the point where nothing could count against it — and if the latter, whether that should comfort me or alarm me. Personally, I would find it most persuasive if you could identify a consequence the view is committed to in some other domain — persistence, properties, or the causal structure of the special sciences — that I would balk at accepting.
