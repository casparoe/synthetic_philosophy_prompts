I run my school's philosophy club -- about fourteen students, grades ten through twelve, and we meet for roughly seventy minutes in a classroom where I have a laptop, a projector, and a whiteboard. Most members have never read any philosophy of mind, but a few of us recently got into a long argument after someone described the color phi phenomenon: when a red dot is flashed on one side of a screen and then a green dot is flashed on the other side, viewers report seeing a single dot move across and change color in the middle -- even though, at the moment the dot seems to turn green, the green dot hasn't appeared yet. Our argument was about whether the brain really experienced the color change before it could have known the second color, or whether the experience happened 'on time' and our memory of it was rewritten afterward. We couldn't find any experiment we could run that would settle it, which I gather is roughly the point philosophers make when they distinguish Orwellian revision (the experience was veridical but memory falsifies the record afterward) from Stalinesque revision (the brain stages a doctored version of events before experience happens at all).

Design an activity for my club that makes everyone feel the force of this puzzle rather than just hear about it. Ideally, participants should first undergo the illusion themselves in a controlled way, then be led -- through their own attempts to pin down what they saw and when -- into realizing that no amount of introspective reporting seems able to distinguish 'I saw it change midway' from 'I remember it as having changed midway.' I want a precise run-of-show: what stimuli to prepare (including how to build or approximate the animation without special software, and a low-tech fallback in case the projector fails), how to sequence the trials, what to have participants write down and at exactly which moments, and how to structure any comparisons between immediate reports and later recollections. Please include at least one clever variation or control -- for instance, something involving a third flash, a delayed report, or a prediction task -- that deepens the bind rather than merely repeating it.

Then give me a debrief plan that maps specific moments of the activity onto the Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque distinction and onto the stronger claim that there may be no fact of the matter about which revision is correct. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- participants who don't see the illusion, timing glitches, a member who insists they can just introspect harder -- and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce from the inside and which part I'll simply have to state as a claim rather than stage as an experience.
