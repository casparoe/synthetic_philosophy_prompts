I've been turning over a pattern that keeps surfacing in arguments about whether the state should compensate people for disadvantages, and I'd like to know whether it has an established name in the luck egalitarianism literature or in nearby discussions of responsibility.

The pattern is this: a disadvantage is attributed to a free choice at one level of description and to circumstances beyond the person's control at a slightly deeper level, and the argument oscillates between the two without ever settling. Three examples. First, a town where smoking is far more common among people who grew up poor — when the question of funding lung-cancer care comes up, one side says smokers chose to smoke, the other says nobody chose the background that made smoking the available comfort and the default social ritual. Second, a man who left school at sixteen, which looks like his decision, until you learn that no one in his family or neighborhood ever presented staying in school as a live option, so the 'choice' was made inside a menu he didn't design. Third, someone who took a dangerous, well-paid job — a real choice, but made from a set of alternatives that was itself unchosen. In each case, zooming in on the moment of decision makes the outcome look like option luck; zooming out one frame makes it look like brute luck; and the two framings seem to nest indefinitely, so that every choice can be redescribed as the product of a prior unchosen situation, while every unchosen situation can be redescribed as containing at least one choice the person could have made differently.

I know this is adjacent to the brute luck/option luck distinction, but that distinction presupposes that you can settle which side of the line a case falls on, whereas my question is about cases where the level of description itself seems to do all the work. I also take it that this is not quite the harshness objection, which — as I understand it — concerns the consequences of holding people responsible even when they genuinely are responsible, not the instability of the responsibility attribution itself. What I want to know is: does this nesting structure, in which responsibility attributions keep sliding between adjacent levels of description and any proposed stopping point looks arbitrary, have a name of its own? If it does, give me the nearest candidates and say precisely how what I've described differs from each of them, so I can see which fits best. If nothing fits, say so plainly, coin a term, define it, and give me a few boundary cases — situations that look similar but fall outside the definition — so the edges are visible. The label matters less to me than the carving.

Finally, I'd like you to be explicit about where your answer is most likely to go wrong. If you conclude there is an established concept for this, tell me what feature of the pattern as I've described it would rule that candidate out, i.e., what would change your mind. If you end up coining something, tell me which nearby concept is most likely to already cover the case and how I would check whether it does.
