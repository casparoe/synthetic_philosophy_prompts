So I've been reading Hume's economic essays (Of Money and Of the Balance of Trade, from the Political Discourses) and I want to pressure-test an interpretation of him.

Here's the setup. In those essays Hume argues, roughly, that the absolute quantity of money in a country doesn't matter for its real wealth, since prices just scale up or down, and that trying to pile up specie through trade surpluses is self-defeating because the inflow raises domestic prices until the surplus reverses (the specie-flow mechanism, as it's now called). But he also concedes something subtler: a *rising* money supply can genuinely stimulate industry for a while, in the interval before prices catch up, because the new coins reach workers and merchants first and get them to work harder. So money is neutral in the long run but not in the transition.

Now consider a modern proposal that some governments have flirted with: create a national strategic reserve of a fixed-supply digital asset, and treat the stockpile itself as a source of national wealth and power, on the grounds that scarcity guarantees its value the way scarcity was supposed to guarantee the value of gold.

Please argue that Hume's own reasoning commits him to rejecting this proposal -- and, more interstingly, that it commits him to rejecting it for *two different* reasons that shouldn't be run together: one drawn from his long-run neutrality claim (a stockpile of intrinsically useless tokens is not wealth in the relevant sense, because wealth for Hume consists in labor and commodities, not in counters), and one drawn from the specie-flow logic (even if the asset held its value, a country that sucks up a scarce medium of exchange just reprices itself, so the "gain" is nominal). Along the way, address the hardest objection to your reading: his concession about the stimulus effects of a *growing* money supply seems to show he doesn't really believe money is a mere counter, so why couldn't a Humean say the reserve works the same way -- a rising asset price that enriches holders and stimulates industry during the transition? Tell me whether that objection actually maps onto the mechanism Hume describes, or whether it smuggles in something (say, wealth effects on consumption rather than cash-in-hand effects on effort) that his argument doesn't license.

Take a clear side: either Hume would condemn the reserve as mercantilism in a new costume, or his transitional non-neutrality gives him grounds to tolerate it as a one-off stimulus. Defend one of these, and say what the best version of the losing side gets right.
