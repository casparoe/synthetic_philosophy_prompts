I'm a retired geologist and lately I've been in a philosophy reading group at the library -- we're doing the Zhuangzi (Chuang Tzu in the old spelling), in James Legge's translation, which I found free online since it's ancient. Alongside it I picked up a popular book on "great skeptical arguments" that has a chapter on Zhuangzi and dreams, and I think the author botches it, but I can't quite put my finger on where.

Here's his gloss: "Zhuangzi's dream passages make the same point Descartes made two thousand years later: since nothing in a dream tells you it's a dream, you can never know you're awake, and so all of waking experience is untrustworthy. His conclusion is that rational inquiry is hopeless -- why argue about anything when it might all be a dream? -- and the famous butterfly story is meant to leave you stuck in that doubt."

Now, the actual text in front of me doesn't read like that to me. Right before the dream material, in Book II ("The Adjustment of Controversies"), Legge has: "When we sleep, the soul communicates with (what is external to us); when we awake, the body is set free." And the butterfly bit doesn't end in doubt as far as I can tell -- it ends with Zhuangzi saying there must be a distinction between himself and the butterfly, and calling this the transformation of things. That sounds less like "everything might be a dream, so despair" and more like a point about things turning into one another.

So: how fair is the popularizer's summary? Please go through it claim by claim -- does the quoted sentence fit someone who thinks dreams are deceptive pseudo-experiences, or does it suggest he's after something else entirely? Is the "same point as Descartes" line right, or does Zhuangzi's argument have a different target (I'd guess it's aimed at the Confucians and Mohists he keeps needling, not at the reliability of the senses)? And does the ending about transformation undercut the reading of the butterfly as a skeptical trap? I'm not a philosopher so tell me plainly where I'm wrong too -- maybe the popular book is fine and I'm overreading one sentance.
