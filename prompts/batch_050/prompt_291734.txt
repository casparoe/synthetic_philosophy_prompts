Write a short story (roughly 800–1200 words) that dramatizes the logic of no-trade theorems in economics — the idea that among rational, self-interested agents with common knowledge of each other's rationality, the mere willingness of a counterparty to trade is itself information that should make you refuse the trade.

The story should be set in a world of your choosing (a village market, a fishing fleet, a space colony's resource exchange, etc.), but its plot must turn on this epistemic trap concretely: a protagonist is offered a sequence of deals or bets, and must reason not about the underlying facts but about what each offer reveals about what the offerer knows. At some point, the protagonist should face a version of the deeper puzzle the theorems raise: if everyone reasons this way, the market should freeze entirely — yet markets in the real world are full of trade. The story should not resolve this puzzle with a tidy speech; instead, let the narrative itself explore some candidate escape (differences in risk tolerance, genuine private needs, noise traders, overconfidence, or honest disagreement about what counts as "information") and show its costs or limits through what happens to the characters.

Requirements:
- Do not mention economists, theorem names, or academic terminology inside the story; the reasoning should live in the characters' voices and choices, in terms natural to their world.
- Include at least one moment where a character updates their beliefs *because* an offer was made or withdrawn, not because of any new fact about the goods themselves.
- End on a note that leaves the reader genuinely uncertain whether the protagonist's final decision was rational caution or self-defeating paralysis.

After the story, add a brief afterword (150–250 words, clearly separated from the story) explaining which features of the no-trade result you chose to dramatize, which simplifications you made, and where you think the story's epistemic logic diverges from how real traders behave.
