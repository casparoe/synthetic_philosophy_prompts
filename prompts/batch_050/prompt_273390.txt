My grandfather died in the spring and when we were clearing out his flat I found this notebook he'd kept on and off since the 50s. Every few years there's a page of plans -- make foreman, pay off the house, get the kids through school, retire somewhere near the sea -- and almost every page ends with some version of "once that's sorted, then I can really start living," except you turn the page and it's just the next set of plans. It messed me up a little, honestly. Then a few weeks later a friend lent me this old essay, some Austrian philosopher from between the wars whose name I definately can't pronounce, arguing that this is exactly the trap: anything you do for the sake of a goal gets its meaning used up the moment the goal is reached, so a life built entirely out of goals is just and-then-what all the way down. His way out was that meaning lives in play -- things done purely for their own sake, the way kids play a game not to get anywhere but because the playing is the point -- and that people are most fully alive when their work or their love or whatever has that same quality of being its own reason.

So I want a poem for him that does this idea justice, and I want it to be a villanelle, for a specific reason. The two repeating lines should be the two sentences that quietly ran his whole life: one is the promise (something in the spirit of "once this is done, then I'll begin") and the other is the question the promise can never answer (something like "and after that, what then?"). Through the tercets those two lines should keep coming back the way they kept coming back in the notebook, each plan giving way to the next one, so the form itself starts to feel like the loop. And then in the final quatrain, where the two refrains finally have to stand side by side, I want them answered -- not argued with, just gently made obsolete -- by an image of play: kids in the street after dark who won't come in for dinner, or my grandfather dancing at my nan's birthday in 1974, something that was never heading anywhere and never needed to. The refrains can bend a little in that last stanza if that's what it takes.

It should work as a poem even for someone who's never heard of any of this, but if you do recognize the idea I'm half-remembering, the structure should feel like the argument itself: the returning lines are the trap, and only the ending steps out of it. No fixed length beyond what the form demands, and I'd rather it be a bit plainspoken than flowery -- he was a plainspoken man.
