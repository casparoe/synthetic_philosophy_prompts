A friend and I -- both longtime readers of the rationalist blogs, both sympathetic to consequentialism -- fell into an argument last week that we couldn't resolve, and I'd like your help settling the conceptual part of it. The occasion was Bentham's famous dismissal of natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts." My friend takes the line to be a straightforward triumph: rights-talk that floats free of law and institutions is literally meaningless, and anyone who appeals to rights people have independently of what any legal system grants them is making a category error, full stop. I pushed back that the slogan is doing several different jobs at once, and that the strong reading my friend wants is much less defensible than the weaker readings -- but when pressed, I couldn't cleanly separate the readings or say which ones a charitable Bentham actually needed. The rationalist-adjacent version of this comes up constantly: someone invokes the line (or its spirit) to dissolve appeals to rights in debates about animal welfare, AI moral patienthood, or whether individuals have claims against their own governments, and the conversation stalls because nobody specifies what exactly is being denied.

So: lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that "nonsense upon stilts" could be expressing. I'm imagining a spectrum, but please correct my taxonomy if it's wrong: at one end, something like a semantic claim that rights-language outside positive law is strictly meaningless, the way talk of obligations created by a nonexistent authority might be; then a metaphysical claim that there are no such entities as pre-legal rights, whatever the words mean; then a deflationary conceptual claim that rights just are legal artifacts, so that "natural right" is a contradiction in terms like "unowned property"; then a purely normative claim that, whatever natural rights would be, no appeal to them should move us against what utility recommends; and finally a political-diagnostic claim that natural-rights rhetoric functions in practice as a way of dressing up one's policy preferences as unquestionable, which is a claim about what the language does rather than about whether anything it says is true. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive defensible position, or likely false -- and, crucially, which readings are available to a utilitarian without circularity, since a utilitarian can't argue that natural rights don't exist on the ground that recognizing them would have bad consequences.

Then do the two harder steps. First, for each reading, say whether it would actually support the use my friend (and the internet generally) puts the slogan to, namely shutting down an argument in which someone appeals to a right that no current legal system recognizes. I suspect most of the defensible readings leave that move unsupported, and I want to know if I'm right. Second, steelman the natural-rights side at its best rather than its worst: if we read talk of natural rights as a way of marking especially weighty or especially general interests that any well-designed legal system ought to protect -- a kind of compressed counsel to legislators rather than a metaphysical posit -- does anything in Bentham's critique still bite? Please flag explicitly where you've had to reconstruct rather than report Bentham's own argument, since I haven't read the primary text and don't want to mistake a plausible reconstruction for the historical view.
