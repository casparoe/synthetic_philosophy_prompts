I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book where the whole plot turns on whether a character is actually good or just reliably does good-looking things, and I've realized I don't have a working theory of the difference. My editor keeps asking in the margins whether this or that act is really admirable, and I keep not knowing how to answer. So before I read any more ethics, I want the spec: what would a theory of moral worth -- of when doing the right thing redounds to the agent's credit because she did it for the right reasons -- actually have to deliver?

Give me the requirements list. Some cases I already suspect it has to handle, but add your own: the shopkeeper who deals honestly only because cheating is bad for business; the character whose motives are genuinely mixed (saves her rival partly from compassion, partly so the rival owes her); the person who does the right thing for a reason that's false -- she thinks the act is required by some inherited rulebook she'd disown on reflection, but the act happens to be right anyway; the Huck Finn type whose considered moral beliefs are rotten and who does the right thing against them, out of a sympathy he regards as weakness; and the one my book actually needs, the character who would have behaved identically whatever the right thing turned out to be -- the right-making features of the situation aren't doing any work in her psychology, she's just standing where decency happens to be. For each requirement, the case behind it and why a theory that fails it is useless for building people.

Then the distinctions it can't collapse: acting in accordance with the right vs from the right; the reasons there are vs the reasons that actually move somebody; whether it's the act that's worthy or the agent who gets the credit; and whatever the right way is to handle the thing my characters keep doing, where they care about the person infront of them rather than about morality as such -- I gather some theories treat that as one thought too many and others treat it as the only motivation worth having, and I want a theory that at least takes a stand. Also: does the theory have to say anything about whether worth comes in degrees, or can it stay binary?

Two closers. First, which single requirement on your list knocks out the most standard contenders, and is there a pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied? Second, briefly -- genuinely briefly -- score the main pictures against the list: the Kantian from-duty view, the good-motive or sentimentalist view, and the reasons-responsiveness family. Keep the scoring thin; the list is what I'm going to build characters out of.
