I direct a small private foundation with an annual grantmaking budget of about $12 million, and we have a problem that our bylaws never anticipated. Our three program officers hold genuinely divergent moral views, not just different hunches about cost-effectiveness. One is a fairly strict animal-welfare prioritizer who thinks farmed-animal suffering dominates everything else; one is committed to near-term global health; the third takes existential risk and the long-run future seriously. Every allocation meeting degenerates into each person relitigating first principles, and last year we deadlocked so badly that the board parked a third of the budget in treasury bills, which everyone agreed was the worst outcome on any of our views. A colleague in effective altruism circles told me this is exactly the situation the literature on "moral trade" is meant for — the idea that agents with different values can often both do better by their own lights through compromise, budget-splitting, or mutual concessions than by fighting for winner-take-all — but I've only ever seen it discussed abstractly, never turned into something a grants committee can run at 4 p.m. on a Tuesday.

Please write a decision procedure we can actually use to allocate the annual budget across our three portfolios, grounded in what the moral-trade idea really does and does not license. The procedure should be a sequence of steps we run in order at each allocation meeting: what question each step asks, what counts as a sufficient answer before moving on, and where the procedure stops and hands the decision to the board as a judgment call rather than pretending to settle it. It needs to handle the cases where trade is easy (pure differences in cause weighting, where a proportional split may dominate) as well as the cases where the theory gets uncomfortable — and I want you to be specific about these: one officer believes another's preferred cause is not merely less valuable but actively harmful, so "compromise" means funding something she regards as wrongdoing; one officer claims his cause is so overwhelmingly important that any split is a catastrophe, i.e., he has no incentive to trade honestly; and one officer privately suspects the others' cost-effectiveness estimates are inflated but can't prove it. Say what the procedure does in each case, even if the honest answer is "the procedure cannot fix this, and here is the meeting protocol for when it happens."

Then apply the procedure to two worked cases: (a) a straightforward request to split $3 million between deworming programs and corporate cage-free campaigns, where the disagreement is only about weighting; and (b) a harder one where the animal-welfare officer asks us to fund advocacy against a farming practice, and the global-health officer objects that the same advocacy, if successful, raises food prices in regions where she funds nutrition programs — so the trade runs through an actual empirical and moral conflict, not just divergent values. One of these should produce an allocation I won't like; tell me which and why the procedure recommends it anyway.

Format: give the procedure itself in prose first. Then summarize the steps as a table with columns Step, Question to Answer, Sufficient Answer Looks Like, Failure Mode, and Judgment Call? (yes/no, with a one-line reason if yes). After the worked cases, include a JSON object with one field per case (case name), each containing: outcome (a short verdict), allocation (the recommended split), dominant_consideration (the step of the procedure that decided it), and asker_will_dislike (true/false with a one-sentence justification).
