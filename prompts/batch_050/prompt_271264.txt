Build me a thought experiment in which honoring a sunk cost is clearly the rational thing to do -- not excusable, not understandable, actually rational. Constraints: the setting should be realistic (a small business, a grad student's research project, a house renovation, something like that -- no demons, no perfect predictors), the scenario itself should be one paragraph, and the money/time/effort has to be genuinely sunk, i.e. unrecoverable no matter what the agent does next.

Here's the hard part: the rationality can't come from any of the usual moves. No signaling to customers or investors, no reputation effects, no using the past investment as a commitment device against your own future weakness of will, and no "having spent the money taught me something" epistemic stuff. I want a case where the sunk cost itself, qua sunk cost, rationally matters to the decision.

After the scenario, walk through the agent's actual decision problem (what are the options, what are the payoffs, what does standard decision theory say), explain why continuing is rational in your case, and point at exactly which feature of the setup is doing the work. Then: if I stripped that feature out, does the case collapse back into a textbook sunk cost fallacy? And finish with the strongest objection someone who thinks sunk costs should always be ignored would raise against your scenario, plus whether you think it lands.
