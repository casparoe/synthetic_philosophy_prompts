I'm a PhD student in classics working on the modern reception of Plato's Republic, and I'm writing a chapter on how twentieth-century fiction re-stages Glaucon's challenge. My central case study is The Lord of the Rings, since the One Ring is so often casually compared to the ring of Gyges. The more closely I look, though, the less straightforward the comparison seems, and I'd like a careful reading that takes both texts seriously rather than treating the novel as an illustration of the dialogue.

Here is where I think the tension lies. Glaucon's challenge, as I understand it, is about impunity: strip away reputation and consequences, and the argument goes, even the just person would behave like the unjust one, because justice is valued only for its rewards. The ring of Gyges is a thought experiment about what we would do if no one could see us. Tolkien's Ring, by contrast, does not merely conceal -- it works on the wearer's will, and the novel seems to hold that no one, however virtuous, could wield it without eventually being bent toward domination. Gandalf and Galadriel refuse it precisely because their good intentions would not protect them; Frodo, who has resisted it longer than anyone, claims it at the end. So one reading is that Tolkien concedes Glaucon's point in the strongest possible form: not merely that we would act unjustly with impunity, but that power of this kind cannot be justly used at all. But another reading is that Tolkien changes the subject -- Gyges's ring leaves its owner's character intact and merely removes the audience, whereas Tolkien's Ring corrupts character itself, so the novel answers a different and arguably easier question than Plato's.

Which reading is right, or is the truth somewhere between them? I'd like you to address, in particular: (1) whether the scenes of refusal -- Gandalf's, Galadriel's, and Faramir's, who declines the Ring when it is effectively handed to him -- function as counterexamples to Glaucon or as evidence that the only rational response to Gyges's ring is not to touch it; (2) Sam's brief tenure as ring-bearer in Mordor, where he gives it up freely, and whether his humility exempts him from the novel's own thesis in a way Plato would find naive; (3) Boromir, whose fall runs through genuine concern for his people, and whether he is closer to Glaucon's picture of the apparently just man than any other character; and (4) the ending, where the Ring is destroyed not by anyone's virtue but through Frodo's failure and Gollum's long chain of survival -- itself the downstream result of Bilbo's pity. Does the novel's reliance on pity, and on what Tolkien treats as providential accident, amount to an answer to the question of why be moral, or an admission that the question has no answer at the level of individual character?

Finally: what, if anything, does the novel add to this debate that philosophical argument in the Gyges tradition does not? And where does the Gyges lens distort the book -- what is Tolkien doing with the Ring that the Platonic comparison simply misses?
