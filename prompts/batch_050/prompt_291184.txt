I got into a long argument with friends after a dinner-party conversation about whether kidney sales should be legalized, and one friend made a point I haven't been able to place in anything I've read since. She said the strangest consequence wouldn't be the sales themselves but what they'd do to everyone who never sells anything. Her example: suppose a kidney goes for $50,000 on a legal market. Now if your brother needs a kidney and you give him yours, you haven't just done a beautiful thing -- you've handed him $50,000, and everyone in the family knows it, including the siblings who kept both their kidneys and now look like they declined to write a check. Her claim was that the existence of the price re-describes every unpaid transfer of the thing, whether or not anyone actually buys or sells. Since that conversation I keep seeing the pattern. In my neighborhood, one family started paying a teenager to walk their dog, and almost immediately the informal arrangement where neighbors walked each other's dogs as favors got awkward -- asking your neighbor now feels like asking for fifteen dollars, and doing it without being asked feels like making a gift of fifteen dollars. And a colleague who worked abroad told me that in a country where you can pay an expediting fee to skip the queue at a government office, a clerk who processes your paperwork quickly as a courtesy has, in effect, tipped you the fee.

What strikes me is that none of the standard objections to markets seem to describe this. It isn't that people's motives get crowded out -- my neighbor might still walk my dog happily, for the same reasons as before -- and it isn't that the good itself gets degraded by being bought and sold, because in these cases the good in question was never sold at all; only a price existing somewhere changed what the unpaid version of the act means and what obligations trail it. I've seen phrases like commodification and the corrupting effect of markets, but those seem to be about things that actually enter a market, whereas I'm asking about the shadow a price casts on transactions that stay outside it.

Is there an established name or literature for this specific phenomenon -- the way a market price reclassifies nearby non-market transfers, turning favors into quantifiable gifts and non-giving into withholding a known sum? If there is, please explain how the phenomenon as I've described it differs from the nearest concepts (motivation crowding, the argument that markets corrupt goods, and studies where attaching a price to an act changes how people treat that act), and say which fit best and why. If nothing established fits, I'd rather you said so and coined a term: give it a definition, and mark its boundaries -- for instance, whether it only happens when the market is new or nearby, and whether goods that have always had prices produce it at all. I'm not after a bare label; I want the carving. Please write your answer in American English.
