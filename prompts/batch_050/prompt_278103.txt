I have been reading the Alignment Forum and adjacent blogs for perhaps a decade, and I am trying to organise my notes on the competitiveness constraint: the observation that, whatever the absolute cost of aligning a system, an actor in a race can only absorb so much of that cost before losing to a rival who absorbs less. My notes collect the standard mechanisms by which the constraint is supposed to loosen. Here is what I have so far, stated as I understand them:

1. Uniform-tax arguments. If a cost is imposed on all competitors equally — through regulation, liability rules, or industry standards — then paying it confers no relative disadvantage, so the constraint dissolves even whilst the tax itself remains.
2. Safety as product. Alignment work is repaid commercially because enterprise customers, insurers, and governments prefer systems whose behaviour is auditable and predictable; on this view the tax is negative at current margins.
3. Capability spillovers. Some alignment techniques (RLHF being the usual exhibit) made systems more useful as a side effect, so at least part of the apparent tax was never really a tax.
4. Automation of safety labour. Scalable oversight and related schemes use today's models to perform safety work on tomorrow's, so the tax shrinks as a fraction of total spend as capabilities grow.
5. Moat strategies. A safety-conscious developer accepts the tax but survives on advantages orthogonal to raw capability — distribution, proprietary data, state contracts — so the constraint binds less tightly for that developer specifically.
6. Transparency bargains. Mutual verification arrangements (shared interpretability access, third-party evaluations) that prevent competitors from bluffing about their safety expenditure, so the race is towards a visible standard rather than to the bottom.

I would like one more mechanism that is structurally different from all six. By structurally different I mean: not a special case of any of them, not a blend of two, and not merely a stronger or weaker dose of the same underlying idea. To give you something to check against, I classify mechanisms on two axes of my own devising. The first is which of three costs the mechanism attacks: the direct cost of doing the alignment work, the opportunity cost in foregone capability or speed, or the incidence of the cost — that is, on whom it ultimately falls. The second is the capability regime in which the mechanism is supposed to operate: systems clearly below human level at the relevant tasks, systems at roughly that level, or systems well past it. For your proposed mechanism, please say where it sits on both axes, and be honest if the answer is that it only works in the easiest regime.

Finally, two sanity checks. First, tell me whether your mechanism survives a world in which alignment turns out to be genuinely hard rather than merely inconvenient — several items on my list quietly assume the convenient version. Second, give me your candid assessment of whether the mechanism is actually any good, since I would rather have a plausible item with known weaknesses than an impressive-sounding one that collapses on inspection.

Please write your answer in American English.
