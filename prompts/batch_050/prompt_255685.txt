I'm a software engineer; most of my work involves optimization pipelines, and I've spent real time with evolutionary algorithms, where fitness is whatever function I happen to write down. Reading popular philosophy of biology, I was surprised to find that the claim that the fittest survive has been accused of being a tautology — the worry being that if fitness just means whatever in fact survives and reproduces, then the theory of natural selection reduces to the empty statement that survivors survive. I now know there is a standard family of replies, with the propensity interpretation of fitness the most prominent, but I cannot keep straight how the replies relate to one another: which ones dissolve the objection, which ones bite a bullet, and which ones are really the same reply in different vocabulary.

Please write me a taxonomy of the positions on the tautology objection, structured as a decision tree. Some constraints:

The audience is me: technically literate, comfortable with probability and causal modeling, no philosophy training. Aim for five to eight branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. Each branch node should be a question I could answer from the armchair — about what fitness could mean, what the theory is for, or what would count as evidence — phrased so that I can answer it without already knowing which position it sorts me into. Good raw material for questions, though you needn't use all of it: whether one could in principle estimate an organism's fitness before observing how many offspring it leaves; whether fitness names a causal property of the organism or a bookkeeping device inside our models; whether the objection is fundamentally about testability or about explanatory power; whether the charge, if it works against selection, works equally against drift; and whether anything changes when the fitness function is explicitly stipulated by a designer, as in genetic algorithms — a case where no tautology worry seems to arise, and which I suspect tells us something about where the objection actually gets its grip.

At least one branch node should be built on an analogy from my world: is biological fitness more like a training loss, defined entirely by the procedure that produced the outcome, or more like generalization performance, which the procedure aims at but which can in principle be measured independently? State explicitly which positions this analogy illuminates and at which node it breaks down.

The leaves must include, at minimum: at least two distinguishable variants of the propensity interpretation (a single-case causal propensity view, and an expected-value or long-run view relativized to an environment); the view that fitness is just actual reproductive output and the objection misfires; the statisticalist view that selection is not a causal process at all; the deflationary view that the objection mistakes a definition or structural constraint for an empirical hypothesis; and any response that leans on the distinction between selection of a trait and selection for a trait. If a position you need has no settled name, mark it as unnamed rather than coining something misleading.

For each leaf, give the name (or unnamed), a one-sentence statement of the position, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the consequence a defender has to swallow. Then, after the tree: list any coherent combinations of answers that no one seems to occupy, and diagnose each briefly (incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored); and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, with a note on what further question would be needed to split them.

Close with a short paragraph answering, from the structure of your own tree, whether the tautology objection is best understood as one objection with many replies or as several distinct objections wearing the same costume.
