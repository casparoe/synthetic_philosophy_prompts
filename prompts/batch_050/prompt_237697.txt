I teach an after-school philosophy-and-physics circle at my daughter's high school. A few weeks ago she asked me, over dinner, why the water in a spinning bucket climbs the sides — and when I reached for the answer I remembered from my own schooling, I realized I could not say what the water's rotation is rotation *relative to*. That conversation is why I volunteered to run next month's session, and I want to build it around the exchange between Newton and his relationist critics on absolute space. I am not asking you to teach the session; I am asking you to plan how I should teach it.

Act as a philosopher of physics with many years of experience teaching this material to bright non-specialists, and think carefully through the pedagogy before committing to a plan. The audience is roughly fifteen eleventh- and twelfth-graders who know Newton's laws, have used F = ma, and have heard the phrase centrifugal force, but have done no philosophy. I have fifty minutes and a whiteboard.

The conceptual spine of the session should be a passage from the Scholium to the Definitions in Newton's *Principia* that I will read aloud in paraphrase (I do not want to project the text, which is long): Newton asks us to imagine two globes connected by a cord, revolving about their common center of gravity in an otherwise empty space, and observes that the tension in the cord would reveal the globes' endeavor to recede from the axis of their motion — and hence the quantity of their true circular motion — even though there is nothing else in the universe for the globes to move relative to. I chose this over the famous bucket because I want the students to feel the pressure on the relationist before they meet the familiar case. Please organize the plan around making that passage do its work: what Newton takes it to establish, what a relationist can say back, and whether the class should be left thinking Newton won.

The plan should specify, in this order: (1) the sequence in which the ideas must be introduced — what depends on what, and why the staging matters if the argument is to feel inevitable rather than asserted; (2) the carrying example for each stage, including what the bucket demonstration must show and, just as important, what it must not be allowed to suggest, and where the globes case should enter relative to it; (3) the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring — I anticipate at least these: that the centrifugal force simply explains the concave surface, that the water obviously rotates relative to the room, that Mach long ago settled the matter by pointing to the distant stars, and that relativity has since refuted Newton outright — and for each, exactly where in the sequence I should head it off and how, without letting any of them derail the session; (4) one question I can pose at the end whose answers would show whether the distinction between relative and absolute motion has actually landed; and (5) what I should openly decline to cover at this level — I assume general relativity and the modern substantivalism literature belong on that list — and the one-sentence bridge I should give students who want more.

Finally, hold yourself to the same standard I am asking for: tell me where the plan's reasoning is most likely to fail with a real class, and what would change your mind — specifically, which single student question or objection, if posed mid-session, would most plausibly show that the plan has misordered its ideas or overstated what Newton's globes establish, and how you would restructure in response.

Please write the plan in American English, at whatever length the content requires, but not more than about 1,200 words.
