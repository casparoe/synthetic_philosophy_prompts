Background: I'm on the ethics committee of a long-term care facility, and we've been asked to write a standing procedure for staff for cases where a resident's advance directive conflicts with what the resident, who now has moderate-to-advanced dementia, seems to want right now. I know there's a philosophical debate here about "precedent autonomy" and whether the earlier self's authority carries over when the person has changed this much, plus the counter-argument that at some point the current person is effectively a different patient with their own interests that mostly concern their present comfort. I don't need you to settle that debate. I need you to do the harder, less glamorous thing: turn it into a procedure.

So concretely, I'd like:

1. A decision procedure a charge nurse or attending can actually run at 2am, in maybe 15-20 minutes, when a directive and a resident's current behavior pull apart. It should say what evidence counts as the resident "genuinely dissenting" versus just being confused or sundowning, how much the directive's specificity matters (e.g., "no feeding tube" written and notarized vs. "I never want to be kept alive like that" said to a daughter once), and where family input goes in the order of operations.
2. An honest list of the points where the procedure is smuggling in a philosophical verdict while pretending to be neutral paperwork. I suspect you can't actually write this procedure without taking a side on whether the earlier self outranks the current one, so I'd rather you say explicitly where you've picked a side and why, than hide it.
3. Worked applications to three cases: (a) a resident who clearly enjoys eating but whose directive says no tube feeding if she can no longer swallow, and now she can't swallow safely; (b) a resident with a directive refusing antibiotics who keeps getting aspirational pneumonias and seems, in his confused way, to fight being made comfortable; and (c) one case of your choosing where your own procedure gives an answer I probably won't like -- flag that one explicitly and tell me why you think it's still right.
4. What the procedure flat-out cannot handle, so we know when to escalate to the full committee instead of running the checklist.
