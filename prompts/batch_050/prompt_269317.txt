Some background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired experimental biologist (worked on animal behaviour for forty years), and since retiring I've fallen down the rabbit hole of philosophy of mind and language. Right now I'm chewing on the poverty of the stimulus argument for innate linguistic knowledge, and I want to pressure-test it against a real case rather than the usual toy examples.

The case I have in mind is Nicaraguan Sign Language. As I understand the story (correct me if I've garbled something): before the late 1970s, deaf children in Nicaragua were mostly isolated, each using idiosyncratic "homesign" gestures with their hearing families. Then a school for the deaf opened in Managua, and for the first time large numbers of deaf children were thrown together. The adults tried teaching them lipreading and Spanish, which largely failed, but out on the playground the kids pooled their homesigns into a shared pidgin-like system, and -- here's the part that got everyone's attention -- younger cohorts of children entering the school took that rough system and spontaneously elaborated it into something with real grammatical structure: consistent word order, spatial modulation for grammatical roles, the works. Nobody taught them this. The "input" they recieved from the older kids was by most measures a degenerate, inconsistent pidgin.

So here's what I'd like. Analyze this episode through the lens of the poverty of the stimulus argument. Be disciplined about the mapping: what exactly counts as "the stimulus" here, what would a nativist like Chomsky or a bioprogram-type theorist predict, and what would an empiricist or usage-based theorist predict instead? Then run it both directions: does the case actually confirm the nativist reading, or does the fact that the language emerged gradually, socially, and across cohorts undercut it -- i.e. maybe the stimulus wasn't impoverished in the way the argument requires, since the children had each other? And in the other direction, what does the episode reveal about the poverty of the stimulus argument itself? Does it show the argument was never really about total absence of input but about something subtler (the mismatch between input quality and output complexity)? What observation in a case like this would have *disconfirmed* nativism, and if you can't specify one, is the argument doing any real empirical work at all?

I'm a scientist, so I care a lot about that last question. Don't just declare a winner -- tell me where each side's theory goes silent or has to get creative.
