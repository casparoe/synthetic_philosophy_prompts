I have been drafting an essay on path dependence and counterfactual history, aimed at an audience of philosophically literate non-specialists. Below is the current draft. After it, I list the specific kinds of comments I am looking for. Note that the draft deliberately avoids citations; please do not treat their absence as a flaw to be fixed, and do not supply any.

**The Level at Which History Matters**

1. Path dependence is usually introduced with humble objects. The standard example is the typewriter keyboard: a layout adopted under the constraints of early mechanical typewriters persists long after those constraints vanished, because training, compatibility, and switching costs outweigh the benefits of alternatives. Similar stories are told about video cassette formats, railway gauges, and electrical standards. From such cases, two very different morals are commonly drawn. The weak moral is that history matters: small early events can have large later effects. The strong moral is that history traps: once a path is taken, inferior arrangements persist that would never have been chosen had the early coin flips landed differently. This essay argues that the strong moral is rarely established, because the question it answers — would the same outcome have arrived anyway? — is ambiguous until we specify the level of description at which "the outcome" is individuated.

2. Consider three levels. At the level of function, the outcome is that people have a fast way to produce text. At the level of form, the outcome is that text is produced on a keyboard with a stable spatial arrangement. At the level of standard, the outcome is the dominance of one particular arrangement over its rivals. The counterfactual question receives a different answer at each level. Plausibly, the function would have been achieved under almost any early history, since typewriting answered a standing need. Plausibly, the standard is contingent, since a different pattern of early adoption would have entrenched a different layout. The interesting case is the form, and there the answer is genuinely unclear: perhaps any history of mechanical text production converges on something recognizably keyboard-like, and perhaps not.

3. I therefore propose a level-relative criterion. An outcome is path dependent at a level of description L if and only if small, well-documented early differences would have changed which outcome, individuated at L, obtains. The criterion has two attractive consequences. First, it explains why the long-running dispute between lock-in theorists and their skeptics so often fails to resolve: the lock-in theorist individuates outcomes at the level of standard, the skeptic individuates them at the level of function, and on this diagnosis both are correct about the level they have in mind. Second, it suggests that the question whether history is locked in should be abandoned as ill-posed and replaced by a family of questions, one per level of description.

4. I also want to defend a stronger claim, which the criterion alone does not license. The levels are not independent of one another, and they are not fixed over time. What begins as contingency at the level of standard can migrate to higher levels. An entrenched layout trains generations of typists, dictates the design of input devices, and eventually shapes the interfaces of technologies that no longer resemble typewriters at all. As this happens, the functions themselves are redefined relative to the contingent standard: what counts as typing skill, or even as text production, comes to be specified in terms of the arrangement that history happened to deliver. So a criterion applied at a single time slice — the criterion of section 3 — may systematically understate long-run lock-in, because it holds fixed a system of levels that the historical process itself is busy revising.

5. The conclusion is that history is neither locked in nor easily escaped. The degree of lock-in is a property of a time and a level of description jointly, and genuine long-run path dependence consists in the tendency of low-level contingency to redefine what counts as the outcome at higher levels.
