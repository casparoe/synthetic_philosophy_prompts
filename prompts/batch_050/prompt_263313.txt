I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and last week my dissertation advisor threw out a line that's been bugging me ever since. We were talking about Galileo's falling bodies and Einstein chasing the light beam, and he said a thought experiment is just a regular experiment minus the apparatus — same epistemic logic, same kind of warrant for its conclusion, only run "in the laboratory of the mind" instead of the lab. He used this to shut down my worry that thought experiments are doing something epistemically fishy: his point was that if you trust experiments, you should trust thought experiments for exactly the same reasons. A colleague in my cohort heard this and countered with the analogy that a thought experiment is more like an argument in a lab coat — the experiment framing is pure costume and everything probative is just compressed premises and inference.

So please act as an expert in philosophy of science who has worked on thought experiments specifically, think carefully, and start with a short brainstorm of the main considerations before settling into your analysis. Audit my advisor's analogy. First give me the explicit correspondence table: if a thought experiment is really an experiment, what plays the role of the apparatus, the raw data or observations, the controls, the measurement error / error bars, the replication by independent labs, and the surprising result nature refuses to give you? Then tell me where the mapping breaks. The disanalogy that bothers me most is that real experiments can surprise you — nature can veto your expectations — whereas a thought experiment seems to contain its conclusion in its setup from the start. But maybe that's also true of some real experiments, or maybe the "surprise" in a thought experiment (nobody expected Galileo's conclusion) is enough. You decide.

Second, and this is the part I care most about for my chapter: does the conclusion the analogy was doing work for — that thought experiments deserve the same epistemic trust as physical experiments — actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for that conclusion from ones that are real but harmless. And if neither my advisor's analogy nor my colleague's argument-in-a-lab-coat picture is right, what's the better one? I keep half-remembering proposals that treat thought experiments as drawing on a stockpile of background experience (Mach-flavored) or as revealing something a priori (the rationalist reading), so feel free to situate your verdict among those. Please reach an actual verdict rather than splitting the difference mushily — I need a defensible position, not a survey.
