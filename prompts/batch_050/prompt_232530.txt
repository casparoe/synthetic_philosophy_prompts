A close friend of mine was seriously assaulted last year, and with the sentencing hearing coming up, I've been reading around the philosophy of punishment -- compatibilists like Daniel Dennett on one side, free-will skeptics like Gregg Caruso on the other. A housemate of mine, a philosophy graduate student, keeps deploying an analogy she takes to settle most of it. Her pitch runs like this: a dangerous offender is like a person infected with a dangerous pathogen. We don't blame the carrier of a disease, no matter how many people they might infect; we quarantine them, humanely, for exactly as long as they remain contagious, and we try to cure them in the meantime. Likewise, she says, society may justifiably incapacitate dangerous people and should invest in rehabilitating them, but the entire apparatus of blame, resentment, and desert -- the idea that the offender has it coming, that the sentence should express condemnation -- is as out of place as cursing a tuberculosis patient. She adds that quarantine is actually more permissive than punishment, since we'd release the carrier the moment they're no longer infectious, which is why retributive sentencing is not just cruel but irrational. I'll admit the analogy has some pull with me, but when I picture the man who hurt my friend, the quarantine picture feels like it leaves something out, and I can't tell whether that's insight or just my thirst for payback talking.

Could you audit the analogy rigorously? I'd like the explicit correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the pathogen, the carrier, contagiousness, quarantine, treatment, the public-health authority -- and then a careful separation of the disanalogies that actually threaten my housemate's conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, I'm wondering about things like: whether intent and agency in the target break the mapping to a mere vector of harm; whether punishment's communicative or expressive function has any analogue in public health; whether proportionality (punishment calibrated to the gravity of the offense, not just to ongoing dangerousness) survives the analogy or is precisely what it's meant to eliminate; and whether the analogy quietly presupposes the very free-will skepticism it's supposed to support, rather than arguing for it. Finally, does the no-blame, quarantine-only conclusion survive once the important disanalogies are on the table -- and if the analogy can't carry it, is there a better one that captures whatever truth the quarantine picture has?
