At a reading group on decision theory, two graduate students spent most of the session arguing about a case the convenor had put on the board, and I left without a clear sense of who had won. The case: a driver must travel along a highway with two consecutive exits. Exiting at the first exit gets him to a destination worth 0; exiting at the second exit is worth 4; missing both and driving on to the end of the highway is worth 1. The catch is that the driver is absent-minded: the two exits look identical to him, he cannot keep count, and when he arrives at an exit he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. Whatever he decides at an exit, he will decide at any exit, since his situation is the same each time. The convenor asked: with what probability should he exit?

The first student, Mara, said the answer is one-third. Reason: before setting out, the driver would compute that exiting with probability p yields expected payoff 4p(1 − p) + (1 − p)², which is maximized at p = 1/3, and since his situation at the exit is exactly the one he anticipated, the plan survives arrival. The second student, Jonas, replied that this confuses planning with deciding. At the exit, he said, the driver should hold fixed that his future self will follow the plan — exit with probability 1/3 at the next exit if there is one — and then compare: exiting now is worth 2 in expectation (he is at either exit with equal chance, so half of nothing plus half of four), while continuing is worth only 1.5. So the driver, reasoning correctly at the moment of choice, will exit for certain — which destroys the plan it was based on. His conclusion was that imperfect recall makes rationality self-undermining here: there is no recommendation that survives being thought at the exit.

Mara answered that Jonas's equal-chance premise is the mistake. Conditional on facing an exit at all, the driver is not at the two exits with equal probability: he is guaranteed to face the first exit, but faces the second only if he continued past the first, which under the plan happens with probability 2/3. So his credence of being at the first exit should be 3/5, not 1/2, and with those numbers exiting and continuing come out exactly equal — the plan is stable after all, not self-undermining. Jonas conceded the arithmetic but said the stability was hollow: the driver follows the plan only because he is indifferent, and since continuing and exiting tie, exiting with probability one is just as licensed by his own lights, which means the supposedly rational recommendation exerts no actual constraint at the moment of decision. Mara said this last move treats "exit now" and "exit next time" as separate choices when they are literally the same choice made from the same information, and that Jonas's whole objection depends on that split. Jonas said the split is just what decision theory is: evaluate the act in front of you, given your beliefs and your expectations about the future.

Can you go through the exchange round by round and say who is right about each specific claim — the one-third answer, the equal-chance credences, the 3/5 correction, the indifference objection, and the same-choice reply? I'd also like your view on what, if anything, both are missing: does the case really show something troubling about rationality under imperfect recall, or does it dissolve once some distinction they both neglect is drawn? Please adjudicate the arguments as actually made rather than just picking a winner overall.
