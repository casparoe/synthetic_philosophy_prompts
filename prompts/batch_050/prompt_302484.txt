At a meeting of our reading group on Indian philosophy, two fellow members, Dev and Mara, got into an extended argument about the Mīmāṃsā defense of the Veda as an authorless text, and about the school's doctrine that cognitions are intrinsically valid. I've reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can:

(1) Dev argued that the authorlessness of the Veda is not a mystical curiosity but an epistemic thesis. Every defect in testimony, he said, traces to a defect in a speaker — ignorance, deception, faulty memory, a slip of the tongue. A text with no author is a text with no possible source of such defects, so it is the only testimony that could warrant belief about matters like dharma, where no empirical check is available. The absence of an author is the credential, not an embarrassment to be explained away.

(2) Mara replied that this inverts the actual epistemology of testimony. We trust testimony because we can assess the testifier's competence and sincerity; remove the speaker and you have removed not merely the possible defects but the entire basis for trust. An unauthored text is not testimony at all — it is like finding marks in a canyon, carved by wind, that happen to spell out a sentence about dharma. However striking the marks, they give one no more reason to believe the sentence than a message spelled by drifting seaweed.

(3) Dev answered that the canyon analogy begs the question, because it assumes that meaning requires an utterer. The Mīmāṃsakas hold that the connection between words and their meanings is intrinsic and eternal, not established by anyone's stipulation or use. So the Veda's words mean what they mean whether or not anyone ever spoke them: the text is not a pattern that merely resembles language, but language with sense, prior to any speaker. The analogy to accidental marks therefore never gets started.

(4) Mara granted the point about meaning for the sake of argument and pressed a different one: meaning was never the issue — warrant is. Suppose the sentences are genuinely meaningful; why believe them? Here, she said, the school's own doctrine of intrinsic validity is made to do the work: every cognition is valid as it arises and is overturned only by a defeater, so the cognition produced by reading the Veda arrives already warranted. But that principle, applied generally, is far too generous — it would validate her neighbor's conviction that the postman is poisoning him until someone produced a defeater — and if it is applied only to the Veda, the whole structure is special pleading.

(5) Dev replied that intrinsic validity is not generosity but the only coherent default. If a cognition's validity had to be certified by something outside itself, the certification would need its own certification, and so on without end; everyone, he claimed, is an intrinsic-validity theorist about something, and the Mīmāṃsakas are simply honest about where the regress must stop. Nor does the doctrine protect the neighbor's delusion, which is defeated routinely — the claim concerns the default standing of cognitions, not their indefeasibility.

(6) Mara closed by arguing that the regress argument proves too much in a different direction: a school committed to it must explain perceptual illusion, and the Mīmāṃsakas do so by treating error as a mere failure to notice the difference between two veridical cognitions — as though there were no genuine error, only incomplete noticing. But a tradition that cannot straightforwardly admit that error occurs cannot be trusted to police its own central text. If nothing could ever count as discovering that the Veda is wrong about something, then calling it "valid unless defeated" is empty: it is dogmatism with a fallback clause, not a theory of warrant.
