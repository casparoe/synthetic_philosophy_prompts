ok so here's a fun one: write me a job advertisement, around 500 words, posted by the HR department of a mind that runs on the Cartesian Theater model -- the picture where everything you see, hear, and feel gets piped to one special inner screen where "it all comes together" for an audience of one. They're hiring a homunculus to do the watching.

The ad should read like a real corporate job posting (responsibilities, requirements, benefits, equal-opportunity statement, the works) and the comedy should come from the posting accidentally laying bare everything Dennett thinks is wrong with this picture. Some bits to work in:

- Duties include watching the screen after all the sensory feeds arrive, plus handling the awkward timing issues, since the feeds don't all show up at once and someone has to decide how long to wait before the "show" starts
- A note that the new hire will sometimes have to choose between editing the material before it screens versus letting it screen and then rewriting the official record of what was shown afterwards (the posting can proudly describe these as two company-approved workflows without noticing what they imply)
- Salary negotiable, but the successful candidate must be a fully-fledged understander -- no "stupid" homunculi who just pass signals along, management has been burned before
- The equal-opportunity statement or an FAQ that reveals the real structural problem: since the homunculus is itself a little person who watches a screen, it presumably needs its own theater and its own viewer, so the ad keeps getting cross-posted as nested sub-ads for positions inside positions, and the org chart is... still being finalized
- Maybe a line about how applicants from the "Multiple Drafts" school keep leaving rude comments on the listing, which HR insists is just jealousy

Stay deadpan the whole way through -- the ad itself should never admit there's anything strange going on. Then, after the ad, give me a short author's note (a paragraph is fine, doesn't count toward the 500) saying where the satire exaggerates or leans on a contested reading of Dennett, and which objection a defender of the theater picture could raise that would most threaten to make the jokes land unfairly.
