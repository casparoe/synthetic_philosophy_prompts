A colleague who teaches our department's introductory course recently circulated a handout summarizing Wittgenstein's later philosophy of religion as follows: "Wittgenstein held that religious language is not in the business of making factual claims at all. To say 'I believe in God' or 'I believe in the Last Judgment' is not to hold a hypothesis about what exists or what will happen; it is to express an emotional attitude or a commitment to a way of living, closer to saying 'I trust you' than to asserting a proposition. Because religious utterances are moves within their own language-game, they are immune to evidential criticism: the believer who ignores historical evidence against the resurrection is therefore being perfectly consistent, and the atheist who offers such evidence simply hasn't understood the grammar of faith. In this way Wittgenstein showed that religion and science can never conflict, since they occupy incommensurable games."

I suspect this is a reasonable summary of a certain reading of Wittgenstein but a questionable summary of Wittgenstein himself, though I am not confident where exactly the slippage occurs. Acting as a specialist in Wittgenstein's later philosophy, please audit the handout carefully. Begin by briefly brainstorming, for each of its main claims, what textual or interpretive basis it might have (e.g., the Lectures on Religious Belief, the treatment of pictures and practice in On Certainty and Culture and Value) before rendering judgment. Then go claim by claim: which parts would a careful Wittgenstein scholar accept, which trade on conflating an expressivist reconstruction with Wittgenstein's own more elusive position, and which rest on moves Wittgenstein arguably resisted — for instance, treating "language-game" as a license for insulation from criticism, or attributing to him a general doctrine about religion and science. Conclude with an assessment of whether the handout's closing claim about the impossibility of conflict follows from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, and indicate briefly what you would tell the colleague to change.
