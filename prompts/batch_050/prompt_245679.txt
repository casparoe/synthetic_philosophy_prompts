I am a second-year PhD student in cognitive science preparing a two-page research proposal for an internal seed grant (12 months, part-time alongside my dissertation), and I'd like you to draft it for me. The topic is the "Letter Spirit" problem: what makes an enormous variety of shapes all count as instances of the letter 'a', and what makes twenty-six different letters all count as written in the same style.

I want the proposal built around a specific angle rather than the usual framing. Standard accounts treat letter recognition as a shape-classification problem — match the input against a template or a learned feature bundle, with style as noise to be normalized away. I want to test a rival, roughly structuralist hypothesis: that the identity of a letter is constituted not by its shape but by its position in a system of contrasts, so that an 'a' is an 'a' partly in virtue of not being an 'o', a 'd', or a 'u' within its alphabet — and that style ("spirit") is a second, independent level of the same kind, constituted by how those contrasts are realized across the whole alphabet rather than by any features of individual letters. The signature prediction of this view is a dissociation: it should be possible to construct chimeric letterforms that are ambiguous in identity (readable as either of two letters in isolation) but unambiguous in style, and conversely forms whose identity is clear but whose stylistic allegiance is genuinely indeterminate — and the two kinds of judgment should pattern independently when readers are tested.

In the proposal, please do the following: (1) State the research question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and explain why existing approaches — template/prototype models on one side, analogy-based accounts on the other — leave this specific dissociation question open rather than already answering it. (2) Lay out the approach: a small program of behavioral experiments using systematically constructed letterform stimuli, including what the stimulus set would have to look like and what measurements would decide between the structuralist hypothesis and shape-based rivals. Explain why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives (e.g., training a neural network classifier and probing its representations, or pure conceptual analysis of the category 'letter'). (3) Say what a positive and a negative result would each look like, what would count as the project failing as opposed to the hypothesis being false, and what the structuralist account predicts about cross-script cases (e.g., whether a reader who cannot read a script can nonetheless perceive its style) that the rival account does not. (4) Give an honest scope assessment: which parts are achievable in twelve months part-time, and what would have to be cut first. Please do not include citations or a literature review — I will fill in references myself — but feel free to refer to positions and arguments by name or description. The tone should be that of a grant proposal written by someone who expects skeptical reviewers. End with a single-sentence summary of the proposed project as the last line, beginning "Summary:".
