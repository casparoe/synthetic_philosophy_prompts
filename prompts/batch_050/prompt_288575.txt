Like many people whose philosophical training came mostly from reading rationalist blogs and forums, my working vocabulary for thinking about agents is built from decision theory, game theory, and evolutionary psychology: utility functions, revealed preferences, one-shot versus iterated games, signalling, commitment devices, and the standard debunking move in which a moral intuition gets redescribed as an adaptation. I am genuinely fluent in that vocabulary, so please do not simplify it for me.

I keep running into Levinas secondhand, and the summaries describe a cluster of claims I cannot get a grip on: that ethics is prior to ontology, that the encounter with what he calls the face of the Other issues an unconditional claim on me before any reasoning or agreement takes place, that my responsibility for the Other is asymmetric and does not depend on reciprocity, that this responsibility is in some sense infinite and only grows as it is discharged, and that the self in this picture is less a choosing agent than a hostage. I tried Totality and Infinity once and bounced off the prose.

What I want is a systematic translation of this position into the vocabulary above, element by element. For each element, tell me which parts translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion (and name the distortion), and which have no expression in the target framework at all. A few ground rules and specific requests:

1. Do not let the translation cheat. Rendering the claim of the face as "the agent has a strong preference not to harm others" or "other-regarding terminal values" seems like exactly the move Levinas is trying to block, since his point is that the claim is prior to and independent of anything I prefer or choose. If the framework simply cannot represent an obligation that is neither chosen nor a preference, say that plainly rather than smuggling one in.

2. The ev-psych redescription — the face as a hard-to-fake signal that triggers social emotions shaped by repeated interaction — is one I could generate myself. What I want to know is whether that redescription counts as a translation of Levinas's claim, a rival explanation of the phenomenon he is describing, or a category error, and how one would tell.

3. The one part that seems to translate suspiciously well is his account of the third party: when a second Other arrives, I must compare incomparable claims, and ethics gets compromised into justice, calculation, and politics. That looks almost identical to the move from a dyadic interaction to an aggregation problem. Tell me whether this translation is genuine or only superficial, because a translation this neat makes me suspicious that I am projecting.

4. End with a verdict on the residue. Of what fails to survive translation, how much is a genuine defect of the decision-theoretic framework — something it cannot say but should be able to — and how much is a sign that the original claim was less precise than its presentation suggested? I am honestly uncertain which way this will cut, and I would rather get a defended answer than a diplomatic split.
