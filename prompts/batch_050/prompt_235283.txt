Exam question: "Hegel's account of the state's relation to the individual in the Philosophy of Right is often read as the key to his notorious claim that war has an 'ethical moment.' Using the passage below, explain why Hegel thinks the liberal or contractarian picture of the state cannot make sense of the sacrifice war demands, and assess whether this commits him to the view that states may do whatever they like in their external relations." The passage (from §258 of Hegel's Elements of the Philosophy of Right, in the Dyde translation) was printed on the exam: "If the state is confused with civil society, and if its specific end is laid down as the security and protection of property and personal freedom, then the interest of the individuals as such becomes the ultimate end of their association, and it follows that membership of the state is something optional. But the state’s relation to the individual is quite different from this. Since the state is mind objectified, it is only as one of its members that the individual himself has objectivity, genuine individuality, and an ethical life."

Here's the student's answer, roughly transcribed: "Hegel's target in the passage is the social contract tradition, where the state is basically an insurance arrangement for protecting life and property, and on that picture membership is optional in principle — you could always leave if the premiums got too high. Hegel thinks this inverts the real relation: the individual only has 'ethical life' as a member, so the state can't be a means to private ends. This is why war matters for him. If the state existed to protect property and personal freedom, asking citizens to die for it would be irrational — you'd be destroying the very thing the state exists to secure. Because the state is instead the individual's ethical substance, the demand of sacrifice in war is intelligible, and war exposes what Hegel calls the vanity of temporal, finite things. So Hegel's point isn't that war is fun or glorious; war is instrumentally valuable because it produces courage and civic solidarity in the citizenry, the qualities Machiavelli said republics need to survive. On the external question: Hegel holds that states face each other in a state of nature with no authority above them, and he famously rejects Kant's hope for perpetual peace through a league of states. Since there is no power to enforce treaties between sovereigns, treaties don't genuinely bind states, and a state acts within its rights when it tears up an agreement that its welfare requires breaking. In that sense yes, Hegel is committed to saying states may do whatever their survival demands externally, though internally they remain rational ethical orders."
