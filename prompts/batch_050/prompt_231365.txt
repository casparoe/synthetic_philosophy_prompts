I'm a university student taking a bioethics seminar, and as part of a practicum I'm shadowing the scarce-resource allocation committee of a regional hospital network. The committee currently resolves conflicts between groups of patients by saving the greater number, full stop, and I've been asked to draft an alternative standing protocol grounded in John Taurek's position in his essay "Should the Numbers Count?" -- roughly, that when each person's claim is equal, no one's loss is made worse by the fact that others would suffer the same loss, so there is no obligation to save the five rather than the one, and a fair procedure may give the lone individual an even chance. Here is the situation the protocol must govern: about once a month the committee faces a case in which one indivisible resource (a single dose of a scarce drug, one available ECMO machine, one transplantable organ with a narrow window) could go to one patient or, alternatively, to each of several patients whose individual needs are smaller but add up to the same resource. Decisions must be made within about two hours by an on-call team of three, documented in writing, and signed off by the chief medical officer.

Please write the protocol as a numbered decision procedure the team could actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a settled answer to each, and the stopping points where the procedure refuses the case and hands it to a person's judgment because the issue is no longer procedural. The conceptual work I care about most is this: a Taurekian procedure still has to choose a lottery design, and that choice is not innocent. Flipping a single coin between the two groups gives the lone patient a one-half chance; drawing lots among all six individuals gives him a one-in-six chance and makes it five times more likely the larger group wins. Tell me which design the protocol should adopt to stay faithful to Taurek's reasoning rather than quietly reintroducing aggregation, and justify the choice in terms his argument would recognize. Then mark the exact step at which a numbers-counting protocol would diverge from yours, so the committee can see precisely what it would be giving up.

Run the finished procedure on three cases, showing the trace question by question. First, Taurek's own structure: one patient needs the entire stock of a drug, five others each need one-fifth, all six have equal prognosis and equal claim. Second, a harder variant: the lone patient is already connected to the machine when the three other candidates arrive, and the team feels a pull not to withdraw treatment from someone currently receiving it -- say whether the protocol may honor that pull or must treat it as irrelevant history. Third, a recurring pattern: the network faces a one-versus-two version of this conflict every few weeks, and one committee member argues that whichever rule is chosen, its repeated application should be evaluated by its aggregate death toll over a year -- say whether the protocol can even make sense of that objection from within Taurek's view, and where it lands.

Include at least one worked case where the procedure issues a verdict a reasonable reader will hate -- for instance, the coin comes up for the lone patient and five people die who would certainly have been saved -- and state, without flinching, what the written documentation should say to the families and to the review board. Also flag the failure modes: where claims turn out not to be equal (differing prognoses, differing urgency), where the procedure's demand for equal claims is itself a substantive commitment rather than a neutral default, and anything else a procedure built on this view structurally cannot settle. Write your answer in American English.
