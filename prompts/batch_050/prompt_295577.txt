So I host a philosophy podcast and I've booked a guest I'm genuinely excited about: a scholar of Russian literature whose new book argues, with a straight face, that the Grand Inquisitor was right. Not right as a literary device -- right about people. Her view, as far as I can tell, is that the old cardinal in Ivan Karamazov's prose poem diagnoses something true and uncomfortable: that most human beings don't actually want freedom, that they experience it as a burden and a source of torment, and that taking it away and replacing it with bread, miracle, mystery and authority is an act of compassion rather than tyranny. Christ's mistake, on her reading, was refusing the three temptations out of a kind of contempt for what ordinary people can actually bear.

What I want to avoid is an interview where she just gets to restate the book's thesis at me for forty minutes. Draft nine or ten interview questions that press on the places where her position is actually load-bearing. Roughly what I'd like covered:

- The empirical core: the claim that people, given the choice, will reliably hand their freedom over to whoever offers them bread and certainty. If she says "look around, they already do," what's the follow-up that distinguishes people trading freedom under duress from people not wanting it at all?
- The structure of the temptations scene: whether miracle, mystery and authority really exhaust the options, or whether the poem rigs the game by making Christ's alternative an insultingly high bar that only saints could clear.
- The paternalism problem: the Inquisitor's arrangement works only if a small elite lies to everyone else for their own good and shoulders the guilt of doing so. Does she think happiness produced by deception still counts as happiness, and does she bite the bullet on the elite knowing better?
- The kiss. The prisoner answers with silence and a kiss, and the Inquisitor lets him go anyway. I want a question about whether her defense survives that scene on it's own terms -- whether the kiss is a refutation she has to explain away or something her view can absorb.
- Ivan and Dostoevsky: the poem is told by a man sliding toward despair, and its author pretty clearly didn't think the Inquisitor was the hero. Does defending him require her to claim that the people who wrote the argument misunderstood it?

For each question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, since her signature move is saying some version of "you're confusing what's flattering to believe with what's true" and then sitting back.

One last thing: she's funny and combative in a charming way, so pointed questions are welcome, but no gotchas -- I want the strongest version of each objection, not traps.
