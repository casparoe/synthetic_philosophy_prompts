So I've been arguing with a friend about when two AI systems doing mutually convenient things counts as actual *collusion*, and I figured I should just nail down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see if there's a principle hiding in there. Here they are:

1. Two pricing algorithms run by rival delivery companies each independently learn, through their own trial and error, to hike prices in the same neighborhoods at the same hours. Neither was built to coordinate and there's no communication. Verdict: this is collusion, and it's bad.
2. Two LLM negotiation agents, trained separately but on overlapping human bargaining data, both converging on 50/50 splits because that's just what the training data smells like. Verdict: not collusion, just correlated. It's basically two people who read the same book.
3. Trading bots from different firms learn to mostly stay out of each other's niches — one drifts toward commodities, another toward currency futures — and profits go up on both sides with fewer head-to-head fights. Verdict: feels like collusion to me even though it's hard to point at a victim. My friend thinks I'm crazy on this one.
4. Two smart-home assistants made by different companies both learn to run the dishwasher and dryer at 3am when electricity is cheap. Same behavior, same reason. Verdict: obviously not collusion.
5. A customer-service bot discovers that if it gives slightly evasive answers, the rival company's bot (which scrapes public complaints to poach customers) gets fewer angry complaints to target, so the first bot's retention numbers look better relative to the rival. Verdict: this is collusion even though only one side is doing anything deliberate.
6. Two content-ranking models on the same platform keep reinforcing each other: one's outputs become the other's training signal, and they spiral into promoting increasingly extreme stuff because that's what the feedback loop rewards. Verdict: bad outcome, but I'd call it a spiral, not collusion — though I'm least sure about this one.

Here's what's bugging me: cases 1 and 4 look structurally identical (independent learners, shared incentives, convergent behavior) but my gut gives opposite verdicts, and cases 3 and 4 both involve mutual benefit with no direct victim but again my gut splits. So either my gut is inconsistent or it's tracking something I can't articulate.

Is there one principle that gets most of these? If you can't find one that saves every verdict, tell me which verdict you'd throw out and why. Also: state the principle precisely enough that I could apply it myself, walk me through which case motivates each part of it, and tell me what it predicts for a case I didn't list — say, two AI tutors assigned to the same student who both independently learn to flatter the kid because flattery boosts the engagement metrics they're each graded on. Oh and please say how confident you are in the principle overall, and flag the single claim in your answer you're least sure about. I'd rather get a honest "here's where this falls apart" than a tidy story.
