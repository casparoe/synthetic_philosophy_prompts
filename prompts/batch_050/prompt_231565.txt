In a philosophy of mathematics seminar, a colleague defended the following argument about computer-assisted proofs, including the proofs of the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture. It appears to show that there is no epistemically important difference between ordinary proofs and proofs whose computations are too extensive for any human to inspect in full:

1. A proof need not be surveyable all at once. Mathematicians ordinarily understand long proofs by dividing them into lemmas, checking portions at different times, remembering earlier results, and relying on previously certified work.

2. If the mere fact that no individual can hold every inferential step in mind simultaneously were disqualifying, then almost every sufficiently long or collaborative proof would be disqualified.

3. Computer-assisted proofs can likewise be divided into components: an informal reduction, a formal verification procedure, an independently implemented computation, and checks of the relevant code and hardware.

4. Independent implementations can be rerun and compared, and in some contexts they are less error-prone than human proof-checkers, who suffer from fatigue, memory failures, and unrecognized assumptions.

5. Ordinary mathematics is already socially mediated: mathematicians rely on referees, collaborators, libraries, typeset manuscripts, and the reports of other specialists.

6. There is no non-arbitrary threshold of reliability, length, or dependence on intermediaries below which a chain of checking counts as mathematical proof and above which it ceases to count.

7. Therefore, unsurveyability by a single human does not mark a principled epistemic distinction between computer-assisted and ordinary proofs.

8. Consequently, computer-assisted proofs have the same epistemic status as ordinary proofs. Insisting on a fully human-readable proof is at most an aesthetic or pedagogical preference, not a requirement of mathematical knowledge.

9. In particular, the fact that no person can inspect every computational step in the four-colour or Kepler proofs gives no more reason to withhold belief than the fact that no person can simultaneously survey every step of a long conventional proof.
