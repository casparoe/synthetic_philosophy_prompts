I'm a third-year student in a course on social epistemology and the philosophy of technology, and I'm writing a paper on deepfakes. The position I'm currently inclined to defend runs as follows. The real danger of synthetic media is not that we will be deceived by particular fakes — we were always deceivable, and most people never verified footage frame-by-frame anyway. The danger is that audiovisual recording has historically functioned as an epistemic backstop: the final court of appeal against testimony, official denial, and institutional authority. Video of police misconduct, of crowd sizes, of war crimes could settle disputes that words alone could not, because producing such footage required the events to have actually occurred in front of a camera. Deepfakes sever that connection. Once fabrication is cheap and convincing, genuine footage loses its settling power — not because viewers are fooled by fakes, but because real evidence can now be dismissed as possibly fake (what some call the liar's dividend). On this view, the harm persists even if detection tools improve, because the backstop depends on a settled public expectation that recording is hard to fake, and that expectation, once broken, may not be restorable by technical means.

My question for you: play it straight and map this position's exposure. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Consider candidate defeaters of different kinds — empirical (how audiences actually respond to known fake footage; whether the liar's dividend has measurably materialized or remains hypothetical), historical (whether photography, audio recording, or Photoshop ever played the backstop role I've assigned to video, and what happened to public trust as each became manipulable), technical (whether provenance-based approaches — cryptographic signing at capture, content credentials — could rebuild the settling function on a different foundation than un-fakeability), and conceptual (whether my notion of a backstop was ever accurate, or whether recordings always derived their authority from testimony and institutional trust rather than the other way around — in which case deepfakes change less than I claim).

For each candidate defeater, say whether it would be decisive, merely awkward, or easily absorbed by the position. I'm especially interested in two things. First, what would a defender of this view herself concede as counting against it — not what critics say, but what the view actually predicts such that the prediction could fail? Second, I'm worried the position insulates itself by retreating: when confronted with evidence that people still trust video, the defender shifts from "fakes deceive us" to "the mere possibility of fakes corrodes trust," and from there to "the corrosion is slow and not yet visible." At what point does that sequence of qualifications render the position unfalsifiable, and is unfalsifiability here a vice or just a sign that the thesis concerns long-run structural change that short-run evidence cannot settle?

Personally, I'd find the historical line most persuasive if it can be made precise: if it turns out that earlier recording technologies survived their own manipulation crises with the backstop function intact, I'd want to know exactly what preserved it then and whether that feature is present or absent now. Please end with an honest verdict on how exposed the position actually is — including the possibility that it's well insulated, and whether that should worry me as its defender.
