I teach a statistics course at a liberal arts college, and each fall I do one session on probability in culture before we get into inference properly. This year I want to build that session around the opening of Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead, where the two characters are passing time on the road and a coin keeps coming up heads, dozens of times consecutively, while Guildenstern grows increasingly unsettled and starts running through possible explanations.

Read that scene through the lens of the frequentist–Bayesian divide, and then through the replication crisis, as follows. First, give me the two analyses a statistician of each school would actually produce. On the frequentist side: what does a p-value for a long run of heads under the null of a fair coin even establish, and does the scene expose the classic ambiguity about whether the experiment was fixed in advance -- would the result be equally suspicious if you had watched a million strangers flip coins all day and finally found the one with the streak? On the Bayesian side: how should a rational Guildenstern update across the scene, and what does his answer depend on -- in particular, how much work is done by his prior over fair coins, biased coins, a rigged bet with a stranger, and the possibility that the ordinary laws of the world no longer apply to him? I'd like you to take seriously that his list of candidate explanations in the scene is essentially a model comparison, and to say whether the play is dramatizing good inference or a parody of it.

Second, the harder question. The audience eventually realizes something the characters only sense: Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are characters inside a scripted tragedy, so there is no data-generating process at all -- no coin, no mechanism, only an author's fixed text in which their deaths are already written. If that's right, is every statistical question they ask a category error, or is it exactly the right response to their situation? Connect this to the replication crisis: much of that literature is about researchers who treat data as the output of a clean sampling process when the actual process includes selective reporting, flexible analyses, and choices made after seeing the results. Is the play, on this reading, a story about agents who can't tell whether they're in an experiment or in a narrative -- and is that structurally the predicament of a working scientist reading the published literature?

Please flag plainly if you think any part of this framing is confused -- for instance, if you think applying inferential statistics to a scripted world is a mistake the play wants us to notice rather than a lens worth applying, or if you think the replication-crisis analogy strains the text. I'd rather have that objection argued than a reading that politely papers over it. Close by telling me which specific beats of the scene you'd have the class discuss, and what the play shows about uncertainty that a statistics lecture can't.
