I teach moral philosophy at a mid-sized research university, and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper at a small invited workshop on Buddhist ethics in dialogue with Western moral theory — thirty minutes of talk, thirty of Q&A. My paper pushes back against the currently fashionable reading of early Buddhist ethics as a form of consequentialism, and it leans heavily on the Ambalatthika-rahulovada Sutta (MN 61), the Buddha's advice to his son Rahula. Everyone cites the mirror simile from that text; my argument turns instead on the retrospective third stage of the reflection formula, which in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation runs: "Having done a bodily action, you should reflect on it: 'This bodily action I have done — did it lead to self-affliction, to the affliction of others, or to both? Was it an unskillful bodily action, with painful consequences, painful results?' If, on reflection, you know that it led to self-affliction, to the affliction of others, or to both; it was an unskillful bodily action with painful consequences, painful results, then you should confess it, reveal it, lay it open to the Teacher or to a knowledgeable companion in the holy life. Having confessed it... you should exercise restraint in the future. But if on reflection you know that it did not lead to affliction... it was a skillful bodily action with pleasant consequences, pleasant results, then you should stay mentally refreshed & joyful, training day & night in skillful mental qualities." My thesis: the outcome-talk in this passage is not a criterion of right action but a diagnostic instrument in a regime of intention-purification — confession, restraint, and joyful training are the responses of someone treating anticipated and remembered harm as evidence about the state of one's motivations, not as disvalue to be minimized — so the passage supports a self-cultivation or agent-relative virtue reading, and the consequentialist reconstruction mistakes a sign of rightness for its ground. I also argue, as a subsidiary point, that the formula's equal weighting of self-affliction with the affliction of others is an embarrassment for any welfarist aggregation reading.

The audience matters here, and it's not a friendly one. There will be two Pāli philologists, one of whom is my assigned respondent and has a reputation for dismantling papers that import Western theoretical categories into the canon; I expect him to press me on the semantics of kusala/akusala, on the genre of the text (advice to a young novice, not a treatise), and on whether my reading over-philosophizes what is pedagogically plain. There are three analytic moral philosophers, two of them sympathetic to the consequentialist reconstruction, who will press from the other side: if the test is merely diagnostic, why is it framed in terms of consequences at all; does my view collapse into a rule-consequentialism of training rules; what do I make of the "self, others, or both" scope. And there are two scholar-practitioners trained in Theravāda monastic lineages, whose objections I find hardest to predict. The philologists hold the prestige in this room; if I lose them, I lose everyone.
