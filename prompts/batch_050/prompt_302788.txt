I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory, and this term two of my strongest students have been relitigating the Allais paradox all semester: one is an unrepentant expected utility theorist who thinks the independence axiom is as close to a requirement of rationality as anything in the field, and the other thinks the common-consequence effect is a rational pattern of preference that EU theory wrongly pathologizes. For their final project I've forbidden them from writing dueling papers and required a single joint statement of the kind adversarial collaborations produce, and I'd like you to draft it for them so I can show them what a good one looks like. The document should open with a numbered list of claims both can sign: what the standard Allais choice problems actually present to subjects, what the independence axiom formally says (including the distinction between its normative and descriptive readings), what the von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage results do and do not establish, and the fact that both sides accept transitivity and some form of dominance. Then state their remaining disagreements as precise propositions, each worded so that its own defender would accept the formulation rather than protest it -- I want the exact sentence where they part ways, not caricatures like "people are irrational" versus "axioms are wrong."

The heart of the document should be a cruxes section, and I'll suggest seeds they should feel free to correct or supplement. I suspect one crux is whether the Allais pattern survives reflection by an agent who fully understands the axiom it violates, and if so, what that shows: the EU partisan will predict that reflective, statistically literate agents largely recant, while the Allais defender will predict they largely don't, and both should be able to agree that this bears on the dispute. Another candidate crux is whether the force of the paradox comes from anticipated regret or from the certainty effect, and here the test both would accept might be reformulations of the problem designed to remove regret or to remove the certainty structure, with each side's prediction about choice patterns in the reformulated problems written down in advance. A third might be whether dynamic consistency arguments (money-pump and related constructions) actually connect to the static axiom, or quietly smuggle in extra premises about how preferences across time relate to preferences at a time. For each crux, have the document record what evidence or argument each side would count as cutting against its own position.

Finally, the document should include a short section on which parts of their semester-long dispute turned out to be verbal -- my own suspicion is that some of it trades on an equivocation between independence as a principle about how rational preferences must be structured and independence as a psychological claim about how people do choose -- and a closing section both sign stating what would settle the remaining genuine disagreement, or explaining why they agree it cannot be settled empirically. Write it as the actual document they would submit, in their shared first-person-plural voice, with the agreed claims numbered and the cruxes each given a short heading.
