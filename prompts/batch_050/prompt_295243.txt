I am trying to determine whether my intuitions about punishment can be captured by one coherent principle. Treat punishment as intentional hard treatment imposed by the state for an offence, not as any measure that merely reduces crime.

My verdicts are these. A calculating fraudster who no longer poses any danger should still receive some punishment even if it would deter no one, because the offence deserves condemnation. A shoplifter whose offending is driven by a treatable mental illness should receive compulsory treatment rather than punishment if prison would make reoffending more likely. A severe exemplary sentence for tax evasion would be wrong even if it produced a substantial net social benefit. Two equally culpable offenders should not receive materially different sentences merely because publicity makes one case especially useful as a deterrent. A murderer who has been radically transformed over fifty years, and whose prosecution would seriously impede an otherwise successful process of communal reconciliation, should not be imprisoned simply because imprisonment was once deserved. Finally, a victim’s plea for leniency should sometimes mitigate punishment, but should not allow an offender to purchase impunity by pressuring or compensating the victim.

What principle, or principled combination of retributivist and consequentialist constraints, best captures these verdicts? Please distinguish desert as a ceiling from desert as a reason requiring punishment, identify which verdict motivates each part of your answer, and test the resulting view against two further cases I have not listed. If preserving every verdict requires an unacceptably ad hoc theory, say which verdict should be abandoned and why. Please answer in British English, without citations or a general survey of the literature.
