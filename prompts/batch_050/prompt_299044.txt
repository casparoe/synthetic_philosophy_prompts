I'm prepping an interview for a philosophy-of-art podcast with a scholar who's just published a book defending Schopenhauer's theory of music -- specifically the claim that music, unlike the other arts, is not a copy of the Platonic Ideas but a direct copy of the Will itself. He's sharp and tends to handle objections by restating the theory more poetically rather than answering. Could you draft me a sequence of questions that starts from the core of his view and then presses at the actual sore spots?

Things I'd want covered: how a "copy" relation is even supposed to work here given that music doesn't represent anything (and whether his usual answer -- that melody mirrors the structure of striving, delay, and satisfaction -- just sneaks representation back in under a different name); how his view is supposed to differ from a pure formalism like Hanslick's if the emotions music expresses are supposed to be contentless; what he makes of opera and program music, which on Schopenhauer's own terms should be aesthetic downgrades; and the puzzle of why directly copying the Will, which is supposed to be the source of suffering, is so deeply pleasurable. I'd also love a question or two that makes him commit on whether musical greatness becomes, on this theory, a metaphysical ranking -- i.e., whether Beethoven is literally closer to the truth about reality than Bach or than a pop song, and whether he'd bite that bullet.

End with one question about what could possibly count as evidence against the theory, since unfalsifiable-by-construction is the worry I most want to pin him on.
