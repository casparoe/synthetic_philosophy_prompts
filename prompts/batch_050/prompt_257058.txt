In a policy memo I am drafting, a colleague argues that granting legal personhood to advanced AI systems should be uncontroversial because the law already grants personhood to corporations: if a legal fiction can let a corporation sue and be sued, hold property, and enter contracts, the same device can simply be extended to an AI system, and resistance to this rests on a confusion about what legal personhood even is. Write, in American English, an audit of this analogy. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what, on the AI side, is supposed to play the role of the shareholders, the directors, the registered agent, the corporate assets — and then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, say which features of a corporation the analogy quietly assumes an AI system possesses, and which of the actual functions of corporate personhood (concentrating liability in a reachable target, enabling contracting, shielding investors) genuinely depend on those features rather than on the bare legal label. Separate the disanalogies that threaten my colleague's conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, take a definite position: does the corporate-person analogy support extending personhood to AI systems as they actually operate today, or does the audit show that the analogy itself was carrying the conclusion? If you think some other legal device — agency, trusts, or something else — would do the relevant work better, name it and say why.
