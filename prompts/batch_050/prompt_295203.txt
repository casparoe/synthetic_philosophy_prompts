I'm a novelist, and my current book has a character -- an agronomist in a small coastal country -- who spends the 1960s saying the national fishery will collapse within twenty years if nothing changes. It collapses forty-five years later, and even then it's arguably warming currents that finish it off, not overfishing. My writing group is split on whether he gets to say "I told you so," and I've realized I have gut verdicts but no principle. As an anchor, here's Malthus in the preface to An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), laying out the terms on which his own gloomy forecast should be judged:

"If he should succeed in drawing the attention of more able men to what he conceives to be the principal difficulty in the way to the improvement of society and should, in consequence, see this difficulty removed, even in theory, he will gladly retract his present opinions and rejoice in a conviction of his error."

My verdicts:

- Malthus himself: the mechanism he described was real and did operate, but two centuries of yields and falling birth rates make me call the forecast wrong -- and by the terms of that passage he'd have to agree, which oddly makes me respect the theory more, not less.
- My fishery character: right but early. The force he named really did destroy the thing he said it would destroy, just slower, and the warming-current part feels like a detail, not a refutation.
- Paul Ehrlich's predictions of mass famine in the decades right after his book came out: falsified, and when defenders say "he meant if nothing changed," that feels like cheating -- everything is if nothing changed.
- Fusion people saying commercial fusion is thirty years away, every decade since the fifties: not even a prediction anymore, just a vibe, and it earns nothing if fusion ever shows up.
- My neighbor who predicted a housing crash for ten years straight and then claimed vindication when 2008 arrived for reasons (structured credit, leverage) he never once mentioned: not vindicated, even though the event happened and he was "early."

The problem is I can't reconcile the fishery with the housing crash -- both named a thing that came true late and partly for other reasons, and I'm counting one as right-but-early and the other as dumb luck. So: is there a principle that seperates genuine right-but-earliness from being wrong with a good excuse? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and tell me what it does with Malthus -- especially whether his preface sentence, taken seriously, is the right test, or whether it lets theories off too easy by quietly swapping the mechanism in for the prediction.
