So I'm a novelist (mostly literary fiction, this is my first real swing at sci-fi) and I'm stuck on a problem that I suspect is secretly a philosophy problem wearing a trench coat. My book has three non-human intelligent characters. One is a swarm of bioengineered wasps: the swarm as a whole does clever things, but no individual wasp learns anything ever, they were just bred over generations to behave that way. One is the protagonist's dog, uplifted slightly, which basically figures out the world by trying stuff and remembering what got it treats or got it hurt. And one is a ship's computer that, before it does anything risky, sort of plays out the scenario internally and discards the plans that would fail without ever actually executing them.

Here's the thing: my beta readers keep saying all three "feel the same," like they're all just humans in different costumes, and I think they're right but I can't articulate what SHOULD distinguish them. Like, what is the actual deep difference between being smart because your ancestors were selected into it, being smart because you personally learn from reward and punishment, and being smart because you can simulate? How would each of them differently behave when they hit a totally novel situation the story throws at them? What are the characteristic ways each one fails, freezes, or gets stuck -- because I feel like great characterization lives in the failure modes.

And one more question that's driving the book's ending: my protagonist is teaching the dog words, and I want to know if I'm cheating. Is there something genuinely different that happens when a creature gets to import other minds' tools -- language, labels, tricks it didn't invent -- versus just thinking harder on its own? Is that a quantitative boost or a qualitative jump to a different kind of mind entirely? I'd love an answer that takes the fictional setup seriously and tells me where my instincts are right and where I'm smuggling in bad assumptions about what minds even are.
