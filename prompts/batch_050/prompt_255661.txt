For a philosophy class project I tried to reconstruct how Descartes gets from the cogito to dualism, and the argument I came up with bothers me because it seems to prove too much: if it works, one short line of thought settles that I am an immaterial soul, and I can't believe it's that easy. But I also can't find the step to reject. Here it is:

(1) I can doubt whether my body exists, since everything I take to be bodily experience could in principle be a dream or the product of a deceiving demon.
(2) I cannot doubt that I exist while I am doubting, because doubting that I exist is itself something I do — the doubt confirms the doubter.
(3) So "I exist" is certain for me in a way that "my body exists" is not.
(4) If my mind were simply identical to my body (or to some part of it, like the brain), then whatever held of the mind would hold of the body and vice versa; in particular, it would be impossible for the existence of one to be doubtable while the existence of the other is not.
(5) By (1)–(3), my body's existence is doubtable while my mind's existence is not.
(6) Therefore my mind is not identical with my body: it is something that could exist without a body at all — an immaterial thinking thing.

My worry is that the argument's shape looks reusable. If the cogito can carry this much weight, then similar reasoning from what I can and can't doubt seems like it could establish all sorts of surprising separations between things I thought were identical, and I suspect the argument would thereby "prove" things that are false. If I had to keep something, I'd most like to keep (2), since it really does seem impossible to doubt my own existence mid-doubt. But I don't see why (4) should be in trouble either — identical things surely share all their features.

Please tell me exactly which step goes wrong, explain why it looked fine, and say what it costs to reject it versus rejecting one of the others instead. Maximum 500 words.
