I'm a philosophy professor who, for my sins, sits on our university's alumni honors committee, and I have to write the decision memo for our meeting next month. Here's the case. A retired general, class of '63, is up for having his distinguished alumni award revoked. As a cadet he was involved in a hazing incident that left another student permanently injured; it was covered up at the time and only came to light decades later. Here's the wrinkle: at a 1991 internal review, he testified and clearly remembered the whole episode -- it's in the record, he described it in detail. But he had a stroke a few years back, and while he remembers his career fine, including going through the 1991 review (he recalls "having to answer for all that"), he now has no memory whatsoever of the incident itself. His lawyers argue he is, in the only sense that matters for blame, no longer the person who did it -- you can't hold someone accountable for something that's simply not part of their remembered life. The injured man's family wants the award gone and says a man who remembers being the officer who remembered is the same man.

Write the memo: a concrete recommendation (revoke, keep, or something in between with conditions), and actually grapple with the identity issue rather than handwaving it. Give the strongest version of the side you reject, because I have to defend this in the room.
