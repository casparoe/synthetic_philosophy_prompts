Exam question: "Explain the 'expanding circle' account of moral progress. What does it claim about how and why the scope of moral concern has grown over history, and what is the strongest objection to it?"

Student's answer:
"The expanding circle account, defended by Peter Singer, says that moral progress throughout history has consisted in steadily widening the boundary of who counts morally. We started out caring only about kin and tribe, then extended concern to the nation, then to all human beings regardless of race or sex, and more recently to animals. Abolition of slavery and women's suffrage are the obvious milestones. Singer's explanation is that moral reasoning is what pushes the circle outward: reason lets us take an impartial standpoint, and from that standpoint we can see that the interests of outsiders matter just as much as our own, which is his principle of equal consideration of interests. So the historical expansion of the circle shows that equal consideration of interests is the correct moral principle -- the fact that societies have progressively come to recognize the interests of more and more beings proves that their interests were objectively equal all along. Given how consistently the circle has expanded, we can expect it to keep expanding until it covers all sentient beings; moral progress has a direction and further expansion is more or less inevitable, since reason can't be un-invented. The strongest objection is that the account is too optimistic about human nature -- people are strongly biased toward their own group, and partiality to family and friends isn't something reason can or should eliminate, so the circle may not be able to expand much further and maybe shouldn't."
