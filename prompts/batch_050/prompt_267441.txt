I am a retired research chemist. For the last twelve years of my career I sat on government advisory committees — chemical safety first, then civil contingency planning — and in retirement I have joined a philosophy reading group. We have just spent two sessions on the problem of dirty hands: whether a political actor can do the right thing and yet still have done something wrong. The others argued through famous examples from history; I found I could only think through cases, several of them close to my own experience. I have written down my verdicts as honestly as I can, and I want to know whether a single principle captures them — or whether the pattern is merely the professional deformation of a scientist rather than a moral view at all.

The cases, with my verdicts:

1. A minister authorizes a drone strike on the leader of a terrorist cell, accepting near-certainty that two of the man's children will die; the intelligence says the cell is days from a mass-casualty attack and there is no other window. My verdict: justified, and clean. The minister should lose no sleep beyond what the arithmetic of the thing already cost. Talk of stained hands here strikes me as self-indulgence.

2. Early in a pandemic, a senior scientific adviser, fearing a public run on respirators that would leave hospitals unprotected, tells the press that masks are of little use to the general public, though he believes otherwise. My verdict: wrong — and I would call it wrong even if I were convinced it saved lives, because he lied about the science itself, the one thing his office exists to tell straight.

3. Police receive a credible, specific threat against a public marathon two days off. They cancel the event "on grounds of unsafe weather" rather than disclose the threat, partly to avoid panic, partly to protect an ongoing investigation. The truth comes out within a week. My verdict: entirely fine. I can feel that this sits oddly with case 2, and I cannot yet say why.

4. The government purchases a large quantity of vaccines from a regime that will use both the money and the photograph to entrench itself. My verdict: fine, no residue. Refusal would have been grandstanding at other people's expense.

5. A junior member of my old committee once leaked to a journalist an internal report that the department had, in my view improperly, sat on. The leak did real good, and real harm to people who had trusted her. My verdict: she was right to do it, and she also betrayed the committee; I would not have her back. Both halves of that verdict are load-bearing.

6. In a ministers' briefing I once answered "the risk is low" in a tone I knew would be heard as "negligible," because the honest version — low, with a tail we cannot rule out — had already been tried and had lost the room. Nothing bad came of it. Of everything on this list, this is the one that still troubles me, and I notice it troubles me more than case 1, which seems backwards. My verdict: I did something wrong, though I could not point to a victim.

7. The case I go back and forth on: more than once I stayed on a committee while the government pursued a policy I regarded as reckless, reasoning that my resignation would merely hand the seat to someone worse and forfeit whatever moderating influence I had. I still do not know whether that was stewardship or vanity.

What I want from you: tell me whether one principle — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without you — captures verdicts 1 through 6, and what it says about 7. My own tentative principle is that the moral residue tracks dishonesty rather than harm; test it against the cases and repair or replace it. I want to know in particular whether cases 2 and 3 can be kept apart without contrivance, and whether my indifference to case 1 and my shame about case 6 can both be principled or whether one of them has to go.

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

- **The principle** (~150 words): your best candidate, or two if no single one comes close, stated precisely.
- **Clause by clause** (~250 words): which of my cases motivates each clause, so I can see where the parts come from.
- **The awkward pair** (~150 words): how the principle handles cases 2 and 3, and at what cost.
- **Cases I did not raise** (~150 words): its verdicts on two further cases — a central banker who lies to depositors to stop a run, and a general who writes letters of condolence he knows to be false — and whether those verdicts should be acceptable to me.
- **The cases that are mine** (~150 words): what it says about case 7, and whether my shame about case 6 survives.
- **What I must surrender** (~100 words): which of my stated verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate — and, since you will have noticed it, whether the whole pattern is a moral insight or a chemist's guild loyalty dressed up as one.
