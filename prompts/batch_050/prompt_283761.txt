I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the philosophy of math. I keep running into this question philosophers apparently love: is 2 an element of 4? The setup, as I understand it, is that on one standard way of building numbers out of sets (von Neumann's), 4 = {0, 1, 2, 3}, so yes, 2 ∈ 4 — but on another way (Zermelo's) the answer is no, and people take this to show numbers can't just *be* sets. Fine. But when I intervewed two philosophers about structuralism — the idea that numbers are just positions in a structure, not objects with their own insides — they gave me opposite spins. One said structuralism dissolves the question: asking whether 2 ∈ 4 is a category error, like asking whether shortstop is a part of second base. The other said structuralism doesn't dissolve anything, it just moves the problem up a level — now you ask whether the "2-position" in the natural numbers is the same position as the "2-position" in the reals, or whether the structure of the naturals is identical to the structure of the evens, and you're right back in the same mess. Can you explain, at gut level, who's actually right here? Specifically: (1) why the structuralist thinks the set question was ill-posed rather than just unsolved, and whether that's a real insight or just definitional fiat; (2) whether the "same problem one level up" objection really is the same problem or a different one wearing a trench coat; and (3) what work the phrase "position in a structure" is doing and whether it can bear that weight. No citations needed — I just need to actually get this before my deadline.
