I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long piece on why clever, well-informed people continue to disagree. A central exhibit is Aumann's agreement theorem: the result that two agents who share a prior, and whose posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, must assign the event the same probability. I have been following an extended, mostly fruitless exchange between two camps of researchers, and for the piece I want to reconstruct the joint statement they should have written had they run a proper adversarial collaboration. Draft that document for me.

The two camps are as follows:

- The convergence camp holds that the theorem, together with its dynamic extension (two parties who take turns announcing their current posterior, updating on the other's announcement at each step, will converge to a shared estimate in finitely many rounds), has genuine normative bite for human reasoners. On their view, persistent disagreement between honest, mutually respected interlocutors is a sign that at least one party is failing somewhere: holding a private prior they have not examined, refusing to treat the other's opinion as evidence, or simply not listening. They think the idealised assumptions are best read as a standard of rationality to approximate, not a description to satisfy, and they point to phenomena like prediction markets as evidence that the ideal is approachable in practice.

- The independence camp holds that the theorem is a rigorous result about creatures that do not and cannot exist, and that it tells us almost nothing about actual disagreement. Their case rests on three points. First, real agents are computationally bounded, and exact Bayesian updating is intractable in any realistically rich domain, so the agents the theorem describes are not idealisations of us but a different species of agent altogether. Second, the common prior assumption does the heavy lifting and is precisely what disputants typically lack. Third, even granting the framework, a bare posterior is not self-interpreting: when you tell me your probability, I can only update on it correctly if I know how your evidence maps to your estimates, and in general I would need something close to your entire body of private evidence to do so -- at which point we have not had a conversation but a merger. They conclude that respectful, durable disagreement is often fully rational and that the convergence camp is mistaking a mathematical curiosity for an epistemic duty.

The document should contain the following:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims: statements about what the theorem does and does not assume and establish (including the difference between common knowledge and merely deep mutual knowledge, and whether the dynamic version sidesteps any of the static version's assumptions), phrased so that both camps would sign them. Include at least one agreed claim that each camp would find mildly uncomfortable.

2. A disagreements table: each row stating one residual disagreement in wording both sides would accept as a fair statement of their own position, followed by a one-line gloss of each camp's stance.

3. A cruxes section: identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one camp to retreat substantially. For each crux, specify a case, protocol, or observation that both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts. At least one crux should concern whether the intractability of ideal updating severs the link between the theorem's agents and human ones, and at least one should be testable against a concrete, real-world institution or experimental design rather than a thought experiment.

4. A closing note identifying any part of the dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, with your reasoning -- and if you judge that no part is verbal, say so and explain why.

Neither camp should be made to look foolish; each holds a defensible position that the other has partly misunderstood. Please write the answer in British English.
