I am defending a deliberately strong version of moral generalism, and I want its exposure mapped rather than its virtues rehearsed. By “completeness generalism” I mean not the banal claim that principles are sometimes useful, nor the psychological claim that good agents consciously consult rules, but this: for every morally assessable act-token there is a complete non-evaluative description of the situation, and a finite or at least recursively specifiable set of principles, such that the correct verdict is fixed by those principles once the description is total. Apparent exceptions are therefore always one of three things: an incomplete description, a misstated principle, or a higher-order defeat condition that could itself have been stated in advance. On this view, particularists are right that reasons are context-sensitive, but wrong to infer that context-sensitivity outruns codifiability; “holism” names the complexity of the code, not its absence.

The pressure point is Aristotle’s complaint in Book VI of the Nicomachean Ethics: “But such a statement, though true, is by no means clear; for not only here but in all other pursuits which are objects of knowledge it is indeed true to say that we must not exert ourselves nor relax our efforts too much nor too little, but to an intermediate extent and as the right rule dictates; but if a man had only this knowledge he would be none the wiser e.g. we should not know what sort of medicines to apply to our body if some one were to say 'all those which the medical art prescribes, and which agree with the practice of one who possesses the art'. Hence it is necessary with regard to the states of the soul also not only that this true statement should be made, but also that it should be determined what is the right rule and what is the standard that fixes it.” Read one way, this is friendly to my position: Aristotle does not deny that there is a “right rule” and a “standard”; he denies that gesturing at them is enough. Read another way, it is hostile: the practically wise person seems to possess something that cannot be transmitted by giving the rule to someone who lacks the relevant perception. Treat the passage as a test case. Which reading is stronger, and what exactly would completeness generalism have to say about the difference among four things: the existence of a right standard, its articulability, its sufficiency for instruction, and its authority when articulated principle and cultivated perception conflict?

Then tell me what would count against the view, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Include, but do not limit yourself to: genuine reason-polarity reversals where the very same consideration changes valence and no further morally relevant difference can be specified without circularity; cases in which every proposed exception clause generates a fresh residue, suggesting moral open texture rather than unfinished housekeeping; thick concepts whose evaluative and descriptive components cannot be separated without changing the verdict; results or analogies from nonmonotonic reasoning, default logic, skill acquisition, or medical diagnosis that would show codifiability failing even where truth is not in doubt; and sociological evidence that the most reliable judges converge in ways rule-followers systematically miss. For each candidate defeater, say what the world would have to be like for it to work, whether a committed completeness generalist should concede it as a defeater or dismiss it as autobiography about limited agents, and what redescription or retreat it would still leave available.

Be especially strict about insulation. If every counterexample is absorbed by adding “unless defeating condition C obtains,” at what point has completeness generalism become the claim that morality is codifiable if it is codifiable? State the strongest formulation that still risks something, identify the prediction it makes that particularism does not, and name the single case—realistic rather than science-fictional—that would most economically decide between them. Do not provide citations or a bibliography; argue from the structure of the views, the quoted passage, and examples you construct yourself.
