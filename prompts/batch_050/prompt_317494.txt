I am a philosophy professor serving a rotating term on my university's Research Computing Governance Committee, and I have been asked to draft the committee's decision memo on the following case. Please write that memo.

Our shared high-performance computing cluster is chronically oversubscribed. The vendor's scheduler supports exactly two modes, and we must select one for the coming three-year contract period.

Mode A (uniform throttling). Every job is slowed slightly so that no user ever waits long for a job to start. Modeling predicts that each of our roughly 4,000 research users will lose about 25 hours per year to accumulated small delays: irritating, occasionally the cause of a missed internal milestone, never ruinous. Total projected loss: about 100,000 researcher-hours annually. However, the model also shows a statistical tail of roughly 60 users per year — unidentifiable in advance — whose particular workloads interact badly with throttling and who would each lose 300 hours or more.

Mode B (strict priority windows). Almost all routine delay disappears, but projects whose computations are tied to immovable external deadlines — a climate group with fixed satellite overpass windows, a team analyzing clinical-trial data against a regulatory filing date, and a few others, about 20 researchers across six labs — will predictably fail to secure the contiguous blocks they need, losing results that took years to prepare and cannot simply be re-run. These victims are identifiable now, and the committee holds a discretionary fund that could compensate them (renting commercial computing time at roughly triple the cost, hiring additional staff), covering perhaps two-thirds of their loss.

The memo must recommend one mode, with an actionable bottom line. It should state the precedent we are setting: a general rule about when, if ever, very many trivial burdens can collectively outweigh a few devastating ones; whether the advance identifiability and compensability of Mode B's victims changes the calculus, or merely prices it; and how the unidentifiable tail under Mode A should count. The strongest argument for the rejected option should be stated well enough that its proponents would recognize it as their own, and the memo should specify what further evidence, if obtainable, would warrant reversing the decision. Argue from the case itself rather than from the philosophical literature, and flag honestly if the vendor's no-hybrid constraint is doing more work in the reasoning than it should.
