I want a case analysis of the 1924 Leopold and Loeb trial through the lens of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. Quick refresher on both so we're on the same page. Leopold and Loeb were two wealthy, brilliant Chicago teenagers who murdered a 14-year-old boy, Bobby Franks, largely to prove they could pull off the perfect crime. They pled guilty, so the only live question was sentencing, and Clarence Darrow gave his legendary marathon closing argument begging the judge to spare them the noose. His pitch, roughly: these boys did not make themselves. Heredity, upbringing, too much money, too much Nietzsche, the whole machinery of their psychology was assembled by forces they never chose, and you can't hang a person for being what the world made him. It worked -- life sentences.

Strawson's Basic Argument, meanwhile: what you do flows from the way you are; to be truly responsible for what you do you'd have to be truly responsible for the way you are; but to be responsible for the way you are you'd have to have intentionally brought it about that you are that way, on the basis of principles you chose, which just raises the same question one step back -- infinite regress, terminable only by being causa sui, which is impossible. So nobody is ultimately morally responsible for anything, full stop, and note the argument doesn't care whether determinism is true.

Now the analysis, in both directions. First, map Darrow's speech onto the Basic Argument premise by premise -- which premises does he actually commit to, and which does he swap out for something else? My suspicion is the interesting answer is that Darrow smuggles in determinism plus psychiatric abnormality (diseased minds, they were still teenagers) exactly where Strawson pointedly needs neither. Strawson's argument works just as well for sane middle-aged accountants in an indeterministic universe. So is Darrow's defense a version of the Basic Argument at all, or a different argument -- diminished capacity, comparative mitigation -- wearing its clothes?

Second, the direction the theory can't easily travel. Strawson's conclusion is universal, but a sentancing hearing is a comparative exercise: the judge must distinguish these defendants from the next ones. If Darrow's mitigation works, it works for every defendant who ever lived, which means it either abolishes desert-based punishment wholesale or it works for nobody. Where exactly does the Basic Argument go silent inside a courtroom -- at guilt (mens rea survives untouched, right?), at forward-looking sentencing (deterrence, incapacitation), or somewhere else? And there's an uglier version of the problem: as actually deployed, you-didn't-make-yourself mitigation tracks privilege -- the wealthier the defendant, the more experts available to testify about how their environment determined them. Does this episode show the Basic Argument functioning in practice as an excuse for the well-lawyered, and does that count against the argument itself or only against its courtroom use?

Third, run the episode back at Strawson. The judge did spare them, and plenty of people felt the pull of Darrow's speech even while finding the crime monstrous. Is that evidence for the Basic Argument -- proof that even ordinary folks feel ultimate desert crumble once the causal story gets vivid enough? Or does it cut the other way: nobody, Darrow included, concluded that therefore no one deserves punishment; they concluded these two deserved less. That suggests the live intuition is comparative responsibility, a notion the Basic Argument basicly doesn't touch and maybe can't even state. End with a verdict: does the trial confirm the Basic Argument, expose it as too blunt an instrument for the questions that actually matter, or reveal that it was never an argument about legal responsibility in the first place?
