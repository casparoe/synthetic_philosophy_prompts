In policy debates over brain–computer interfaces and consumer neurotechnology, advocates of "neurorights" routinely argue that neural data should be regulated the way genetic data is: both are said to be uniquely identifying, impossible to change, and revelatory of facts the person may not know about themselves, and the analogy is then used to license importing the protective regime built for genetic information — heightened consent requirements, bans on discriminatory use, restrictions on secondary sale — onto data collected by headsets, implants, and neuromarketing devices. Act as a philosopher working at the intersection of neuroethics and the philosophy of privacy, and audit this analogy carefully. Begin by brainstorming the explicit correspondences: what plays the role of the genome's fixed sequence, of its implications for biological relatives, of its forensic use, and of the doctrine of genetic exceptionalism itself. Then work out where the mapping breaks: neural data is dynamic rather than static; a BCI is often not merely a sensor but a potential actuator, so the "data" is continuous with intervention in a way a sequenced genome is not; the sensitive content is inferred by decoding models rather than read off a sequence, so what the data reveals depends on future technical progress in a way that may have no genetic analogue. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the regulatory conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic, and say whether the special-protection conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — and if the genetic analogy does more to mislead than to guide, say whether a better source analogue exists (a private diary? medical imaging? voice recordings?) or whether the comparison should be abandoned altogether.
