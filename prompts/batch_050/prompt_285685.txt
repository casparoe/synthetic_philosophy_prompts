I'm in a criminal law and philosophy seminar this term, and we've spent the semester working through the orthodox view that criminal conviction requires both a wrongful act and a culpable mental state — purpose, knowledge, recklessness, or at least negligence. The professor insists this is the defining feature that distinguishes criminal punishment from mere regulation, and modern codes (the Model Penal Code is our standard reference) enshrine it as a default. But the same legal systems that profess this orthodoxy maintain a large class of strict liability offenses where no mental state need be proven at all, and some of these are hard to dismiss as edge cases. A shopkeeper who sells alcohol to a minor can be convicted even when the minor presented an expertly forged ID that would have fooled anyone, and reasonable reliance on the ID is no defense. A company can be criminally liable for a pollution discharge even when its safety procedures met or exceeded every industry standard, so no one was careless, let alone reckless. What struck me is that the system seems to talk out of both sides of its mouth: it treats the absence of fault as a reason to acquit in one courtroom and as legally irrelevant in the next, sometimes for conduct that results in a criminal record in both.

Here is what I'd like worked out. What view of criminal responsibility, and of what punishment is for, does the practice of strict criminal liability presuppose? Several candidates occur to me, and I'm not sure which, if any, the practice actually needs. It might presuppose that outcomes rather than mental states are what liability ultimately tracks — that the law cares about the harm done and uses the defendant as the nearest point of intervention. It might instead presuppose a division of labor within the law itself: that some offenses are not really condemnations at all but regulatory prices, and that calling them "criminal" is just a labeling convention. Or it might rest on a kind of implied consent: that anyone who voluntarily enters a heavily regulated line of business — selling liquor, handling hazardous materials — has accepted the risk of liability without fault as a condition of entry, so that fault was assessed at the moment of entry rather than at the moment of the offense.

Please do three things. First, reconstruct which of these theories (or which other theory) the practice of strict liability most plausibly presupposes, distinguishing carefully between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely fits comfortably — I suspect it is underdetermined and that several rival theories would license the same outcomes, so say where the underdetermination lies. Second, assess whether the presupposed theory is consistent with the orthodox mens rea principle as it operates elsewhere in the same system: is there a principled line between the offenses where fault is required and those where it isn't, or is the boundary historical and political rather than philosophical? Third, tell me what would have to change in actual legal practice if we committed fully to whichever theory you think the practice presupposes — for instance, if strict liability offenses really are regulatory prices rather than condemnations, should they carry different names, different penalties, different procedural protections, and no criminal record, and what would be lost if they did?
