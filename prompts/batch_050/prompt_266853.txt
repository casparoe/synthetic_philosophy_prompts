So I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about the fight over what "disease" even means -- the naturalists who think it's a biological fact you could in principle settle in a lab (the Boorse-ish line: disease is a failure of a part to perform its statistically normal species-typical function), versus the normativists who think calling something a disease is always partly a value judgment and there's no purely biological line to find. My editor keeps pushing me to take a side, and honestly I've come around to the naturalist side. Not because the normativist examples aren't annoying for it -- they are -- but because it seems to me like the only version of the view that doesn't make medicine's categories hostage to whoever's doing the valuing that decade.

Here's what I need for the piece: what would actually count against naturalism? And I don't just want the greatest hits (homosexuality getting depathologized, drapetomania, the usual "values sneak in" stuff). I want the real exposure map. Which specific cases does the naturalist *have* to be able to classify a certain way, and what happens if they can't? I'm thinking about things like: aging (if decline with age is statistically normal for the species, is nothing about being 85 a disease?), infertility in someone who doesn't want kids, pregnancy in someone who does, mild stuff like presbyopia that basically everyone gets, and the mismatch argument from evolutionary medicine -- the idea that a trait can be functioning exactly as designed and still be bad for you in a modern environment. Does the mismatch point break the naturalist picture or does it just show that "healthy" and "well-adapted to your current environment" come apart, which the naturalist might happily accept?

Also the reference class problem: normal relative to *what* group -- age, sex, ancestry, population? I've seen people say the whole statistical-function move collapses here, and others say it's a detail. Which is it? And when blood pressure or LDL thresholds get redrawn by committees, is that evidence against naturalism, or is it just risk management wearing a white coat, which naturalists can concede without giving up anything?

I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one, be honest about whether a committed naturalist would concede it counts against the view or whether they'd bat it away -- I don't want critics' objections dressed up as internal problems. And finally: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? Every time I press a naturalist they retreat to "well, function talk is doing the work" and function talk turns out to be its own swamp. If the view can't lose, is that a problem for it, or is "disease is a naturalistic notion" more like a framework bet than an empirical claim? I need to know what I'd have to see to change my mind, becuase right now I'm not sure anything would.
