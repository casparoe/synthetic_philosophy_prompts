So I cover AI stuff for a magazine, and I keep circling this idea people in the safety world have, that we'd like AI systems that "don't cause side effects" or "stay low-impact" while doing their jobs. I want to stress-test my own gut on what counts as a side effect, so here are my verdicts on some cases. Tell me what principle, if any, unifies them.

Warehouse robot takes a longer route so it doesn't block the corridor people use: obviously good, no issue. Same robot opens a window to cool the room and rain ruins some papers: bad side effect, should've been discouraged. Robot waters the plant and the plant grows: fine, even though that's a change to the world. Robot cleans a spill with bleach and lifts a carpet stain the owner actually liked: bad, feels like the window case. Robot makes the owner coffee every morning and the coffee changes his mood and alertness all day: fine, that's literally the job, but by any "did the world change because of the robot" measure it's a big change. Robot defuses a gas leak it notices on its route: big change to what would have happened, obviously good. Robot keeps everything in the house exactly as it was at 9am, including physically stopping the owner from moving his own furniture: horrifying, but it's "preventing change" just like the gas leak. Robot knocks over a vase, overnight orders an identical replacement, owner never notices: net zero change, but something still feels off, like it got away with something. And the one I flip-flop on: robot does the bookkeeping so well that the owner, who used to enjoy it, never does it again and the skill atrophies. Some days I think that's a side effect, some days I think that's just what hiring anyone does.

Here's my trouble. My gut says the coffee and the gas leak are fine but the window and the furniture-freezer are not, and "amount of change" clearly isn't doing the work, since the good cases can change plenty and the bad case can change almost nothing. Is there a principle that gets these right without smuggling in a complete theory of what's good for the owner -- because if we had that, we wouldn't need a seperate low-impact idea at all? And if every principle that sorts my cases ends up needing to know what the owner values, should I conclude that "avoid side effects" was never a real shortcut, or is there some middle position? Also tell me which of my verdicts you think I'd have to give up to get a clean principle, and what the principle says about the bookkeeping case.
