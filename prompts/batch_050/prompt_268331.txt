In my reporting on how expert bodies make decisions, I keep encountering a structural oddity I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know whether the literature does.

Two instances. First, a university committee voting on whether to fund a new research center, under an agreed rule: fund it only if the proposal is scientifically sound and the budget allows it. Nine members vote on each question separately. Six judge the science sound; six judge the budget sufficient; but only four judge both. So a majority endorses each consideration, while a majority answers the bottom-line question, fund it or not, with no. The committee's collective position is apparently: the science is sound, the money exists, and the center should not be funded. Second, a magazine's editorial board deciding whether to publish an investigation, with the rule: publish only if the reporting is accurate and the public interest justifies it. Same arithmetic, same result, and considerable embarrassment when a reader points out that the board's stated views appear inconsistent.

Does this configuration — majority support for every premise, majority rejection of the conclusion those premises jointly entail, with no one disputing the logic — have an established name? I suspect it is not the same as the famous voting paradox in which preferences cycle (the group prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A), since here there are no cycles and every individual is perfectly consistent. If an established term exists, tell me how the phenomenon relates to that cyclical case and to ordinary group irrationality; if not, coin one, define it precisely, and mark its boundaries. Two further questions I care about: does anything change, conceptually, if the members vote only on the premises and the chair simply announces the conclusion as following automatically, rather than putting it to a vote? And is there any voting procedure that avoids the problem without simply deciding in advance whether premises or conclusions take priority? A bare label won't satisfy me — I want to understand exactly where the edges of this phenomenon lie.
