I am writing a paper on W. E. B. Du Bois's aesthetics, anchored in "Criteria of Negro Art," the address he gave in June 1926 at the NAACP's celebration of Carter G. Woodson's Spingarn Medal and published in The Crisis that October, in which he declares that all art is propaganda and always has been, and urges Black artists to stop apologizing for this and instead choose their propaganda deliberately. I have collected what I take to be the standard interpretive and critical moves in the literature on this essay, and I am stuck: everything I have drafted as a fresh contribution turns out, on inspection, to be one of the moves below in new clothes. One ground rule before the list: a genuinely new move must not be a rewording of any item, a special case of one, or two of them bolted together.

The moves I already have: (1) The conjunctural reading: the universal claim is really a strategic license for counter-propaganda. In a market that offered Black artists a choice between minstrel caricature and primitivist exoticism for white consumption, and against a visual culture saturated with racist imagery, declaring all art propaganda authorizes answering propaganda with propaganda; the thesis is cultural strategy for 1926, not aesthetic theory. (2) The unmasking reading: the point of saying all art is propaganda is to deny the art-for-art's-sake camp its self-image. The purists produce propaganda too, for a status quo whose values pass as neutral; so the thesis is symmetrical and deflationary rather than a special plea for tendentious Black art. (3) The autonomy objection, associated with Alain Locke and the younger Harlem Renaissance cohort: subordinating art to propaganda flattens it into didacticism, and the demand for positive race images is itself a censorship that reproduces the moralistic gatekeeping it opposes; the essay's doctrine licenses bad art. (4) The practice-versus-theory tension: the Du Bois that posterity most admires, above all The Souls of Black Folk, is prized for aesthetic qualities that outrun any propagandistic brief, while his most deliberately propagandistic fiction, Dark Princess, which he reportedly called his favorite of his books, is widely judged his weakest; so either the theory misdescribes his own best practice, or propaganda in his mouth means something far thinner than advocacy. (5) The reconciliation reading: Du Bois says in the essay that he does not care a damn for any art that is not beautiful and great, so his position is not instrumentalism; propaganda names the direction the artist's power serves, beauty remains the criterion of success, and the real claim is that for a despised people the two cannot be pried apart.

Give me one more move, an interpretation or an objection, that survives the ground rule. It should engage the essay itself or its immediate context (for instance, the symposium on the portrayal of Black characters that Du Bois ran in The Crisis that same year, or his editorial practice at the magazine) rather than the generic debate about art and politics, and it should be the sort of thing a careful reader of the essay could actually defend. Then do two things: explain, item by item, why it is not merely a restatement or combination of (1) through (5); and assess it honestly, meaning that if it collapses into one of the listed moves under pressure, or if it is new but weak, you say so and say why. A colleague has already proposed to me that the essay is fundamentally about freedom, with propaganda as the price of admission to white attention; I judged that a restatement of (1), and I mention it so you can see the bar I am applying. If you think I judged wrongly, you may say so, but then that correction is your answer and you must defend it on the same terms.
