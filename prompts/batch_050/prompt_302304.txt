Two of my colleagues have been arguing for years about insect sentience and have finally agreed to write up where they stand. One is a hedonic utilitarian persuaded by the scale argument: insects outnumber us by an almost incomprehensible factor, so even a small probability that they have valenced experiences means their expected welfare may dominate the moral calculus, and it is irrational to demand certainty before acting on that. The other is a skeptic who holds that we have no positive evidence of valenced experience in insects, that reflexive and learning behavior can be produced by very simple control systems, and that multiplying an unfounded probability by a vast number is not reasoning but numerical theater. Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should produce: the claims both can sign (about the scale of insect populations and harms, about what behavioral and neuroanatomical evidence can and cannot establish, about whether species membership as such is morally relevant); the precise statements of where they still differ, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes -- I expect one to be what would actually count as evidence of valenced experience in a nervous system of a hundred thousand to a million neurons, and another to be how one ought to reason when the relevant probability cannot be assigned from evidence at all; and for each crux, a concrete experiment, observation, or argument that both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, identify which part and say why. Format it as a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements section, followed by the cruxes with their tests and predictions.
