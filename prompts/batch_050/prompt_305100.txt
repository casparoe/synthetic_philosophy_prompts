You are a professional philosopher with expertise in population ethics and normative theory, and you have coached many graduate students through hostile seminar Q&As. Think carefully and step by step about what follows; before drafting your final answer, privately brainstorm the full range of objections this particular audience would raise, then select and organize the best material.

Here is my situation. Next month I am giving a department colloquium defending total utilitarianism in population ethics by openly biting the bullet on the repugnant conclusion. My argument sketch runs as follows: (1) the impossibility results in population axiology show that every view must reject some deeply held intuition, so the question is which rejection costs least; (2) our intuitive recoil from a vast population of lives barely worth living is an artifact of how we imagine that world -- we picture drab, monotonous, crowded lives rather than lives that are genuinely, if modestly, positive; (3) once we correct for this imaginative distortion and for our poor intuitive grip on large quantities of welfare, the repugnant conclusion loses its sting; and (4) the alternatives (average utilitarianism, critical-level views, lexical thresholds) each generate conclusions at least as counterintuitive, including ones most philosophers flinch from more than the repugnant conclusion itself.

The audience: a philosophy department colloquium. About a third of the room works on ethics or political philosophy but not population ethics specifically; they know the repugnant conclusion as a standard objection but have not internalized the impossibility theorems. There are two or three people who do work on population ethics and know the impossibility results well, and they are the ones I most fear. There are also a couple of Kantians who dislike aggregative consequentialism on principle, and one economist who thinks in terms of social choice theory and will frame objections in terms of the formal axioms rather than intuitions.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. It should contain:

1. The eight to ten objections I am most likely to get from this specific audience, ordered by how damaging each would be if I handle it badly, not by how famous the objection is in the literature. Distinguish clearly between the objections the population ethics specialists will raise (the technically serious ones) and the objections the rest of the room will raise (the rhetorically dangerous ones, where a clumsy answer loses the audience even if it is philosophically fine).

2. Each objection phrased the way that audience member would actually phrase it in a question period -- the Kantian's version should sound like a Kantian, the economist's like someone thinking about axiom violations, the non-specialist's like someone whose intuition I have just insulted.

3. For each objection: a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, plus a candid note on where that reply is soft or where a skilled questioner could push back a second time.

4. A section on what I should concede gracefully versus what I must hold -- in particular, whether my "imaginative distortion" move survives contact with someone who claims to have imagined the repugnant conclusion's world carefully and still finds it repugnant.

5. The question I am hoping nobody asks, and a fallback answer for it in case someone does.

Write the prep sheet in American English. Aim for something I could actually study the week before the talk: concrete, candid about my vulnerabilities, and calibrated to this audience rather than to an idealized seminar of population axiology specialists.
