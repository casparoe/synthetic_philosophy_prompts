My day job is building structural causal models for an insurance company, and outside of work I'm in a reading group with a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD. Last week she tried to explain epiphenomenalism about consciousness to me -- roughly, the view that phenomenal experiences (what pain feels like, what red looks like) are caused by brain processes but themselves cause nothing, not even our beliefs and reports about them. She then presented what she called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experiences are causally inert, then the experience of pain plays no role in producing my judgment that I am in pain, and it starts to look mysterious how that judgment could be accurate, or even how it could be about the experience at all. She found this a serious problem for the view. I found myself wanting to say: you've just described a leaf node. Two children of a common cause correlate without either causing the other; this is the most ordinary structure in my world. We argued for an hour and got nowhere, because we kept talking past each other's vocabulary.

So here is what I want. Restate the epiphenomenalist position and the paradox of phenomenal judgment in the vocabulary I actually work in: directed acyclic graphs, d-separation, interventions and the do-operator, Markov blankets, common-cause confounding, information flow, feature importance for a downstream predictor. Be as precise as you can -- if epiphenomenalism is the claim that phenomenal variables are leaf nodes, say exactly which arrows that removes and what it does and does not imply about conditional dependence between experience and judgment; if the paradox is the claim that a certain judgment could not track a certain variable, say what tracking would even mean given the graph. I am fluent in this framework (Pearl-style causal inference, some information theory), so do not water it down.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which pieces of the philosophical debate translate cleanly into graphical language? Which translate only by being distorted -- for instance, I suspect something gets lost when the philosopher's talk of a judgment being *about* an experience becomes mere statistical dependence, since my models have no notion of aboutness, but I want you to check that rather than take my word for it. And which parts of the debate have no expression in this vocabulary at all -- the role of first-person acquaintance, the worry that the epiphenomenalist undermines her own argument by making it, anything else? Finally, give me your verdict on the residue: does it show that causal-graph vocabulary is missing something real (aboutness, evidence, knowledge), or does it show that the paradox was an artifact of imprecise language that dissolves once you draw the arrows? Please do not smuggle the answer into the translation step by quietly building the conclusion into the graph. American English, please.
