Multi-member appellate courts typically decide cases by voting on each legal issue separately rather than on the outcome directly: each judge votes on whether the statute applies, whether the procedural requirements were met, and so on, and the judgment is whatever follows logically from the issue-by-issue majorities. As is well known, this procedure can yield results no individual judge endorses -- a majority may hold that the statute applies and a majority that the requirements were met, while a majority of judges would nonetheless decide for the defendant, each dissenting on a different issue. Some courts therefore vote only on outcomes. What does the issue-by-issue practice presuppose about what a court is and what its decisions consist in? That a court can itself hold views, rather than merely aggregate them? That a court's holdings, unlike an individual's, need not answer to any single judge's reasoning? That what binds future courts is a set of propositions rather than a disposition of the case? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the court as a collective reasoner, say whether the practice is coherent given that theory or merely sustained by the fact that the incoherence rarely surfaces, and assess whether outcome-only voting resolves the underlying problem or simply conceals it -- and what, if anything, is lost if courts can no longer be said to hold views at all.
