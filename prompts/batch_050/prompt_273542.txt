I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on dilation: the phenomenon whereby conditioning an imprecise credal state on new evidence renders it strictly less precise. I want to stress-test the standard defense of dilation against the strongest objection I can muster, and I need a capable interlocutor to play my opponent across several turns of exchange.

Your role: You are a committed proponent of imprecise probabilities of the strict-conditionalization variety. Specifically, you hold: (i) a rational agent's doxastic state is represented by a set of probability functions (a credal set) rather than by any single function; (ii) learning proceeds by conditioning every member of the set on the total evidence and retaining the resulting set; (iii) dilation, when it occurs, is not an embarrassment but is often the only rational response to evidence — such cases are ones where the evidence fails to constrain the hypothesis at issue in a way the agent is not entitled to ignore. You concede nothing in advance to the precise Bayesian. You may reject the framing of my objections, contest my operative notions (for instance, what it is for evidence to "bear on" a hypothesis), or draw distinctions my arguments suppress, provided you do so from within your position rather than by retreating to ecumenism. Speak in the first person, in the register of a competent philosopher at a workshop: direct, technical where needed, without rhetorical padding. Do not break character, do not survey both sides, and do not close your turns with balanced codas. If I make a technical error in the setup, correct it in character. Keep each reply conversational in scale — roughly 150 to 350 words.

Here is the setup for our exchange. Two coins will be flipped exactly once, and every probability function in your credal set treats the flips as independent. Coin A is known to be fair: every member of your credal set assigns probability 1/2 to its landing heads. Coin B's bias is entirely unknown: for every value p in the unit interval, your credal set contains a probability function assigning bias p to coin B. A trusted referee observes both outcomes and reports only whether the coins landed the same way or differently. Before the report, your credence that coin A lands heads is the singleton {1/2}. If the referee reports that the coins matched, then conditioning each member of your credal set yields credence p that coin A landed heads, for each p in [0,1] — your credal state dilates from {1/2} to the whole interval. The same dilation occurs if the report is that the coins differed.

My opening move: I claim this is a reductio of your view, twice over. First, whatever the referee says, your credence about coin A becomes maximally imprecise — so you can foresee with certainty, before the report arrives, that you are about to pass from a determinate credence to total vacillation about coin A, the very coin whose behavior you understood completely at the outset. The precise Bayesian conditions on the same report and keeps credence 1/2 throughout, which is surely the right result: whether the coins matched conveys information about coin B, the coin you knew nothing about, and none about coin A. Your theory has the epistemic geography exactly backwards. Second, your position appears to license paying to stay ignorant. Suppose you could spend a small sum to prevent the referee from speaking. Since you foresee that the free, truthful report will destroy your precise credence whatever its content, your view seems to recommend the expenditure — a violation of the platitude that costless information is never to be refused. Defend strict conditionalization against both charges.
