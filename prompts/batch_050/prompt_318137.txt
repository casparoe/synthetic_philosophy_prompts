I sit on the claims-review board of a mid-size company that licenses software agents to enterprise customers. Roughly six times a year, a product team comes to us wanting marketing language approved — that a system "understands" the customer's request, "knows" the inventory, "wants" to minimize downtime, "decides" which tickets to escalate. Legal is increasingly worried about AI-washing exposure, and my board gets one working day per review. The members are two engineers, a lawyer, a marketing lead, and me; none of them has read any philosophy of mind, and I have read only a little. I want a written procedure we can run in that day, grounded in the actual philosophical dispute over original and derived intentionality, that ends in an approved or revised wording for each verb the product team proposes.

The dispute, as I understand it. Searle holds that intentionality comes in two grades: minds, or brains, have it originally, intrinsically, in virtue of their causal makeup, whereas artifacts — texts, computers, thermostats — have it at best derivatively, insofar as some interpreter treats their states as meaning something. Dennett replies with a thought experiment about a coin acceptor: a machine built in the United States to detect US quarters is shipped to Panama, where quarter-balboa coins the machine cannot tell apart from quarters circulate, and it reliably sorts those instead. Which does it really detect? Dennett's answer is that there is no deeper fact of the matter than the interpretation it is useful for us to adopt — and he adds that the same is true of us, since our own content-conferring dispositions are products of natural selection rather than of any intrinsic semantic glow. If Dennett is right, then the line my board is tempted to lean on — mere derived intentionality in our products, real intentionality in our customers — is not the bright line Searle promises, and our review procedure cannot simply presuppose it.

What I want is a procedure, written as an ordered sequence of tests a committee without philosophical training can apply to a proposed marketing claim, that preserves what the dispute actually establishes instead of settling it by fiat. For each test, give the question to be asked, what evidence counts as passing it, what happens to the proposed wording if it fails, and whether failure is terminal or can be cured by rewording. Mark explicitly the points where the procedure must make a judgment call because the philosophy is genuinely unsettled, say which distinctions you simplified away and which you refused to drop, and include a short statement of what the procedure cannot settle. Then run it on three cases and give the surviving wording for each: a customer-service language model marketed as understanding the customer's problem; a coin sorter one of our subsidiaries literally redeployed in a foreign currency zone; and a warehouse routing system whose reinforcement learning has produced proxy objectives nobody designed or intended. At least one of these should come out in a way our marketing team will hate — say which, and why. Finally, state how confident you are in the procedure as a whole, and single out the one step or test you are least sure of, with your reason.
