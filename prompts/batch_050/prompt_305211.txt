I belong to a political theory reading group, and ahead of our session on pragmatism one of the members circulated a one-page study guide on Dewey. Its central paragraph runs as follows: 'Dewey's experimentalism holds that a democratic society should be run on the model of a laboratory. Laws, institutions, and policies are hypotheses: we enact them, observe whether they deliver the intended results, and keep or scrap them accordingly. Elections, a free press, and public debate are the measuring instruments of this laboratory — they register whether a policy has worked the way a gauge registers pressure, and the ideal citizen is therefore essentially a careful reader of instruments. Where a social problem is sufficiently technical, Dewey's view licenses handing it to trained specialists, just as we hand bridge-building to engineers. It follows that democracy's worth is instrumental and conditional: it is simply the most reliable error-detection device available for collective problems, and if some other arrangement detected errors better, Dewey would have no further reason to prefer it. The one absolute in his position is that there are no absolutes: a healthy democratic community treats every commitment as provisional and refuses on principle to appeal to fixed ends.'

The technocratic flavor of this bothered me, so I pulled up Dewey's Reconstruction in Philosophy (1920), the book that grew out of lectures he gave in Tokyo in 1919. I have not read far yet, but what I have read sits awkwardly with the guide. In the preface Dewey writes: "I have tried for the most part to set forth the forces which make intellectual reconstruction inevitable and to prefigure some of the lines upon which it must proceed." And the book's analytic table of contents describes intelligence as "hypothetical and inventive," says that "truth is adverbial, not a thing," and lists among the reconstruction's themes "growth as the end," the "moral test of social institutions," and "freedom and democracy." None of this sounds like a thinker who regards citizens as gauges and politics as engineering — but I cannot say precisely where the guide's picture breaks down, since much of it does match what I vaguely associate with pragmatism.

So: how fair is the study guide? Please go through its main claims one at a time — policies as hypotheses to be retained or discarded according to results; elections and the press as measuring instruments with citizens as their readers; the delegation of technical problems to specialists; democracy's value as purely instrumental and conditional; and the refusal of fixed ends — and for each, tell me whether a careful reader of Dewey would accept it outright, accept it only with a qualification the guide omits, or reject it as a distortion. Where the quoted fragments bear on a claim, explain how: does "growth as the end" confirm or undermine the guide's talk of keeping policies that deliver the intended results, and does the preface sentence support or complicate the laboratory metaphor? Finally, does the guide's conditional conclusion — that Dewey would abandon democracy if a better error-detector came along — follow from anything he actually held, and if not, what does the corrected view say democracy is for?
