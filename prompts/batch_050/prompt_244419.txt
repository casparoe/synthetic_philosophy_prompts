So I'm writing a piece for a film-and-philosophy blog about Pixar's Coco, and I want to read it through ubuntu and relational accounts of personhood instead of the usual follow-your-dreams take. Here's where I've gotten and where I'm stuck.

The Land of the Dead looks like a straight-up dramatization of the idea that you are who you are through your relations to others: the dead persist only as long as the living remember them, and the so-called final death arrives when the last person who knew you forgets (the Chicharrón scene). This maps almost eerily onto John Mbiti's notion of the living-dead — the recently dead who remain persons in a diminished way for as long as they're remembered, before sinking into nameless spirithood. But the film also seems to smuggle in a much more individualist plot: Miguel's arc is about a singular talent and dream that the family has to learn to acommodate. So is Coco actually endorsing something like Menkiti's strong communitarianism, where personhood is conferred by the community and can be withheld or revoked, or Gyekye's moderate version, where the individual has features that aren't exhausted by communal relations? Or is it trying to have it both ways and landing in incoherence?

Two more things I'd like the reading to take seriously. First, Ernesto de la Cruz is remembered by millions, yet the film treats that remembrance as worthless because it's built on a lie — fame as a counterfeit of community. Does ubuntu have the resources to explain why mass-mediated remembrance doesn't count as genuine relational recognition, or is the film just freelancing there? Second, the resolution runs through truth-telling (Mamá Coco's letters exposing Ernesto) and the family's reconciliation with Héctor, whom they collectively erased. Tutu ties ubuntu tightly to forgiveness and restorative truth-telling, so on the surface the film is very Tutu-ish — but does it let reconciliation come too cheap, given the Riveras nearly let Héctor fade out of existence entirely?

Be honest about where the film resists the reading — its a kids' movie about music, not a treatise, and I'd rather flag the mismatches than force them. And end with what the film adds to this debate that the philosophy papers don't. No citations needed, I'll chase down sources myself.
