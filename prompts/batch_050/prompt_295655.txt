I'm a grad student and I've been voluntold to run one 50-minute discussion section in an intro political economy course. The week's topic is information cascades — how perfectly rational people who can observe each other's actions but not each other's private information can end up all doing the same thing, even when most of them privately suspect it's the wrong thing. The students are mostly second-years with basic probability but no game theory, and the timing is awkward: they've just finished a unit celebrating the wisdom of crowds, so they're arriving primed to think that when many people independently converge on a choice, the aggregation is doing epistemic work. The cascade result basically says the opposite — that sequential public decisions can aggregate almost no information at all, and that a crowd's unanimity can be nearly worthless evidence. I want them to actually feel the vertigo of that, not just nod along.

So I don't want the explanation itself, I want a plan for it. Lay out the order the pieces have to come in and what depends on what — I'm pretty sure the private-signal-vs-public-action distinction has to be load-bearing before anything else works, but tell me if I'm wrong. Pick the one carrying example to build the whole section around (I've seen versions with urn draws, with two restaurants on a street, with executives second-guessing a hire) and say what the example must have and must lack to do the job — e.g., does it need sequential decisions, coarse observable actions, no way to communicate uncertainty? Then the misconception map: the wrong turns these students will predictably take, in roughly the order they'll take them (my guesses: "this is just conformity/social pressure," "a long cascade must be right because so many people agree," "cascades are stable once they form," "this only happens because people are biased or stupid"), and at what point in the sequence each one should be headed off rather than answered after the fact. Include the fragility point somewhere — how a small piece of public information can shatter a long cascade — since I think that's the moment the idea really lands. Finally: what check for understanding at the end would show they can transfer the idea to a new case (say, why every new restaurant in town copies the menu of the successful one) rather than just recite the example, and what should I explicitly refuse to get into at this level — I'm tempted to wave at the Bayesian updating math, but maybe even that is a mistake.
