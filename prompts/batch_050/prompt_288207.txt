I'm designing a graduate seminar session on moral psychology, pairing the empirical literature on psychopathy with the Coen brothers' No Country for Old Men, and I want a careful philosophical reading of Anton Chigurh as a test case for the question whether psychopaths know that what they do is wrong. For the session I've been working with a three-grade taxonomy of moral knowledge, and I'd like you to use it as the spine of the reading: discriminative knowledge is the ability to sort moral from merely conventional wrongs, standardly probed by asking whether a transgression would still be wrong if an authority figure permitted it — a task on which diagnosed psychopaths characteristically fail, treating even harm-based wrongs as authority-dependent; justificatory knowledge is the ability to back a verdict with reasons of the right kind; and agential knowledge is whatever it takes for a verdict to actually enter one's practical deliberation and set one's ends. The received picture in the literature is that psychopaths may retain something at the first grade while failing at the second or third. Chigurh seems to invert the whole profile: no one's permission or prohibition could bear on what he does, so he would presumably pass the authority-dependence test with ease; he offers elaborate quasi-justifications to his victims; and his judgments unmistakably govern his conduct. Yet none of it tracks harm, persons, or anything recognizably moral. Does the film construct, in effect, a psychopath who passes the standard tests for moral knowledge — and thereby show that the tests are measuring the wrong thing?

Please organize the reading around the scenes where this is most vivid. In the gas station coin toss, Chigurh appears affronted not by cruelty but by unearned possession — the proprietor married into the store, has lived here all his life without knowing what he has been staking — and what Chigurh polices is desert and fate, not suffering. Is that a perverted moral sensibility or a formally perfect counterfeit of one, and what does the difference consist in? In the final scene, Carla Jean refuses to call the coin and tells him, in effect, that the coin has no agency in the matter, that it is only him: read her refusal as a philosophical diagnosis of what Chigurh is doing — outsourcing his agency to a randomizer, simulating the form of justification (offering chances, giving reasons, keeping his word to Moss) while evacuating its substance. Then take up the car crash that follows immediately after, in which blind contingency nearly kills him and he walks off paying a boy for a shirt to bind his wound: is the film answering his coin with a coin of its own, and does that amount to a verdict on the coherence of his fatalism? Use Sheriff Bell as the counterweight — a man whose moral knowledge and empathy are intact, whose understanding protects no one, and whose dreams at the end are the film's real closing statement. Does the pairing of Chigurh and Bell suggest the film is less interested in whether psychopaths know the good than in whether knowledge of the good ever does what moral psychology assumes it does?

Be honest about where the lens strains. Chigurh is plainly not a clinical portrait; he is built as an allegorical figure of fate or death, and I want you to mark precisely the point at which citing him as evidence about actual psychopathy becomes illegitimate — and to say whether fiction can nonetheless function as data here, for instance by supplying a vivid case in which the three grades of moral knowledge visibly come apart in a way the lab studies cannot easily stage. Finally, say which of the three grades the film itself seems to treat as the one that matters, and what No Country adds to the philosophical debate that the empirical papers and the armchair arguments do not.
