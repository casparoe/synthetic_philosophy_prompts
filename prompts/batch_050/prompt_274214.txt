I'm one of five board members of the San Verde River Basin Authority, and at next month's meeting we vote on the biggest capital project in our agency's history: a $1.8B desalination plant, versus a cheaper bundle of wastewater recycling, aggressive conservation, and raising the dam on our main reservoir. The whole vote basically turns on how much our dry-season inflows are going to drop by the 2060s, and honestly I'm lost on how to weigh the science.

Here's the setup. Our consultants downscaled a 22-model global climate ensemble to the basin. Median result: inflows down about 25% by 2065, but the individual models range from -5% to -45%. Staff's report averages all 22, treats the spread like a probability distribution, and concludes there's roughly an 80% chance inflows cross the threshold where desal becomes the cheaper option over the plant's life. On that basis they recommend we approve.

Two things are bugging me. First, a hydrologist on our advisory panel pointed out that at least 9 of the 22 models share atmospheric code from the same three modeling centers, and two are literally the same model run at different resolutions -- so the 80% is counting some models two or three times, and the "spread" isn't a sample of independent anything. Second, a new convection-permitting regional model, not part of the ensemble, actually resolves our coastal mountain range and projects only an 8% decline. Its team basically argues that one model that gets the local physics right beats 22 coarse ones that don't. Meanwhile the cheap portfolio performs okay even in the driest scenarios, it just costs more per acre-foot -- and the desal plant is a $1.8B stranded asset if inflows hold up.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote, around 800 words: recommend approve, reject, or approve a phased version with explicit triggers, and give the reasoning. Specifically it needs to (a) explain why the ensemble spread isn't a probability and what the board should do with the projections instead -- weighting models by skill and independence, storyline approaches, robustness analysis across scenarios, whatever you think actually holds up; (b) take the new regional model seriously: when, if ever, does one arguably-better model outweigh ensemble agreement, and does it here; (c) settle whether the model disagreement even matters for this particular decision, i.e. which option ranks best across the whole plausible range rather than under a single probability distribution we can't really defend; and (d) state the strongest argument for whichever option you recommend against, fairly enough that its supporters would recongize their own position in it. End with an actionable bottom line: what we vote for, under what conditions, and what future observations would trigger us to change course.
