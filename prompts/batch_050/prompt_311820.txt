I tutor a small adult-ed philosophy group -- five people, smart, no background, we meet in the back room of a library. Next session is the grandfather paradox, and I want to build the whole evening around David Lewis's "The Paradoxes of Time Travel." Specifically around the passage where Lewis tells the story of Tim, who travels back to 1921 with a rifle intending to kill his grandfather, and Lewis argues that Tim both can and cannot do it: he has the gun, the training, the close range, no interference in sight -- so in one sense of "can" he can -- yet grandfather lived to sire Tim's father, so in the sense that matters for what actually happens, he cannot, and something (a slipped step, a jammed gun, a sudden cramp) will always intervene. Lewis's point, as I take it, is that "can" is always relative to a set of facts held fixed, and once you separate the two senses the paradox dissolves without any metaphysical ban on time travel.

Don't give me a lecture on Lewis. Give me a plan for teaching this in ninety minutes. What has to come first -- I'm assuming they need the distinction between changing the past and affecting it before the Tim story lands, but maybe that's backwards -- and what's the actual dependency order? Should Tim be the carrying example, or does the story's melodrama get in the way, and if not Tim then what? Where in the sequence will the group predictably go wrong: I'm expecting at least "so the universe magically conspires to stop him," "Lewis is just saying it never happens, which dodges the question," and someone insisting that free will means the can that's about Tim's abilities is the only real one. Tell me at what point in the evening each of these forms and where to defuse it. Also: one question I can pose at the end that would show me whether the two-senses-of-can move actually landed, and which parts of Lewis's paper (personal vs. external time, say) I should explicitly bracket as beyond this session rather than let them swallow it.
