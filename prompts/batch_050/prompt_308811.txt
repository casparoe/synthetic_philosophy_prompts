Here is my situation. Next month I'm giving a department colloquium talk titled "Easy Knowledge Without Tears," defending the following position on the bootstrapping problem. I argue that the standard diagnosis is wrong: there is nothing epistemically defective about the easy-knowledge inference — reasoning from what a gauge reads, and from the fact that things are as it reads, to the conclusion that the gauge is reliable. Such reasoning transmits justification and yields knowledge, as defenders of basic knowledge like Cohen maintain against critics like Vogel. But, my distinctive claim goes, what bootstrapping reasoning cannot do is calibrate: it cannot raise your rational confidence in a source above the confidence that source was antecedently entitled to. Self-verification is like a witness testifying to their own honesty — however many times the testimony is repeated, it restates the initial warrant rather than adding to it. So easy knowledge is genuine but epistemically inert: it cannot be parlayed into any standing you did not already have, and the felt wrongness of bootstrapping tracks this inertness rather than any failure of justification. I use this to split the difference between the "basic knowledge is unproblematic" camp and the "bootstrapping exposes a defect in reliabilist-style theories" camp.
