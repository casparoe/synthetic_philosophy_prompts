Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about psychopathy and moral knowledge. Her position: psychopaths plainly know that what they do is wrong — they can state the relevant norms, they hide their crimes, they know what a jury would say — so their deficit is motivational, not cognitive. Mine: genuinely knowing that something is wrong requires more than being able to recite the rule; it involves something like understanding the reason-giving force of others' interests, and the standard findings (e.g., psychopathic offenders treating moral and conventional transgressions as more alike than controls do) suggest that this is precisely what's missing. We circled for hours without either of us conceding anything.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology, with real command of both the conceptual literature on moral understanding and the empirical work on psychopathy. Then give me your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: a genuine empirical dispute about what the data show, a conceptual dispute about what "knowing that it's wrong" requires (and whether knowledge of morality is partly affective), or two parties answering different questions — one about linguistic/classificatory competence, one about moral understanding — under a single label. My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis: both sides quietly presuppose that moral knowledge is one unified capacity, and the disagreement dissolves or at least sharpens if you split it into components. Test that hypothesis against the best version of each side. Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a particular kind of experiment or evidence, or a reframing of the question — or argue, if you think so, that no resolution should be expected because the question itself is ill-posed.
