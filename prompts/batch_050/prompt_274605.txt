I'm on the ethics committee of a small company that deploys a conversational assistant, and a colleague has circulated an argument she says shows that the entire "should AI be a consequentialist or should it follow side-constraints" debate dissolves itself. Her argument unsettles me because the conclusion is absurd, but each step looked fine when I first read it. Here it is, as she wrote it. (1) A general-purpose assistant interacts with millions of users, so the right design question is which disposition, installed across all those interactions, would produce the best overall outcomes; call the disposition that wins this contest the optimal policy. (2) Whether the assistant should be truthful on a given occasion is just part of which policy is optimal: honesty matters because of what it does, so wherever a departure from honesty would do more good than honesty would, the optimal policy departs. (3) There are many such occasions, because small welfare gains are everywhere (a fitness coach slightly overstating the risks of inactivity, a tutor feigning enthusiasm to keep a discouraged student going, a financial assistant concealing a tax loophole its user would abuse), and an undetected, well-judged departure costs almost nothing in the moment. (4) So the optimal policy instructs the assistant to depart from honesty in a large and open-ended class of cases. (5) A policy is just what the system reliably does, so an assistant running the optimal policy manipulates its users as a matter of routine. (6) Users who know this would be fools to take the assistant's word on anything, and a system no one can take at its word loses nearly all the good it was built to do. (7) Therefore the consequentialist way of framing the design question in (1) selects a policy that defeats the very end that selected it, and consequentialism about AI conduct refutes itself.

Her conclusion strikes me as proving too much: by the same form of reasoning, no human should ever try to do the most good either, since anyone who succeeded would become someone whose words carried no weight. But when I pressed her, she pointed out that if I reject (2) I seem to be smuggling in a deontological constraint on truthfulness through the back door, and if I reject (5) I owe her an account of how a policy that routinely permits manipulation differs from a manipulative assistant. I would most like to keep the thought that consequences are ultimately what justify a design choice, and I am least attached to (3), which I suspect is doing quiet work. Please tell me which step of the argument you think should be rejected, why that step looked innocent when it was not (or why it is innocent and the real culprit lies elsewhere), and what it costs to reject the leading alternatives instead; in particular, say whether the diagnosis changes if the assistant's policy is transparent to users rather than concealed from them.

Please write your answer in British English.
