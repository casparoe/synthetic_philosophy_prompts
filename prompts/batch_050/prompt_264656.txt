So I've been reading Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (that 1895 Mind piece) and I keep going around in circles, which I guess is fitting given the author. Here's the bit I'm stuck on, from the middle of the dialogue:

"And might there not also be some reader who would say 'I accept A and B as true, but I don't accept the Hypothetical'?"
"Certainly there might. He, also, had better take to football."
"And neither of these readers," the Tortoise continued, is as yet under any logical necessity to accept Z as true?"
"Quite so," Achilles assented.
"Well, now, I want you to consider me as a reader of the second kind, and to force me, logically, to accept Z as true."

What I can't get my head around is what the "reader of the second kind" is actually doing, psychologically or logically speaking. Accepting A and B as true while refusing the hypothetical "if A and B are true, Z must be true" -- is that even a coherent position? Because my instinct says: if Z genuinely follows from A and B, then refusing the hypothetical just is denying that the argument is valid, and then the whole dispute is about validity, not about whether anyone can be "forced." But Carroll has Achilles treat the refusal as something to fix by writing one more proposition in the notebook, and that fix obviously just reboots the problem.

So, a few things I'd like help with. First: is the reader of the second kind best understood as doubting a fact (that this inference is valid) or as refusing to do something (to perform the inference)? The dialogue seems to slide between these and I can't tell if that's Carroll being sloppy or Carroll being clever. Second: why does Achilles respond by adding C as a premise instead of pointing at a rule? Is the moral supposed to be that rules of inference can't be written in the notebook as propositions, or something subtler -- like even if you could write them down, it wouldn't help? Third: suppose the Tortoise tried the opposite trick -- granted every premise you offer, including all the hypotheticals, but refused to apply modus ponens as a rule. Does the same regress fire, or does something different happen? Does the passage as written even have the resources to answer that? Also, dumb question, but is the football joke doing any work beyond being a joke?

Explain it like I'm smart but stuck, and flag where the passage itself is ambiguous vs. where I'm just misreading.
