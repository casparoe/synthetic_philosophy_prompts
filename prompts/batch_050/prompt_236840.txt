I'm TA-ing a cross-listed seminar this semester called something like Formal Systems and Political Order, and for one unit I have the students reading the chapters of Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979) on tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level alongside a constitutional law casebook. Hofstadter's idea, as my students have grasped it, is roughly this: a hierarchy of levels is tangled when something at the top reaches back down and alters the levels that generate it, like Escher's two hands each drawing the other, and the inviolate level is whichever level sits beneath all the tangles, the one the system can rely on but never itself revise. He gestures at the United States Constitution as a near-miss case: it looks like the inviolate level of the legal hierarchy, except that Article V builds an amendment procedure into the document itself, and Article V even contains a proviso apparently entrenching each state's equal suffrage in the Senate against amendment, which raises the further puzzle of whether that proviso protects itself or could be amended away in two steps. What I want is an essay I can hand out as a model case analysis, taking Marbury v. Madison (1803) as the episode and the tangled hierarchy framework as the lens. The salient facts, in case they matter: outgoing President Adams appointed William Marbury a justice of the peace; his commission was signed and sealed but never delivered; the incoming Jefferson administration, through Secretary of State Madison, refused to deliver it; Marbury petitioned the Supreme Court directly for a writ of mandamus under a provision of the Judiciary Act of 1789. Chief Justice Marshall, writing for the Court, held that Marbury had a legal right to the commission and that the law must afford a remedy, but that the statutory provision granting the Court original jurisdiction over such petitions conflicted with Article III's enumeration of the Court's original jurisdiction, and was therefore unconstitutional and void — the first time the Court struck down an act of Congress, accomplished in the very same breath by which it disclaimed the power to act in the case at hand.

The analysis should be disciplined about the mapping before it gets clever. First, identify the levels of the legal hierarchy as the case presents them (statute, constitutional text, the court that authoritatively says what the text means) and locate the strange loop precisely: the Court's authority to interpret the Constitution derives from the Constitution, yet the operative meaning of that document is fixed by the Court — so who, exactly, is drawing whom, and does the case involve one loop or several nested ones? Second, characterize Marshall's opinion as a level-crossing move: by ruling against his own jurisdiction he avoided a direct confrontation with Jefferson, who could simply have ignored an order to deliver the commission, while simultaneously asserting the much larger power of judicial review. Is that best understood as the system stabilizing a potential paradox, as one level quietly annexing another, or as evidence that the hierarchy was never tangled in the first place because the Court, lacking any enforcement apparatus of its own, was never actually at the top? Third, take up the question of the inviolate level: where is it in this system, if anywhere? Consider whether the de facto inviolate level is not the constitutional text but the acquiescence of the political branches and the public — a level that is not in the document at all — and, if useful, bring in Cooper v. Aaron (1958), where the Court declared its own interpretations of the Constitution binding on state officials as the supreme law of the land, as a later tightening of the same loop, and the Article V self-entrenchment puzzle as the document's own failed attempt to install an inviolate level from inside.

Then run the analysis in the other direction, and be honest about where the framework goes quiet. Tangled hierarchies in Hofstadter's treatment are properties of self-contained formal systems, where a loop can generate genuine paradox; a legal order is neither self-contained nor fully formal, its loops are temporally slow and mediated by human actors with the option of defiance, and no contradiction ever actually erupted in 1803 — the system muddled through. So: what does the episode show about the theory? Does Marbury confirm that real-world hierarchies can remain stably tangled indefinitely precisely because their loops are damped by slowness, discretion, and exit options, or does it instead reveal that the tangled hierarchy vocabulary, imported from logic and cognition, loses its discriminating power when applied to institutions — that calling this a strange loop is a metaphor that papers over the genuinely interesting phenomenon, namely that the inviolate level of a legal system turns out to be a social equilibrium rather than a rule? If part of the mapping fails, say which part and why rather than forcing the framework to pronounce on everything. The essay should be rigorous enough that a student who has read both texts could reconstruct the argument from it, and it should not rest on any historical details beyond the ones given above.
