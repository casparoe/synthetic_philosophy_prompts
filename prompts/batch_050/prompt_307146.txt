A colleague of mine built the following argument to defend computer-assisted proofs against the charge that an unsurveyable case analysis is not a real proof. The argument was meant to vindicate things like the Appel–Haken proof of the four-colour theorem, where the verification of the reducible configurations runs to far more cases than any human could check in a lifetime. We now both think it proves too much, but we cannot agree on where it goes wrong. Here it is.

(1) One is justified in accepting a theorem on the basis of a purported proof only if every step of the proof has been checked. (2) Checking a step just is verifying it by a reliable method: a checker counts as having checked a step in virtue of producing the right verdicts with high reliability, not in virtue of any further mental act such as understanding or insight. (3) Computers verify arithmetic and case distinctions far more reliably than any human checker. (4) Therefore the computer's run constitutes a checking of the entire case analysis, and the four-colour theorem is proved in exactly the same sense as any hand-checked theorem. (5) But reliability is reliability, whatever its source. If a sealed device of unknown design, or a guru, or a trained neural network had asserted nothing but true theorems for decades and then asserted some open conjecture C, then by (2)–(4) its track record would equally constitute a checking, and we would be justified in accepting C as proved without ever seeing an argument for it. (6) So if the four-colour theorem is proved, then oracle-mathematics is legitimate: a sufficiently reliable black box could settle conjectures for us, and there would be nothing epistemically defective about mathematics conducted that way.

I find (6) absurd — a proof, unlike a certificate from an oracle, must at least in principle be followable by a mathematician — but I want to keep both the claim that the four-colour theorem is genuinely proved and the claim that proof differs from testimony. My colleague says that is exactly the combination the argument rules out, and that if I refuse (6) I owe him a step.
