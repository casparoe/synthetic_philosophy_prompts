Quick question while I'm preping my early modern survey course — my students ask some version of this every year and I never feel like I give them a satisfying answer.

As I understand it, the cogito in the Meditations is basically a deductively valid syllogism: everything that thinks exists; I am thinking; therefore I exist. Fine. And the conclusion it secures is not just that some thinking is happening but that *I*, a particular thinking subject, exist — the very same res cogitans whose nature Descartes goes on to unpack later in the Second Meditation. So here's my puzzle. If the cogito already proves the existence of the "I" as a persistant thinking thing, why on earth does Descartes need the whole Third Meditation apparatus — the causal argument for God, God as guarantor of clear and distinct perception — before he can be confident that he exists? Shouldn't the syllogism have settled his own existence once and for all, for all times, not merely in the fleeting moment of doubting? What's left for God to underwrite, exactly?

I suspect the answer has something to do with the evil demon only threatening beliefs about the external world and not beliefs about one's own mental states, but I want to make sure I'm not missing something about the logic of the argument itself.
