I'm putting together a little booklet for my medieval ethics students and want a poem for the Abelard chapter, so here's the ask: write me a poem, about 24-32 lines, built around two characters. The first has fully consented in his heart to a crime -- say, stealing from the church poor-box -- but is stopped before he can do anything (the box is empty, a watchman appears, whatever you like). The second actually performs the outward act -- his hands take the coins -- but without ever consenting: he's been coerced, or tricked, or doesn't know what he's carrying. Neither character should be named, and Abelard shouldn't be name-dropped anywhere either.

Formally, I'd like alternating stanzas in each character's voice, and here's the structural bit: as the poem goes on the two voices should get harder and harder to tell apart -- same imagery, same rhythm, eventually near-identical lines -- until in the final stanza the only thing that distinguishes them is that one consented and one didn't. The poem should land, without preaching, on why the man with clean hands is the sinner and the man with dirty hands isn't, i.e. why the guilt lives in the consent rather than the deed.

Start your reply with the poem itself, no preface or commentary, and use British English spellings.
