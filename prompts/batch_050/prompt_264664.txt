So my formal epistemology reading group spent all of last month on imprecise probability -- sets of credence functions instead of one, the Ellsberg paradox, all that -- and afterwards me and a friend got into it over beers about whether any of this is actually livable advice or just an elaborate way of never having to commit to a number. He dared me to imagine the consulting firm that would result from taking it completely seriously. So here's the ask: write me the website for that firm. A glossy landing page plus an FAQ section for a fictional decision-consulting startup (name it whatever you like) that has fully, sincerely adopted imprecise probability as its product. Deadpan corporate-marketing voice throughout, somewhere around 600 words.

The humor has to come from the actual theory, not a strawman version of it. The firm refuses to quote single probabilities to clients, only intervals, because false precision is irrational; its analysts will sometimes decline to take EITHER side of a bet a client proposes and patiently explain why that's not inconsistency; occasionally new evidence comes in and the firm's forecasts get LESS confident, which the sales page presents as a premium feature under some name like "dynamic humility"; the in-house decision algorithm is Γ-maximin, meaning it always plans for the worst distribution in the set, which gets marketed as peace of mind. Somewhere in the FAQ there should be a defensive answer to "isn't this just a way of never being wrong?", and maybe a testimonial from a client who left a rival firm of precise Bayesians that went bankrupt by being confidently, exactly, precisely wrong. If there's room, one of the FAQ questions could be from an exasperated client who just wants to know if it's going to rain on the day of the product launch.

Keep it fully deadpan -- no winking at the reader, no moments where the copy admits this is absurd. A reader who knows the literature should be able to pick out Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion, dilation, and the Dutch-book pressure the firm is carefully dodging, and should feel the satire is earned rather than lazy. A reader who doesn't know any of it should still find it funny as a piece about a company that has made a business model out of hedging. The satire should mostly target the imprecise side's genuine weirdness, but it's fine if precise Bayesianism takes a few hits on the way through.
