He sent me three datasets (well, summaries of them) and asked which ones he should actually worry about. I'm out of my depth, so I'm asking you: act as a statistician who moonlights as a philosopher of science, think carefully, and do some quick brainstorming about causal structure before you commit to anything.

(A) Across all five clinics, a new exercise protocol beats the old one on patient recovery scores, but he's worried the effect flips if you split patients by injury severity, since the new protocol was mostly given to mild cases at the clinics that adopted it early. (B) Overall, clinics with more weekend hours have worse average outcomes than clinics without, but weekend hours were added precisely at the busy urban locations that take the hardest cases. (C) A new intake app reduced average wait times company-wide, and it also reduced wait times within each individual clinic -- but he read somewhere that Simpson's paradox can still hide somewhere "in the margins" and wants to know if there's any way the app could secretly be bad despite agreeing with itself at every level of aggregation he can think of.

Rank the three situations by how likely you think a genuine Simpson-type reversal (or a similarly misleading aggregate) is lurking, giving each a score out of 10 and a sentence or two justifying the ranking. For the one you rank highest, tell him exactly what breakdown or additional variable he'd need to check to resolve it, and whether the paradox is really the problem or whether the deeper issue is something else (confounding, selection, regression to the mean, whatever you think it is). And for (C) specifically: is he right that a result consistent at every level can still mislead, or is he just paranoid now? Give me your actual verdict, not a hedged both-sides answer.
