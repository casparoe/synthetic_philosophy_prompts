I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course in behavioral genetics, and our professor has assigned each of us a "public misunderstanding" project: we have to design, on paper, a fifty-minute workshop for first-year students and campus journalists on what heritability estimates do and do not mean, with the workshop then critiqued by peers. I have to submit the plan itself, not the workshop content, and I want the plan to be genuinely well-designed rather than a list of facts.

Please design that plan. Specifically, I need:

First, the dependency structure: which concepts must be in place before the word "heritability" can be introduced without doing damage (I'm assuming variance, and the population-level rather than individual-level reading of a percentage, are among them, but tell me the full order and why each step requires the previous one), and where in the sequence it becomes safe to say that a trait can be highly heritable and still strongly malleable without the audience hearing a contradiction.

Second, the carrying example. I need one concrete scenario to thread through the whole workshop. My instinct was height, but I suspect its familiarity is exactly the problem — everyone "knows" height is genetic, so it may smuggle in the very misconception I'm trying to remove. Either defend height or propose a better carrier and explain what properties it must have (and which tempting properties it must lack) to do the work.

Third, the misconception map. I expect at least: heritability as a measure of how much of a person's trait is caused by their genes; heritability as fixed across environments; and high heritability as ruling out environmental intervention. For each one, tell me when in the sequence it will naturally form, whether to head it off preemptively or let it surface and then correct it, and exactly what move corrects it. Also tell me if there's a subtler misconception I'm likely to miss — one that survives the obvious corrections.

Fourth, the check for understanding: one question or exercise at the end whose answers would show whether the idea actually landed, and what wrong answers would reveal about where the sequence failed.

Finally, what I should openly decline to cover at this level (Falconer's formula, twin-study assumptions, GWAS-based estimates, or whatever you judge) and the one-sentence way to mark each omission so the audience knows it exists without being pulled into it.
