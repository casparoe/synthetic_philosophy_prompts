I am a retired entomologist — I spent some forty years working on the social Hymenoptera — and since hanging up my sweep net I have been auditing an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of biology at my local university. The lecturer, knowing my background, has asked me to help mark a set of practice exam responses on the topic of biological individuality, a subject on which my old fieldwork and my new philosophical reading turn out to intersect in ways I had not anticipated. Marking philosophical prose is proving harder than marking practicals ever was, so before I commit to any grades I would like an independent assessment of one response, both to calibrate my own judgement and to make sure I am being fair.

The question set was: "What makes something an individual organism rather than a mere group or colony of organisms? Answer with reference to eusocial insect colonies, drawing on at least two criteria of biological individuality, and evaluate whether the term 'superorganism' denotes a genuine biological category or is merely a suggestive metaphor."

The student's response reads as follows:

"Whether an ant or honeybee colony is itself an organism depends on which criterion of individuality one applies. On the evolutionary criterion developed by Queller and Strassmann, individuality is a matter of degree, measured by the extent of cooperation among the parts and the absence of conflict between them. Eusocial colonies score highly on cooperation: foraging, brood care, nest defence and thermoregulation are divided among specialised castes, and a honeybee colony can hold the temperature of its winter cluster within narrow bounds, a homeostatic achievement of which no single bee is capable. The colony also does well on conflict, since the workers are sterile and therefore have no reproductive interests of their own to pursue against those of the queen. The colony thus approaches what Maynard Smith and Szathmáry describe as a new level of individuality produced by a major evolutionary transition: entities that once reproduced independently can now replicate only as parts of a larger whole, much as the cells of a metazoan body surrendered their autonomy.

"A physiological criterion points the same way. Just as the metazoan body separates an immortal germline from a disposable soma (Weismann's distinction), the colony separates a reproductive caste from a worker caste, so that the queen functions as the colony's germline and the workers as its soma. Hamilton explained why such an arrangement evolves in the Hymenoptera: haplodiploidy makes sisters more closely related to one another than to their own offspring, and this is the genetic precondition for the evolution of a sterile worker caste, and hence for eusociality itself. Moreover, when a new colony is founded by a single inseminated queen, as in many ants, the whole society passes through a single-individual bottleneck, a mechanism widely credited with suppressing conflict and aligning the interests of the parts.

"Taken together, these considerations suggest that 'superorganism' is more than a metaphor: the eusocial colony is an organism in its own right, though its individuality remains a matter of degree and falls short of the tight integration of a vertebrate body."

Please mark the response out of 100 and provide the specific comments that justify the mark. I would like a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: which claims are accurate, both biologically and philosophically; which, if any, are mistaken, overstated or imprecise; whether the argument hangs together and genuinely addresses the question of metaphor versus literal organismhood; and what a stronger answer would have included or left out.
