I volunteer at a community grant-making trust in our town (we dish out maybe £60k a year to local projects) and at our last meeting two of the other trustees had a proper go at each other over whether we should even be a local trust. It kicked off when someone floated partnering with a charity doing cataract surgery overseas, since the same money restores sight for dozens of people rather than, say, repainting a scout hut. Dan said it was a no-brainer: nationality is morally arbitrary, nobody earns the passport they're born with, and a person losing their sight in Malawi matters exactly as much as a person losing theirs in Doncaster, so the money should go wherever it does the most good. Rachel pushed back that we co-govern this place -- we all pay into the same council tax, obey the same laws, vote in the same elections -- and that kind of shared scheme generates obligations to the people inside it that we just don't have to people outside it, same way you have duties to your family you don't have to strangers. Dan said the family analogy backfires: you're allowed to favour your kids with your own money, but the trust's funds aren't his money, they're held for the public, and no-one thinks a council officer can steer public contracts to his mates because 'associative duties'. Also, he said, the reciprocity point is dodgy because the global poor are excluded from our scheme largely by our own border controls, so you can't use their exclusion as the grounds for excluding them. Rachel replied that if his best argument is that we *harm* foreigners through the global order, that's a much stronger and more controversial claim than 'everyone counts equally', and he'd need to show the harm rather than assert it; whereas her cooperation argument doesn't say foreigners matter less, just that membership creates extra duties on top of the baseline, which he never actually denied. Dan's last move was to say 'extra duties on top' is doing all the work -- if the baseline duty is equal moral consideration, then facing a choice between restoring sight for forty people abroad or a modest benefit for forty people here, the membership duty has to be enormous to tip the scales, and she hasn't said how big it is or why. At that point the chair moved us on and nothing got decided. Could you go through it move by move and say who's right about what -- whose arguments actually land, which ones smuggle in what they're meant to prove, and is there anything important neither of them raised?
