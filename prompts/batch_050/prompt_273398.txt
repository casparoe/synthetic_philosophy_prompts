I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been turning an analogy over in my mind for months and would like it audited properly. Everyone I know calls their smartphone their "second brain" or their "external memory." I long assumed this was a loose joke, but I recently learned that some philosophers — I believe Andy Clark and David Chalmers are the names — have defended something close to the literal version: that a notebook or device can genuinely be part of a person's mind, not merely a tool the mind uses. Their thought experiment, as I understand it, contrasts a woman who remembers an address in the ordinary biological way with a man whose memory is impaired and who keeps such facts in a notebook he carries everywhere, consults automatically, and trusts completely. The claim is that because the notebook plays the same functional role for him that memory plays for her, the information counts as part of his mind even when the notebook is closed.

This analogy is now doing real argumentative work around me, and that is what I want tested. A friend of mine leans on it to conclude two things: first, that taking away someone's phone is morally closer to inflicting a minor brain injury than to confiscating a possession, and second, that the familiar hand-wringing about people "outsourcing their thinking" to devices is confused, since relying on a phone for storage and navigation is, as he puts it, no different in principle from relying on a hippocampus. I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I cannot locate the exact seam where it gives way.

What I would like from you:

1. An explicit correspondence table. What, in the phone case, plays the role of encoding, storage, retrieval, consolidation, and forgetting? Where does the man-with-the-notebook case fit in as the intended bridge between ordinary memory and the phone?
2. A catalogue of the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for my friend's two conclusions and those that are real but harmless. Candidates I have worried about: the difference in speed and effort of access; the fact that I endorse my memories automatically but can distrust my phone; the question of whether a constantly used tool thereby becomes part of the user rather than merely a cause of the user's thoughts; and the worry that if the phone counts, so do my spouse, my filing cabinet, and the whole internet, which seems like a reductio.
3. A verdict on each conclusion separately. Does "no different in principle" survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And does the stronger moral claim — that confiscation is closer to injury than to theft — stand or fall with it, or does it have independent legs?
4. If the notebook/phone analogy is the wrong comparison, tell me what the right one is, and what conclusion the better analogy would actually license.

Throughout, please indicate how confident you are in each claim you make, and at the end single out the single point in your answer where you are least sure of yourself and say why.
