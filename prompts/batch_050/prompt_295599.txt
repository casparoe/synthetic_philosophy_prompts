Write a villanelle spoken by someone who has rigged a quantum suicide device -- a detector coupled to a lethal mechanism, triggered each minute on the outcome of a quantum measurement with two equiprobable results -- and who is now standing at the console in the interval before the nth trial, having survived n - 1 of them. The two refrains should carry the two perspectives the thought experiment forces apart: from the first-person view, the speaker reasons that they will only ever find themselves on branches where the mechanism failed to fire, so survival is the only outcome they can ever experience; from the third-person view, an onlooker watches the proportion of branches containing a living speaker shrink by half with every trial, and expects to be standing over a body within the hour. Use the villanelle's machinery to do the conceptual work: the refrains are, after all, the lines that persist unchanged through every stanza while the tercets around them multiply and thin, and the final quatrain is the first place the two refrains are made to occupy the same room -- which is exactly what the two perspectives never quite manage in the literature. Someone who knows the Everett interpretation should be able to identify which refrain speaks from which perspective and why their juxtaposition in the closing lines is the problem rather than the solution. The poem should not name Everett, branches, or measure explicitly; the physics should be load-bearing but implicit. After the poem, add a brief prose note (separate from it, not part of the nineteen lines) stating where the poem commits to claims that are genuinely contested -- for instance, whether anything like the first-person inference follows from many-worlds at all, and what the poem assumes about the bearing of branch measure on what the speaker should expect -- and keep that note honest about which parts are settled quantum mechanics, which are contested interpretation, and which are leaps the poem makes for the sake of the conceit.
