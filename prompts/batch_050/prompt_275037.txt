So I've been chewing on this for a while, mostly because of arguments with my brother-in-law at thanksgiving, and I want to know if the positions I've landed on actually fit together or if I'm contradicting myself somewhere. Here's roughly where I'm at:

1. The government shouldn't pass a law if the only real reason for it is religious. If the best someone can say for a law is "the bible forbids it," that's not a good enough reason to impose it on everybody, including people who don't share that faith.

2. At the same time, I think it was completely right — admirable, honestly — for civil rights leaders to argue against segregation in openly religious terms, from pulpits, quoting scripture. And I don't buy the idea that they should have translated it all into neutral secular language first. The religious case stood on its own and it worked.

3. What matters is whether a law itself can be defended with reasons anyone could accept. A politician's private motivations are their own business. So if a senator votes for environmental protections because he believes God commanded him to be a steward of creation, fine, no problem at all.

4. But I do think ordinary voters should try to vote on the basis of reasons that aren't just religious. I'm definately bothered by someone walking into the booth and voting purely on what their church teaches.

5. And I don't think religious people should have to "check their faith at the door" to participate in politics. That seems unfair, since for a lot of people their faith is the deepest thing about them.

Is this a coherent package? If not, tell me exactly which pieces are fighting each other, in order of how bad the problem is, and what the least painful fix would be in each case — like which one I'd have to give up and what it would cost me. I genuinely can't tell where the weak spot is, or if there even is one.
