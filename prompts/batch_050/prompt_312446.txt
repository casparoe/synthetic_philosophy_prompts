I'm a novelist, most of the way through a draft of a book in which a man spends three chapters talking his sister out of marrying someone he is certain will make her unhappy. My writing group split down the middle: half found the brother devoted, half found him quietly manipulative, and the argument made me realize I don't actually know where I draw the line between moving someone and mishandling them. So I've been collecting cases and consulting my gut, and I can't find the principle behind my own verdicts. Here they are. Laying out for my brother, in deliberately harrowing detail, what our grandfather's last years with emphysema were like, in order to frighten him into quitting -- every word true, chosen entirely for effect: legitimate, I'm confident. The furniture salesman who tells me the discount ends tonight when the identical sale starts again on Monday: manipulation, obviously. A billboard showing a real, dying smoker, offering no argument whatsoever, only the image: legitimate, I think, though I notice it persuades by pure visceral force. A drug commercial in which every spoken sentence is accurate but the risks are murmured at speed over footage of grandchildren and sunlit meadows: manipulation, despite the total absence of lies. A friend who wept -- genuinely, she insists, and I believe her -- in the middle of asking me for a loan, and I gave in: I came away feeling manipulated, though she deceived me about nothing. And then my own chapter twelve, engineered over four drafts so that readers will grieve a character they've only known for two hundred pages: I want to say that's not manipulation at all, that's simply the craft. But the jacket copy my editor proposed, hinting at a love story the book does not contain, I refused as manipulative, and I'd refuse it again.

A few candidate principles keep occurring to me and each seems to fail somewhere. Deception can't be the line, since the billboard doesn't deceive and the drug ad's sin isn't falsehood. Bypassing reason in favor of emotion can't be it either, unless I'm prepared to condemn the billboard and most of what I do at my desk. Whether the persuader's end serves the target's interests or their own does a lot of work -- it acquits me with my brother and convicts the salesman -- but it makes a hash of the friend with the loan, who really did need the money and whose distress was real, and it seems to acquit the drug company whenever the drug is genuinely good for someone. Something about whether the emotional register is *consonant* with the facts or arranged to drown them out comes closest to capturing the billboard-and-commercial pair, but it feels more like a symptom of the thing than the thing itself.

So here is what I'd like. Give me a principle, or a small set of candidates stated precisely enough that I could apply them mechanically, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate -- I suspect at least one has to go, and I'd rather know which than save them all at the price of a principle with a clause bolted on for every case. Then test the survivors against situations I haven't offered: a barrister's closing speech that is scrupulously factual but curated to lead a jury somewhere the full record wouldn't; a teacher who praises mediocre work to build a timid student's confidence; flattery delivered just before a favor is requested. Finally, apply whatever survives to my two standing puzzles. Does your principle convict me as a manipulator of my readers, and if it acquits me, what exactly does the acquittal rest on -- something about consent, about the openness of fiction's contract, about the absence of a transaction? And what verdict does it return on the brother in my draft: is he the devoted sibling half my writing group sees, or the quiet manipulator the other half does, and on what grounds?
