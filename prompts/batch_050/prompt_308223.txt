Is there an established name for the following pattern in evolutionary explanation? I keep noticing a way of defending the claim that a trait of an organism exists because natural selection put it there, and the defense works not by holding the claim fixed but by rotating it. A researcher proposes that some feature exists because it serves a particular purpose. When it turns out that the feature does that job badly, or that it appeared long before the job did, or that creatures lacking the feature do the job perfectly well, the researcher does not abandon the explanation. Instead, the explanation quietly moves: to a different purpose, to a different period in the past, to a different level (the trait itself is no longer said to have been shaped directly, but its ingredients are still credited to selection somewhere upstream), or to a competing demand whose strength is inferred from exactly the shortfall it is supposed to explain. Each individual move can be scientifically legitimate in isolation. What strikes me is the pattern across many cases: the conclusion that the trait is a product of selection survives every possible finding, because whichever specific version of it gets refuted is swapped for another version.

Two instances I have encountered. First, a structure in an animal group was explained for decades as existing for the sake of one function; when the fossil record showed the structure predated that function, the same structure kept its status as a showcase of selection while its assigned purpose was changed twice within a decade, each time with undiminished confidence. Second, a human behavioral tendency was explained as serving a particular benefit; when cross-cultural data failed to match the prediction, the explanation was not reconsidered but relocated to an unobservable ancestral environment, and when that relocation was challenged, it was relocated again to a different ancestral benefit.

I realize this is in the neighborhood of things people gesture at with the phrase "just-so stories" and with the Gould and Lewontin critique of adaptationism, but I do not think it is identical to either. A just-so story is a single unsupported plausible tale, whereas what I am describing is a practice of serial replacement among tales. And the spandrels critique is an argument that adaptationists neglect non-selective origins, whereas my phenomenon is a defensive habit that could persist even in someone who accepts that critique in the abstract. It also seems adjacent to, but narrower than, the general philosophical worry about theories that no evidence could count against.

Does this pattern have a name of its own in philosophy of biology or in the methodological literature on evolutionary inference? If it does, I would like to know how the literature distinguishes it from ordinary theoretical revision, which also involves replacing failed claims with improved ones. If it does not, could you propose a name and a precise definition, including where the line falls between the pathological version of this practice and its perfectly reasonable counterpart — since surely a hypothesis sometimes really should be revised rather than discarded? The label alone will not satisfy me; I want the carving to be exact enough that I could classify a new case as an instance or a non-instance.
