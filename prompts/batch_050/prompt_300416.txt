I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long feature on a debate in global economic history that I suspect is really a debate about what makes a question well posed. The familiar framing is "Why Britain and not China?" -- the claim that industrialisation broke out in Britain rather than in the most advanced parts of China, despite the two looking surprisingly comparable a few centuries earlier. Against this stands a family of sceptics who argue that the question itself is malformed before any evidence is gathered.

To stress-test my own treatment, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two fictional economic historians, both first-rate, neither a caricature:

- **Dr Anaya Rao** defends the question. Her case rests on three pillars. First, contrastive "why X and not Y" questions are the ordinary engine of causal inquiry -- we ask why the aspirin relieved the headache and not the backache without demanding that heads and backs be symmetric units, so the asymmetry between a small island and a vast empire is no objection in principle. Second, the question has been extraordinarily productive: it generated the argument associated with Kenneth Pomeranz that easy access to coal and to land-intensive commodities from the Americas relaxed constraints that bound the Yangzi Delta as tightly as Britain, and whether or not that argument is right, it is the kind of discovery only a well-posed question can produce. Third, the question inherits the respectable pedigree of the older Needham question about why modern science arose in Europe rather than China, and abandoning it now would orphan decades of cumulative scholarship.

- **Professor Marcus Feld** argues the question is ill-posed. His case also rests on three pillars. First, the question compares unlike units -- a nation-state of ten million against a continental empire of three hundred million -- and unlike units license arbitrary contrast classes: swap the units to "Why the Yangzi Delta and not Lancashire?" and the question dissolves, because both regions were roughly comparable in living standards and the divergence becomes a property of nowhere in particular. Second, the question suffers what he calls retrospective licensing: because we know Britain industrialised, any feature Britain had and China lacked counts as a candidate cause, so the question can never fail -- every difference found is declared the answer, and a question that cannot fail cannot be well posed. Third, the verdict is hostage to the chosen date: ask "Why 1800 and not 1400?" and the same evidence reorganises itself, suggesting the question tracks our vantage point rather than a joint in the world.

Requirements for the dialogue:

1. Each character must respond to the other's actual arguments, not restate prepared positions. In particular, Feld must answer the aspirin-and-backache point on its own terms, and Rao must answer the retrospective-licensing charge rather than merely repeating that the question has been fruitful.
2. At some point the two should agree to run the question through a shared diagnostic: reformulate it with the units swapped and with the date shifted, and examine whether any answer survives both transformations. Each should predict a different outcome of this test, and at least one prediction should turn out wrong.
3. Each character must concede at least one substantive point -- something that costs their position, not a courtesy.
4. The dialogue should not end in agreement to disagree. It should end with the two jointly identifying the crux: the single proposition on which their disagreement turns, stated precisely enough that a reader could in principle check it against evidence.
5. Length: roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words. Name the speakers in script format. No stage directions beyond the minimal, and no narrator resolving the dispute.
6. Write in British English throughout, including the characters' register.
