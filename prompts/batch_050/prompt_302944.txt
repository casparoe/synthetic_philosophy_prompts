I work in the foresight unit of a sovereign wealth fund with a genuinely long mandate: we hold infrastructure concessions, land, and equity stakes intended to pay out over fifty to one hundred years. My job, roughly once a quarter, is to write a "regime longevity assessment" for each country where we're considering a long-dated position -- not a prediction of what will happen politically, but an estimate of how likely the current institutional order (property regime, contract enforcement, currency framework, constitutional settlement) is to survive in recognizably continuous form across our investment horizon.

My predecessor did this narratively: he'd read about the country, form an impression of its stability, and write up a story. Our board has decided this is unacceptable, citing the general finding that inside-view forecasting is overconfident and that reference-class forecasting -- anchoring on what happened to comparable cases in the past -- does better. They've asked me to build a formal procedure grounded in the historical record of states and empires: how long political orders actually last, in distributions rather than anecdotes.

Please design this procedure. Specifically:

1. How to define and select a reference class when every state differs from every other in some respect -- which features (age of the regime, region, wealth, regime type, size, recent shocks) are legitimate grounds for narrowing the class, and at what point narrowing becomes cherry-picking the past you wanted. Give me a stopping rule for how narrow is too narrow, and what to do when the defensible class has only a handful of members.

2. How to handle the censoring problem: most historical orders we might compare against ended at a known date, but our target and many comparables are still running, so naive averages of completed lifespans systematically mislead. Explain how to reason with survival curves instead of averages, and whether some version of the heuristic that an enduring thing's expected remaining life scales with its age is defensible here, where it breaks down, and what its known failure modes are for political entities.

3. How much weight to give the base rate versus the inside view. I don't want a procedure that forbids country knowledge -- sometimes the specifics genuinely matter -- but I need explicit rules for when case-specific information licenses moving off the base rate, how far it's allowed to move the estimate, and who signs off on the deviation. Include the failure mode where every analyst believes their country is the exception, and how the procedure detects that pattern in our own track record.

4. What to do when the horizon in question exceeds the typical lifespan of anything in the reference class -- i.e., when the base rate answer is essentially "almost certainly not" for every country, which is true but operationally useless for choosing between them.

Mark clearly which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls the procedure cannot eliminate. Note that assessments are reviewed annually by a committee of non-specialists, so the procedure's outputs must be explainable in plain language.

Then run the procedure on two worked cases you construct: a wealthy, institutionally old constitutional democracy where the base rate and the inside view agree, and a young, rapidly growing state whose enthusiastic boosters all have inside-view reasons why the historical base rates don't apply -- the second being the case where I expect the procedure to deliver an answer the investment committee will not want to hear. End by stating what this procedure structurally cannot do, even if executed perfectly.
