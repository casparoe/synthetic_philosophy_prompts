A colleague presented me with the following argument, which he takes to vindicate Hume against the standard objection that the case against miracles proves too much. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I cannot find the flaw either. (1) A miracle is by definition a violation of a law of nature. (2) Our evidence for any law of nature is the entire uniform course of experience in its favour, which is the strongest evidence anything can have. (3) Testimony for an event is evidence only in proportion to the observed reliability of testimony of that kind, which is necessarily weaker. (4) Therefore no testimony can make it rational to believe a miracle occurred. (5) Any report that, if accepted, would require us to abandon our best-established beliefs stands in exactly the same evidential position as a miracle report: the weight of our entire past experience counts against it. (6) Therefore we should never credit testimony that contradicts our best-established beliefs. (7) Yet science plainly does and should credit such reports, since anomalous results that overturned the best-established beliefs of the day have repeatedly been accepted on the strength of testimony and experiment. (8) So something in Hume's reasoning, or in this extension of it, must be mistaken. He suggests the trouble lies in (7), since we could in principle have checked the anomalous results ourselves, but that seems to me to concede far too much to the sceptic. Which step should I actually reject, why does it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost?
