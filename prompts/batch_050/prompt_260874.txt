In 1851 Frederick Douglass publicly broke with William Lloyd Garrison over the U.S. Constitution. For years Douglass had accepted the Garrisonian view that the Constitution was a pro-slavery compact — tainted by the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, and the protection of the slave trade until 1808 — and that abolitionists should refuse to vote or hold office under it. Then he reversed himself and began arguing that the Constitution, rightly read, is an anti-slavery document: that its text nowhere names slavery, that its principles of liberty and equal protection condemn it, and that abolitionists should fight slavery through political action under the Constitution rather than withdraw from a corrupt system.
