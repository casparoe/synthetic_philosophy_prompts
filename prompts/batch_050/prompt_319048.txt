I am a PhD student in moral psychology, and I drafted an argument for a seminar that now seems to prove too much. Suppose we say that knowing an action is morally wrong requires at least correctly classifying it as wrong under conditions that exclude guessing and mere verbal mimicry, plus grasping an appropriate victim-centered reason such as harm or violation of rights. Many psychopathic offenders appear to meet that minimum: they distinguish moral from conventional transgressions and can say why coercion, injury, or betrayal are wrong. Add the judgment-internalist claim that if an agent knows φ-ing is wrong, the wrongness of φ-ing counts for that agent as at least a defeasible reason not to φ. Say further that a consideration counts for an agent as a reason only if it can make some difference to the agent’s motivation across possible deliberations. But many psychopathic offenders act deliberately, with intact instrumental rationality and full awareness of the nonmoral facts, while the wrongness of the act makes no such motivational difference unless punishment or reward is introduced. So either they lack moral knowledge despite satisfying the minimal epistemic conditions, or wrongness can be fully known while counting as no reason at all. The second horn seems to generalize beyond psychopathy: for any agent, knowing better would be compatible with the known wrong providing literally no reason against doing it, which makes blame, remorse, and moral education look rationally pointless. Which premise or inference should I reject? I would prefer to keep the empirical constraints about classification and motivational flatness, but I want the cheapest rejection, an explanation of why the rejected step looked innocent, and what each alternative costs.
