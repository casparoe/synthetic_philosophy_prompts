I'm teaching a unit on decision-making in a philosophy & film class and I'm ending it with Arrival (the 2016 Villeneuve one with Amy Adams). I want to get the students to read the movie through L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience, and honestly I'm not sure the fit is as clean as it first looks, so I wanted a second opinion before I write the lecture. Paul's core claim, as I understand it, is that certain big choices — becoming a parent being her go-to example — can't be made "rationally" in the standard decision-theoretic way, because the experience transforms your values and your very phenomenology, and you can't know beforehand what it will be like or who you'll be on the other side. Arrival seems tailor-made for this: Louise chooses to have a daughter, Hannah, knowing in advance that Hannah will die young of a disease, because the heptapod language is rewiring how she experiences time.

But here's where I think it gets genuinely tricky and maybe more interesting than the obvious reading: Paul's whole problem is epistemic — the trouble is that you *can't* know what the experience will be like in advance, so you can't weigh outcomes by your future values. Louise arguably *does* have the knowledge, or something close to it, since she experiences her future directly. Does that mean Arrival is a counterexample to Paul — a case where foreknowledge dissolves the transformative-experience problem and Louise's choice really is rationally assessable? Or does the film actually vindicate Paul in a subtler way, since "knowing" the future through the heptapod lens might still not be the same as knowing what it's like to live it, and since Louise's values are presumably themselves transformed by seeing all of it at once? In other words: does foreknowledge remove the epistemic barrier, or just relocate it?

If you were helping me prep this, how would you lay the reading out? I'd like to know which specific scenes you'd point the class to (the flashforward structure, her choice not to tell Ian, the "despite knowing the journey" bit near the end), whether you think the film ultimately sides with Louise's choice being rational, admirable but irrational, or somehow outside that whole frame, and where the reading breaks down — I want to be honest with the students about where the film resists the philosophy rather than forcing it into an illustration. Also curious whether the film adds anything to the debate that Paul's thought experiments (the vampire one etc.) can't: does dramatizing someone who *can* see the consequences show us something argument alone doesn't?
