Bit of background first. I spent forty years as a geophysicist — seismic inversion mostly, so inverse problems and model-fitting were my daily bread — and since retiring I've been slowly working through the philosophy shelf with a reading group of other late converts. This month it's Hume, and I've just hit this sentence in A Treatise of Human Nature (David Hume, Book I, Part III, Section 6), where he's asking what principle could possibly ground our inferences from the observed to the unobserved:

"if Reason determin'd us, it would proceed upon that principle that instances, of which we have had no experience, must resemble those, of which we have had experience, and that the course of nature continues always uniformly the same."

His point, as I take it, is that reason can't establish that principle without going in a circle, since any argument drawn from past experience already presupposes it.

Now here's what's been nagging me. Back in my working life, the closest thing we had to Hume's worry was the no-free-lunch theorems of Wolpert and Macready: averaged over every possible objective function — every possible world, loosely speaking — no learning or search algorithm outperforms any other, and one algorithm's edge on some class of problems is exactly cancelled out elsewhere. A chap in my reading group announced last week that machine learning has proved Hume right, and I pushed back, but I'm honestly not sure my pushback was any better than his slogan. Could you explain the following, in British English, in maybe 700 words (the Hume passage doesn't count towards that):

1. Wether Hume's argument and the no-free-lunch theorems are even the same kind of claim. Hume's conclusion is about justification — no non-circular reasoning can establish his uniformity principle — whereas the theorems are mathematical results about expected performance averaged over a space of problems. Does a result about averages across all possible worlds actually bear on what justifies inference in the one world we happen to inhabit? Those two things seem to me to need seperating, but my friend treats them as identical.

2. The averaging in the theorems has to be taken against some distribution over objective functions, and the standard version uses a uniform one. But isn't a uniform distribution over all possible worlds itself a hefty assumption — in effect a prior? If so, do the theorems genuinely vindicate Hume, or do they quietly answer his question by stipulation: build a uniformity-style assumption into the averaging and then announce that assumptions are unavoidable? I suspect something fishy here but can't put my finger on it.

3. The flip side: my old datasets were nothing like uniform draws from the space of all possible signals. They were compressible, structured, full of regularities, and tuned algorithms earned their keep on them. Doesn't that mean there are free lunches in worlds like ours — so the real question becomes what justifies our confidence that the actual world is one of the structured ones, which looks awfully like Hume's original question turning up again by the back door?

4. Lastly, in Section V of the Enquiry Hume offers his own "sceptical solution" — custom or habit does the work that reason can't. Is there a respectable analogy between Hume's custom and what we'd now call inductive bias, the built-in leanings that let a learner beat the averages precisely in the worlds it's tuned for? Or is that reading twenty-first-century machinery into an eighteenth-century text?

Set me straight wherever I've garbled things — I'm new at this, and my reading group will smell blood at the next session.
