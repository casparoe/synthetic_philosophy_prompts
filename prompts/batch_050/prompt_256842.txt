I'm giving a short talk before a chamber music concert whose program is built around slow movements, and I'd like to close the talk by reading a poem that hands the audience a puzzle to carry into the music. Write me a villanelle — five tercets and a closing quatrain, the two refrain lines returning as the form requires — that works through the following difficulty: a passage for solo cello can be sad, and yet sounds are not the kind of thing that can feel anything; no one in the concert hall believes the instrument is suffering or that anyone in particular is; and still, listeners report hearing genuine sadness in the music and deliberately seek it out, which is a strange way to treat something genuinely sad. Let the two refrains state the two horns of that puzzle, so that the villanelle's compulsive returns enact the way the question comes back no matter how many times it seems to have been answered. Across the tercets, have the poem try on, without endorsing, the standard escape routes: that the music moves the way sad people move and speak; that we half-imagine a someone behind the notes whose grief we're overhearing; that the sadness is really ours, projected onto a sound that only triggers it. The closing quatrain should not resolve the puzzle — I'd like the audience to sit with it during the first slow movement — but it may gesture at why the puzzle resists the usual ways puzzles get solved. The listeners have no philosophical training, so keep technical vocabulary out, though someone who knows this debate should be able to recognize each escape route as it appears. Iambic pentameter or close to it; slant rhymes are welcome if they serve the mood.
