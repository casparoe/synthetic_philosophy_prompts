I'm a high school student preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club, and I've chosen to present on sentences of the form "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining." I understand that such sentences can be true -- presumably it has rained many times without my noticing -- and yet something seems deeply wrong with actually asserting one. Please explain, at a level that goes beyond the usual one-paragraph treatment, what exactly generates this wrongness, and then help me work through a series of follow-up puzzles I plan to raise during the Q&A:

First, does the explanation for the wrongness of "It is raining, but I do not believe it is raining" extend in the same way to "It is raining, but I believe it is not raining," or are these two sentences defective for different reasons? If the reasons differ, which version of the sentence strikes you as more fundamentally confused?

Second, does the defect survive when the sentence is embedded rather than asserted outright? For instance, is there anything wrong with supposing, for the sake of argument, that it is raining and I do not believe it, or with the conditional "If it is raining and I do not believe it, then my umbrella is still in my closet"? What does your answer here suggest about whether the problem belongs to the meaning of the sentence or to the act of asserting it?

Third, consider tense and person shifts: "It was raining last Tuesday, but I did not believe it was" seems perfectly fine, as does "It is raining, but she does not believe it." Please explain precisely which feature of the original sentence -- the first person, the present tense, or something else -- is doing the essential work, and why.

Finally, I came up with a variant of my own that I could not settle: "It is raining, but I have no idea whether I believe that it is raining." Is this sentence defective in the same way as the original? It seems to me that answering this forces one to take a stand on whether people have automatic self-knowledge of their own beliefs, so please explain what this variant reveals about the assumptions hidden in the standard diagnosis of the original puzzle.
