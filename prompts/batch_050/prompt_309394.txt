So I've gone all in on a particular defence of Locke's memory criterion of personal identity, and I want you to stress-test it before I embarass myself in a reading group. Here's the set-up. Reid's brave officer objection (Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man, 1785, the essay on memory) says: the general remembers being the young officer, the young officer remembered being the flogged schoolboy, but the general can't remember the flogging -- so on Locke's criterion the general is and is not the same person as the boy, contradiction. My position is that the standard repair completely defuses this: personal identity isn't direct memory connection but the ancestral of it -- chains of overlapping memory links. The general is the boy because the chain runs through the officer, even though no single link connects them. Transitivity restored, objection dissolved.

Now the bit I actually lean on, which I haven't seen made much of. In the Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, ch. xxvii, §3, Locke writes about living things: "For in them the variation of great parcels of matter alters not the identity : an oak growing from a plant to a great tree, and then lopped, is still the same oak ; and a colt grown up to a horse, sometimes fat, sometimes lean, is all the while the same horse" [...]. My reading: Locke was already comfortable, for oaks and horses, with identity riding on a continuity relation rather than on any persisting element -- so extending the same move from matter-overlap to memory-overlap is not a modern patch but genuinely Lockean in spirit. The chain repair is just treating persons the way Locke treats oaks.

My question: what would count against this position? I want it pulled apart on both fronts:

1. The philosophical front. What breaks the chain repair itself? I know fission is the obvious one (chains branch, transitivity spits out identity between the two branches, which is impossible) so don't just stop there -- tell me what else: whether ancestral-of-memory is so weak it lets in too much, whether the repair quietly abandons whatever made the memory criterion attractive in the first place, whether it collapses into organism continuity anyway.

2. The textual front. How load-bearing is my oak passage really? Locke in the very same chapter, §1, says "one thing cannot have two beginnings of existence, nor two things one beginning", which sounds much stricter, and he spends the rest of the chapter distinguishing man from person. If the oak/colt stuff is doing a different job than I think -- say, just defining the identity of a mass versus a living organisation, with persons getting a genuinely different treatment -- does my defence lose anything, or was the textual appeal only ever decorative?

Rank the defeaters from fatal down to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed neo-Lockean chain theorist would concede it counts against the view or brush it off as a bullet worth biting. And be honest at the end: if the position comes out well insulated, say whether that's because it's true or because it's been qualified into shapelessness. A few hundred words, no need for citations or a bibliography, and in British English please.
