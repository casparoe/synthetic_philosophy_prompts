So here's the deal. I'm a retired marine ecologist and about two years ago I joined a philosophy reading group that meets at our local library — mostly other retirees, a couple of grad students who slum it with us, usually 8 to 12 people on a given evening. We've been chewing on Parfit's Reasons and Persons, and we keep circling around what I gather are called spectrum arguments: you line up a long chain of cases where each step seems like an improvement (or at least no worse), and then the endpoint of the chain is something nobody in their right mind would accept. The repugnant conclusion was the one that got us, but we also did the headache-and-torture one. Half the group says this just shows "better than" isn't transitive and we should get comfortable with that (one of the grad students keeps mentioning Temkin). The other half — including me, probably the scientist in me — says a relation like "better than" that can loop back on itself isn't a real relation at all, so one of the little steps has to be doing hidden work, even if we can't feel it.

Here's what I want: design an activity or game I can run with this group, roughly 75 minutes, low tech (index cards and a whiteboard are available, no money stakes, no computers), that makes the participants *feel* the squeeze rather than just hear about it. I want everyone to walk willingly down some version of the staircase — approving or choosing each small step as reasonable — and then be genuinely rattled when they see where they ended up, so that afterwards the group has something concrete to point at when they argue about whether the failure was in transitivity itself or in one of the steps. Please give me actual rules and the sequence of steps or choices, not just a general sketch. Crucially, tell me how to structure it so people don't see the trap coming in advance — if the grad students smell a repugnant conclusion at step three, the whole thing deflates. I also don't want a game where one clever person can "win" by refusing to play along; the design should make even stubborn consistency feel costly in the moment.

Then give me a debrief plan: questions I can pose that map specific moments from the game onto the philosophical options — rejecting a particular step, accepting that "all things considered better than" can be non-transitive, denying that the endpoints are really comparable, or insisting that tiny differences still count as differences. Be honest with me about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (people treating the hypothetical too flippantly is my guess, but you've presumably thought about this more than me) and how to recover. And finally, tell me plainly which feature of the real philosophical problem the game *can't* capture and I'll have to supply by just talking — I don't want to oversell a parlor trick as an argument.
