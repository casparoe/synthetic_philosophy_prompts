I work as an analyst at a think tank and a big chunk of my job is reviewing briefing memos before they go up the chain. Lately every other memo leans on a historical analogy -- "this is Munich 1938 all over again," "we can't let this become another Vietnam," "classic Thucydides trap dynamics" -- and my boss has noticed that the analogy a memo reaches for seems to do more work than the actual evidence in it. She's asked me to come up with a standard stress-test that any analyst here can run on a historical analogy before it makes it into a finished product, so that analogies become a way of generating questions rather than a shortcut to a conclusion. I've read enough to know the academic literature on this basically says analogies mislead because people match on vivid surface features (appeasement, quagmire, rising power vs. ruling power) instead of the structural stuff that actually drove the outcome, and because each of these famous cases gets invoked so often it's become a rhetorical weapon more than a comparison. But I don't know how to turn that into something a busy analyst can actually do in half an hour.

So: write me a procedure, as a numbered sequence of steps with explicit pass/fail or flag-for-discussion criteria at each one. It should force the analyst to state what the analogy is actually claiming (is it a prediction, a moral framing, or just atmosphere?), to list the features of the historical case that were causally load-bearing versus incidental, to check whether those features are present in the current case, and to actively hunt for the disanalogies -- including asking what the strongest version of the opposite analogy would be, since for every Munich there's a WWI-style "overreaction to a small crisis" waiting in the wings. It should also specify what to do when the analyst doesn't know the historical case well enough to fill in a step (a real problem here -- half our staff know Munich as a vibe, not an event). Include at least one step where the procedure deliberately can't settle the question and has to hand it back to human judgment, and say so plainly.

Then run the procedure on two worked examples: (1) a memo arguing that failing to respond forcefully to a regional power's seizure of a small neighbor's territory is "our Munich moment," and (2) a memo arguing that a proposed open-ended military training mission abroad is "the first step into another Vietnam." Ideally one of these should come out surviving the procedure and the other shouldn't, so my team can see what passing and failing actually look like. Finally, be honest about the procedure's own failure modes: where is it most likely to give a false sense of rigor, and what evidence or argument about how policymakers actually use analogies would make you think this whole checklist approach is misguided?
