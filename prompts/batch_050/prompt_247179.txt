I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, working on a piece about whether history can be objective. In draft after draft I encounter the same analogy, used by defenders of the old Rankean ambition to recover the past as it actually was: the historian is like a judge (or sometimes a detective), the sources are witnesses, source criticism is cross-examination, and the finished monograph is a verdict reached strictly on the evidence. The analogy is made to carry real argumentative weight: if courts can reach justified verdicts about contested past events without having been there, the argument goes, so can historians — and skeptics like E.H. Carr or Hayden White, who stress that historians select and emplot facts from the standpoint of the present, are simply confusing the fallibility of individual judges with the impossibility of judgment.

Audit this analogy for me. Give the explicit mapping (what corresponds to what), then identify where it breaks: consider, at minimum, that the historian's witnesses cannot be called or cross-examined and survive only as documents someone chose to preserve, that there is no standing body of law to apply, that the judge in the historical case is also the one who decides which events count as the crime, and that in White's hands the verdict itself takes the form of a narrative genre chosen in advance. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the conclusion the analogy is used to support or is merely decorative. Then tell me whether the objectivist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — perhaps on different grounds — and whether you can offer a better analogy for what historians actually do. Please indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the point where your analysis is least secure.
