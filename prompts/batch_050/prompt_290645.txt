I'm in a reading group on political philosophy and we keep hitting the same wall. Whenever we discuss whether religious believers may justify laws by appeal to their faith, someone -- usually the same guy -- wheels out the translation analogy: religious reasons in public political debate are like remarks in a foreign language. They might be perfectly meaningful among the people who speak that language, but in a pluralist democracy the conversation is conducted in a shared public tongue, so before a religious reason can count for or against a law that binds everyone, it has to be translated into secular, generally accessible terms. An untranslated reason is like addressing parliament in a language most citizens don't speak: not wicked, just not a move in the shared game. He uses this to conclude that the standard liberal position is basically obviously right, and that believers who object to it are like tourists annoyed that the locals won't learn their language.

I have two worries I can't sort out. First, translation between natural languages is supposed to be roughly meaning-preserving -- a good translation of a sentence says the same thing. But a believer who says a policy is wrong because it defies God's will might deny that any secular restatement says the same thing at all; the secular version strikes her as a different claim, maybe a shallower one. So is the analogy actually translation, or is it paraphrase-into-a-vocabulary-someone-else-controls, and does the difference matter for whether the demand is fair? Second, translation is normally symmetric -- anyone's remarks can need translating depending on the audience. But in the political case, the "translation" burden seems to fall only on the religious side; the secular liberal never has to translate her autonomy-talk into the believer's idiom before it counts. My reading group friend says this asymmetry is fine because secular language is just the default lingua franca, like English at an international conference, and nobody owes anyone translation *into* French.

So, audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the foreign language, the shared language, the translator, fluency, mistranslation, the audience that can't be expected to learn -- and say how tight each correspondence is. Then the breakdowns: places where political justification is importantly unlike linguistic translation (e.g., is there a fact of the matter about a "correct" translation of a religious reason? does the shared public language pre-exist the debate the way English pre-exists the conference, or is it itself one of the things being fought over? can you even state some religious reasons in a secular idiom without remainder, or is the "untranslatable remainder" doing all the work in believers' objections?). Finally, tell me whether the conclusion my friend draws from it survives: does auditing the analogy leave the translation requirement standing, does it push toward a weaker proviso (say, translated reasons must be available *somewhere* in the debate, not produced by the believer herself), or does it suggest the whole translation picture was smuggling in the liberal conclusion from the start? If the analogy mostly fails, what's a better one for whatever truth it was trying to capture?
