At a recent colloquium on theories of well-being, a colleague moved to discredit hedonism outright with a familiar one-liner: happiness cannot be pursued directly, and can only arrive as a by-product of aiming at something else. He offered this not as practical counsel for the unhappy but as an objection to hedonism as a theory of what is good for a person, and the audience appeared to take it as nearly decisive. I have since realized how many different settings this sentence recurs in. Sometimes it is advice to the melancholy. Sometimes it is a reason for thinking that a utilitarian agent ought not to calculate on every occasion, since deliberation aimed at good outcomes can reliably produce worse ones. Sometimes it is supposed to show that any view making pleasure the sole good contains the seeds of its own defeat. I suspect these are not one thesis but several, of markedly different strengths, and that their habitual fusion does real damage in discussion.

What I would like is a disambiguation I can put to use in my own writing. Please lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims this slogan might be making. At minimum, distinguish: claims about the phenomenology of absorption and the interference caused by attending to one's own pleasure; claims about what it is instrumentally rational to aim at given one's ends, where a goal is best achieved indirectly; claims about whether a theory of the good can coherently instruct agents not to consult it in deliberation, and whether that is a defect of the theory or merely a feature of agents; and any stronger claim on which hedonism is self-contradictory or self-undermining in its very content, rather than merely awkward to act upon. For each reading, state whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible empirical or conceptual thesis, or simply false; what sort of evidence or argument would bear on it; and, crucially, whether its truth would actually touch hedonism as a theory of well-being, or only the prudential conduct of agents who accept hedonism. A theory can be true while being bad to deliberate from, and I want the boundary between those two verdicts drawn sharply rather than assumed. Finally, identify which reading my colleague most plausibly needed in order to reach his anti-hedonist conclusion, and assess whether that reading survives scrutiny, given that he presented it as needing no argument at all.
