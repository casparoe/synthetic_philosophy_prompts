A friend and I argued until late last night about why anyone voluntarily spends an evening with a devastating film or play — works whose entire point is to make audiences grieve, flinch, or despair — and we found that every explanation we could produce fell into one of five families:

1. The Aristotelian answer: tragedy arouses pity and fear and, in doing so, purges or refines them, so the suffering is instrumental to a kind of emotional release.
2. The conversion answer (associated with Hume): the artistry of the representation — its eloquence, structure, and beauty — transforms or outweighs the painful emotion, leaving a net pleasure.
3. The control answer: negative emotion becomes enjoyable once it is decoupled from real threat, because we know we are safe and can stop at any time; the sadness of art is the sadness of a roller coaster.
4. The richness answer: we do not value the experience for pleasure at all but for meaning, insight, or a sense of being fully alive to what matters, and a good life contains such experiences even when they hurt.
5. The meta-response answer: the first-order distress is real, but it is accompanied by a second-order satisfaction in our own capacity for sympathy and moral response, which redeems the whole.

Neither of us could find a position that was not already on this list, which makes me suspect we were merely reciting a syllabus. Give me one more solution to the paradox that is genuinely distinct: not a rewording, special case, or hybrid of the five above. State it precisely, say which feature of the phenomenon it explains best (that we freely choose sad art, that we return to favorites we know will hurt, or that audiences sometimes prefer the sadder version of a story), and then give your honest assessment of whether it survives the obvious objections better than the five — or whether, on reflection, it collapses back into one of them.
