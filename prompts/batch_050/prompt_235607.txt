So I run this small philosophy night at a bar near me (we get like 8 people on a good night) and last week someone brought up Fitch's knowability paradox and it completely derailed the evening in the best way. Half the room was like "obviously some truths are just unknowable, who cares" and the other half was like "no no no, if you admit that you've lost something huge," and by the end I realized people were talking past each other because they actually held different positions and didn't know it. One guy kept insisting he was a "verificationist" while also saying stuff that sounded nothing like it.

So for next week I want to hand out a decision tree / flowchart that sorts people by which response to the paradox they actually hold. Here's what I need:

- 7 to 9 diagnostic questions, each a concrete case or a claim someone can react to without knowing any of the jargon. Stuff like: a specific fact nobody will ever check (who exactly stole the garden gnome from the porch in 1987, no records, everyone involved is dead) -- is that knowable *in principle*? Or: if you genuinely know a conjunction, do you thereby know each conjunct? That kind of thing. No question should be "do you accept the knowability principle?" because they'd just guess at what sounds smart.
- The leaves should include at least: the realist who just accepts that some truths are unknowable and thinks the proof is fine and boring; the person who restricts the knowability principle to some privileged class of truths (basic truths, or non-"I don't know this" style truths); the person who makes knowledge situation- or time-relative so that "p is true and nobody knows it" can still be knowable in a different situation; the person who bites the bullet and says alright, then all truths are actually known; and the person who thinks the derivation itself sneaks something past you in the logic.
- Build the tree from the logical space first, not from the list of names -- i.e. figure out which combinations of answers are even possible, then locate the positions in that space, and if some combination is empty, say briefly why (incoherent? unattractive? just nobody's staked it out?).
- If the tree can't separate two of the positions with my questions, flag that honestly instead of forcing a split.

Keep it usable for drunk-ish smart people with no philosophy background, so nothing longer than a page. Start with the tree itself, no intro, no recap of the paradox, I'll do that part myself at the table.
