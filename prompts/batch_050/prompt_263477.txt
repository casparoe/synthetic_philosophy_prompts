I work in formal verification, mostly model checking concurrent systems, and I keep hearing about Molinism in a podcast I listen to. Restate the core doctrine of middle knowledge -- that God knows, prior to any decree about what to create, what every possible free creature would freely do in every circumstance it could be placed in -- in the vocabulary I actually use: transition systems, nondeterministic choice, counterfactuals over execution traces, abstraction, exhaustive exploration of the state space. Be as precise as you can; I'd rather you say "this bit doesn't map" than stretch an analogy. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the doctrine translate cleanly (I suspect the prevolitional/volitional/postvolitional structure maps onto something like design-time vs runtime?), which parts you had to fudge, and which have no counterpart at all in how model checkers handle nondeterminism. In particular I want to know about the grounding objection -- the complaint that nothing makes these "would freely do" counterfactuals true before God decides anything, since the creature doesn't exist yet and its choice isn't determined by the circumstances. In my world, asking what a nondeterministic system "would" do on a given input just isn't a well-posed question; the answer is the set of possible traces, full stop. Is the Molinist claiming something with no analog in my framework, or does my framework already contain the tools to say what they're saying and it comes out false? If the residue is on my side, tell me what model checking can't express; if it's on theirs, tell me whether the doctrine is doing work my tools reveal to be empty.
