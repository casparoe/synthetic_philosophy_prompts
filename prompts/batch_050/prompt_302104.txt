**Exam question:**

Spectrum arguments (associated with Rachels and Temkin) purport to show that the relation "all-things-considered better than" is not transitive.

(a) Reconstruct the strongest version of such an argument you can. Specify the spectrum you are using, state each premise explicitly, and make clear exactly which relation must fail to be transitive for the argument to go through. (8 marks)

(b) Critics sometimes dismiss spectrum arguments as merely another sorites puzzle about vague predicates. Explain why this dismissal misses the point — that is, what distinguishes the spectrum argument from a sorites paradox about, say, "heap." (6 marks)

(c) Suppose the argument succeeds and "better than" is intransitive. Does it follow that rational agents may permissibly have intransitive preferences, notwithstanding money-pump arguments? Explain whether and why the intransitivity of a value relation bears on the rationality of preference. (6 marks)

**Student's answer:**

(a) The spectrum argument originates in Parfit's discussion in *Reasons and Persons*, where he constructed a sequence of populations to generate the repugnant conclusion; Rachels and Temkin later adapted the technique into a general challenge to transitivity. Consider Temkin's version, which is intrapersonal and so avoids complications about population ethics. Imagine a sequence of outcomes A, B, C, …, Z. In A, you undergo two years of excruciating torture. In each subsequent outcome, the pain is very slightly milder but lasts considerably longer, so that by Z you face many years of mild discomfort — say, a faint headache. The premises are: (1) For each adjacent pair, the two outcomes are equally bad, since the difference in intensity is imperceptible and the difference in duration cannot make one worse when the pains are experienced as the same. (2) Z is clearly better than A — years of mild discomfort are plainly preferable to two years of agony. But if each adjacent pair is equally bad, then by the transitivity of "better than" we would have to conclude that A and Z are equally bad, contradicting (2). Since premises (1) and (2) are each compelling, we must reject the transitivity of "better than."

(b) A sorites paradox trades on the vagueness of a predicate like "heap": no single grain makes the difference between heap and non-heap, yet enough grains do. The spectrum argument is different because it does not rely on vague predicates at all. "Better than" is not vague in the way "heap" is; the comparisons in premise (1) are precise judgments about pairs of outcomes, not borderline applications of a vague term. So the standard solutions to the sorites — epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theories — have no grip here, and the argument must be faced on its own terms.

(c) If "better than" is intransitive, then intransitive preferences need not be irrational. The money-pump argument shows that an agent with intransitive preferences can be led through a series of trades, each of which she accepts, back to where she started minus some money. But this argument targets preferences, not betterness. Once we have shown that the value relation itself is intransitive, the agent who prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A may simply be tracking the facts about value. The money pump therefore loses its force: being pumped is only a sign of irrationality if we assume the preferences misrepresent a transitive value ordering, which is precisely what the spectrum argument has refuted. So the intransitivity of "better than" directly licenses intransitive preferences, and the money-pump objection collapses.
