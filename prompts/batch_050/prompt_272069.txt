Last night I spent three hours arguing with a close friend about a case that has gotten under my skin: an engineer at a medical-device firm, bound by a signed confidentiality agreement, who discovers that her firm is knowingly shipping a defective component and leaks the documentation to a regulator. She maintained that the engineer betrayed her employer, full stop, and that dressing the leak up as loyalty is sophistry. I defended the engineer. I have since tried to write down what I actually believe, because I want to know whether my verdict in the case follows from my principles or merely decorated them. Here is where I have landed:

(a) Loyalty is a genuine source of moral reasons, not a bias to be corrected for. The bare fact that someone is my friend, my colleague, or my institution can give me obligations that an uninvolved stranger lacks, and it is no defect in me that I act on them.

(b) Loyalty's demands are fixed by the constitutive aims of the relationship in question. What loyalty to a firm requires is set by what the firm is for; what loyalty to a friend requires is set by the goods the friendship exists to realize. So when the friend or the institution itself betrays those aims, loyalty can license opposition rather than protective silence.

(c) Whistleblowing is therefore not inherently disloyal. The engineer can be acting from loyalty to the firm -- to its real purposes, its other employees, the patients it exists to serve -- even against the express wishes of its current leadership.

(d) A voluntarily undertaken promise of confidentiality binds even when breaking it is the only available means of preventing serious wrongdoing. The wrong of promise-breaking is not erased by good consequences, though the promise-breaker may in some situations be blameless, even praiseworthy, all things considered.

(e) Betrayal is a distinctive moral category. To betray someone is to wrong them in a way that cannot be reduced to the harm caused or the expectations frustrated, which is why selling out a friend is worse than inflicting an equivalent loss on a stranger.

My questions: Are these five commitments jointly tenable, or have I smuggled in an inconsistency somewhere between (b) and (d)? Please go through the pairs and tell me which are in genuine tension, which are only apparently so, and how severe each real tension is. For each genuine tension, state precisely which premise has to give and what surrendering it costs. Note that my verdict in the engineer case depends on (b) and (c) doing the work while (d) stays quiet -- if that is the crux, say so explicitly rather than letting me discover it. Finally, if you had to pick a single revision that restores the most coherence at the lowest price, which would it be? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad.
