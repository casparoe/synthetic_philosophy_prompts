I retired from a research career in biology (mostly physiology) about four years ago and have been going through a philosophy reading list since. I'm on the Zhuangzi now, in a translation a friend lent me, and alongside it I read one of those popular "wisdom of the East" paperbacks, which summarizes Zhuangzi on death like this:

"Zhuangzi taught that death is simply a transformation of qi, the way water becomes ice, so nothing is really lost and the person continues on in a new form. That is why, when his wife died, he celebrated, drumming on a pot and singing. For Zhuangzi the individual self is an illusion in the first place -- nobody can finally say whether they are a man dreaming he is a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he is a man -- and since there is no persisting self to lose, grief rests on a conceptual mistake. The sage therefore does not mourn. Zhuangzi's teaching on death is in essence Epicurus's with Chinese characteristics: stop fearing death, and stop grieving."

Now, some of this matches what I found in the actual text, but some of it rubbed me the wrong way, and I can't tell if that's the paperback's fault or mine. The qi business sounds to a scientist like a conservation law doing philosophical work it maybe wasn't asked to do, and the "no self, so grief is a mistake" move sounds more Buddhist than what I've been reading, but I'm not confident.

Can you audit it? What parts would a careful reader of the Zhuangzi accept, what's a defensible simplification, and what's an actual misreading -- especially the wife episode, which I remember differently from how they tell it. I'm also suspicious of the word "continues" and of the tidy instruction at the end. Does the conclusion (don't fear, don't mourn) follow from the accurate parts, or does the summary only get there by quietly strengthening the text into something more doctrinaire? Also, seperately: is it even right to treat Zhuangzi as having a "teaching" here, given how he writes?
