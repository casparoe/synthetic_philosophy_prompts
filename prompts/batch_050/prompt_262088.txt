Last weekend a friend and I argued deep into the night over the puzzle of the self-torturer, and I still cannot locate where his reasoning breaks. The setup, as we stipulated it: a device is permanently attached to you with a dial of 1,000 settings; each notch upward adds an imperceptible increment of pain and pays you a fixed, significant sum; the dial cannot be turned back. His argument ran as follows. (1) For every setting n, you prefer n+1 to n, since the pain difference is imperceptible while the payment is plainly noticeable. (2) Preference is transitive: if n+1 beats n for every n, then 1,000 beats 0. (3) But at 1,000 you are in agony and would surrender everything you earned to return to 0, so you do not prefer 1,000 to 0. (4) Something must give, and the least costly casualty is transitivity itself: rational preferences can be intransitive wherever options differ along an imperceptible continuum. (5) Such continua are everywhere — careers built one day's choices at a time, health lost one indulgence at a time, savings eroded one small purchase at a time — so intransitivity is a general feature of ordinary rational choice, not a curiosity of torture devices. (6) Consequently the money-pump argument for transitivity also fails: each step of a pump can be individually rational, so the pump demonstrates nothing. Steps (5) and (6) seem to me to prove too much — they would license treating any slow drift into a ruined life as rational, and I refuse to accept that. If forced to choose, I would rather give up (1) than abandon transitivity, but I cannot articulate what justifies that preference. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
