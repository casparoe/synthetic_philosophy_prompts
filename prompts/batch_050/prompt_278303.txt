Write a dialogue of roughly 800–1,000 words between two philosophers, both at the top of their game, arguing late at night over a whiteboard about the causal exclusion argument. One defends the argument in its strongest form: if every physical event that has a cause at all has a sufficient physical cause, then a mental property can cause a physical event only if that event is systematically overdetermined -- and mental-to-physical causation is not plausibly like the benign cases of overdetermination. The other defends non-reductive mental causation: mental properties are distinct from, though realized by, physical properties, and they are genuinely causally efficacious anyway.

Structure the dispute as follows. Each character must, at some point, construct an original concrete analogy on the whiteboard -- a machine, a circuit, a voting procedure, anything with parts -- that is meant to capture the structure of their own position, and each must then locate precisely the joint where the other's analogy fails to match the mental-physical case. The exclusionist's analogy should be built to make mental causation look like redundant double-causation; the physicalist's should be built to make the mental-physical relation look like a single causal process described at two levels. Do not let either character merely assert that their analogy is the better fit: each must give a reason tied to the actual details of the case they built.

The dialogue must also pass through the generalization problem: the physicalist presses that the exclusion reasoning, if sound, seems to drain causal efficacy from the hardness of a hammer, the temperature of a gas, and every other higher-level property, not just mental ones; the exclusionist must either bite that bullet with dignity or draw a principled line, and whichever they choose, the physicalist must attack that specific choice rather than repeat the original point.

Both interlocutors must actually listen: include at least two moments where a character concedes a premise or reformulates their claim under pressure, and at least one moment where a character correctly anticipates the other's reply before it is made. Neither may be given a lazy or outdated version of the opposing view.

Do not let the dialogue end in an amicable draw or in mutual applause for the depth of the problem. By the final exchange, one character must land a move that the other visibly cannot answer within the conversation, and it should be clear which one it is. Then, in an afterword of about 100 words, commit to a verdict: state which position you take to survive the exchange and why, identify the single premise or inference of the exclusion argument you would attack first if you wanted to rescue the losing side, and say honestly whether the winning move depends on any contested assumption about realization, causation, or levels of description that the loser could still contest on another day.
