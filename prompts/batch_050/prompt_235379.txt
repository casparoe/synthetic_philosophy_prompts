So I spent 35 years as a hydrologist (mostly groundwater modeling, nothing philosophical about it) and since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole, as apparently happens to a certain kind of retired scientist. Next month I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner of my oldest friend, a pure mathematician who's spent his whole career proving theorems in algebraic topology. He's a total Platonist in the naive working-mathematician sense: he'll tell you over beers that he didn't invent anything, he just went and looked at what was already out there, and that a proved theorem is proved forever, unlike my models which he never tired of pointing out get revised every five years. Meanwhile I've been reading Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, where the whole drama of the polyhedron formula is that proofs get patched, counterexamples get "monster-barred," definitions get renegotiated, and the theorem you end up with isn't quite the one you started with. I find this hilarious given the occasion and I want the toast to gently poke him with it.

Here's the hard part. The room is maybe half mathematicians, some of whom will definately know the Lakatos book and will catch sloppy versions of the argument instantly, and half family and neighbors who don't know what a polyhedron is and frankly don't care. I want a toast of about five minutes that builds its affectionate ribbing on this one idea -- that maybe his life's work looks less like uncovering timeless truths and more like forty years of negotiating with monsters -- but that lands as warmth and not as a seminar. The family should get the joke at the level of "even mathematicians argue about what counts as proved," and the mathematicians should get the real version. Also, and I say this as a scientist who knows how it feels when pop-philosophy mangles your field: please don't write the toast as if Lakatos simply won and Platonism is refuted. If there's a note in the toast itself, or a note to me alongside it, I'd like it to mark out what's fairly settled about how mathematical practice actually works versus where philosophers of mathematics genuinely still fight -- e.g. whether the quasi-empirical picture describes the finished product or just the messy process of getting there. Don't let the toast present a contested view as the consensus just because it makes a better joke.
