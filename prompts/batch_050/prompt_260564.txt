I am designing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of AI safety and cognitive delegation. I need one essay question on HCH (Humans Consulting HCH) and the factored cognition hypothesis, answerable in 75 minutes, that cannot be completed by reciting the standard gloss -- i.e., that HCH is a tree of a human consulting copies of itself and that iterated amplification trains a model to approximate it. The question should force students to engage the genuinely contested premises. Specifically, I want them to confront at least two of the following cruxes: first, that the factored cognition hypothesis is an empirical claim about the decomposability of hard cognitive tasks into subtasks solvable by a short-lived agent with limited context, and not a definitional truth about HCH; second, the tension between the worry that recursive decomposition degrades information (each hand-off is lossy, so depth compounds error) and the reply that the root agent can re-query, cross-check, and aggregate subagent answers, so the interesting question is which error structure dominates; third, whether the limiting behavior of HCH should be understood as recovering the human's idealized judgment (a reflective-equilibrium picture) or as producing something the original human would not recognize as their own view, and what hangs on the difference for using HCH as an alignment target.

Please deliver: (1) the exam question itself, phrased so that a student who only memorized the definition will produce an obviously inadequate answer, with a note on the word count expected; (2) a model answer of roughly 600–700 words written at the level of a strong student in this seminar, taking a definite position rather than surveying both sides; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 that identifies five must-hit moves, states the marks attached to each, and for each move names the specific way weak answers typically go wrong -- for instance, treating the decomposition question as settled by the definition of HCH, or conflating the epistemic question (does recursion preserve judgment?) with the engineering question (can a model be trained to imitate the tree?).
