I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm currently working on a long magazine piece about the ethics of border control. My research has left me less interested in the standard arguments than in the pattern of intuitions held by a colleague I've been interviewing — a political philosopher whose case-by-case verdicts strike me as coherent even though I can't yet articulate the rule she's applying. Her positions so far: a host may exclude anyone from a private dinner party for any reason whatsoever; a church may restrict membership to those who profess its creed; a private golf club may reject applicants on entirely arbitrary grounds, such as disliking an applicant's accent; a restaurant may not turn away customers on the basis of race; a private university may select students by academic promise but not by race or religion; and the United States may set its overall immigration levels as its voters see fit and select among applicants by skills and family ties, but may not exclude by race. She insists all of this hangs together, and I'm inclined to believe her, but I'd like to state the underlying principle in the piece rather than merely listing cases.

The occasion for asking is a new case whose treatment I need to anticipate before our next interview. Suppose a Western democracy proposes to refuse entry to all prospective immigrants of one particular religion, defending the policy not on security grounds but as a means of preserving the country's historic cultural character. Predict her verdict — but more importantly, reconstruct the candidate principles that could generate her six existing verdicts, since I suspect at least two or three quite different rules are consistent with them (say, a rule about which kinds of associations own their membership versus operate in the open market; a rule about whether the exclusion tracks the association's legitimate constitutive purpose; a rule about whether the exclusion entrenches a hereditary status hierarchy). Tell me what each candidate predicts about the religion-based exclusion, which prediction you find most likely given the texture of her earlier judgments, and what further hypothetical case I could put to her that would best discriminate among the surviving principles.

One more thing: if one of her existing verdicts is doing disproportionate work in fixing the principle — that is, if dropping or reversing a single verdict would leave the field of candidate rules much wider — flag that verdict for me, since it's the part of the interview I'll want to quote at length.
