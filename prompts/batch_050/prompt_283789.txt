I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on rational choice: 16 students, one 80-minute session. I want a classroom game whose payoff structure makes superrational reasoning genuinely tempting to students before they ever hear the term. By superrational reasoning I mean Hofstadter's idea that when agents face identical decision problems and each knows the others reason just as they do, each may conclude: whatever I end up choosing, the others, reasoning as I do, will choose as well — and choose on that basis, even when an individually dominating alternative exists.

Design the game to these constraints:

1. Do not use the prisoner's dilemma or any standard named game or its familiar payoff numbers. Invent an original collective choice problem with concrete payoffs (points convertible into something small but real — I will supply chocolate) in which each player has a strictly dominating selfish option and there is a collectively far better symmetric outcome. Play must be simultaneous, anonymous, genuinely one-shot, and untraceable, so no student can plausibly hope for reciprocity or reputation effects.

2. Run the session in two phases. Phase one is the invented game as described. Phase two is a wedge round that pulls apart the two grounds a student might have for the cooperative-symmetric choice: (i) the prediction that the others will in fact reason the way they do, and (ii) the sense that their own choice somehow settles what the others do. One possible device is a variant in which students are told, within the fiction, that their counterpart is a deterministic replica of themselves versus a stranger who merely shares their habits of thought — but use a better design if you have one. The wedge round should force students to state which belief they were actually relying on in phase one.

3. No lecture and no terminology beforehand; the word "superrationality" first appears in the debrief.

Deliver: the complete rules and payoff tables; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes; the debrief plan, mapping specific predictable in-game moments (for instance, the student who says "if I take the selfish option, so will everyone who thinks like me") onto the components of the idea; a list of what will predictably go wrong in practice — students assuming repeated play, students attempting to coordinate aloud, the strong student who states the dominance argument in the first two minutes — with handling for each that does not collapse the activity; and an honest note on what the game cannot reproduce, for instance that it can make symmetric reasoning feel compelling but cannot show it to be rational, and that chocolate stakes differ from real dilemmas.

In the debrief plan and the note, keep settled matters separate from contested ones. It is settled that, given genuinely independent choices, standard game theory treats the dominating option as uniquely rational and that superrationality is not an accepted solution concept. It is genuinely contested whether symmetry-respecting reasoning of this kind can be rational, and how it relates to Newcomb-type cases and rival decision theories; do not let the design or the debrief present Hofstadter's verdict as the consensus.

At most 1,000 words.
