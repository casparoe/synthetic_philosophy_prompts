I am a PhD student in political theory writing a dissertation chapter on the epistemic defense of majority rule, and I need a thought experiment built to specification. The chapter argues that the standard invocation of the Condorcet jury theorem fails under a realistic structure of correlated judgment, and I want a case that generates that failure rather than stipulating it.

Construct a scenario meeting all of the following constraints: (1) a decision-making body renders binary verdicts with an objectively correct answer — a hiring committee, a diagnostic panel, a parole board, or similar; realistic, no science fiction; (2) every member is individually competent, in that each one, deciding alone across the run of cases the body faces, would reach the correct verdict strictly more often than not — the result may not be produced by staffing the body with incompetent members; (3) the majority verdict of the body is nonetheless wrong more often than right, and becomes less reliable as more members of the same kind are added; (4) no bribery, deception, or strategic voting — every member votes their sincere, privately formed judgment; (5) the scenario itself in a single paragraph.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it works. State which formal condition of the theorem it violates. Show, with rough but explicit arithmetic, why individual competence above one-half is compatible with majority accuracy below one-half and with accuracy that falls as the body grows. Identify the single feature of the case doing the work, and describe what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed while everything else is held fixed. Then give the strongest objection a defender of epistemic majoritarianism would press — for instance, that the case exploits an illegitimate, global notion of competence, or that real deliberation would break the structure — and say candidly whether you think the objection succeeds.

One request concerning calibration: the mathematics here is settled, but much of what surrounds it is not. Please keep three things visibly distinct — what follows from the theorem as a matter of proof; what is robustly supported empirically about how real collective bodies actually judge; and what remains genuinely contested among specialists, such as whether anything like the independence condition is ever approximated by deliberating humans, or how competence should be defined when performance varies across case types. Do not present a contested position as the consensus view.
