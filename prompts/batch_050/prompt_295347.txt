I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, in the last year or so, has been working his way into philosophy, and I have landed in the literature on advanced AI systems. At a reading group I attend, we were debating where research effort and funding ought to go, and a colleague shut down a long stretch of the discussion with a sentence I have since heard repeated in several places: "We don't need to align an AI system; we just need to control it." In context it was being used to argue that work on instilling goals or values into models is a distraction, and that the serious safety work is all in monitoring, sandboxing, access restrictions, tripwires, and the like. My instinct from engineering is that this conflates several quite different claims, some of which I suspect are near-tautologies and some of which are false for sufficiently capable systems, but I cannot yet lay them apart cleanly.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher who is also well versed in the AI safety literature, and think carefully before answering — I would rather you first brainstorm a generous set of candidate readings and then prune than offer the first three that occur to you. What I want is a disambiguation, not an essay defending one side. Specifically:

1. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing. I suspect these will differ along several axes: what "align" is taken to mean (getting the system to want what we want versus merely to do what we say); what "control" is taken to mean (restricting capabilities and affordances, monitoring and interrupting, structural measures designed to work even on the assumption that the system is actively misaligned, or something vaguer); whether the claim is that control makes alignment unnecessary, that control is what alignment research should actually consist in, or merely that control buys time; and whether the claim is made about present-day systems, near-future systems, or arbitrarily capable ones. Feel free to restructure those axes if my physicist's carve-up is not the natural one.

2. For each reading, give a verdict: is it trivial or definitional (true but unable to do argumentative work), substantive and defensible, substantive and contentious, or straightforwardly false — and say briefly why. Where a reading's plausibility depends on how capable the system is assumed to become, say so explicitly, since I suspect several readings are true for today's models and false in the limit.

3. Say which of these readings, if any, would actually support the use to which my colleague put the slogan — namely, concluding that alignment research deserves less effort relative to control-style measures — and which readings he would need to be assuming, perhaps without realising it. If none of the defensible readings gets him his conclusion, say that plainly.

4. Finally, note any reading that people who repeat the slogan most likely have in mind, even if it is not the most defensible one, since I would like to know what I am actually hearing at these meetings.

Please write the answer in British English. A structured format with headings or numbered points would suit me best, and where you draw on standard distinctions from the field, state them in your own words rather than pointing me to sources.
