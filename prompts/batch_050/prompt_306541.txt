I run a small internal forecasting program at a mid-sized software company — about 40 forecasters, maybe 60 questions a quarter, small cash prizes, mostly for calibration practice and to help leadership think. This came to a head at an offsite last month when my cofounder and I got into a long argument about whether half our questions even make sense. The problem: a lot of the questions people want to forecast are about things we control. "Will we ship the redesign by Q3?" "Will we open the Berlin office?" "Will I still be at this company in two years?" Some forecasters say "100%, I'm the one building it, just watch me," others refuse to put any number down because they're the one who'll decide, and one person told me that assigning a probability to his own open choice felt like it settled the choice before he'd actually deliberated — like saying 90% to grad school means you've already gone. I think he's onto something (I've heard the phrase that deliberation crowds out prediction, that you can't cleanly do both at once) but I also think people overapply it, because obviously you can predict your own behavior in lots of cases — I can be 95% sure I'll order the usual at lunch without feeling my agency drain away.

What I want is a written procedure I can hand to forecasters and question-writers that tells them how to handle any question whose outcome depends on a decision by us — by the company, or by the forecaster personally. It should be runnable in a few minutes per question: what to check, in what order, and what to do at each branch. Specifically it needs to cover: when it's fine to just treat your own future action as another event and forecast it like anything else; when the question should be rewritten so the decision is pulled out (e.g. conditional questions like "if we commit to the redesign in June, does it ship by Q3?"); when a forecast shouldn't be run at all because the number itself feeds back into the decision (a low forecast for the Berlin office demoralizes the team pushing for it); and what to do when the forecaster is the decision-maker — forecast before deliberating, after deciding, or both, and what to do with the fact that the two numbers will differ. Flag the steps that are genuine judgment calls rather than mechanical, and say where the procedure will definately misfire.

Two more things. First, actually run the procedure on these three questions from last quarter's list and show the work: "Will our CEO approve the Berlin office?" (someone else's decision, but the forecast circulates to her), "Will we hit Q4 revenue if the new pricing launches on schedule?" and "Will I still be working here in two years?" (this one was submitted by an engineer about herself). At least one of these should come out with an answer I won't love. Second, I don't want a balanced survey of positions on whether you can assign probabilities to your own choices — the procedure has to take a definite stand on when that's legitimate and when it isn't, commit to it, and defend the commitment, even though I know this is contested territory and reasonable people land elsewhere. If the stand it takes has weird implications (e.g. it implies my grad-school friend is right and some questions are unanswerable by the person they most concern), own that rather than smoothing it over.
