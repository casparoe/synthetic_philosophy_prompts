I am a high school student, and in my literature class we recently read excerpts from Leo Tolstoy's treatise What Is Art? I found his position fascinating and rather extreme. As I understand him, Tolstoy held that genuine art consists in one person consciously transmitting a feeling he has actually lived through, by means of external signs, so that other people are "infected" by that same feeling; he judged art by the sincerity, individuality, and clarity of that transmission, and by whether it unites people in a shared moral and emotional life. On this basis he dismissed a great deal of celebrated art, including, remarkably, most of his own novels, as counterfeit art produced to entertain a leisured class rather than to communicate real feeling. Our teacher has asked us to write an essay applying Tolstoy's theory to art forms that did not exist in his lifetime, and I would like help thinking through two cases that seem to pull his theory in opposite directions.

The first case is AI image generation: a person types a short sentence into a program and receives, within seconds, a striking image, perhaps a stormy seascape that fills viewers with genuine awe and melancholy. No artist experienced any storm, any awe, or any melancholy; yet viewers are undeniably moved. The second case is the flood of short videos made by ordinary teenagers and shared among millions: unpolished clips of dances, kitchen mishaps, or a grandfather laughing, which plainly transmit joy and affection between strangers who will never meet. My intuition is that Tolstoy might condemn the first and celebrate the second, since he championed the art of the common people against refined elite art, and the videos look like folk art for our century. But the matter seems less simple: many viral videos are imitative, trend-chasing, and made for attention rather than to convey a feeling the creator truly lived, which sounds like exactly the insincerity Tolstoy attacked, while some people who use AI tools report carefully crafting prompts to express a genuine inner vision, which sounds like exactly the sincerity he demanded.

Please reconstruct, as carefully as you can, what Tolstoy would most plausibly say about each case, reasoning from his actual criteria rather than from what a generic moralist might say. In particular: does AI-generated imagery fail his test because no feeling passes from maker to viewer, even though viewers are genuinely infected by something, and if so, does that reveal that his definition is really about the audience's experience or really about the artist's? And where do you think his theory is put under the most pressure by these two cases: does the AI case expose a flaw in tying art to the artist's inner life, or would Tolstoy take it as vindication? I would like your reasoning, not just a verdict, since the essay requires me to defend an interpretation.
