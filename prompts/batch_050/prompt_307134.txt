I'm taking a seminar on nineteenth-century British moral philosophy, and at a dinner after last week's meeting two classmates got into an argument that has stayed with me because both of them seemed to be doing better than the usual level of these debates. I only observed and didn't weigh in, so I'll try to report both sides fairly. Maya took the egoist position. Her opening move was that a practical reason has to be a reason for someone -- a consideration that grips an agent from that agent's standpoint -- and that a principle requiring her to count her own good as exactly one unit among billions asks her to abandon the only standpoint from which reasons can get any purchase on her at all. So rational egoism, she said, is at least as defensible as any impartial morality. Tomás replied that once you grant pleasure is good and suffering bad wherever they occur, giving special weight to your own merely because it is yours is as arbitrary as giving special weight to what happens on Tuesdays; impartial benevolence toward all sentient beings is simply self-evident to anyone who reflects carefully. Maya's second move was to press on the phrase "self-evident": committed egoists find egoism equally self-evident, she said, so self-evidence can't adjudicate between them, and you cannot derive a duty from what happens to seem obvious to you. Tomás conceded he could not prove his principle to a holdout, but argued this is a limitation of proof rather than of the principle, since egoism too must rest on an unprovable axiom, and his axiom at least builds in no favoritism toward any particular person. He then added a third move: pure egoism is self-undermining in practice, because a society of consistent egoists would do worse even by egoist standards. Maya's reply to that was that the empirical point concedes her game -- if egoism itself recommends cooperative and restrained behavior, then it already yields everything we need from morality without importing impartial axioms at all.

Here is what I'd like from you. First, adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the moves above (Maya's opening, Tomás's self-evidence claim, Maya's rejoinder about self-evidence, Tomás's unprovability concession, his self-undermining argument, and Maya's final reply), say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification for each verdict. Second, say what, if anything, both parties are missing -- in particular whether their standoff is resolvable in principle or whether each side can remain fully rational while rejecting the other's foundational claim, and what difference it would make if one could establish that the universe is arranged so that duty and self-interest always coincide in the long run. Third, connect the dispute to what our seminar has been covering: our professor keeps insisting that a certain Victorian moralist regarded the axioms of egoism and universal benevolence as equally self-evident and irreconcilable by reason alone, calling this the deepest problem of ethics, and I'd like to know whether my classmates' dinner argument is a genuine instance of that problem or only a superficial resemblance.
