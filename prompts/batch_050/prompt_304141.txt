OK so I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, but I got into an argument with a friend about antinatalism and ended up actually reading a summary of David Benatar's asymmetry argument. The version I got: for a person who exists, pain is bad and pleasure is good, but for someone who never exists, the absence of pain is good while the absence of pleasure is "not bad" because there's nobody there to be deprived. And from that asymmetry he concludes that coming into existence is always a net harm, even for happy people.

Here's my engineer problem with it: it smells like a division-by-zero bug. The whole argument hinges on evaluating "the absence of pleasure is not bad" for a subject that doesn't exist, and I'm not sure that expression has a defined value at all, vs. Benatar thinking it evaluates to "not bad" and his critics thinking it should evaluate to "bad." So the debate seems to be about how to handle a null pointer, and I suspect both sides are smuggling in a convention.

So give me your actual credence, as a number, that the asymmetry argument is sound — meaning the asymmetry is coherent AND it actually holds AND it genuinely implies that creating people is a net harm. But decompose it, don't just give me a bare number:

1. Probability that evaluating goods/bads for a never-existing subject is even coherent (my "defined value" worry).
2. Probability the asymmetry holds given that the evaluation is coherent — i.e., that absence-of-pleasure for nonexistent people is genuinely not bad rather than symmetrically bad.
3. Probability the conclusion follows even granting 1 and 2 — does "never a benefit to exist" actually get you to antinatalism, or is there a gap (e.g., duties, or the asymmetry cutting the other way for preventing suffering)?
4. Any term I'm missing.
