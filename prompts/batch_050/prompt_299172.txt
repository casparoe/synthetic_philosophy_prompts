Me and a friend got into a long, weirdly heated argument last night after she said it is obscene to rank atrocities such as the Holocaust and the Holodomor, while I said that refusing every comparison makes it hard to talk honestly about reparations, memorial priorities, punishment, or prevention. We were going in circles untill 2am because we kept sliding between different claims: whether the evidence permits a ranking, whether morality permits one, whether making the comparison publicly degrades victims, and whether institutions sometimes have to choose anyway. For a mixed undergrad/grad seminar, design a decision-tree sorting quiz that starts from the minimal commitment that all mass atrocities are gravely wrong and ends at a named position on comparative judgment.

Build the tree from the logical space first rather than from standard labels. Separate at least these issues: what exactly is being compared, such as deaths, suffering, intent, cruelty, lasting damage, culpability, or prevention priority; whether those dimensions are commensurable; whether factual uncertainty blocks judgment; whether a true comparison can still be expressively wrong; and whether the answer changes in courts, museums, classrooms, reparations decisions, or ordinary conversation. Use at most nine diagnostic questions and produce six to eight leaves. Required leaves include a robust comparabilist, a plural-metric comparabilist, an epistemic non-comparabilist, an intrinsic incomparabilist, an expressive or performative anti-comparativist, and a contextual institutional comparativist; add a quietist or any other important position if you think the space requires one. Give the leaves descriptive names rather than relying on jargon.

Each branch should be a concrete question that someone can answer without knowing which position it leads toward, with yes, no, or depends answers. The questions must include cases involving the same death toll but different intentions, the same methods but very different numbers, deeply imperfect survivor testimony, an institution forced to allocate limited resources, and a memorial speech claiming that one atrocity was worse than another. At each leaf, state the position’s core commitments, what comparisons its holder can still make, what would count against the view, and its predicted answer pattern. Flag any leaves the questions cannot cleanly distinguish, and note any coherent-looking answer combination that has no leaf, briefly diagnosing whether it seems incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Do not rank actual historical events yourself.

Start directly with the decision tree, with no preface or restatement of the task. Put any self-criticism in a short Pressure points section at the end, especially if one of my distinctions collapses or if the tree secretly presupposes a controversial theory of value. And if you think the whole task as posed is confused or cannot sort people by belief rather than vocabulary, say that plainly instead of politely forcing it.
