In the philosophy of mathematics seminar I attend, a recurring move is made whenever the Church–Turing thesis comes up. Someone says that a Turing machine is "just an idealized human clerk with pencil and paper," and that this settles the epistemic status of the thesis: because the machine is simply the clerk with the inessential features stripped away, the thesis is an analysis of the concept of calculability rather than an empirical conjecture, and so no discovery in physics or cognitive science could falsify it. The move traces back to Turing's own presentation. In §1 of A. M. Turing's "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" (1936), he writes:

"We may compare a man in the process of computing a real number to a machine which is only capable of a finite number of conditions q1, q2, ..., qR which will be called 'm-configurations'. The machine is supplied with a 'tape', (the analogue of paper) running through it, and divided into sections (called 'squares') each capable of bearing a 'symbol'. At any moment there is just one square, say the r-th, bearing the symbol S(r) which is 'in the machine'. ... The 'scanned symbol' is the only one of which the machine is, so to speak, 'directly aware'. However, by altering its m-configuration the machine can effectively remember some of the symbols which it has 'seen' (scanned) previously."

Turing then states his contention that the machine's operations "include all those which are used in the computation of a number," and remarks that the justification for the definitions "lies in the fact that the human memory is necessarily limited."

I would like an audit of this analogy and of the argumentative work it is being made to do, both by Turing and by my seminar-mates. Specifically:

1. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the clerk's states of mind, the sheet of paper, the symbol currently being looked at, scratch work, the clerk's attention, and the instruction booklet the clerk follows. Where Turing's text supplies the mapping directly, say so; where you are extending it, flag that.

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter from the ones that do not. Candidates to consider: the clerk is fallible and tires, while the machine is defined as error-free; the clerk's memory is not merely limited but reconstructive and unreliable; the tape is unbounded while any actual clerk dies; the clerk can step back, reinterpret instructions, and notice patterns not codified in any rule; the machine's m-configurations are stipulated to be finitely many, which is precisely what needs defending if the machine is to capture everything the clerk could in principle do. Which of these are mere idealizations of the frictionless-plane sort, and which threaten to build the conclusion into the model?

3. On the basis of that audit, assess the conclusion the analogy is used to support: that the identification of computability with effective calculability is a conceptual analysis rather than an empirical claim about human beings or the physical world. Does the audit leave that conclusion standing, or does it reveal that the "human clerk" picture cannot by itself carry the weight — so that the thesis must rest partly on something else (e.g., convergence of independently proposed formalisms, or an implicit empirical premise about what human calculators can do)?

4. Finally, say whether the same audit cuts differently against Church's lambda-calculus formulation, which lacks the clerk analogy entirely, and if you think a better analogy than the clerk is available for conveying what the thesis claims, supply it and explain what it gets right that the clerk picture does not.
