Two friends of mine, Marco and Denise, got into a long arguement over a line from the Zhuangzi that I showed them last week. Its from the chapter called "The Adjustment of Controversies" in the old public-domain James Legge translation, and the line goes: "When we sleep, the soul communicates with (what is external to us); when we awake, the body is set free."

Marco said this line proves Zhuangzi was no dream skeptic: if the soul genuinely "communicates with what is external" while we sleep, then even dreams reach outside us, so the classic worry that any experience might be a dream never even gets going in this text.

Denise said he's reading way too much into one sentence. Elsewhere in the same chapter, she pointed out, Zhuangzi says you only know you were dreaming after you wake up, and floats the idea that this whole life might be a great dream that we only wake from at death. So the "communicates" bit is just describing what dreaming seems like from the inside, not claiming dreams really hook us onto the outside world.

Marco's comeback: a real skeptic couldn't even draw the sleep/waking contrast the sentence depends on. The line only means something if waking is the state where the body gets "set free" and actually deals with things, so at most Zhuangzi distrusts dreams, not waking life -- the contrast itself presupposes that waking puts us in touch with reality.

Denise answered that this is exactly what the great-dream idea undoes: if ordinary waking might itself be a dream relative to some greater waking, then "set free" is only provisional, and the contrast Marco leans on could be between two states that are both dreams. She did admit the quoted sentence by itself doesnt prove skepticism, just that it fits with it.

They asked me to settle it and I honestly couldnt. Please adjudicate move by move: who is right about which specific point, which of the four moves is weakest, and is there something both of them are missing about what a single sentence like this can and can't show?
