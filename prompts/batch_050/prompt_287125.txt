Take the 2006 Pluto demotion and run it through the debate about natural kinds. Quick recap of the facts in case they're fuzzy: the IAU voted to adopt a three-part definition of "planet" (orbits the Sun, massive enough to be round under its own gravity, and has cleared its orbital neighborhood), Pluto fails the third criterion, it got reclassified as a "dwarf planet," there was enormous public backlash that persists to this day, and a bunch of planetary scientists (Alan Stern being the loudest) never accepted the decision and pushed a rival geophysical definition instead. New Horizons flying past in 2015 and showing Pluto to be geologically alive did not help the IAU's PR situation.

Here's what I want: use this episode as a test case for the main positions on natural kinds. Does "planet" name a natural kind at all, and does it matter? Walk me through what a Kripke/Putnam-style essentialist should say happened in Prague (was the IAU discovering something, stipulating something, or committing a category error?), what a homeostatic-property-cluster person like Boyd should say (does the cluster of orbital dynamics, formation history, and composition hang together tightly enough that "cleared the neighborhood" tracks a real joint, or is it a gerrymandered criterion designed to get the answer to eight?), and what a promiscuous realist like Dupré should say (is the geophysical definition just as legitimate as the dynamical one, serving different interests, and is the fight therefore not really about Pluto at all?).

Then run it back the other direction: what does the episode show about the theories? A few things that seem load-bearing to me: (1) natural kind talk was built for cases like gold and water, where there's no committee and no vote -- does the fact that a definitional vote felt to everyone like it *mattered* show that kind terms carry normative or practical weight that essentialism and cluster theory both underdescribe? (2) the comparison with "species," where everyone sort of accepts pluralism and nobody riots, versus "planet," where people rioted -- what explains the difference, and can any of the theories predict which kind terms will provoke backlash? (3) the clearing-the-neighborhood criterion is graded and time-dependent (Earth presumably hasn't always cleared its neighborhood; Neptune "has" Pluto in some sense) -- is that a problem for the IAU definition on kind-theoretic grounds, or just a rhetorical gotcha?

Where you think a theory just doesn't have anything to say about some feature of the episode, say so instead of forcing it. And end with a verdict: is Pluto an anomaly for natural kind realism, a confirmation of pluralism, or evidence that the whole "carving nature at its joints" framing was the wrong tool for a category that's basically administrative?
