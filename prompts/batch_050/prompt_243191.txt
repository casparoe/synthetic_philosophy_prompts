Our department's social choice reading group has this ritual where everyone circulates a one-page statement of their settled views before the session and then the others try to break it. I'm a political theory PhD student (dissertation adjacent to formal epistemology and collective decision-making) and I want a pre-mortem before the group tears mine apart, because last time someone got absolutely shredded and I'd rather not repeat that. Here's roughly where I've landed: (a) For ordinary up-or-down committee decisions, the group should just go with the majority on each question separately — May's theorem (anonymity, neutrality, positive responsiveness) basically settles that simple majority is the uniquely fair rule, and I don't see a serious rival. (b) Whatever the group ends up endorsing, the total judgment set has to be consistent and complete; a collective that judges p, judges if-p-then-q, and then refuses q is malfunctioning, full stop. (c) Because of (b), in discursive-dilemma style cases (doctrinal paradox and friends) the right fix is the premise-based procedure: majority vote on the premises, let logic settle the conclusion, and if a majority happens to reject that conclusion, so be it — consistency is non-negotiable. (d) When there's a fact of the matter (juries, expert panels, forecasting tournaments), the justification for majority rule is epistemic: Condorcet's jury theorem — if individuals are better than chance and roughly independent, the majority outperforms any member and bigger groups are better. (e) Supermajority requirements for weighty stuff like constitutional amendments are perfectly legitimate and don't conflict with anything above; they're just a higher bar for higher stakes. (f) The same majoritarian machinery should also cover cases with no fact of the matter at all — the department picking a restaurant, a committee choosing a logo — since majority rule is also how you fairly aggregate pure preferences.
