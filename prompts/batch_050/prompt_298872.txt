I'm a retired chemist and since hanging up my lab coat I've been picking my way through the philosophy of mind shelf at the local library, and last week it spilled over into real life. I had dinner at my neighbour Alan's place with our mutual friend Diane, and the two of them got into a proper argument about dreams that neither of them would drop. It started because Alan described a long, vivid dream about flying over his childhood village, and said something like "I experienced every second of it, it felt completely real at the time." Diane, who's been reading Norman Malcolm, said hold on -- that's exactly what Malcolm denies. Her version of the argument: the only thing we can ever check is the report you give on waking; there's no way to compare the report against an experience that supposedly happened at 3am, so talking about an experience during sleep, with a duration, that felt a certain way, is confused. What you have is a waking impression that you dreamed, full stop.

Alan wasn't having it. He said that's just verificationism run wild -- plenty of things are real even when we can't check them after the fact, and the feeling of remembering a dream is nothing like the feeling of having merely made something up on waking. He then brought up Dennett's cassette theory, which he'd half-remembered from somewhere, and used it against her: Dennett himself thinks the dream narrative is only composed at the moment of waking, played into memory like a pre-recorded tape, yet nobody would call Dennett a skeptic about dreams the way Malcolm is. So, Alan argued, even if Diane is right that the "experience during sleep" story is wrong, Malcolm's conclusion doesn't follow -- you can deny the overnight experience without denying that the morning's vivid sense of having dreamed is a genuine phenomenon in need of explanation. Diane shot back that this concedes her whole point and just relabels it, and that Alan can't have it both ways: either something was going on in his head at 3am that his report tracks, or it wasn't. Alan said there's a third option -- the report tracks a memory composition process at waking -- and Diane said that's not a third option, that's her position with extra steps. Then they both looked at me, and honestly I couldn't tell who had the better of it, since I kept flip-flopping.

Could you go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move? For each move, say whether it's sound, partly right, or mistaken, and flag any point where either of them misrepresents Malcolm or Dennett (I only know these views secondhand, so I want to know if my friends got the philosophy right). Also say what both of them might be missing. I'd like the whole thing in plain English since I'm still new to the jargon, and please end with a final line of the form "Verdict: [who is more right overall, in a short phrase]".
