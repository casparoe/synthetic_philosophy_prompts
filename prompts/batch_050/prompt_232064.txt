I'm starting a phd in moral philosophy and my supervisor has told me to carve my thesis out of the literature on the separateness of persons objection to consequentialism — the Rawls-style complaint that utilitarianism reasons about society the way one person reasons about their own life, adding up costs and benefits across people as if that were just like trading off your own present against your own future, and thereby failing to take seriously the distinction between persons. I've got about 2.5-3 years of funding left, and I want to spend it on whichever open question in this area genuinely matters most, not whichever has the most published back-and-forth.

The candidate questions I keep bumping into:

- Whether the objection really only lands on simple sum-ranking utilitarianism, or whether it generalizes to every aggregative view (prioritarianism, sufficientism, etc.) — and whether "give extra weight to the worse off" is an actual fix or just a relabeling that still pools people's fates.
- Whether Harsanyi-style aggregation theorems basically dissolve the objection by deriving utilitarianism from individually plausible axioms, or whether they just quietly build in the very impersonality the objection is complaining about.
- What happens to the objection if you take Parfit-style reductionism about persons seriously — if personal identity is not deep, does separateness lose its moral force, and would that same argument also undermine aggregation *within* one life (sacrificing your younger self for your older self)?
- Whether contractualism (Scanlon's individualist restriction on reasons) is actually a workable replacement, given that it seems to choke on cases where numbers intuitively should count, like saving five strangers instead of one.
- Whether the objection has any real practical teeth — for cost-benefit analysis, health resource allocation, longtermist cause prioritization — or whether it's a purely theoretical worry that changes nothing downstream.
