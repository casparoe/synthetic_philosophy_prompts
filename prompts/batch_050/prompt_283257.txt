The opening of Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" (Essays, Book I, Chapter 30, in Charles Cotton's translation) recounts that when King Pyrrhus inspected the Roman army drawn up against him, he remarked: "I know not," said he, "what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations) "these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it." Montaigne draws the lesson himself: "By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report."

This lesson sits in an uneasy relation to the rest of the essay. On the one hand, Montaigne spends much of the chapter deflating European confidence: he suggests that we call barbarous whatever is foreign to our own customs, that the Brazilians he describes live closer to a natural standard than we do, and that European cruelties of his own century exceed anything the cannibals do to their enemies. On the other hand, the Pyrrhus anecdote is not an argument for suspending judgment across cultures — it is an argument for judging *better*, by reason rather than by hearsay and habit.

Now suppose Montaigne were alive today and asked to take a position on the modern human rights framework — a body of declarations and institutions that claims to state norms binding on every culture, and that its critics (including some governments, some anthropologists, and some postcolonial thinkers) denounce as Western parochialism dressed up as universal reason. The question I want you to answer: where would Montaigne come down?

In your answer, please do the following:

1. Use the quoted passage to resist the lazy reading. Explain why the Pyrrhus anecdote makes it impossible to cast Montaigne as a simple relativist who thinks each culture's practices are beyond outside judgment.

2. But take the relativist reading seriously too. Reconstruct the strongest case that Montaigne would view today's universal human rights discourse as exactly the kind of thing his essay warns against: a local custom (ours) mistaking itself for reason.

3. Adjudicate. Would Montaigne sign the declaration, refuse to sign it, or sign it while warning its drafters about something? Your answer should turn on the distinction the essay itself draws between two ways of condemning a foreign practice — condemning it because it is foreign, and condemning it by some standard that survives scrutiny of one's own society. If Montaigne has such a standard, say what it is and whether it is robust enough to support anything like modern universalism; if he doesn't, say what follows.

4. Test your conclusion against one hard contemporary case of your choosing: a practice defended by its practitioners as integral to their culture and condemned by outsiders as a violation of universal dignity. What would Montaigne's method actually direct us to *do* in that case — and is his method, in the end, usable at all?
