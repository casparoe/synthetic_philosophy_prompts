Several years ago I came across the argument, which I associate with essays by Brian Tomasik and others writing on wild animal suffering, that habitat destruction is, on net, a good thing for animals. The rough story: most wild animals are small-bodied, short-lived, r-selected creatures whose lives consist of a brief window of experience bookended by starvation, predation, disease, or exposure, and whose populations are kept in check by constant massive juvenile mortality; since most of these lives plausibly contain more suffering than pleasure, reducing the total number of such lives by converting habitat to parking lots, farmland, or managed forest reduces total suffering. I have found myself increasingly persuaded by this, to the point of defending it at a reading group last month, where it went over about as well as you'd expect. But I want to stress-test it seriously rather than just enjoy being contrarian.

So: what would count against this position? I'd like you to take the position itself apart into its load-bearing components -- something like the empirical claim that average wild animal lives are net-negative, the demographic claim that habitat destruction durably reduces wild animal populations rather than merely displacing them or selecting for even more miserable survivors, and the ethical claim that preventing net-negative lives is good rather than merely neutral -- and tell me what evidence, arguments, or results would defeat each one. Some specific things I want addressed: What would welfare-relevant biological measurements (stress hormones, health biomarkers, mortality-cause distributions across species) have to show to sink the empirical claim, and is existing evidence of that kind even capable of settling how animals weight their own experiences? Does the argument survive the observation that habitat destruction often kills existing animals acutely and painfully (fire, flooding, machinery) rather than simply preventing future births, and would a defender concede that as a defeater or absorb it as a one-time cost? What about the counterargument that the position proves too much -- that by the same logic we should prefer sterilizing or paving over the entire biosphere, or that an analogous argument applied to impoverished human populations would be monstrous? Is that a fatal reductio, or is there a principled disanalogy a defender can stand on?

Please also assess whether the position as usually stated is actually exposed to anything at all, or whether it tends to get qualified into unfalsifiability -- every seeming counterexample (a thriving, low-mortality population; evidence of rich positive experiences in the wild) absorbed by moving to a different species, a different habitat type, or a different welfare metric. If it is insulated in that way, say plainly whether you think that's a defect in the view or just what a robust argument looks like. I'd like the candidate defeaters ranked roughly from decisive to merely awkward, with a clear bottom line on which ones you think a committed defender would themselves have to concede as genuine threats, as opposed to objections only critics find forceful. Please write your response as continuous prose, like a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
