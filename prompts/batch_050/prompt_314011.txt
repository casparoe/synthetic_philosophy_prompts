Last night, over a long dinner that turned into a longer argument, a friend and I found ourselves deadlocked over John Stuart Mill's posthumously published Chapters on Socialism (drafted beginning in 1869 and printed in 1879). Our dispute turned on the opening of his Introductory chapter:

"In the great country beyond the Atlantic, which is now well-nigh the most powerful country in the world, and will soon be indisputably so, manhood suffrage prevails. ... In Great Britain the suffrage is not yet so widely extended, but the last Reform Act admitted within what is called the pale of the Constitution so large a body of those who live on weekly wages, that as soon and as often as these shall choose to act together as a class ... they will exercise, though not a complete ascendancy, a very great influence on legislation. Now these are the very class which, in the vocabulary of the higher ranks, are said to have no stake in the country. Of course they have in reality the greatest stake, since their daily bread depends on its prosperity."

My friend's position is that the intervening century and a half simply refuted Mill: universal suffrage arrived on schedule, yet wage-earners never acted together as a class in favor of common ownership — they built welfare states instead of abolishing capital, and in recent decades working-class voters in Britain, America, and France have increasingly backed parties skeptical of redistribution, while salaried professionals have moved left. On her reading, the passage shows that Mill naively assumed material interest would translate mechanically into class-based voting, and that he would have to concede the mistake. My counterclaim is that the passage already contains its own correction: if those who live on weekly wages truly have the greatest stake in the country's prosperity precisely because their daily bread depends on it, then Mill has himself supplied the reason why electoral ascendancy would produce gradualism rather than expropriation, and he would read both the welfare-state settlement and the recent inversion of class voting as vindications of his analysis, not embarrassments. Which of us is reading him correctly? Please adjudicate by reconstructing how Mill, reasoning from this passage and from his late views on socialism more broadly, would explain why the ballot box did not deliver socialism, what he would make of working-class voters who now distrust redistributive politics, and whether he would stand by this passage's expectations or revise them. I would like your answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion; the quotation above does not count toward the five.
