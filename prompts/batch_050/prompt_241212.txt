I'm in a reading group on twentieth-century philosophy of mind and we just got to Sellars. A friend who took a seminar on him last year tried to explain the myth of the given to me over coffee, and his version went roughly like this: "Sellars's argument in Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind is that the whole idea of the 'given' -- the idea that there's some raw experiential content that just shows up in consciousness and that we have incorrigible, non-inferential access to -- is a myth, because there's no such thing as raw experiential content at all. What empiricists called sense impressions are really just dispositions to make judgments, so seeing a red triangle is nothing over and above being inclined to say 'red triangle.' That's why Sellars is basically an eliminativist about qualia avant la lettre, and why his view collapses into Ryle-style behaviorism. The positive upshot is that all awareness is linguistic: if you don't have the words for something, you literally can't be aware of it, which is also his argument that animals and infants don't really have experiences, just stimulus-response patterns."

I've read maybe the first fifteen sections of the essay and this doesn't smell right to me -- I distinctly remember Sellars spending a lot of time on sheafs of impressions and inner episodes that don't seem like they could be mere dispositions to talk, and the guy I talked to has a habit of rounding everyone off to the nearest -ism. But I can't yet say exactly where he's misreading it versus just compressing it. Could you go through his summary claim by claim and tell me: what a careful Sellarsian would accept as a fair (if quick) gloss, what's a defensible simplification, and what's an actual misrepresentation of the position? In particular I'm confused about the relationship between the epistemological claim (nothing is given as a foundation for knowledge) and the apparent metaphysical claim (there's nothing non-conceptual in experience at all) -- my friend treats them as the same thing and I'm not sure Sellars does. Also, does the inference from "awareness requires concepts" to "infants and animals have no experiences" actually follow, and did Sellars draw it? Finally, if the behaviorism/eliminativism characterization is wrong, what is the actual positive account of inner episodes, roughly, so I don't walk into the reading group with my friend's version in my head?
