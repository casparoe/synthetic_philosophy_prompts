I help run a small grant-giving charity, and at our last board meeting we got into a fight about who should get a say in setting our funding priorities. One board member kept invoking what he called the principle that everyone affected by a decision should have a say in making it. In his mouth it did a lot of work: he used it to argue that grant applicants abroad should be represented in our priority-setting, that the general public shouldn't be, and -- this is where I got lost -- that donors whose money we haven't yet spent should count but past donors shouldn't. When I pressed him, he said I was conflating different versions of the principle, which struck me as a way of having it all ways at once.

I'd like a proper disambiguation of that slogan -- everyone affected by a decision should have a say in it. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. Some axes I suspect matter, but correct or extend this list: what 'affected' means (actually affected versus possibly affected versus having a stake or interest at risk, and whether being coerced or legally bound is different from merely being impacted); whether the principle distributes a veto, an equal vote, a weighted say proportional to how much one is affected, or just a right to be consulted; and whether it's meant as a criterion for drawing constituency boundaries at all, or only as a corrective within an already-fixed polity. For each reading, say whether it's trivially true, substantive but defensible, or implausible on reflection, and note what absurdities follow if you take it literally -- I've heard it complained that the strong version gives everyone on Earth a vote on everything, and I want to know which readings actually avoid that.

Most importantly, for each reading, tell me whether it would support the three specific uses my colleague made of it: including prospective foreign beneficiaries, excluding the unaffected public, and counting current but not past donors. I suspect his three applications only cohere if he slides between readings, and I'd like to be able to say exactly where the slide happens. Please indicate how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, whether that's a precisification you're not certain philosophers would endorse or a verdict that could go the other way.
