So Benjamin Franklin in his Autobiography describes his project of arriving at moral perfection: thirteen virtues, a little book with a grid of virtues against days of the week, focusing on one virtue per week while tallying his slips in all the others, and erasing the marks so he could reuse the pages.

Say he gets resurrected and someone hands him a smartphone loaded with the modern version of all this: habit trackers with streaks and confetti animations, an app that charges him five bucks every time he skips the gym, a watch that buzzes when he's been sitting too long, and quantified-self people posting their stats for their followers.

Would he see this stuff as his scheme finally perfected, or as a corruption of it? A few things to weigh: his method depended on erasing the record and just doing better next week, whereas streak apps make the unbroken record itself the thing people protect (folks jogging in place at 11:58pm to keep a streak alive). On the other hand he was a printer, an inventor, and a shameless self-promoter, and he only added Humility as virtue thirteen because a friend told him everyone found him proud -- so maybe he'd be first in line for the feedback loops and social features? And note how his own project actually ended: Order beat him, and he consoled himself with the anecdote of the man who got tired of grinding and decided a speckled axe was best after all. Would the older Franklin buy the premium subscription, or write a satirical letter mocking the whole buisness? Give me your best case for what his actual verdict would be, grounded in what he says about the project rather than the cartoon version of Franklin.
