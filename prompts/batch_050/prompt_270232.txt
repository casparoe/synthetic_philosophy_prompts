Two friends got into an argument after Nia read several detailed reviews of a film and told people it was visually magnificent, even though she hadn’t seen it. She said the critic had described its colors, editing, and compositions very specifically, and had an extremely reliable track record of predicting Nia’s own reactions.

Marta said Nia might know that she would probably enjoy the film, but she didn’t know that it was visually magnificent. Aesthetic judgment, she argued, requires acquaintance: testimony can prepare you to notice something, but it can’t replace actually perceiving it.

Nia answered that this was an artificial distinction. Her judgment wasn’t merely a prediction about pleasure; it was what she called an aesthetic proxy judgment -- a judgment that an attentive viewer with her background and sensibility would find the film magnificent. Given the critic’s proven reliability, she said, that proxy judgment could count as knowledge.

Marta replied that Nia was smuggling her own sensibility into the critic’s verdict. The critic’s testimony might justify confidence, but if Nia later watched the film and found its images cold or cluttered, her original claim would simply have been wrong.

Nia said Marta’s view had an odd consequence: if Nia watched the film and agreed, her judgment wouldn’t really change, even though it would suddenly become knowledge. Marta said that wasn’t odd at all -- that’s just the difference between reliable evidence and aesthetic acquaintance.

Who’s right at each step? Is Nia’s proxy judgment a genuine aesthetic judgment, a useful substitute for one, or just a prediction wearing fancier clothes?
