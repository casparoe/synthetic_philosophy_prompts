The debate over Frankfurt-style cases has been running for more than half a century, and from where I sit in our department's reading group it looks genuinely stuck rather than merely difficult. One side holds that a carefully constructed case -- an agent who acts on her own for her own reasons, while a counterfactual intervener stands ready to force her hand if she shows any sign of doing otherwise, and never needs to act -- shows that someone can be morally responsible for an action even though she could not have done otherwise, and hence that the principle of alternative possibilities is false. The other side keeps answering with moves that have been on the table for decades: that any sign the intervener would respond to constitutes a flicker of freedom, a residual alternative the agent retains; that the cases face a dilemma, since they either presuppose determinism and thereby beg the question against incompatibilist intuitions, or allow indeterminism and thereby leave alternatives open; and that whatever intuitive pull the cases have is outweighed by the theoretical costs of abandoning the principle. Each side has replies to the other's replies, and the last twenty years of literature look to me like increasingly elaborate case-engineering on one side met by increasingly fine-grained flicker-hunting on the other, with no convergence in sight.

I am not asking for a verdict on who is right. I want a diagnosis of why the debate has stalled and what kind of disagreement it actually is. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and nothing more, is that the impasse is not really about the cases at all but about the evidential standing of intuitions: one side treats the intuitive judgment about a well-told vignette as data that can overturn a general principle, while the other treats the principle as secure enough that any intuition seemingly conflicting with it must be explained away. An alternative hypothesis I have considered is that the dispute is covertly verbal -- that the two sides mean different things by 'could have done otherwise' and are arguing past each other.
