I’m teaching a 75-minute upper-level philosophy seminar on anthropic reasoning, with about 16 students. Design a classroom simulation that makes the difference between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption *happen to them*, rather than just having me explain the equations. I also want the activity to set up the “presumptuous philosopher” worry: the sense that SIA can make a theory seem supported just because it predicts lots more observers.

Here’s the half-formed mechanic I have in mind. Before each round, you secretly choose between two “universe protocols” that differ in how many observer-positions they create, then distribute sealed role cards or activate students’ phones accordingly. Some students may turn out to be “dormant” — physically present, but not observers in that round — so that we can simulate possible people without pretending they can act. Each active student gets partial evidence, has to state or bet on which protocol was chosen, and then we reveal the generating procedure. You should improve or replace this mechanic if it won’t cleanly distinguish SSA from SIA.

Please give me:

- exact setup, materials, and rules, including how to handle dormant students so they aren’t bored or accidentally leaking information;
- at least three rounds with genuinely different epistemic situations, not just the same coin flip with new labels;
- a points or token scheme that lets students bet under each assumption, preferably without real money and without rewarding confident guessing too much;
- one round where changing the relevant reference class changes the answer, and one round where conditioning on “I exist” versus “someone exactly like me exists” pulls apart;
- a way to make the presumptuous-philosopher reaction occur naturally, ideally with some students objecting that the betting rule is rewarding bloated hypotheses;
- a timeline and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto SSA, SIA, conditionalization, reference classes, and selection effects;
- what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run it, especially students confusing “I was selected” with “my existence is evidence”;
- an honest note about which feature of real anthropic reasoning the classroom setup cannot reproduce.

Keep it runnable with ordinary classroom stuff, and don’t turn it into a bibliography or survey of the literature. I want a game, not a lecture with dice sprinkled on top.
