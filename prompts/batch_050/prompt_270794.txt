So I run pre-retirement workshops at a community college (six sessions, couples sometimes come, mostly people 58-70, mixed incomes) and every cohort I end up improvising the same conversation about what aging actually takes from you and what it doesn't. A friend who's a classicist told me to stop improvising and just steal the structure from Cicero's De Senectute, where Cato lays out the four charges against old age -- it pulls you out of active work, it weakens the body, it strips away the pleasures, and it stands near death -- and answers each in turn. I read it and honestly the framework is better than most of what my field puts in worksheets. Near the end (Falconer translation, section 71) Cato says: "just as apples when they are green are with difficulty plucked from the tree, but when ripe and mellow fall of themselves, so, with the young, death comes as a result of force, while with the old it is the result of ripeness."

Here's what I want: turn Cicero's fourfold analysis, including this ripeness business, into an actual protocol I can run -- something like an ordered set of questions and exercises per session, where each of the four charges gets diagnosed in the client (which one is actually biting for *them*, they're never all equal) and then met with the Ciceronian counter, translated out of Roman-aristocrat idiom. For the ripeness passage specifically, I want the protocol to treat it as the piece of homework, not a greeting card: what would it concretely take for a client to experience their own decline as ripening rather than extinguishing, what practices in sessions 1-5 set that up, and what do you do with someone for whom that framing is just false -- say a client with a degenerative diagnosis where the fire really is being put out with water, so to speak. Also flag where Cicero's advice presumes you spent your youth cultivating your mind and friendships and so breaks down for clients who didn't (his line about the inner resources has to survive contact with someone whose whole identity was their job).

Do it as a session-by-session procedure with the decision points spelled out, then run it against three cases I'll see constantly: the 64-year-old divorced engineer whose life was his career and who treats retirement as a funeral, the 70-year-old in chronic pain who is cheerful and engaged but whose body makes half of Cato's reassurances ring hollow, and the recently widowed client who actually *agrees* with the ripeness idea in principle and uses it to skip grieving -- the case where, I suspect, your procedure will tell me to shut down a client who's saying all the right things, and I'd be tempted to let it slide. End with what the protocol can't do (depression screening, medical stuff, anything where Cicero is just wrong by modern lights) and where I have to excercise judgment no checklist will cover.
