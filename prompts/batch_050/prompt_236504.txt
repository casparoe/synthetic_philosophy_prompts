I work as the participation coordinator in the administration of a mid-sized European city of about 200,000 people. Since last year, our council has required that the citizen panels attached to the participatory budget and a handful of other programs be recruited by civic lottery rather than open signup — a decision forced by a public fight over whether the old panels were, as the local paper put it, the same forty hobbyists in different combinations. I convene eight to ten such panels a year. I have a modest stipend budget, roughly two staff-days per panel for designing the recruitment, no statistician on staff, and every design choice has to be defensible twice over: to the council, and to the 199,975 residents who were not drawn. A colleague with a classics background told me this was all worked out in Athens and pointed me to Aristotle's Politics, Book VI, chapter 2, in Benjamin Jowett's translation, where Aristotle lists the institutional marks of a democracy: "that the appointment to all offices, or to all but those which require experience and skill, should be made by lot; that no property qualification should be required for offices, or only a very low one; that a man should not hold the same office twice, or not often, or in the case of few except military offices: that the tenure of all offices, or of as many as possible, should be brief, that all men should sit in judgment, or that judges selected out of all should judge, in all matters, or in most and in the greatest and most important- such as the scrutiny of accounts, the constitution, and private contracts; that the assembly should be supreme over all causes, or at any rate over the most important, and the magistrates over none or only over a very few."

Turn this catalogue into a standing procedure I can follow each time a panel is convened — a sequence of tests and decisions in words, not an essay about the passage. Three translation problems matter most to me. First, what plays the role of the experience-and-skill exception in my setting: which of my bodies should be exempted from the lottery and constituted some other way, and by what test, so that the exception does not quietly swallow the rule — engineers will claim that everything is technical, and activists will claim that nothing is. Second, which of the other features survive translation into panel design and which become counterproductive: rotation and brief tenure look different when a panel needs six meetings merely to understand the sewage budget, and payment, which Aristotle treats as what keeps the poor eligible, interacts oddly with my stipend budget and with participants' tax situations. Third, where the procedure will predictably misfire: the lotteries Aristotle describes drew from a citizen body that could be compelled to serve, whereas my invitees can decline, and the people who decline differ systematically from those who accept, so the lottery I actually run is never quite the one the passage describes. Say honestly which of these mismatches can be repaired procedurally and which cannot.

Then apply the finished procedure to three panels I have coming up: a 25-seat budget panel drawn from all residents; a 9-seat standing committee that reviews engineers' reports for our flood-defense program; and a 30-seat youth climate panel drawn from residents aged 15 to 19, where parental consent and school schedules constrain everything. In at least one case, the procedure should deliver an answer I will not like — for instance, telling me that a body must not be lotteried at all, or that last year's outstanding panelist cannot simply be asked back. Mark the points where judgment cannot be proceduralized, and state plainly what the procedure cannot do. Begin directly with the procedure itself — the first sentence should state its standing rule — with no preamble and no restating of my question or the passage. Keep the whole response to roughly a thousand words.
