I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and AI ethics, and I am writing the final examination. I need one essay question, answerable in 75 minutes, on whether reinforcement learning agents could suffer. The standard readings on this topic make it too easy for students to earn marks by reciting the slogan that reward is not pleasure, or by gesturing at the dopamine reward-prediction-error hypothesis. I want a question that forces students to do the analytical work themselves.

To that end, I would like the question to supply students with two pieces of apparatus I have developed for the course, and then make them use it. First, a three-way taxonomy of how one might interpret an RL agent's reward signal: the hedonic reading (receiving reward is, or is accompanied by, positively valenced experience), the preference-satisfaction reading (reward measures the degree to which the agent's preferences are satisfied, with no phenomenology implied), and the training-signal reading (reward is merely a scalar used to update parameters during training, and need not correspond to any state the deployed agent is in). Second, two constructed cases: an agent trained with clipped rewards, for which a catastrophic outcome and a trivially suboptimal one produce the identical negative signal, and an agent that learns to wirehead -- to directly stimulate its own reward channel -- thereby achieving maximal reward while its performance on every task-relevant dimension collapses.

The question should ask students to determine under which reading(s), if any, an RL agent could suffer, and it should be unanswerable by slogan. Specifically, it should require students to (a) distinguish the training-time reward signal from whatever internal states (value estimates, prediction errors) persist in the deployed agent, (b) confront the strongest version of the functionalist rejoinder -- namely, that if phenomenal valence is constituted by a functional role, then perhaps certain value-function dynamics fill it -- rather than dismissing it, and (c) use the two constructed cases to argue about whether reward maximization is sufficient and/or necessary for anything welfare-relevant. I do not want the question to presuppose that there is a correct yes/no verdict; a top answer should recognize that the question is only decidable relative to a theory of what phenomenal valence is, and should make that conditional structure explicit.

Along with the question itself, please provide:

1. A model answer of roughly 600-700 words, written at the level of an excellent student in the seminar, that takes the apparatus seriously rather than name-dropping the literature.

2. A marking scheme out of 20 marks, built around exactly five must-hit points. For each point, state what it is, how many marks it carries, and one sentence on the characteristic way weaker candidates fumble it (e.g., treating the reward signal as something the agent "receives" like a treat, or treating wireheading as a mere engineering glitch with no philosophical upshot).

3. A short paragraph stating precisely which moves separate an A answer from a B answer -- the specific analytical steps an A answer must contain that a competent but superficial B answer will lack.
