I work in administration at a regional hospital network, and over the past year I've fallen down a rabbit hole trying to make sense of statistics where a conclusion flips depending on how you group the data. I've read enough to know this phenomenon has a name, but what I can't find is guidance on what I'm actually supposed to *do* when I encounter it. So let me give you my verdicts on the cases I've been chewing over, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies them. First case: a new surgical technique outperforms the standard one among both mild cases and severe cases taken separately, yet looks worse overall because surgeons reserved it mostly for severe cases; here I'm confident the split numbers are the honest ones and the pooled figure is a mirage. Second case: two baseball players where one has the higher batting average in every single season but the lower average over their combined careers, because at-bats were distributed so unevenly; here, oddly, my verdict flips, since if I'm hiring a hitter for next year I want the one with the better career numbers, and the season-by-season breakdown feels like pedantry. Third case: our network's flagship hospital shows worse survival rates than the rural affiliates overall, but better rates within every patient-risk category, because it takes the hardest cases; my verdict is that the flagship is genuinely the better hospital, matching my first verdict. Fourth case: a supplement appears to help within every age bracket tested, but the brackets each contain only a handful of people, and I suspect the whole thing is noise; my verdict is that here I'd rather pool everything, which seems to contradict my first and third verdicts, since I'm now choosing the aggregate over the split. Fifth case: a study finds that within every income group, people who carry a certain gene variant earn slightly less, yet the variant-holders earn more overall; this one just leaves me paralyzed, and I can't even say which number I'd trust.

What I want is a principle that tells me, before I look at which answer I'd prefer, whether the aggregated or the disaggregated figure is the one that deserves my confidence. My hunch is that the right principle has something to do with whether the grouping variable is *upstream* of the outcome in some causal sense, but that hunch struggles with my baseball verdict, where the grouping seems causally relevant and yet I still want the aggregate, and with my supplement verdict, where the reason to pool feels purely statistical rather than causal. So: is there a single coherent principle behind my verdicts, or a small family of them? Please state the principle as precisely as you can, show me which of my five verdicts it vindicates and which it forces me to abandon, and tell me what it would say about cases I haven't considered -- for instance, when the grouping variable is something the subjects chose themselves, like which hospital to attend, rather than something fixed about them. If the honest answer is that my baseball intuition simply has to go, I'd rather hear that than receive a principle twisted out of shape to save it.
