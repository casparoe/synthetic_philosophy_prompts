I've been reading rationalist blogs for well over a decade, and I've recently ended up administering a small private research fund — call it three postdoc-years spread over three years — aimed at the philosophy of complicity: what it is to participate in wrongdoing that one neither caused nor could have prevented. The end goal is a working account of when ordinary participation in large-scale harms makes someone blameworthy, with payoff for practical questions people actually face (consumption choices, employment, investing, voting) rather than purely theoretical ones. I've been drafting the research agenda using the importance-tractability-neglectedness heuristic, but I'm not confident I'm applying it to the right questions, and I'd like the prioritization itself stress-tested.

My current list of candidate open problems:

1. The inefficacy problem. When an individual's contribution to a collective harm is imperceptible or fully offset by others' behavior — one purchase of factory-farmed meat, one vote for a bad policy — does complicity attach anyway, and if so, in virtue of what: expected effects, participation in a joint action, sharing an intention?
2. The overdetermination problem. How to assign blame when the harm would have occurred identically without any given participant, so that no one is a but-for cause of anything.
3. Institutional complicity. Whether merely occupying a role — middle manager, pension-fund beneficiary, employee at a company with harmful externalities — makes one complicit in what the institution does, absent any endorsing intention.
4. Retrospective complicity. Whether benefiting from past wrongdoing (inherited wealth, infrastructure built by exploited labor) is a form of complicity at all, or a distinct moral category that has been wrongly filed under the same heading.
5. Complicity through inattention. Cases where the agent could trivially have known what they were participating in and didn't look — whether willful ignorance is a mode of participation or a separate failing that complicity theory shouldn't absorb.
6. The exit-option problem. Whether the availability and cost of exit (quitting, divesting, emigrating) determines complicity, or merely modulates its degree.

Three things I want from you. First, a dependency analysis: which of these questions constrain the answers to which others. My working suspicion is that 1 and 2 are secretly the same question about the metaphysics of contribution, and that 3 through 6 are all downstream of it, but I may have the graph wrong. Second, an honest ITN assessment: which items are neglected because they're hard rather than unimportant, which are tractable with existing tools (formal models of collective action, doctrines of accomplice liability from law), and which require new conceptual machinery that three postdoc-years plausibly can't produce. Third, what would change your ranking — a specific finding, distinction, or result that would reorder the list.

After the analysis, give me a structured summary as a table with these columns: Question (number and short name); Priority rank (1 through 6); Importance (high/medium/low with a one-sentence justification); Tractability in three postdoc-years (high/medium/low); Neglectedness (high/medium/low); Upstream of (which other question numbers depend on it); Key crux (the single consideration that most determines where it lands); and What would demote it (the finding that would most reduce its priority).
