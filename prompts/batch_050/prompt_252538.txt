The audience I'm writing for is fluent in the usual discourse about whether large language models understand anything, so I don't want a piece that rehashes the stochastic parrots exchange; I want to make a stronger and stranger claim. Below is the current draft, and after it I've listed the kinds of comments I want. Please be adversarial rather than encouraging — I'm past the point where reassurance helps, and I'd rather have the piece's weaknesses identified now than in the comment section.

The draft, working title "The Severed Signal":

Every few months the discourse about large language models replays the same exchange. One side says the models are statistical mimics that produce the form of language without any of the understanding behind it. The other side points to emergent capabilities and replies that the distinction between real understanding and a sufficiently good simulation of it may not survive scrutiny. Both sides, I want to argue, accept a premise they should reject: that there is a fact of the matter here, waiting to be discovered, and that the disagreement is about evidence. I think the disagreement is about a concept that has outlived the conditions it was built for, and the cleanest way to see this is through Dennett's notion of a strange inversion of reasoning.

Dennett's canonical examples are Darwin and Turing. Before Darwin, it seemed obvious that the exquisite competence on display in the living world — the eye, the immune system, the spider's web — had to be the product of comprehension: a designing intelligence that understood what it was making. Darwin inverted the order. Competence can arise from a mindless process of variation and selection, with no comprehender anywhere in the causal chain; comprehension, where it exists at all, is a late product of such processes, not their source. Before Turing, it seemed obvious that arithmetical competence required a calculating mind that grasped what the symbols meant. Turing inverted the order again: a mechanism shuffling symbols according to rules it does not understand can compute anything computable. The general lesson is that comprehension is the effect of competence rather than its cause, and that the mind's self-image as the source of its own achievements is systematically misleading.

Large language models, I claim, force a third inversion, and this one cuts in a different place. The earlier inversions relocated comprehension inside a naturalist story: it exists, but downstream. The current case attacks the concept itself. Our ordinary notion of understanding was reverse-engineered in a world containing exactly one kind of fluent language user. Fluent, responsive, context-sensitive speech was a perfectly reliable signal of a comprehending mind behind it, so the concept could afford to be built on that correlation; it never had to specify what it would say about a case where the correlation broke. That case has now arrived. Asking whether a model really understands is like asking whether a document is genuine in a world where the watermark that used to define genuineness has become trivially reproducible. There is no further fact to find; there is a decision to make about how to extend the concept, and different extensions will serve different purposes.

This is not the deflationary claim that the question is merely verbal — or rather, it is that claim, but defended against the usual sneer. Questions about how to extend concepts are settled by what we need the concepts for, and here the stakes are real. If understanding is what licenses trust, the operative question becomes which competences we are prepared to rely on in the absence of the signal that used to vouch for them. Natural selection faced a version of this problem: it built organisms that perform competently in domains they comprehend not at all, and it got away with it because environments were stable enough to keep competence tracking the things a comprehender would track. Our environment is not stable, and the correlation itself is now what is in doubt.

I expect the standard objections and will gesture at them briefly. The Chinese Room is an intuition pump whose force depends on smuggling in the speed and integration of the whole system while inviting us to judge the homunculus. The octopus thought experiment shows that a model trained on text alone lacks direct causal contact with the referents of its words, but indirect grounding — text produced by embodied agents whose words were grounded — may be grounding enough for much of what we care about; the cable from the island to the mainland is long, but not necessarily cut. Embodiment matters for some competences and I concede those. None of this touches the central claim, which is neither that the models understand nor that they don't, but that the question as usually posed has stopped having a determinate answer, and that the policy question — where in the stack is comprehension actually load-bearing? — is the one worth our time.

End of draft. Here is the feedback I want:

1. Accuracy of the framing. Is my rendering of Darwin's and Turing's inversions faithful to Dennett's actual argument, or have I distorted it? In particular, I have a nagging worry that Dennett himself would reject my conclusion — that he takes understanding to be real, gradable, and attributable in degrees to systems with no inner comprehender, which would make my "the concept no longer applies determinately" move a departure from him rather than an extension of him. Tell me whether that worry is well founded and how damaging it is.

2. The central move. The essay goes from "the concept of understanding was built on a correlation that has now broken" to "the question has no determinate answer." Is that inference defended anywhere in the piece, or only asserted? What would the strongest opposing position look like — the view that there remains a substantive fact about whether these systems understand, independent of our classificatory convenience?

3. The "third inversion" label. Darwin's and Turing's inversions are claims about causal order — competence preceding comprehension. Mine looks more like a claim about conceptual indeterminacy. Am I trading on the label while making a different kind of claim, and if so, should I rename the move or restrict the thesis?
