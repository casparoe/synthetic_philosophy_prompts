I'm in an undergraduate philosophy of language seminar, and this week we read Grice's Logic and Conversation. I was sick for the discussion section, so a friend sent me the companion notes from a philosophy podcast she swears by, and I've been working through them instead. The notes summarize Grice as follows: "Grice's big idea was that communication runs on cooperation. Speakers and hearers operate under a Cooperative Principle — make your contribution fit the purposes of the exchange — cashed out in four maxims: give enough information but not too much (quantity), don't say what you believe is false or baseless (quality), be relevant (relation), and be clear and orderly (manner). When a speaker breaks one of these rules, the hearer notices and figures out what the speaker really meant; that extra content is a conversational implicature. So implicatures are essentially a speaker's hidden meanings — anything they intend you to pick up beyond their literal words, the way a hint or an insinuation works — and in fact everything an utterance conveys beyond its literal content counts as one. The maxims themselves are rules of good conversational manners: violating them is akin to lying or being rude, which is why irony works the way it does — saying 'lovely weather' during a storm is a small lie that the hearer forgives because they recognize the intention behind it. Grice's ultimate conclusion was that semantics and pragmatics collapse into one another: what a sentence means is nothing over and above what speakers standardly use it to communicate, so there is no such thing as context-independent sentence meaning."

Something about the ending bothers me, because our textbook presents Grice as someone who wanted to keep semantics spare and push the extra explanatory work onto pragmatics — which sounds like the opposite of a collapse — but the podcast is generally reliable and I can't tell whether it's just compressing for a general audience or genuinely getting him wrong.
