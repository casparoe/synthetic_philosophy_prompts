At a department dinner last month, a colleague who does business ethics and one who does development economics got into an argument about a case I made up, and neither would budge. I would like you to stage that argument properly, as a dialogue in which both characters are equally sharp and actually answer each other's points rather than re-asserting their opening positions. Here is the case. A multinational clothing company is choosing where to build its next factory. In Region A, outside options are decent, so to attract workers it would have to pay wages near the local norm and would improve its employees' lives only modestly. In Region B, the realistic alternative for most workers is subsistence farming or worse, so the company can hire at extremely low wages -- and it chooses B precisely because the desperation there makes labor cheap. Thousands queue for the jobs, everyone hired is made substantially better off relative to their actual alternatives, and all consent freely and with full information about conditions. The first character holds that the arrangement in B is a paradigm of wrongful exploitation: the company takes unfair advantage of the workers' vulnerability by capturing nearly all the surplus the transaction creates, and the fact that the workers consent and benefit shows that exploitation need not involve harm or coercion. The second character holds that a transaction cannot be wrongfully exploitative when it leaves both parties better off and nobody is deceived or forced; if the wage is allegedly "unfair," she will demand to know what non-arbitrary standard fixes the fair one, and she will insist that the real complaint concerns the background injustice that made the workers desperate -- an injustice this company did not cause and would only make worse by staying away.

Give the dialogue a second act with a genuine complication: the company's wage budget for the factory is fixed, so a philanthropically minded manager proposes paying each worker a much higher, "fairer" wage, which necessarily means hiring far fewer of the people in the queue. The characters should fight over whether excluding more of the desperate in order to pay the lucky ones better is a moral improvement, a moral wash, or worse than the status quo -- and over what that implication does to each of their original positions. Keep the exchange concrete throughout: they should press each other on actual cases, on who bears responsibility for background conditions, on whether consent is doing moral work here or merely recording the absence of alternatives, and on whether the first character's fairness standard collapses into intuition and the second character's better-off test proves too much.

Finally, do not let the dialogue end in a polite stalemate or an agreement to disagree. The exchange must reach a definite outcome: either one character concedes a real point and the position that survives is stated clearly at the end, or the two genuinely converge on what feature of the case determines its permissibility. Then append a brief author's note, outside the dialogue, committing to a verdict: state which side the exchange has shown to be stronger, name the single consideration that did the decisive work, and say candidly what the strongest remaining objection to your verdict is.
