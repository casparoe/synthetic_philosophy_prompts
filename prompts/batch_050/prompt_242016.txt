Write a short story of roughly 1,200–1,600 words that dramatizes a result from decision theory sometimes called the value-of-information theorem: for an ideally rational Bayesian agent facing a decision, acquiring free information beforehand can never make things worse in expectation, since the agent can always disregard what it learns, and it can sometimes make things strictly better — so such an agent should always accept free information, however unwelcome it might seem.

Your protagonist is a retired actuary in a small coastal town who has organized her entire life around this principle and defends it with genuine rigor. The town council is offered, at no monetary cost, a geological survey that would settle whether the harbor — the town's only economic asset — rests on a slowly destabilizing fault. Nothing could be done with the answer: there is no money to relocate the harbor, reinforce it, or insure against its loss, and no buyer would pay more for the town's fishing fleet on the strength of a clean report. The council votes to decline the survey without looking at it. She alone argues they should look.

Build the story around the following requirements. First, include one scene in which the protagonist states the theorem's logic precisely but in plain speech — a listener with no mathematics should be able to follow why "we can always ignore it" does all the work in the argument, and why an expected-utility maximizer cannot be made worse off by knowledge. Second, without resorting to narration that lectures, let the events of the story exhibit at least two distinct ways the theorem's premises fail for actual people: for instance, that real agents are not indifferent to what they believe independently of what they decide, that deliberation itself is costly, that information alters how people treat one another rather than only what choices they make, or that humans do not update like Bayesians once they know. Third, give at least one council member a serious, non-strawman reason for declining that the protagonist cannot dismiss as mere squeamishness. Fourth, end the story without settling whether looking would have been right, and without letting the protagonist recant her principle or be vindicated by a convenient disaster or a clean report.

The story should read as fiction first — with a concrete setting, a small cast of named characters, and at least one scene in which the conflict surfaces through action rather than argument — but a reader familiar with the theorem should recognize that its logical structure, and not just its flavor, is what is at stake in every scene.
