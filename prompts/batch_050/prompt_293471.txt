Last spring I spent four months appealing a denied insurance claim, and somewhere around the third rejection something shifted for me: every appeal I filed was assessed under the very clauses whose application I was disputing, so no improvement in my arguments inside those clauses could possibly have helped. The live question was about the rulebook, not within it. I had known for years, as a slogan, the distinction between working inside a system and stepping outside to reason about the system as an object; the appeal made me feel it. I have now been asked to give a single ninety-minute session at a local high-school math circle — students of fourteen to sixteen, no logic assumed beyond ordinary school algebra — and I want to build the session on exactly that distinction, using Hofstadter's MU-puzzle as the engine: you begin with the string MI and may append a U to any string ending in I, double whatever follows the M, replace any occurrence of III with U, or delete any occurrence of UU, and the challenge is to produce MU. It cannot be done, because the number of I's in any reachable string is never divisible by three while MU contains none; but that fact is invisible from inside the rules and appears only when you stop deriving strings and start asking what all derivable strings have in common. Reproducing that moment — the felt abandonment of pushing symbols in favor of asking what every producible string shares — is the entire point of the session.

What I want is the plan, not the lesson itself. Specifically: the order in which the components must come and what each depends on, in particular what has to be in place about the difference between a derivation and a proof about derivability before the meta-move is even intelligible; whether the MU-puzzle should carry the session or whether you would commit to a different vehicle — I have considered a chess position and a deliberately unfair bureaucratic rulebook, and I suspect both smuggle in meanings that a semantically dead string system is free of, but I want you to decide, and to say what the carrying case must have and must lack; how long to let the students try and fail to derive MU before intervening at all, and what that failure accomplishes that no explanation could; the misconceptions they will predictably bring or form — I anticipate at least four: that jumping out of the system means breaking or changing its rules, that the invariant argument is merely a cleverer derivation, that our collective failure to find MU shows only that nobody was clever enough rather than that no derivation exists, and that the take-home is a glib exhortation to think outside the box — with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than left to harden; the end-of-session check that would show the distinction has landed rather than been recited (my candidate is a small invented system with different rules, where they must prove a named target string unreachable, but tell me if that tests the wrong thing); and what to leave out at this level — Gödel numbering, formal undecidability, self-reference, my own insurance story — including a ruling on whether the personal anecdote should open the session, close it, or be cut.

Commit, please: one ordering, one carrying example, one duration for the failure phase, one placement for the anecdote, each with reasons, rather than a menu of options left for me to choose among. Where you judge a call to be genuinely close, choose anyway and say what evidence about the students would change your mind. And mark the point in your own plan where you are least confident it will survive contact with real fourteen-year-olds, so that I know where to improvise.
