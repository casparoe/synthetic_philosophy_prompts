Act as a political philosopher who specialises in the ethics of migration and is comfortable with the basics of international refugee law. I want a rigorous audit, not a sermon: steelman the position before you criticise it, and be willing to concede whatever the analogy genuinely establishes.

The context. I sit on a community group responding to a proposal that our local authority expand its participation in a refugee resettlement scheme. At a public meeting, an articulate opponent of the proposal reached for Garrett Hardin's lifeboat analogy, quoting the following passage from his essay "Lifeboat Ethics: the Case Against Helping the Poor" (Psychology Today, September 1974):

> Suppose we decide to preserve our small safety factor and admit no more to the lifeboat. Our survival is then possible although we shall have to be constantly on guard against boarding parties. While this last solution clearly offers the only means of our survival, it is morally abhorrent to many people. Some say they feel guilty about their good luck. My reply is simple: "Get out and yield your place to others." This may solve the problem of the guilt-ridden person's conscience, but it does not change the ethics of the lifeboat. The needy person to whom the guilt-ridden person yields his place will not himself feel guilty about his good luck. If he did, he would not climb aboard. The net result of conscience-stricken people giving up their unjustly held seats is the elimination of that sort of conscience from the lifeboat.

From this passage the speaker drew three conclusions: first, that admitting further refugees when resources are strained is not generosity but recklessness, on a par with removing a ship's safety margin; second, that guilt about our relative prosperity is not merely useless but self-defeating, since it selects for people willing to exploit it; and third, that strict border enforcement is simply what lifeboat discipline looks like applied to a nation.

Your task is to audit the analogy as this speaker is using it. Before writing your answer, brainstorm at least eight candidate correspondences and disanalogies (list them briefly), then build the audit around the ones that actually bear on the three conclusions. In particular, address:

1. The correspondence. What exactly maps onto what: the fixed capacity of the hull, the "safety factor", the water, the people already aboard, the helmsman's authority, the act of hauling someone in versus barring them? Where does the mapping depend on treating a country's capacity as fixed in the way a lifeboat's is, when housing stock, labour demand and fiscal capacity are partly products of policy and population themselves?
2. Rescue versus membership. Real maritime practice imposes a duty to rescue persons in distress at sea regardless of capacity concerns; and refugee law distinguishes granting membership (resettlement, naturalisation) from non-refoulement — not returning someone who has already arrived to persecution. Does the lifeboat picture systematically conflate picking a drowning person out of the water with offering them a permanent berth, and does the conflation matter for the speaker's first and third conclusions?
3. Selection versus arrival. Hardin's dilemma is choosing which of a hundred swimmers to admit; asylum seekers largely present themselves. Does the analogy covertly recast spontaneous arrival as a queue-jumping selection problem, and with what effect?
4. The conscience argument. Is the claim that guilty people vacating their seats merely eliminates conscience from the boat a moral argument at all, or a causal prediction dressed as one? Even if true, what would it show — that the seats are justly held, or only that guilt is an unstable motive? Can it carry the speaker's second conclusion?
5. The port from aid to asylum. Hardin wrote the essay principally against food aid and open immigration, on Malthusian grounds about population growth. The speaker has ported it to refugee protection, where the numbers, the legal obligations and the causal stories (including who is responsible for the conditions people flee) differ. Identify which features of the original case survive the port and which were load-bearing only in the original.
6. The verdict. State which of the three conclusions, if any, the analogy can still support once the disanalogies are on the table — distinguishing carefully between a conclusion it refutes, a conclusion it merely fails to establish, and a weaker conclusion in the vicinity that survives (for instance, that pacing and integration capacity are legitimate policy variables even if refusal is not). If a better analogy exists for the speaker's underlying worry, propose one and say what it captures that the lifeboat does not.
