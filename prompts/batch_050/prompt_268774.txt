So I left a PR with zero comments on some gnarly code and a senior dev shot back "write it for your future self." Then the same week a productivity podcast told me to skip my third coffee because I should "be kind to my future self," and a guy on twitter used the same phrase to argue that not saving for retirement is basically stealing. It's clearly doing a LOT of work for one slogan.

I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I want to know what "be kind to your future self" / "do it for your future self" could actually be claiming when you make it precise. Lay it out as a menu of readings, from the most boring to the most wild, and for each one say whether it's plausible and whether it would actually justify the way people use it. Some candidate directions I can half-see but can't pin down:

- The trivial reading: it's just "actions have later consequences, plan ahead," in which case why does everyone say it like it's deep?
- Something about how I'm irrational: apparently economists say people discount the future "hyperbolically" rather than at a steady rate, so I'd take $50 now over $100 in a year but $100 in 13 months over $50 in 12 months, which is the same choice with both dates shifted. Is the slogan claiming that pattern is a bug and the kind thing is to weigh future-me's interests equally with present-me's? Who decided equal weighting is the rational default?
- Something about identity: the phrase literally says my future self is a *self*, like a separate person I'm being mean to. Is there a serious view where future-me is genuinely someone else (the way I'm not the same codebase after enough refactors), and does that make skipping the gym a moral wrong against another person rather than just a bad deal for me?
- Something about time: does the slogan quietly assume future-me already exists somewhere out there in time waiting to recieve the kindness, vs. the view that only the present is real and future-me is just a useful fiction? Does that difference change whether the slogan makes sense?
- The enforcement angle: if future-me is going to betray present-me's plans anyway (buy the coffee, skip the run), is the slogan really advice to tie my own hands now — and is that kindness to future-me or to present-me?

For each reading: state the precise claim, whether it's trivial/defensible/broken, and which of my three encounters (code comments, coffee, retirement) it would actually back up. I suspect the retirement guy is smuggling in the strongest reading and the code comment only needs the weakest one, but tell me if I have that backwards.
