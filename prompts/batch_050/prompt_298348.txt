You are a professional philosopher of mathematics with particular expertise in the epistemology of proof. Think carefully before you write: begin by informally brainstorming the strongest considerations on both sides, then settle on the most defensible version of the position, and only then compose the argument.

Here is the background for your task. A long-standing dispute holds that a mathematical proof yields genuine knowledge only if it is surveyable — that is, only if a human mathematician could in principle work through the whole argument, check each step, and grasp how the conclusion follows. On this view, computer-assisted proofs whose verification rests on billions of case checks no human could ever review (as with certain famous results in graph theory and sphere packing) do not deliver knowledge in the traditional mathematical sense; at best they deliver something closer to well-confirmed empirical belief, since our confidence rests partly on contingent facts about hardware, compilers, and the diligence of the programmers involved. Opponents reply that this proves too much, since human mathematicians are also fallible physical systems relying on memory, notation, and the testimony of referees.

Now consider a hypothetical development that sharpens the dispute. Suppose proof engineers adopt the following architecture, which we will call a verification cascade. The massive case enumeration at the base of a computer-assisted proof is checked not by trusting the original program, but by a second, much smaller and simpler verifier, written independently. That verifier's correctness is in turn established by a third, still simpler program, and so on, until the chain bottoms out in a tiny "kernel" — say, two hundred lines of code implementing only basic logical rules — whose correctness a competent logician genuinely can survey in an afternoon, step by step. Advocates of the cascade claim that whatever surveyability demands, the cascade satisfies it: the unsurveyable bulk has been converted, link by link, into something surveyable at the foundation. Skeptics claim the cascade merely relocates the problem: the links between levels are themselves theorems about the behavior of physical machines, and no cascade can turn an empirical warrant into a mathematical one.

Your task: argue that the verification cascade succeeds — that is, argue that a computer-assisted proof verified through such a cascade confers the same kind of justification, and the same kind of knowledge, as a traditional surveyable proof, so that the unsurveyability objection fails. In developing your argument, address at least the following:

1. Precisely what surveyability was supposed to secure (reliability? understanding? apriority? the social transmission of mathematical knowledge?), and whether the cascade secures each of these or only some of them.
2. The objection that each link in the cascade rests on empirical premises about hardware and software, so that the whole chain inherits an empirical, a posteriori character that traditional proof lacks.
3. The objection that human-surveyed proofs are also mediated by fallible physical processes, and whether this comparison actually helps your case or instead collapses the distinction you need.

Steelman the strongest version of each objection before responding to it. Do not cite or quote specific authors, papers, or historical episodes; conduct the argument entirely at the level of principle, using the terms introduced above. Aim for roughly 700 to 1000 words, written as a continuous piece of philosophical argumentation rather than a bulleted list.
