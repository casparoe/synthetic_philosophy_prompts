I took a philosophy of physics seminar last term and we've basically circled the same question for weeks now, so I want to try asking it as a meta-question instead. The question is whether identical quantum particles — two electrons in an entangled singlet state, say — violate Leibniz's principle that numerically distinct things must differ in some property. On one side you have people who point at the formalism: the two electrons share all their monadic properties and you can't even say which is which, so either the principle fails or the particles aren't really two individuals. On the other side people say they ARE discernible after all, just weakly — each stands to the other in the irreflexive relation of having opposite spin, and that's enough to ground numerical difference. Then a third group replies that this cheapens everything: any two distinct things trivially stand in irreflexive relations to each other (being at some distance from the other, whatever), so if that's all it takes the principle is unfalsifiable and toothless, and maybe the whole framework of treating particles as individuals with identity facts is the mistake. What bugs me is that this exchange has been going for like twenty years and every move on both sides seems well-rehearsed and nobody concedes anything.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the real disagreement isn't about physics or even about identity but about what the principle of identity of indiscernibles is FOR — whether it's a metaphysical discovery about reality that formalisms can confirm or refute, or a methodological constraint that was never testable to begin with — and downstream of that, about when a feature of the formalism (like an antisymmetric state) deserves to be read as a feature of the world rather than an artifact of how we describe it. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there actually a hidden substantive disagreement underneath — about what counts as a genuine relation, or about haecceitism, or something else? Tell me what kind of dispute this really is, using what each side actually argues, and what would even count as progress here: a sharper distinction, a new formal result, or is the honest answer that nothing could settle it?

One more thing: please be careful to separate what's genuinely settled among people who work on this (e.g. the math, what the standard positions say) from what's contested, and don't present one side's framing as the consensus if it isn't.
