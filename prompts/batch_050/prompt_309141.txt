I sit on the grants committee of a private medical research foundation, and we have a proposal on the table that has split us down the middle. A team led by a respectable epidemiologist -- no religious affiliation, a solid publication record -- is requesting substantial funding for an eighteen-month study of reported recoveries associated with a mountain shrine. Their file contains roughly ninety sworn attestations gathered over four decades, of which twelve are backed by before-and-after medical records, and two treating physicians are willing to testify that their patients' recoveries defied prognosis. Several of the documented cases involve diagnoses that are ordinarily fatal. The applicants do not claim anything supernatural occurred; they argue only that the dossier is anomalous enough to warrant serious prospective investigation, and that ignoring it out of disciplinary embarrassment would be a failure of scientific curiosity.

The disagreement on the committee is, at bottom, about Hume's old argument that testimony can establish a miraculous event only when the testimony's being false would be a greater miracle than the event itself. Half of us think this settles the matter: spontaneous remissions are rare but known, the shrine keeps its own registry and solicits the donations that registry helps attract, the attestations come overwhelmingly from pilgrims rather than from disinterested parties, and comparably impressive registries exist at shrines of entirely rival traditions. The other half replies that Hume's maxim, applied consistently, proves too much: it would have licensed dismissing early reports of stones falling from the sky as peasant superstition, and it treats the accumulated testimony of honest strangers as worthless whenever the reported event is improbable enough, which seems to confuse the improbability of an event with the impossibility of learning about it. Complicating matters, our foundation's charter permits funding an anomalous claim only when the committee judges that the evidence's being misleading or erroneous is less probable than the claim's being true -- a clause that essentially codifies Hume's maxim into our governing documents, so we cannot simply set it aside. Meanwhile our staff statistician has circulated a proposal to score each documented case by the likelihood that such a record would exist at all given fraud, error, or ordinary remission, versus given a genuinely unexplained recovery, and to fund only if the aggregate score clears a fixed threshold.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. It should recommend one of approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and it must do three things. First, interpret the charter clause for this case: does a Humean weighing even apply when the reported events are not strict violations of natural law but low-base-rate events retroactively selected into a registry that records successes and is silent about the far larger number of pilgrims who recovered nothing or died? Second, give the strongest available version of the losing side's position, whether that is the physicians' records and the charge of question-begging dismissiveness, or the charter's plain text and the selection effects in the dossier, rather than a strawman. Third, state conditions, if any, in terms the applicants could actually respond to -- for instance, whether funding a prospective, registry-free surveillance protocol would satisfy the charter where the retrospective attestation review does not. The memo will be circulated before Thursday's vote and should be usable as the committee's standing guidance for the extraordinary-claims proposals we receive every year or two.
