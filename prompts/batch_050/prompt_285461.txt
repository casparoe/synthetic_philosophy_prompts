I am a junior serving on my high school's student-faculty Conduct Review Board, and we have been asked to advise the principal on a proposal to replace our current honor-code system with a Civic Points system. Under the proposal, students would earn points for things like reporting cheating, volunteering for school service, and having a clean disciplinary record; points could be exchanged for privileges such as off-campus lunch, prom ticket discounts, and priority course registration. Points would be deducted for rule violations. Supporters say the system makes expectations clear and rewards good behavior instead of merely punishing bad behavior. Opponents say it pays students to be informants, cheapens honesty, and gives up on the idea that a school should help students become genuinely trustworthy people rather than careful point-maximizers.

The dispute reminds me of Han Fei's position in the Han Feizi. Paraphrasing rather than quoting him: Han Fei argues that a ruler governs best not by hoping subjects or ministers will become virtuous, but by establishing clear, public, impartially applied standards backed by predictable rewards and punishments — the so-called two handles — precisely because relying on rare moral exemplars is unreliable and governing through personal favor breeds arbitrariness and manipulation. A Confucian-style critic would answer that good order grows from cultivated character and modeled virtue, and that ruling by incentives produces subjects who comply only when watched and never develop real integrity.

Write a memo of roughly 700 to 900 words from me to the principal and the Board recommending whether the school should adopt, modify, or reject the Civic Points system. The memo should:

1. State the recommendation clearly at the top.
2. Explain how this case tests Han Fei's position: does our situation vindicate his claim that well-designed incentives outperform exhortations to virtue, or does it expose a limit in that claim?
3. Examine where the analogy between Han Fei's state and a high school holds and where it breaks down — consider the stakes involved, whether students can exit, the developmental purpose of a school, and who gets to write and revise the rules.
4. Address the strongest objection on the losing side honestly: if you recommend adopting or modifying the system, take seriously the worry that incentives can crowd out intrinsic honesty, reward gaming and surveillance, and turn classmates into monitors of one another; if you recommend rejecting it, take seriously Han Fei's worry that appeals to virtue are vague, unequally enforced, and dependent on the character of whoever happens to be in charge.
5. Consider a tension internal to the proposal itself: Legalist governance prizes uniform, impersonal rules, yet a school constantly exercises discretion and judgment in discipline. Say whether the points system resolves that tension or merely relocates it.
6. If recommending adoption or modification, specify at least two concrete design features that would make the system acceptable and explain the reasoning behind each; if recommending rejection, state the conditions under which a points-based system would become preferable to the honor code, and vice versa.
7. Note what observable evidence from a trial period would justify changing the recommendation.

Use standard memo format with headings. Do not include citations or a bibliography; reason from the paraphrased positions above and from the facts of the case.
