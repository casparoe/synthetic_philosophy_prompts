Write me a mock government pamphlet, the kind of glossy thing you'd grab at the DMV or get mailed with your voter registration, from an imaginary country that went full futarchy about a decade back. The setup: once per electoral cycle citizens vote on the official national welfare metric, and after that no legislature actually decides anything -- every policy question gets settled by conditional prediction markets estimating how each option would move the metric. The founders were big on that old slogan about voting on values and betting on beliefs, and it's printed in gold foil on the cover. Call it something like "So Your Favorite Policy Lost: A New Citizen's Guide to Life Under the Markets," maybe 500-600 words, with a perky FAQ section and a couple of little "did you know" sidebar boxes.

The voice is chipper, deadpan bureaucratic optimism, and the pamphlet should never once admit anything is wrong. But every reassuring answer should be one of the standard objections to governance by prediction markets turned inside out: who gets to pick the metric, and why voters are trusted to do that but not to pick policies; markets going thin and jumpy on long-horizon stuff like pensions or coastal defenses; the awkward fact that wealthy traders can lean on prices; the metric getting gamed a little more everytime as everyone optimizes for its most measurable bits; and my personal favorite, the fact that bets on policies that were never adopted never actually resolve, so the markets enjoy a literally perfect track record on every road not taken. The humor has to come from the pamphlet explaining all of this earnestly and accurately, as features -- a reader who knows the territory should feel each critique landing without the pamphlet ever winking or naming a problem. No real people, countries, or companies; if you want a pull-quote, invent a proponent like the Minister of Aggregate Foresight.
