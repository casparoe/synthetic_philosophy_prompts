I'm putting together a unit for a freshman seminar on the meaning of life, and I want to pair Kurosawa's Ikiru with some of the contemporary literature, but I'm worried my own reading of the film is too lazy. The standard take is: Watanabe finds out he's dying of stomach cancer, flails around for meaning (family, pleasure, work), finally pours himself into getting a little playground built, and dies content on a swing in the snow. Everyone files it under "create something beyond yourself and you beat death," and I think that's too easy given how the film is actually put together.

Here's what I want unpacked. The back half of the movie is Watanabe's wake, where his coworkers piece together his last months through flashbacks and gradually talk themselves into (and out of) a story about what he did and why. Read that structure against the debate between two families of views: on one side, meaning-in-life views like Susan Wolf's, where meaning is a matter of a person being actively gripped by projects of objective worth, moment by moment; on the other, narrative or "shape of a life" views (Velleman is the obvious name), where the meaning of a life depends on its overall arc, so that a late redemption can retroactively change what the earlier decades of drudgery were. Does Ikiru side with the second view -- the thirty dead years in the records department get redeemed by the last five months -- or does it undercut both, since the only people narrating Watanabe's life are coworkers who keep getting the story wrong, and Watanabe himself is no longer around to confirm any of it? And where does Camus-style absurdity fit: Watanabe never gets a cosmic answer, the bureaucracy undoes his lesson almost immediately, and yet the film clearly doesn't treat his project as futile the way the absurd hero is supposed to embrace futility. I'd also like you to do something with the song he sings twice, once as a young man's cliché in a bar and once at the playground, since the same words seem to mean different things depending on where they fall in the life.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: roughly one on the narrative-shape question, one on who gets to author the meaning of a life, one on the absurdist angle, one on the song, and one on where the film resists whichever reading you ended up giving.
