So I'm writing a paper for my political philosphy seminar arguing that Plato is basically the grandfather of epistocracy -- you know, the modern view (Brennan-style) that political power should be restricted to people who actually know what they're doing, instead of being distributed to everyone equally by default. My plan was to lean on Republic Book VIII, where he trashes democracy. But I actually sat down and read Book VIII closely for the first time and this bit near the start (around 544d-545a) threw me off:

"Are you aware, then," said I, "that there must be as many types of character among men as there are forms of government? Or do you suppose that constitutions spring from the proverbial oak or rock and not from the characters of the citizens, which, as it were, by their momentum and weight in the scales draw other things after them?" "They could not possibly come from any other source," he said.

Here's what's bugging me. If constitutions just ARE the citizens' souls writ large, dragged around by them like weights on a scale, then that seems to cause real trouble for the epistocrat reading of Plato. The modern epistocrat's proposal is basically institutional: keep the population as it is, but reroute decision-making power toward the competent. But this passage seems to say you can't do that -- if the people's character is democratic, the regime gets pulled toward democracy no matter what institutional machinery you bolt on top. So is Plato actually making a much weirder and stronger claim than the epistocrats, namely that the ONLY way to get good rule is to change what people are like (education, censorship of poetry, the whole eugenics-adjacent guardians program), and that tinkering with who holds power is pointless otherwise?

A few specific things I'd like help with: (1) What is the scales metaphor actually doing -- is it a causal claim (character causes regime) or more like a constitutive claim (regime just is character at a bigger scale), and does it matter which? (2) Is Glaucon's instant agreement ("they could not possibly come from any other source") suspicious, like is Plato smuggling in the controversial premise by making it sound obvious? (3) Does this passage actually undercut my paper's thesis, or can the epistocrat reply -- e.g. by saying character is itself partly shaped by institutions, so the causation runs both ways, and Plato's own programme assumes that? Feel free to tell me if I'm misreading the passage entirely.
