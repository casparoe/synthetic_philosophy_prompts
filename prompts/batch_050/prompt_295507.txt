I’m designing teaching materials for a course on Indian epistemology and want a piece that makes the pramāṇas memorable without reducing them to a vocabulary list. Write a deadpan parody FAQ, approximately 650–800 words, issued by the fictional “Municipal Department of Valid Cognition” in a city where every significant assertion must be classified under the classical Nyāya fourfold scheme: pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (analogical identification), and śabda (testimony from a reliable source). The FAQ should answer residents’ questions about everyday disputes—for example, whether there is fire on the hill because smoke is visible, whether a damp pavement proves that it rained, whether an unfamiliar animal can be identified by comparison, and whether a neighbor’s report about a closed bridge counts as knowledge. Let the bureaucracy be officious, but let the underlying epistemology be basically sound: inference should involve an observed sign, a remembered relation of invariable concomitance or vyāpti, and awareness of possible defeaters; testimony should depend on the speaker’s competence and sincerity rather than on mere confidence; analogy should not be treated as just any loose resemblance.

The satire should primarily target institutions that convert fallible ways of knowing into rubber stamps, certificates, and procedural boxes, while also exposing the complications that arise when Nyāya categories meet ordinary life. Include numbered questions and answers, at least one rejected application form, and an official warning about counterfeit or misapplied pramāṇas. The jokes should arise from the technical machinery itself rather than from portraying the department as stupid: for instance, it might object that a resident has mistaken a simile for upamāna, inferred rain from a sign that also accompanies street-cleaning, or attempted to strengthen testimony with an embossed seal. Keep the irony legible without having the narrator openly condemn the system, and make the piece intelligible to a reader who knows only the short definitions supplied above, while rewarding someone familiar with concepts such as pakṣa, hetu, sādhya, and defective inference. Do not turn it into an essay, invent historical quotations, or claim that Nyāya philosophers endorsed municipal licensing.

After the FAQ, add a separate “Note on epistemic liberties” of about 100 words. In it, distinguish the settled core you relied on from points where the piece simplifies or compresses a more complicated history or interpretation; in particular, do not present disputes among Indian schools about the number or nature of pramāṇas as if Nyāya’s fourfold classification were universally accepted.
