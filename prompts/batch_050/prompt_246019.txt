So I write literary fiction — mostly multi-POV stuff, ensemble casts, that kind of thing — and I keep getting into this arguement with a friend who claims the moral logic of my books is basically utilitarian. That sent me down a rabbit hole about the famous separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, the one associated with Rawls: the complaint that when you sum everyone's happiness and trade one person's misery off against another's bigger gain, you're treating distinct lives as if they were one big life, ignoring that the gains and losses land in different people. I gather Parfit has a reply where he argues the boundaries between persons matter less than common sense assumes.

Here's what I'd actually find useful. My native tongue is craft: focalization, point of view, free indirect style, head-hopping, whose scene it is, ensemble structure vs a single protagonist's arc, stakes. Restate the whole debate in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can — the utilitarian aggregation picture, the separateness objection, and Parfit's reply if it fits at all. And keep the translation honest: don't sneak craft assumptions in and pretend they were already part of the philosophy.

Then, the part I care most about — the residue report. What translates cleanly into narrative terms? What only fits if you bend it? And what has no expression in craft vocabulary at all? For the stuff that doesn't translate, I want your verdict on whether that's because the novelist's toolkit is missing something real, or because the philosophers were pointing at something vaguer than it looked.

One thing I keep circling on my own: in a novel there genuinely is one experiencer — the reader — who really does get to live everybody's joys and miseries added together across the whole cast. Does that mean fiction is secretly on the utilitarian's side, or does it show exactly why aggregation only makes sense where a single subject exists to receive the sum?
