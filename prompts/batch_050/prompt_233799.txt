Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether large electorates are actually good at finding the truth. She kept treating the Condorcet jury theorem as if it proves that a big majority will probably get things right whenever the average voter is more likely right than wrong. I think that slides over a bunch of assumptions: independence, sincere voting, binary choices, fixed information, and the difference between a theorem about arbitrarily large groups and a claim about any real electorate. We definately did not settle it.

Please write a decision tree for a graduate political-theory seminar that sorts possible views on what the jury theorem actually licenses. Start from the rough thought that majority judgments can be evidence about some factual matter, and end at a position about when majority rule has epistemic value.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names. Use 7–9 branching questions, each phrased concretely enough that someone could answer without already knowing where it leads. The branches need to distinguish at least:

- whether the issue is each voter’s competence, average competence, or the competence of the pivotal voter;
- binary choices versus choices among three or more options;
- independence conditional on the truth versus voters merely being unconditionally similar;
- sincere voting versus strategic voting;
- fixed private information versus information shaped by deliberation, media, party cues, or social networks;
- asymptotic reliability versus reliability in a finite electorate;
- tracking a common fact versus aggregating conflicting values or preferences;
- whether competence must be above one-half in every environment or only in the environments that actually matter.

Leaves should include compact descriptive labels for the main positions, such as the classical jury theorem, a heterogeneous-competence version, finite but non-asymptotic reliability, correlated-error failure, strategic information aggregation, deliberative dependence, and exits into preference aggregation or judgment-aggregation problems. If some useful combination of answers has no plausible occupied position, include it as an unoccupied leaf and say briefly whether it seems incoherent, merely unattractive, or underexplored. Flag any leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate.

At the end, include a short answer-pattern table so a student can trace their answers to a leaf without reading the whole tree. Also clearly separate what is mathematically settled about the theorem from what is empirically or philosophically contested when people apply it to democracy. Don’t make a contested interpretation sound like the consensus.
