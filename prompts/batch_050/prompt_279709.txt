"This argument is stronger than it first appears, though it ultimately fails.

Most wild animals are r-selected, meaning they produce huge numbers of offspring, nearly all of whom die young from starvation, predation, or exposure. Since the overwhelming majority of these animals die before reaching maturity, it follows that most wild lives contain more suffering than happiness. A world with fewer wild animals is therefore plausibly a world with less suffering.

The animals directly killed by destruction (bulldozers, fires, displacement) are often treated as the main harm, but this is a one-time cost. If the habitat stays destroyed, say it's turned into a parking lot, the animals are never replaced, so all the suffering those animals and their descendants would have experienced is permanently prevented. On a utilitarian view where we sum total welfare, that looks like a net gain over time.

The real problem is that habitat loss doesn't actually reduce the number of animals in the long run. The survivors get crowded into whatever habitat remains until the population rebounds to the same density it was at before. So the same amount of suffering continues as before, plus the extra deaths during the transition, which makes destruction strictly worse than leaving the habitat alone."
