I serve on the five-person board of a small nonprofit that runs transitional housing for people fleeing abusive partners and stalkers, and we have a policy question coming to a vote next month that I would like help thinking through. For years our staff's informal practice, when someone calls or comes to the door asking whether a particular person is staying with us, has been to say that we can neither confirm nor deny anyone's residency. Recently, though, two of our front-desk volunteers admitted that when a caller strikes them as genuinely menacing, they simply say that no one by that name stays here, because a flat denial ends the conversation faster and they believe a hedged refusal tells a determined abuser exactly what he wants to know. One board member wants to formalize this: authorize staff to issue outright false denials at their discretion. Another objects that an organization which lies, even to bad actors, corrodes the trust of residents, donors, and partner agencies, and that we should hold the line at refusals to answer. A third proposes a middle route where only the director may deny, after some threat assessment. I have been asked to draft the written opinion we will circulate before the meeting.

Some context on where I am coming from: I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for over a decade, and I keep returning to the old discussions of Kant and the murderer at the door -- Kant's late exchange with Benjamin Constant, where he insists one may not lie even to a would-be killer asking after his victim, and the long tradition of responses treating that as the reductio of his ethics. I have never been satisfied with either side as usually presented. The naive consequentialist answer (obviously lie, who cares about the murderer's epistemic state) ignores that deception is a policy with equilibrium effects: if abusers learn that shelters always deny, denials stop being informative but so do truthful answers, and meanwhile our credibility with police, hospitals, and caseworkers is spent down. The absolutist answer ignores that a selectively applied refusal-to-confirm is itself a signal, and arguably a more precise one than a lie, since it is only deployed when there is something to hide. What I want is reasoning that treats the choice among these policies as a decision about what information our institution emits under each scenario, not just a verdict on one hypothetical doorstep.

Please write the opinion I will circulate: a recommendation of one concrete policy -- which of the three options (or a clearly specified alternative) we should adopt, including who may say what and under what conditions -- with the reasoning laid out in a form the board can actually vote on and dissenters can respond to. It should engage the strongest version of the losing side honestly, including the Kantian worry that institutionalized lying corrupts the conditions of trust that make our other work possible, and also the safety-first worry that philosophical scruples are being purchased with residents' security. Please consider second-order effects explicitly: what each policy lets a sophisticated caller infer, what it costs us when the truth later comes out, and whether any policy can be kept secret from the people it is meant to deceive. Aim for something in the range of 500 to 800 words. Format-wise, write it as continuous prose the way one would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and open with the bottom-line recommendation itself before working through the argument.
