At a department happy hour, two grad students I know got into a surprisingly heated arguement about mind uploading by gradual brain replacement, and I stayed out of it because I genuinely couldn’t tell who had the better of it.

The imagined procedure is this: over several months, each biological neuron is paired with a synthetic neuron that receives the same inputs and produces the same outputs. For a week, the synthetic neuron runs alongside the original while remaining “slaved” to it, so the person has one continuous stream of thought and behavior. Then the biological neuron is switched off and removed, leaving the synthetic one in control. By the end, the person has remained awake and apparently unchanged the whole time, but every neuron has been replaced.

Avery says the person obviously survives. There is no interruption of consciousness, no sudden copying event, and every later stage develops directly out of the immediately preceding one. If replacing one neuron doesn’t kill you, and replacing two doesn’t, it’s arbitrary to say that repeating the process eventually does.

Blake says the smoothness is exactly what hides the problem. During the overlap period, the synthetic system could in principle be detached and connected to a second body instead of replacing the original. So the procedure describes a way of producing a psychologically continuous duplicate while the original is gradually destroyed. Calling it “survival” just because only one successor is left running assumes the very thing at issue.

Avery answers that the detachment scenario is irrelevant. In the actual procedure, there is only one integrated system throughout, and the synthetic components are not functioning as a separate person merely because they could have been used to make one. Otherwise, every redundant circuit would secretly count as another mind.

Blake thinks that move is too fast. If the biological neurons remain active during the overlap, then there really are two physical processes with the same pattern, even if their outputs are temporarily merged. At the moment a biological neuron is switched off, why isn’t that the original stream of consciousness being extinguished, while the already-running synthetic stream continues as a replacement?

Avery then shifts ground: personal identity isn’t a matter of which physical material carries the process, but of non-branching psychological and causal continuity. So if the procedure accidentally creates two continuers, neither is uniquely identical to the original; but in the normal non-branching version, the single continuer is the person.

Blake objects that this makes identity depend on an extrinsic fact. Whether you survive an operation shouldn’t depend on whether technicians elsewhere happened to build a second continuer from the same information. If Avery’s theory has that consequence, Blake says, that’s a reason to reject the theory rather than accept branching.

Finally, Avery argues that Blake’s standard is impossible to satisfy: biological matter is constantly being metabolized and replaced anyway, so if continuity of matter were required, ordinary survival would already be dubious. Blake replies that gradual metabolic turnover may preserve the same ongoing biological process, whereas substituting functionally identical parts might preserve only the pattern. Saying “same pattern is all that matters” is a substantive theory, not something the procedure can demonstrate.

Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move. For each side’s main claim, say whether it is strong, weak, or question-begging, and explain why. Distinguish at least three issues they sometimes blur together: (1) whether a person survives, (2) whether phenomenal consciousness continues without a gap, and (3) whether undergoing the procedure would be rational. Also say whether the possibility of branching is a genuine objection to the non-branching case, and end with an overall verdict about who is more nearly right -- or whether the dispute turns on an unresolved ambiguity in “replacement.”
