I'm assembling the statement of positions I distribute to my graduate seminar on practical reason each year, and before I circulate it this time I want it stress-tested. Here is my view on incommensurable values and hard choices, stated as five theses:

(a) Incommensurability is real: for some pairs of options -- a career in law versus a career in music, say, or loyalty to a friend against a significant gain in overall welfare -- neither option is better than the other, nor are they exactly equal in value.

(b) Hard choices are nonetheless rationally navigable. Where values are incommensurable, choosing either option can be rationally permissible, and this permissibility is grounded in reasons rather than being a license to pick arbitrarily; "just pick" is not what rationality says here.

(c) Once one has chosen rationally in such a case, regret is never fitting. Since the rejected option was not better, there is nothing one had most reason to do that one failed to do, and pining after the foregone alternative is a confusion to be talked out of, not a rational response to be honored.

(d) In genuinely tragic moral dilemmas, remorse and moral residue are real and fitting even when one did exactly what one ought: the parent who must choose which child to save acts rightly and yet rightly carries the weight of the child not saved.

(e) Spectrum arguments against incommensurability fail: adding a small improvement to one of two incommensurable options does not thereby make it determinately better, so one cannot grind incommensurability away by increments.

Audit these as a set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and how severe is each? Which apparent tensions dissolve once the relevant distinctions are drawn? For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest revision and what it costs, and then tell me which single change would do the most to make the set cohere. I do not know where the problem is, if there is one, and I have no preference among the theses, so do not flatter any of them. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
