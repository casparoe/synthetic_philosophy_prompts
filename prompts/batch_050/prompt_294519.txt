At a dinner party last weekend, a friend of mine who teaches high school history gave me his summary of the dispute between Mozi and the Confucians over impartial care, and I want to know whether I can trust it before I repeat any of it in a lecture of my own. His version went roughly like this: Mozi taught that we should care for everyone equally, without regard to kinship or distance, because he diagnosed the warfare and cruelty of his era as flowing entirely from partiality -- people favoring their own families, villages, and states over outsiders. If everyone could be brought to care for other people's parents and other people's states exactly as they care for their own, the root cause of conflict would be removed and order would follow. The Confucians, and Mencius in particular, replied that this is psychologically impossible: human affection naturally begins at home and radiates outward in ever-weaker circles, so demanding equal love for strangers and kin is like demanding that a river run uphill. Mencius also pressed a moral objection, arguing that treating one's father as no different from anyone else's father amounts to a denial of fatherhood itself, and he was harsh enough about this that he grouped Mozi together with the egoist Yang Zhu as representing the two great threats to civilized values. My friend concluded that, at bottom, the whole dispute is a disagreement about human psychology -- the Mohists believed our feelings can be retrained by argument and discipline, while the Confucians believed they cannot -- and that modern psychology has effectively settled it in the Confucians' favor.

Something about that concluding move bothers me, though I can't articulate why. I have a vague sense that Mozi's case for impartial care was not primarily a claim about what people can feel, and that the Confucian response was not merely a claim about what people do feel, but I don't know the primary material well enough to say where the summary goes wrong. Please audit it for me: which parts would a scholar of early Chinese thought accept as accurate, which parts are defensible simplifications, and which parts conflate or distort the actual positions? In particular, I'd like to know whether framing the debate as a dispute about psychological malleability is fair to both sides, what a Mohist would say in response to the objection that impartial care is unnatural, and whether the Confucian alternative is simply a description of how affection happens to work or something more than that. Finally, does my friend's verdict -- that the Confucians essentially won -- follow from the accurate parts of his summary, or does it depend on the distortions?
