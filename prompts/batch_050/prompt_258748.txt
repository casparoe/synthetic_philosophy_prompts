Got a philosophy of math challenge for you. Construct a thought experiment in which the question "is 2 an element of 4?" has to actually get answered — real practical stakes ride on the answer, not just philosophers bickering at a conference. Constraints: no aliens, gods, or oracle machines with platonic vision; the scenario itself in two or three sentences; and the case has to be built so that someone working with the von Neumann construction of the naturals says yes (since 4 = {0, 1, 2, 3}), someone working with the Zermelo construction says no, and an ante rem structuralist says the question is ill-posed rather than true or false. The tricky part is making the stakes genuine without anyone in the story simply stipulating which reduction is the real one — if your scenario only works because a character declares some textbook official, that's cheating. What the case is for: I want to show students why some philosophers conclude numbers can't just be sets. So after the scenario, show the work: the verdict each of the three views gives and why, which feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting (I suspect it's whatever forces set-membership talk about numbers to matter in the first place), what happens if you remove that feature, and how a commited set-theoretic platonist would try to wriggle out of the whole thing. Give me the answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each (item 2 can do one sentence per theory), with no intro or conclusion.
