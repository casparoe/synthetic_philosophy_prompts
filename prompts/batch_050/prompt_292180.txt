I am an economist by training, fluent in the vocabulary of contract theory and mechanism design — principals and agents, moral hazard and adverse selection, hold-up problems, commitment devices, incentive compatibility — but I have only a layman's knowledge of political theory. I want to understand the classical republican case against standing armies and in favor of citizen militias: the line of thought running from Machiavelli through the English commonwealth tradition to Rousseau, and still institutionally alive in places like Switzerland, where defense rests on a conscript militia and armed neutrality rather than a large professional force.

Translate that position into the vocabulary I know, as precisely as you can. I expect parts of it to go smoothly: the standing army looks like an agent whose interests diverge from the principal's in an unusually dangerous way; a coup looks like a hold-up problem in which the agent happens to control the decisive asset; the militia looks like an arrangement in which principal and agent are the same people, so the incentive problem dissolves rather than gets solved. Push the translation as far as it honestly goes, and include the standard efficiency rejoinder — division of labor, comparative advantage, soldiering as firm-specific human capital that amateurs cannot replicate — together with the republican replies to it, translated on the same terms.

Then give me the residue report. Which republican claims map cleanly onto contract-theoretic notions, and does anything is gained by the mapping — does, say, the fear of Caesarism become sharper or duller once stated as a commitment problem? Which claims translate only with distortion? I suspect the civic-virtue material belongs here, since virtue is not obviously a preference an economist can take as given without begging the question, nor an incentive without circularity. And which claims have no expression at all in the framework — the suggestion, if it is one, that bearing arms partly constitutes citizenship rather than merely protecting it, or that military service forms the very character it presupposes? For each failure, say whether it looks like a limitation of the economic vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was doing rhetorical work that survives badly under precision.

Two constraints. First, do not smuggle: if you find yourself assuming that preferences are fixed, that the state can be modeled as a single principal, or that security is a well-defined output the principal wants maximized, flag the assumption explicitly rather than building it silently into the translation. Second, close with your honest judgment about which side of the residue is more interesting — what contract theory cannot say about the citizen-soldier, or what the republican tradition was never really saying in the first place.
