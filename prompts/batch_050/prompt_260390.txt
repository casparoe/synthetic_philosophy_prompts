Here's a distinction I want a case built around. An approval-directed agent just does whatever it expects its overseer will rate most highly; a goal-directed agent does whatever best achieves some objective that's been written down in advance. The standard worry about approval-direction is that it rewards looking good over being good -- sycophancy, hiding problems, managing impressions. I want the reverse case, which is much harder to construct: a scenario where the approval-directed agent ends up doing better *by the overseer's own lights* than the goal-directed one, and where this doesn't come from any of the usual cheats. Constraints: an ordinary workplace, not a robot lab; the written objective has to be genuinely well-crafted, the kind of spec a smart manager would actually write, not a straw man with an obvious loophole; and the overseer can't be gullible or inattentive, so the approval-directed agent has to win honestly rather than by performing. Two or three paragraphs for the scenario. Then show me the verdicts -- what each agent actually does and why that follows from its disposition rather than from either of them being dumb. Finally: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the case if you remove it, and the best countermove available to a defender of goal-direction -- is it just "write a better goal," and if so, does that move collapse the whole distinction between the two kinds of agent?
