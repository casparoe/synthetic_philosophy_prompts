I am a retired physicist who has taken up philosophy in earnest only since leaving the laboratory, and I attend a small reading group that has lately been working through G. E. Moore's ethics -- in particular his claims that the appreciation of beauty and the pleasures of personal affection are among the greatest intrinsic goods, and that the value of a whole is not necessarily the sum of the values of its parts. Last week two members of the group fell into a dispute I could not settle, and I should like an adjudication.

The case under discussion was this. A man has cherished a painting for twenty years, believing it to be the work of a particular seventeenth-century Dutch painter. He has spent countless hours admiring it -- the handling of light, the restraint of the palette, what he took to be the quiet mastery of a specific historical hand. It is now established beyond doubt to be a modern forgery. Nothing about the canvas or its visible surface has changed. The question posed to the group was whether he has lost a good he thought he had, and whether the enjoyment of those twenty years was less valuable than he supposed.

Clare argued as follows. The aesthetic good, on Moore's own account, lies in the conscious appreciation of beautiful qualities, and the qualities he actually perceived -- the light, the composition, the surface -- were genuinely before him and genuinely responded to. His experiences were what they were; no discovery about provenance can retrograde their value. What he has lost is only something extrinsic: a belief about authorship, a resale price, a story to tell guests. Had he died without learning the truth, nothing of value would have been lost, and it is perverse to hold that acquiring a true belief can destroy past goods.

Martin replied that this ignores the organic character of Moore's valuable wholes. What the man appreciated was never merely paint; his state of mind included cognition of, and admiration for, qualities the object does not in fact possess -- its age, its place in a tradition, the achievement of a particular painter three centuries dead. Where the cognition built into an appreciation is false, the whole comprising consciousness plus object is not the whole the appreciator took it to be, and its value is accordingly less -- or so Martin took Moore's own treatment of such cases to imply. The man did not merely suffer a new disappointment; he discovered that part of what he had been admiring was never there to be admired.

Clare answered that this proves too much. All enjoyment is accompanied by beliefs, many of them false. If I delight in a friend's wit while wrongly believing she understood my joke, is the evening's pleasure diminished? The beauty response fastens on sensible qualities, which the forgery genuinely has; the rest is biography and market gossip. Martin conceded that beliefs merely accompanying appreciation are one thing, but insisted that beliefs internal to it -- constitutive of what is being admired -- are another, and that the distinction is one Clare herself relies on whenever she would call an emotion fitting or unfitting to its object. Clare's final move was to say that the deflation a person feels on learning of a forgery is disappointment about the future and about status, mistakenly projected backward as a revaluation of past experience, and that a feeling's being natural does not make it a measurement.

Two further complications were raised before the evening ended. Someone asked whether the same analysis applies to friendship: if one's closest friend of twenty years is revealed to have concealed something fundamental -- not a crime, simply a fabricated history -- is the good of that affection retroactively reduced in the same way Martin claims for the painting, or is affection for a person, as opposed to an object, differently structured? And someone else pressed Martin on whether, by his lights, the man's duty is now to feel less pleased when he looks at the painting, and whether a value theory that issues such instructions to the emotions is credible.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess Clare's and Martin's arguments as actually given, including the two complications at the end; say what Moore's framework -- intrinsic value, organic unities, the role of cognition within appreciation -- genuinely implies for the forgery case and for the friendship case, and where the framework underdetermines the answer; and conclude with a verdict on who is more nearly right overall, identifying whatever both parties have missed.
