I have been working through Harsanyi's aggregation theorem for an essay, and it strikes me as settling the long-running dispute between utilitarians and their egalitarian critics. As I understand it, the theorem shows that any social evaluation satisfying the Pareto principle and the axioms of expected utility theory must take the form of a weighted sum of individual utilities; and because the theorem treats all persons symmetrically, the weights must be equal, so that classical utilitarianism turns out to be not a controversial moral doctrine but a theorem of rationality. What puzzles me is that prioritarian and distributively egalitarian views continue to be defended as coherent alternatives, when they seem simply to reject a mathematical result. Are their proponents refusing to accept the theorem, or is there a gap in the reasoning as I have set it out? Please explain, in British English, where — if anywhere — my argument goes wrong.
