I'm trying to work out where the line falls between what a speaker says and what a speaker merely conveys, and I'd like to know whether my case judgments answer to any coherent principle. My verdicts:

1. A professor's entire letter of recommendation for a philosophy job consists of the observation that the candidate is punctual and has excellent handwriting. She never says the candidate is a poor philosopher, but I hold that she communicated exactly that and is answerable for it. I also hold she did not lie.

2. Someone who says "Some of the students passed" when in fact all of them did has spoken misleadingly but not falsely.

3. "Some of the students passed; indeed, all of them did" is awkward but not a contradiction — which I take to confirm that "not all" was never part of what was said in case 2.

4. But "She is poor but honest, though I don't mean to suggest any contrast between the two" does strike me as contradictory. The contrast carried by "but" cannot be shed the way the "not all" can.

5. The sarcastic "What a brilliant plan" is not a lie either: the speaker meant the opposite of the sentence's content and expected to be so understood.

6. A defendant asked where he was on the night of the crime answers "I was home," which is true only because he arrived home after the crime was committed. I judge that he lied — or near enough that the distinction shouldn't protect him.

The trouble is between 3 and 6: "I was home that night — at least from midnight onward" is no contradiction, so whatever test acquits the speaker in 2 and 3 seems to acquit the defendant too, and I don't want to acquit him. Is there a principled account of the said/implicated boundary that preserves all six verdicts, and if not, which one should I surrender? Then apply the resulting account to two cases I haven't listed: a résumé claiming "familiar with Python" from someone who once ran a tutorial script, and "I'll think about it" said to a salesperson by a customer both parties understand to be refusing.
