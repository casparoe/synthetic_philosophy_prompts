Christopher Nolan's film Memento follows Leonard Shelby, who after a head injury can no longer form new long-term memories and pursues the man he believes killed his wife by means of an external system: tattoos on his body, annotated Polaroid photographs, and notes addressed to his future self. Read the film through the philosophical lens of the self as a center of narrative gravity -- Dennett's proposal that a self is not an inner thing but an abstract point, the protagonist of the autobiographical story a brain tells about itself, much as a center of gravity is a useful fiction rather than an object in the box. I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the theory seriously and resists reducing either to the other.

Some questions to work through. First, establish the mapping and then test it: do Leonard's tattoos and Polaroids simply relocate to the outside of the skull the story-writing apparatus Dennett locates inside it, so that Leonard is the limit case in which the center of narrative gravity becomes literally visible? Or does something go wrong with that assimilation -- for instance, that ordinary autobiographical memory revises itself continuously and un-self-consciously, whereas Leonard's archive is frozen, queryable by strangers, and editable by anyone with a pen? Second, take seriously the film's most disturbing structural feature: that the author of Leonard's narrative and its protagonist are different temporal parts of him. The Leonard who writes a note deciding that Teddy will be his next target is deliberately authoring a story that his future self will read as discovered fact. Is this one center of narrative gravity maintained across time-slices by a relation of trust, or is it proof that there is no single center at all -- that Leonard is a succession of selves exploiting one another? Third, the Sammy Jankis subplot, the other amnesiac whose story may be Leonard's own disowned past projected outward: what does it do to the theory if a center of narrative gravity can be maintained precisely by fictionalizing parts of the self and expelling them?

Also bring in the contrast with the view associated with Galen Strawson, that some people experience themselves episodically rather than diachronically, with no felt continuity across time, and that narrativity is neither universal nor required for a good life. Leonard is in one sense the pure episodic being, and in another sense the most radically narrative being imaginable, since narration is the only glue he has. Does the film side with Dennett, with Strawson, or does it dramatize something both miss -- perhaps that a narrative self is not authored but held in custody, and that the interesting question is who can be trusted to hold it? Be honest about where the reading strains: the film arguably trades on an inner continuity -- Leonard's grief, his feel for his wife -- that no archive constitutes, and you should say whether that is a genuine problem for the functionalist reading or just sentimentality the film earns dishonestly. End with what, if anything, Memento adds to this debate that philosophical argument alone does not.
