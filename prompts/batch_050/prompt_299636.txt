I'm prepping a seminar on Kurosawa's Rashomon for my philosophy students and I want to read it through Jain anekāntavāda (the many-sidedness of reality) and syādvāda. Here's the thing I keep going back and forth on: at first glance the film seems like a perfect dramatization of anekāntavāda -- the bandit, the wife, the dead samurai, and the woodcutter each give an account that's true from where they stand but none of which adds up to the whole story. But the more I think about it the more the film seems to push toward something closer to plain skepticism or self-serving relativism, which is exactly what Jain philosophers insisted their doctrine was NOT. So: read the film against the actual doctrine. Which testimonies genuinely function as nayas (partial standpoints each capturing a real aspect), and where does the film slide into the "anything goes, nobody's wrong" position that anekāntavāda is supposed to rule out? Does the woodcutter's account play the role of a more complete synthesis, and if so does the film cheat by treating it as less interested than the others? And does the ending (the baby, the rain clearing) suggest the film thinks truth is still worth acting on even when it's many-sided? I want a reading that's faithful to both sides -- don't flatten the film into an illustration of the blind men and the elephant, and don't distort syādvāda into relativism to make the fit work. If there's a scene where the film genuinely resists the Jain reading, say so.

After the reading, give me a table with one row per testimony, columns: Witness | Standpoint/naya they speak from | Aspect of the truth their account captures | What it distorts or omits | Whether a Jain would grant it syāt-status (conditional truth) or reject it as mithyā (error).
