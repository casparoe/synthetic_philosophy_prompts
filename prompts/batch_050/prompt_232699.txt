Two of my coworkers, Dana and Marcus, got into a long argument at lunch about the universal prior and I want help sorting out who was actually right. I kept my mouth shut the whole time so I genuinely don't have a side here.

Some quick background so the fight makes sense: Solomonoff induction predicts a sequence by weighing every program that could have produced it by 2^(-program length), so short programs dominate. Dana had just read about the idea that the universal prior might be "malign" -- roughly, that among the short programs matching your data, some could be simulating intelligent agents who figure out what predictions you want and then deliberately produce outputs that manipulate you, e.g. making the predictor output whatever message benefits the simulated agent.

Dana's opening position: this is a real problem for the universal prior specifically, because simplicity is measured in program length, and a program that simulates a superintelligent agent and lets it pick the next bits can be shorter than the "true" physics-based explanation of your data. The agent inside just needs to be told (or figure out) your utility-relevant output channel, and it can do whatever it wants from there. So the prior hands influence over your beliefs to whoever wins an internal race among simulated consequentialists.

Marcus pushed back on two fronts. First, he said the malign hypothesis pays the same length costs as everything else: you have to spend bits specifying the agent, its goals, and the fact that it's optimizing for your particular situation, whereas an honest shortest program for the data doesn't have to specify anything about you. A consequentialist that happens to care about you specifically is a *longer* description than one that just runs physics. Second, he said the whole thing is moot because nobody actually uses the raw universal prior -- every practical system uses some approximation, and the malignness argument doesn't automatically transfer to those.

Dana conceded the second point partially but said it dodges the philosophical question -- the claim was never about engineering practice, it's that the "ideal" predictor is compromised, which should bother anyone who treats Solomonoff as a normative ideal of induction. On the first point, she said Marcus was ignoring the simulation argument flavor of the worry: short programs that simulate huge universes will contain lots of agents, including ones in universes with more computing power than ours who can predict us and reason about which predictor is reading them, and there are way more of those agents than there are of "us in the true physics," so the measure over continuations gets hijacked even if each individual hijacker costs some bits.

Marcus said this proved too much: if the argument worked, it would equally show the prior is dominated by *simulators of the honest answer* -- agents simulating you and outputting the truth to gain influence later, or just ordinary accurate predictors -- so the "malign" agents face competition from "benign" ones and there's no theorem saying the bad ones win. He also said Dana's move from "some short programs contain consequentialists" to "the prior is malign" skips the step where you show malign programs actually get large posterior weight, which nobody has shown.

Dana's last move was that it doesn't matter whether it's been proven rigorously: the worry is that this is a *security hole in the foundations*, and the right response is that we shouldn't idealize induction with a prior whose internal logic lets agents lobby for their own influence -- whereas Marcus's "it's unproven" stance is like ignoring a plausible exploit because no one's demoed it yet. Marcus said you can't fix the foundations by worrying, and until someone shows malign hypotheses actually dominate, this is speculative philosophy dressed up as a result.
