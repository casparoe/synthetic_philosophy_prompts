I've been reading about costly signaling and the handicap principle — Zahavi's idea that a signal is credible precisely because it's wasteful, so that only a high-quality bearer can afford the waste — and I want to test my intuitions about cases to see whether some single principle underlies them. Here are my verdicts:

A peacock's tail credibly signals genetic quality: yes, the classic case. A job candidate's degree from an elite university credibly signals diligence and intelligence even if the coursework itself is useless: yes, because a lazy or dull candidate couldn't have got through. A luxury watch signals wealth: yes, because someone poor who bought one would be ruining themselves for a lie. A friend who drives across town at 3 a.m. to help me move signals loyalty: yes, and here the cost is time and effort, not money. A company's expensive ad campaign with no informational content signals confidence in the product: yes, since a firm selling junk wouldn't recoup the expense through repeat business. An apology accompanied by costly restitution signals sincerity: yes; the same apology alone signals almost nothing.

But now the ones that trouble me. A man who burns a pile of cash on camera to show he's rich: this is just as costly as the watch, and arguably purer waste, yet I say it signals nothing but foolishness. A lavish, expensive wedding signals commitment: no — I don't think it does, even though it's exactly the kind of conspicuous expenditure that should qualify, and even though the watch and the ad campaign pass my test. A tattoo signals toughness: I find myself saying yes for the tattoo obtained decades ago when removal was impossible, but no today, when removal is available to anyone with money — though the pain cost is identical.

So my questions: Is there one principle here, or two? My instinct is that Zahavi's account — credibility through sheer waste — covers the peacock and maybe the ad campaign, but the 3 a.m. drive isn't waste at all; it's a cost that falls differently on the loyal and the disloyal. Does the theory reduce to differential costs rather than handicap waste, and if so, am I required to say the watch only signals because wealth makes its cost trivial — which seems to invert what Zahavi wanted? Second, can any version of the theory explain why burning cash fails while the watch succeeds, when both are pure monetary waste? My guess is that the watch is entangled with an independently intelligible activity (owning nice things) and burning cash isn't, but I can't state that precisely. Third, does my tattoo verdict commit me to saying that signals decay whenever faking them gets cheaper — and does the theory predict that, or merely permit it? Finally: which of my verdicts would a clean statement of the handicap principle force me to give up, and which would a differential-cost account force me to give up instead? I'd like the principle stated precisely, with the clause of it that each surviving verdict motivates, and an honest list of the verdicts that can't be saved.
