I spent 38 years as a hydrologist and retired last spring, which is how I ended up with time to read philosophy and also how I got into this mess.

I live in a small housing cooperative whose basement stores the residents’ archived records, including some old field notebooks I donated. During a heavy rainstorm, our main drain became blocked with sand left by a contractor. There is an emergency runoff gate that can prevent flooding, but it has to be opened manually, and it sticks unless someone who knows it opens it slowly. The caretaker who normally handles it was away.

At 6:10 that evening, the chair of the building commitee texted four of us saying that someone nearby should open the gate. There was no roster saying whose job it was. I live four minutes away and know the gate better than anyone, but I assumed one of the others would already be heading over, so I finished cooking and did nothing. Nobody opened it. By 8:30 the basement had flooded.

An engineer who inspected the site says the contractor’s sand was the primary problem, but that opening the emergency gate would very likely have prevented the damage. The board now wants me to make a formal apology and contribute $2,500 toward the insurance deductible. The chair, who sent the vague group text, is not being asked to contribute, and neither are the other three recipients. My instinct is that I never touched the drain, never promised to open the gate, and therefore did not cause the flood. But since retiring and reading more philosophy, I’m less confident that my instinct settles it.

Should I apologize and offer to pay some share? Please tell me who bears what kind of responsibility here and why, distinguishing what helped bring the flood about from who deserves blame. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
