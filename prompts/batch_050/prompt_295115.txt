I've been going around in circles on the epistemic akrasia / higher-order evidence literature for about a year and I want a diagnosis of why it won't resolve, not another survey. For background: the debate is whether rationality can require a "split" state where your first-order belief tracks the first-order evidence (believe p) while your belief about what you should believe tracks the higher-order evidence (the evidence says my epistemic peers mostly reject p, or a neuroscientist tells me my reasoning is compromised tonight). The level-splitters say yes, both beliefs can be rational at once; their opponents say the akratic state -- believing p while believing you shouldn't believe p -- is irrational by definition, so the higher-order evidence must drag your first-order belief along. Everyone knows the arguments by now: the self-defeat problem for conciliatory views (the Equal Weight View is itself peer-disputed, so by its own lights you should lower confidence in it), the spinelessness objection to always deferring, the misleading-evidence cases like the hypoxia and logic-doubt pills, the complaint that level-splitting fragments rationality into disconnected levels. Nobody has moved in years. So here's my question: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My own suspicion is that it's two different questions getting run together -- one about what the total evidence supports, one about what coherence among your own attitudes requires -- and the two sides just weight these differently, with nothing further to argue about. But maybe it's a real substantive fight about whether rationality is fundamentally about accuracy or about internal coherence. Or maybe there's a hidden empirical question about how reliable our judgments of our own reliability are, and no armchair argument can settle it. Diagnose it using what each side actually says, tell me whether my "two questions" hypothesis is right or too convenient, and then tell me what would count as progress -- a distinction nobody's drawn, a formal result, an experiment, or honestly just the admission that this is bedrock and the debate is over except for the shouting. If your answer is that the question itself is ill-posed, argue for that instead of hedging.
