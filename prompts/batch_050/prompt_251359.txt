I write a magazine column on the foundations of statistical reasoning, and for an upcoming feature I've been interviewing two philosophers who have been arguing, politely but relentlessly, about whether there is such a thing as a rationally required prior. One is a subjective Bayesian in the personalist tradition: any probability assignment satisfying the coherence axioms is rationally permissible, and rationality constrains only how you update, not where you start; demanding more, she says, smuggles in metaphysics under the guise of logic. The other is an objective Bayesian of the maximum-entropy school: given a set of background constraints, there is exactly one distribution a rational agent may adopt — the one that maximizes entropy subject to those constraints — and treating priors as a matter of taste is a failure of nerve, not a liberation from dogma. After months of my shuttling between their offices, they've agreed to co-author a short joint statement for the piece, and I've been tasked with drafting it. The draft must not adjudicate between them; it must be a document both would actually sign.

Structure the statement as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims — and push past trivialities: both should be able to sign, for instance, claims about what coherence does and does not guarantee, about what the classical paradoxes of indifference (the wine-and-water problem, Bertrand-style geometric paradoxes) actually show about the principle of indifference, about whether maxent is an inference rule, a consistency condition, or something else, and about what each camp's standard arguments (Dutch book, exchangeability, calibration, worst-case robustness) can and cannot establish. Second, a section stating the residual disagreements, each formulated as a single proposition one side affirms and the other denies, worded so that each camp accepts the characterization of its own position — no caricatures, no loading the dice with words like "arbitrary" or "dogmatic." Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them would force at least one side to revise. I suspect the deepest crux is whether reparameterization underdetermination of maxent is a refutation of objective Bayesianism or merely a technical problem about what counts as the correct variable to be ignorant over; another may be whether rationality is exhausted by internal consistency or includes truth-conduciveness. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or class of applications (physics, machine learning, scientific inference) that both parties accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case will show.

Finally, include a short closing section both can sign distinguishing genuine from verbal disagreement: where, if anywhere, are they arguing past each other — for example, over what "rational" or "prior" means in the first place — and what would count, for each of them, as a result that would make them abandon their position? Keep the whole statement under 900 words, in the measured register of a co-authored academic document, and resist the temptation to let one side get the last word.
