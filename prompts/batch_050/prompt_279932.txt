I am a graduate student trying to rescue an argument I formalised for a paper, and I am stuck: the conclusion strikes me as absurd, yet each step looked innocent when I wrote it down. The context is accuracy-first epistemology, the programme that tries to vindicate norms of rational credence from the premise that accuracy is the fundamental epistemic good for partial belief. Here is the argument.

(1) Accuracy — the closeness of a credence to the truth-value of its object — is the sole fundamental epistemic good for partial beliefs.
(2) If some alternative credence function c* is strictly more accurate than a credence function c at every possible world, then c is epistemically irrational. Accuracy dominance suffices for irrationality.
(3) Every credence function that violates the axioms of probability is accuracy-dominated by one that satisfies them. (This is the standard dominance theorem; I take it as established.)
(4) Therefore every epistemically rational credence function satisfies the probability axioms.
(5) Any function satisfying the probability axioms assigns credence 1 to every logical truth, credence 0 to every logical falsehood, and equal credences to logically equivalent propositions.
(6) Therefore epistemic rationality requires credence 1 in every theorem of pure mathematics, including theorems nobody has yet proved; it requires, for instance, that anyone in 1990 who had credences over arithmetic at all assign Fermat's Last Theorem the same credence as 2 + 2 = 4, namely 1.
(7) No human being meets this standard, none could, and someone who suspends judgement on an open conjecture is not thereby irrational; a purported norm that condemns states no finite agent could avoid is not a genuine norm.
(8) Therefore the accuracy-first package fails: either (1) is false or (2) is false.

Here is what I want from you.

First, a verdict. If the reasoning from (1)–(7) to (8) contains a step that does not follow, name it and explain why it looked valid. If the reasoning is valid, tell me which premise I should give up — the one whose rejection costs least overall — and explain why that premise looked so plausible.

Second, a cost accounting. I would like to keep (1) through (4) intact if at all possible, since my paper relies on the dominance argument for probabilism; but I want the price of that preference stated honestly. In particular, assess at least these three exits and say what each costs: (a) biting the bullet — accepting (6) as a correct statement of an ideal of rationality while denying that genuine epistemic ideals must be attainable, so that (7) overreaches; (b) restricting the framework — holding that dominance arguments bind only credences over an agenda of propositions the agent can grasp, so that unproved conjectures fall outside the norm's scope; (c) rejecting (2) in favour of a weaker bridge principle between accuracy and rationality, and saying what becomes of the argument for probabilism if that bridge is weakened.

Third, a specific worry about exit (b). A colleague proposed it to me but could not say whether an agent who understands an open conjecture — who can state it, deploy it in proofs, and bet on it — thereby counts as having it inside her agenda and is condemned for failing to see its truth. If understanding suffices for inclusion, exit (b) seems to collapse back into the bullet-biting of (a); if understanding does not suffice, then (b) owes me a principled boundary between propositions inside and outside an agent's agenda, and I cannot see where to draw it. Say which horn you take and why.
