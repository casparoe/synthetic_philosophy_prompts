I am a PhD student in philosophy serving as a TA for an upper-level undergraduate course on the ethics of death, and I am grading short-answer responses. The exam question was:

"State Epicurus's argument for the conclusion that death is not bad for the person who dies. Then present what you take to be the strongest objection to it and assess whether that objection succeeds."

Here is the student's answer:

Epicurus argues that death is not bad for us. His reasoning is that everything good or bad for a person consists in that person's experiences, in what they can feel or be aware of. Death is the complete absence of experience, so death itself contains nothing bad. Moreover, there is an asymmetry problem: while we exist, death is not present, and when death is present, we no longer exist. So there is no time at which death is bad for us and no subject for whom it could be bad. On this basis Epicurus also gives his famous symmetry argument: the eternity before our birth did not trouble us, so the eternity after our death should not trouble us either.

The strongest objection is the deprivation response associated with Thomas Nagel. On this view, death is bad because it deprives the person of goods they would otherwise have experienced: continued life would have contained pleasures, projects, and relationships. The badness of death lies in the loss of these future experiences, which the dead person can no longer have or anticipate.

This objection does not succeed, however. Nagel's account still requires the badness of death to consist in some experience, namely the experience of being deprived or of anticipating the loss, and since the dead experience nothing, Epicurus's first premise stands. Furthermore, since no subject exists after death, it follows directly that nothing occurring after death, such as posthumous events, could affect a person's well-being either, so the whole deprivation idea is incoherent. The Epicurean conclusion therefore survives.
