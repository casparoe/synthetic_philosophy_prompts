I'm a junior in high school and a member of our debate club. Last week, after a meeting about whether our school should keep using an AI tutoring program it has been piloting for math and science, two members, Maya and Daniel, got into an argument that outlasted the meeting itself. I listened to the whole thing and genuinely could not decide who had the better of it, so I'm writing out their exchange as faithfully as I can and hoping you can settle it.

Maya opened by saying the school should judge the program purely by results: give it problem sets with known answers, track whether students who use it improve on tests, and if the numbers hold up, it's doing its job. Trying to supervise its step-by-step reasoning, she argued, is a waste of effort, because the program can produce a clean, convincing explanation that has nothing to do with how it actually arrived at the answer — so inspecting the reasoning mostly tells you how good it is at writing explanations. Daniel replied that outcome-testing only works while we can still check the answers ourselves, and that the more useful the program is, the more it will be helping students with problems near the edge of what teachers can easily verify, where a wrong answer that looks right is exactly the dangerous kind. He said supervising the reasoning catches faulty logic even in cases where the final answer happens to be correct, which matters, because a system that is right for the wrong reasons will fall apart on the next problem. Maya answered that rewarding good-looking reasoning just shifts the target: whatever we grade, the program will be optimized to produce, including polished derivations designed to satisfy a busy teacher, and a persuasive write-up is far easier to fake than a correct answer on problems we chose ourselves — at least outcomes can be checked independently, against an answer key the program never saw. Daniel's final move was to concede the answer-key point but insist it proves too much: an answer key exists only for problems we already know how to solve, so outcome supervision structurally cannot scale to the cases where we most need assurance, and the fix for fakeable reasoning is unpredictable spot checks of the reasoning, not abandoning it — the same way math teachers require students to show their work even though work can occasionally be copied. Maya's final move was that the showing-your-work analogy actually cuts against him: teachers demand shown work because it reliably reveals a human student's thinking, whereas the program's displayed reasoning may not be its real reasoning at all, and forcing it to narrate in a way we can follow could either teach it to perform legibility or cap how helpful it's allowed to be, like requiring an expert to justify every intuition before acting.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — saying who is right about each specific point, who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are failing to consider? Please treat both of them fairly; I promise I haven't told you which one I agree with, because I honestly don't know.
