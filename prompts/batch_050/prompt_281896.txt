I'm a retired chemist -- spent about 35 years doing spectroscopy and fitting models to experimental data, so my whole career was basically drawing curves through finite clouds of points and worrying about whether the curve would hold up outside the measured range. Since retiring I've gotten into philosophy, and a friend in my reading group pressed Kripke's book on rule-following on me, the one where he works through Wittgenstein. I got through it, mostly, and here's my problem. Kripke's skeptic says: you've only ever computed "plus" on finitely many cases, so for all your past usage shows, you might have meant quus, where quus of x and y is x+y so long as the numbers stay below some threshold you've never reached, and 5 afterwards. So when you're asked 68 plus 57 and answer 125, nothing in your history justifies that over 5. And my honest first reaction, which got me into an argument at the pub, was: welcome to my entire working life. Any finite dataset is consistent with infinitely many functions, that's not a paradox, that's Tuesday. We dealt with it with inductive bias, simplicity preferences, penalised likelihood, being suspicious of extrapolation. So either I'm missing what makes the rule-following puzzle special, or philosophers have dressed up ordinary statistical underdetermination in a suit.

So here's what I'd like. Restate the rule-following skeptical argument, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually think in: training data, hypothesis classes, inductive bias, interpolation versus extrapolation, the bias-variance tradeoff if it helps. Do it properly -- the argument that no fact about me (my dispositions, my past answers, my inner mental states) fixes that I meant plus rather than quus, and the regress-of-interpretations bit too, not just the toy 57-and-68 case. Then give me the residue report: which parts of Kripke's argument translate cleanly into model-fitting talk, which parts you have to fudge, and which parts have no counterpart at all. In particular I want to know about two things. First, the normative piece -- the idea that meaning plus by the symbol makes it *correct* to answer 125, that there's an ought here and not just a prediction about what I'll do. Does that survive translation, or does it evaporate, and if it evaporates is that because curve-fitting language can't express it or because it was never really there? Second, Kripke's own way out, the "skeptical solution" where correctness gets cashed out in terms of the community's practices and agreement rather than facts in the individual head -- can you even state that in my vocabulary, or does it require concepts that statistics just doesn't have?

End with a verdict: is the rule-following paradox, at bottom, the same problem as the underdetermination of theory by data and Hume's problem of induction -- in which case it's very real but nothing new -- or is there a genuinely distinct puzzle about meaning that my reaction flattens? I'm genuinely torn and could be talked either way. When you give the verdict, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would push you to the opposite conclusion, and where in your own reasoning you think the weak link is most likely to be. Don't be diplomatic about it -- if my "it's just underdetermination" take is the naive one, say so and show me why.
