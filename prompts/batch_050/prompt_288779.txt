I run a small type foundry as a side business, and I've been tying myself in knots over what the industry I work in actually assumes letters *are*. When I started out I naively assumed that if I spent two years drawing a typeface, I owned it. A lawyer friend set me straight: where I live, the shapes of the letters themselves can't be copyrighted at all — anyone is free to stare at my finished font and redraw every glyph from scratch, pixel for pixel, and that's legal — but the font *file* counts as software and is protected, so copying the file is infringement while tracing the shapes isn't. Meanwhile the design community has its own unwritten norms that don't line up with the law at all: selling a straight revival of a dead designer's work is completely fine and even respectable, passing off a living colleague's design as your own is treated as theft even when it's technically legal, and being openly "influenced by" something is fine provided you change enough. And logos muddy it further — a company can trademark its wordmark set in an off-the-shelf typeface, so nobody else can use that *arrangement* of shapes that nobody is allowed to own individually.

So the whole ecosystem seems to treat a letterform as two things at once: a public skeleton that belongs to everyone and can't be owned, plus a private layer of style on top that sort of can. My question: what theory of what makes an 'a' an 'a' does this all presuppose, if any? Does it only make sense if there's some essence of a-ness — a fixed role or abstract form that no one could own even in principle — with mere decoration layered over it, or could you reconstruct the practice on a rival view, like family resemblance (an 'a' is just whatever is close enough to paradigmatic a's) or a reader-side functionalism (an 'a' is whatever competent readers reliably take as one)? Which of those views does the practice quietly rule out, and are the legal bit and the community-norm bit even presupposing the *same* thing, or is the system papering over a contradiction? Finally, if we swapped the presupposed view for the one I originally had — a typeface is an authored work, full stop, like a melody — what would actually have to change about revivals, influence norms, and logo trademarks? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and no restating my question, and write in British English.
