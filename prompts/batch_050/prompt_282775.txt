I have spent the better part of two decades as a journalist covering science and ideas for a general readership, and no beat fills my postbag quite like quantum foundations. Last spring I attended a small workshop on the interpretation of quantum mechanics, expecting to report on the usual skirmishes between Everettians and their opponents, and came away struck instead by one corner of the subject that seems simply to have stopped moving. In a 1998 paper, "The Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics: Many Worlds or Many Words?", the cosmologist Max Tegmark wrote:

It is argued that when environment-induced decoherence is taken into account, the experimental predictions of the MWI are identical to those of the Copenhagen interpretation except for an experiment involving a Byzantine form of "quantum suicide". This makes the choice between them purely a matter of taste, roughly equivalent to whether one believes mathematical language or human language to be more fundamental.

The thought experiment is by now familiar: an apparatus ties a lethal device to the outcome of a repeated quantum measurement, and the Everettian prediction is that the experimenter, from the inside, should find herself surviving run after run, however astronomically improbable that looks to the colleagues waiting outside. What struck me at the workshop was that the quarrel over whether this experiment can do the work Tegmark assigns it — serving as the one empirical discriminator between two otherwise empirically identical interpretations, and hence rescuing the whole dispute from being "purely a matter of taste" — has advanced not at all in roughly twenty-five years. The proponents say: here, at last, is a place where the interpretations part company in what a subject would actually experience. The critics reply with a battery of moves that were all in circulation by the early 2000s: that surviving branches guarantee at most a successor who remembers being you, so the "immortality" on offer is not yours in any sense that should matter to you now; that whatever the experimenter observes is irreducibly first-personal, since no third party ever sees anything but ordinary quantum statistics and a dead colleague, and so fails as public scientific evidence; that biological death is gradual and messy, so the clean branching into "alive" and "dead" the argument needs is a fiction; and that the entire inference presupposes a contested account of what the Born weights even mean in a universe where every outcome occurs. Every move has a counter-move, everyone in the room knows all of them, and nobody budges an inch.

My working hypothesis is that the physics here is not genuinely in dispute — all parties accept the formalism and the decoherence story — and that the real disagreement concerns what evidence for an irreducibly indexical proposition ("I shall go on surviving") could possibly be, together with bedrock intuitions about personal identity across branching that neither side can argue for from below. But I may be wrong, and I would like a diagnosis with reasons rather than another survey of the interpretations. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word "evidence", a hidden empirical question about whether any apparatus (or brain) could ever decohere cleanly enough to run the experiment at all, or two camps answering different questions under one heading — one about what the universe does, the other about what a person is? Test your diagnosis against what the sides actually say, including Tegmark's framing above, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about self-locating belief, a feasibility argument, a change of question — or whether the stalemate is itself evidence that the question was ill-posed from the start.
