I would like a consistency check on a set of views I have arrived at about a much-discussed thought experiment. The case: an eccentric billionaire will pay me £1 million if, at midnight tonight, I genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon — a toxin that causes a day of intense pain but no lasting harm. The payment turns entirely on my sincerely holding the intention at midnight, as verified by a brain scanner; whether I actually drink is irrelevant, and I know all of this in advance. My commitments:

(a) A rational agent could genuinely form the intention at midnight, and forming it is the rational thing to do; the perfectly rational person walks away with the million.
(b) Merely having an intention never by itself gives one a reason to perform the intended act; reasons come from the stakes of the situation, not from one's own say-so.
(c) Rationality requires means–end coherence: intending an end while knowingly refusing the only available means is irrational.
(d) Come tomorrow afternoon, with the money already banked and nothing riding on the drinking itself, drinking would be irrational, and a rational agent simply would not drink.
(e) One cannot intend to do what one confidently believes, at the very time of intending, one will not do.

Are these jointly tenable? Please audit the set: identify which commitments genuinely conflict, which pairs only appear to conflict and why the appearance dissolves on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity, stating for each which commitment would have to be abandoned and what that revision would cost. I am not attached to any particular member of the set, so do not spare whichever one has to go. Please answer in British English.
