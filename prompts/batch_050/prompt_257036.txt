So last night my roommate and I got into a genuinely heated two-hour argument about Kavka's toxin puzzle, the one where a billionaire will pay you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make you miserably ill for a day — and crucially the payout depends only on whether you hold the intention at midnight, not on whether you actually drink the stuff. Her view is that the puzzle is basically fake: any rational person can just form the intention, the same way you can decide to go to the dentist even though you know future-you will dread it, and she thinks bumping the prize to ten million and downgrading the toxin to an hour of queasiness makes this obvious. My position is the opposite: the prize gives you a reason to *want* the intention but no reason to drink, and since you know perfectly well that tomorrow-you will have every reason to stay home, you can't get the genuine intention at midnight no matter how hard you try — the size of the prize doesn't touch the structural problem.

Here's why I need help: next week I'm presenting at our department's grad colloquium defending basically my side of this, with one twist of my own. I'll argue that intentions aren't directly responsive to incentives the way actions are, that the usual "rational resolution" move either smuggles in an external enforcement device (contract, friend with a syringe, whatever) or only gets you the wish that you intended rather than the intention itself — and I'll add a variant I'm calling the rolling toxin, where the billionaire pays out at midnight *every night for a year* conditional on intending to drink the next day, because I think that version is actually winnable (you build a track record with your future selves, so intending tonight becomes rational), and the contrast shows the original puzzle is really about one-shot cases. The audience: mostly action theory and normative ethics grad students and faculty, a couple of Kantians, one decision theorist who loves this stuff way too much, and an experimental philosopher who will definately ask whether ordinary people's intuitions back me up.
