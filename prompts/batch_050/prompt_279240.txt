I've been going back and forth with a friend about Herodotus, the old "father of history vs father of lies" fight that goes back at least to Plutarch's essay on his malice. Her side: the guy just invented informants and wrapped tall tales in source attributions. Mine: passages like this one (Histories, Book 2, ch. 3, Rawlinson translation) show real source-critical instincts, two millenia before anyone taught method:

"I even went to Heliopolis and to Thebes, expressly to try whether the priests of those places would agree in their accounts with the priests at Memphis."

But then she says a good fabulist would write exactly that sentence to look credible, and I can't actually answer that. And here's the thing: both sides seem to have the exact same evidence — the same text, the same archaeology — and yet the debate has run for centuries without converging. My working hypothesis is that it's not a factual disagreement at all but a disguised one about standards: what burden of proof you fairly apply to the very first writer who even claims to check his sources, given that nothing he says can be confirmed from outside. Diagnose the debate rather than pick a winner: is it verbal or substantive, is some hidden empirical question doing the work, and would anything (new evidence, a distinction about genre, something else) actually settle it? Under 400 words, and end with a one-line verdict on whether this can ever be resolved.
