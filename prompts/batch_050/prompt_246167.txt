I've been poking at the low-impact AI idea lately (the whole "get the agent to do its job but avoid side effects" thing, like attainable utility preservation and friends) and I keep hitting the same conceptual wall. Please explain what that wall actually is.

My rough sense: any impact measure has to compare the world after the agent acts to some baseline, and it has to measure "distance" in some representation of the world. And both choices seem to smuggle in exactly the value specification the approach was supposed to avoid. Like, distance measured over raw physical states makes rearranging furniture and breaking a vase look the same; distance measured over human-meaningful descriptions means someone already encoded which descriptions matter. And on the baseline side, compare against what -- the world where the agent does nothing? the world where it doesn't exist? the world as it would've evolved anyway, which requires predicting the future?

So explain: (1) why "avoid side effects" seems to need a value-laden notion of which effects count as effects worth counting, (2) the baseline problem and why each natural choice of baseline breaks in a different way, and (3) whether approaches that try to stay value-agnostic -- penalizing change in *anyone's* attainable utility, or change to a big set of random auxiliary goals -- actually dodge the problem or just move it somewhere less visible. I've heard the complaint that these methods penalize helping exactly as much as harming, which suggests they haven't captured "side effect" at all, they've captured "any causal influence whatsoever". Is that complaint fair, and if so, is there any conceptual space between "fully specified values" and "penalize all change" where a low-impact notion could live?
