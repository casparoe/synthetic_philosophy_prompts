I teach a 3rd-year philosophy of mind seminar, 14 students, one 80-minute session, no equipment beyond paper, pens, and phones they can use as stopwatches. We're on the imagery debate (Kosslyn vs Pylyshyn) and every year students read it as an easy win for the pictures side: rotation times scale with angle, scanning times scale with distance, case closed, why is this even a debate. I want an activity that breaks that.

Design something where the class generates exactly that kind of seductive data on themselves and then watches it stop being evidence. Concretely I'm after:

1. A way for pairs to run mini rotation and/or image-scanning trials on each other and collect times, quick enough that we get usable numbers in maybe 20 minutes. Tell me what the stimuli should actually be (I want something I can prep on index cards, not a software package).

2. Some trick where, without them knowing, I've given different halves of the room different expectations about what the task involves, so the two halves' results come out looking different and they have to figure out why. The point is I want them to feel Pylyshyn's tacit knowledge / demand characteristics objection from the inside before anyone names it -- the reveal should be "your data tells us what you thought you were supposed to do, not what format your mind uses."

3. A structured bit where teams have to actually build a purely propositional "simulator" -- like, a written rulebook a cooperative human could follow with no imagery at all -- that reproduces the same pattern of times (longer for bigger angles, longer for bigger distances). I want them to discover the underdetermination by succeeding at this, not by being told it exists.

4. A debrief that maps each moment of the session onto the real arguments, plus an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce. My own guess is the hard limit is that the actual debate is about cognitive architecture and representational format, and no amount of classroom stopwatch data touches that -- but tell me if you think the limit is somewhere else.

Also: what will predictably go wrong? I'm especially worried about (a) the one student who's already read the literature gaming their own reaction times, and (b) stopwatch noise completely swamping the effect so the data looks like mush. Give me mitigations for both, and a fallback plan if the numbers just don't come out.

Students should leave thinking behavioral data underdetermines format -- not that Pylyshyn won. Keep that distinction in the debrief somewhere, they're always sliding from one to the other.
