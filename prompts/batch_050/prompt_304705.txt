So I've just spent like three hours arguing with my mate Callum about the Meno problem -- why knowledge is meant to be better than mere true belief -- and I'm annoyed because I kept contradicting myself and he kept noticing. Here are my actual gut verdicts on cases. Tell me what principle I'm running on, if there is one.

1. The road-to-Larissa setup. Two drivers both truly believe this road leads to Larissa. One has driven it a dozen times and can talk you through every junction; the other just guessed and got lucky. For the single trip, I honestly think they're exactly as good as each other. Same destination, same result, no difference in value whatsoever. Callum hated this but I'm holding firm.

2. Two students write the same correct answer on an exam. One derived it properly, one half-remembered a mnemonic and guessed right. I think they should get the same mark. A mark rewards the answer, full stop.

3. But -- hiring. I'd pay meaningfully more for the engineer who knows why the bridge design holds up than for one who has a true belief that it holds up but can't say why. Even if both hand in literally identical designs and both bridges stand for a century.

4. Driving through fake barn country (all the roadside barns are facades except the one you happen to look at). My verdict is that your true belief "that's a barn" is worth less there than it would be in normal countryside, even though it guides your driving identically in both places.

5. Someone believes their lottery ticket will lose purely because of the odds, and it loses. I don't think they knew. But I also don't think their belief was missing anything, because the odds were the best grounds anyone could possibly have had. That one felt wobbly even as I said it.

6. If the lucky-guess driver from case 1 hits a confusing detour sign, he'll waver where the experienced driver won't. So knowledge is better under stress. But I wanted to say stability is a seperate good *added on* to the belief, not the belief itself being better. Callum said this distinction does no work.

Cases 1 and 2 versus 3 are obviously the ones fighting each other, and I know it.

My tentative principle, such as it is: knowledge is only worth more than true belief when the situation could change in ways that would shake a mere true belief, and in static cases the two are worth exactly the same. Test that against all six cases, tell me where it breaks, and repair it or replace it. If no principle saves all my verdicts, tell me plainly which one I should drop and defend the choice -- don't just lay out the menu of options and leave me to pick. Also tell me what your repaired principle says about a case we didn't get to: a doctor who truly believes the diagnosis via an unreliable method that happened to deliver this time, versus one who knows, where the treatment prescribed is identical either way.

I want a committed answer with a definite verdict, not a survey. And British English please, I can't be doing with American spellings after the day I've had.
