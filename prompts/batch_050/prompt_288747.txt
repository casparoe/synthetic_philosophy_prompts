At my workplace we run internal prediction markets and hold quarterly forecasting reviews, and the same thing keeps happening. Two analysts will have exchanged views on some question for months -- each knows the other's estimate, each has heard the other's reasoning -- and yet their probabilities stubbornly stay apart. At that point someone invariably deploys the line that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, delivered as if it settles the matter: either one of the two is being irrational, or they have not really disclosed their honest views, and in either case the disagreement itself is treated as a defect to be eliminated rather than data to be respected. Last week it was used to pressure a colleague into moving her estimate toward the team's consensus.

I have a passing familiarity with the formal result this slogan gestures at (something about common knowledge and common priors), and I suspect the slogan conflates several quite different claims of very different strength. What I would like is a careful disambiguation. Lay out, as a list, the distinct precise theses that the slogan could be expressing -- for example, and I do not want to prejudge the menu: a descriptive claim about how actual reasoners behave; a mathematical claim about what follows when two agents' posterior beliefs are common knowledge between them; a weaker claim that persistent disagreement is evidence that at least one party is irrational or dishonest; a normative claim about how one ought to update upon learning that an epistemic peer disagrees; and perhaps a claim about convergence of opinion under continued dialogue rather than about any fixed moment.

For each thesis on your list, please: (a) state the premises it actually requires, being explicit about load-bearing assumptions such as common priors, common knowledge of rationality, and what exactly must be common knowledge (the posteriors themselves, the mere fact of disagreement, the reasoning?); (b) assess whether it is a theorem, a plausible empirical generalization, a contentious normative principle, or simply false, noting obvious counterexamples or failure modes (e.g., disagreements traceable to differing priors, differing evidence neither party can fully transmit, or computational limitations); and (c) say whether that reading would actually license the rhetorical use I described -- namely, treating my colleague's refusal to converge as ipso facto a failure of rationality on her part, rather than, say, a failure of the common-knowledge conditions, or a sign that the slogan does not apply to our situation at all.

I would also welcome a remark at the end on which reading you think the slogan's users typically have in mind, and whether the gap between the strongest true reading and the reading needed for the rhetorical use is doing the real work in such exchanges.
