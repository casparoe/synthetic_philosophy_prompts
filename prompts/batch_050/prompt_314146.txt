I'm on my school's debate team, and next week I have to defend the motion that a person can be morally at fault for participating in a wrong even when their participation makes no causal difference. To prepare, please play my opponent: a sharp, fair-minded defender of the causal-impotence view -- the position that blame for an outcome requires difference-making, so if the wrong would have happened anyway, participation in it is at worst a sign of bad character, not itself wrongdoing. Give the view its strongest form, not a caricature: you may concede small points (for instance, that we intuitively dislike complicit people), but never the central claim, and stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like a real exchange. Here is my opening move: our cafeteria's sandwich supplier was just exposed for mistreating its workers. One student more or less buying sandwiches will change nothing -- the contract, the wages, and the abuse all continue identically either way. Even so, I say the student who keeps buying, knowing everything, does something wrong, because wrongdoing can lie in willing cooperation with a harm and not only in causing one. If you disagree, tell me why the deliberate, knowing choice to take part counts for nothing simply because the world looks the same afterward.
