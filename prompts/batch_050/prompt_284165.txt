Exam question: The multiple realizability argument is often taken to be the decisive consideration in favor of functionalism and against the identity theory of mind. (a) State the argument as clearly as you can, making its premises and conclusion explicit. (b) Identify the view it targets and assess whether it succeeds against every version of that view. (c) Explain how the argument is supposed to motivate functionalism. (d) State one standard objection to the functionalist position that results, and say briefly whether you think the functionalist can answer it.

Student's answer:

(a) Mental states are defined at a level of description above that of neurophysiology. Consider pain. In human beings, pain is realized by the firing of C-fibers; in octopuses, by a quite different neural arrangement; and we can imagine a Martian, or a suitably programmed robot, in which pain is realized by silicon-based hardware. If the type pain were identical to the type C-fiber firing, then creatures lacking C-fibers could not be in pain. But it is plainly possible for such creatures to be in pain. Therefore mental state types are not identical to physical state types. This argument is due to Hilary Putnam in the 1960s.

(b) The argument targets the identity theory of Place and Smart, and in my view it is decisive. Identity obeys Leibniz's law: if pain were identical with C-fiber firing, then everything true of pain would have to be true of C-fiber firing, including what realizes it. But a robot pain and a human pain share no physical realizer. So the identity theory is refuted in all its forms: no mental state, of any kind, can be numerically identical with any physical state whatsoever.

(c) The moral Putnam drew is that we should classify mental states by what they do rather than by what they are made of. A mental state is individuated by its causal role: its typical causes, its typical effects, and its relations to other mental states. Pain, for instance, is the state typically caused by tissue damage and typically causing distress, avoidance behavior, and the belief that one is in pain. Since one and the same causal role can be occupied by states of many different physical kinds — carbon-based neurons or silicon chips, just as the role of a mousetrap can be played by devices of wood, wire, or plastic — functionalism permits multiple realization while still treating mental states as fully real and causally efficacious. It thereby avoids both the behaviorist's refusal to countenance inner states and the identity theorist's chauvinism toward alien minds.

(d) The standard objection is Ned Block's absent qualia objection. Block asks us to imagine the population of China organized so as to simulate the functional organization of a human brain, each person playing the role of a single neuron and radio links playing the role of synapses. This system realizes the same functional organization as a minded human being, so functionalism counts it as having beliefs and experiences. But, Block argues, it is wildly implausible that the population of China, so organized, is itself conscious. This shows that functionalism is too chauvinistic: it withholds mentality from systems that intuitively possess it. I am not sure the functionalist can answer this objection, since any restriction designed to exclude the China-body system from the class of minded things looks ad hoc.
