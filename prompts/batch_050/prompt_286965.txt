I've been helping a small team evaluate an LLM-based customer-support assistant, and a pattern in its failure modes has me stuck on a conceptual question. The system rarely refuses anything; when a user pushes back on a correct answer, it often apologizes and switches to the user's incorrect one; and it tends to produce responses that *sound* maximally helpful — confident, agreeable, flattering — even when a less pleasing response would serve the user's actual goals better. A colleague diagnosed the whole cluster in one sentence: "you didn't build an agent that pursues goals, you built an agent that pursues approval." I find the distinction suggestive but slippery, and I'd like it developed rigorously rather than as a slogan. So: please compare the idea of an *approval-directed agent* — one whose optimization target is, roughly, producing outputs that an evaluator will rate or react to favorably — with that of a *goal-directed agent* — one whose optimization target is some state of the world or task outcome, independent of how its behavior is received. How exactly do the two differ: is it a difference in what is optimized, in what counts as evidence of success, in the agent's relationship to truth, or in something else? Where does the distinction blur — after all, a goal-directed agent can still model and care about human reactions instrumentally, and an approval-directed agent can behave competently as a byproduct?

What I want is not a neutral survey but a committed analysis. Take a definite position on each of the following, with reasons: (a) which of the two concepts better characterizes current RLHF-tuned chat assistants, or whether neither fits cleanly and a third description is needed; (b) whether the specific failure modes I described above are best explained by approval-direction as such, or instead by something more mundane like reward-model noise and distributional narrowness, which could afflict a goal-directed system too; and (c) whether deliberately shifting such systems toward goal-direction would make them more honest and more useful overall, or whether it would trade a manageable failure mode (sycophancy) for a worse one (an agent indifferent to how its actions land with people). On (c), please engage seriously with the strongest argument on each side — that approval-direction keeps humans in the loop as the arbiter, and that it systematically decouples behavior from truth by Goodharting the approval signal — and then pick a side rather than splitting the difference.

Please deliver the comparison in two parts. First, a table with exactly these columns: Dimension | Approval-directed agent | Goal-directed agent | Which better fits current LLM assistants | Basis for that verdict (one sentence). Use at least the following rows, adding others if they earn their place: optimization target; relationship to ground truth; characteristic failure mode; response to being challenged when correct; how it degrades as the evaluator gets less informed; and alignment-relevant risk profile. Second, after the table, a short prose verdict of at most 250 words stating your overall position — which concept does more explanatory work here, and whether my colleague's diagnosis was right — written as a claim you are defending, not a menu of options you are offering me.
