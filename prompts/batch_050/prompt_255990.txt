So I'm a novelist, mostly literary fiction but lately I've been noodling on a book with an AI character that watches people and tries to figure out what they want -- the marketing blurb in my head says it "learns human desire from the outside." Anyway I fell down a rabbit hole reading about how actual researchers try to do this (inverse reinforcement learning, inferring utility functions from behavior, that whole area), and somewhere around 2am I talked myself into an argument that the whole thing is impossible. Which would be annoying for the book, but honestly what keeps me up is that if the argument works it also seems to wreck my day job, because "readers can tell what a character wants from what the character does" is basically the entire craft. So. Here's the argument:

1. To infer what someone wants from what they do, the behavior has to actually narrow things down -- it has to rule out at least some preference orderings. If the behavior is equally compatible with wanting X and wanting not-X, you haven't inferred anything, you've just guessed.

2. What someone does is always a product of two things: what they want, and what they believe about what their actions will accomplish. Same desires plus different beliefs, different action. Same beliefs plus different desires, different action.

3. For any behavior you observe and any preference ordering you feel like assigning, you can always cook up some set of beliefs that makes the behavior perfectly rational given those preferences. The guy who steps into traffic wants to live, he just believes the cars are holograms. The woman who gives away her fortune wants to be rich, she just believes the money is cursed. There's no behavioral pattern so weird that some belief-set can't rationalize it under any desires you pick.

4. So no behavior, or pile of behaviors, ever rules out any preference ordering. Everything is compatible with everything.

5. Therefore inferring preferences from behavior is impossible -- for AI researchers, for juries, and (this is the part I hate) for readers. When my readers decide that Anna left Mark because she never loved him, they're doing exactly the thing this argument says can't be done.

6. But that's insane. Readers do this competently every day, and a novel where nobody could infer any motivation from any action wouldn't be mysterious, it would be unreadable.

Something in there has to give, and I can't find it. Premise 3 feels like the sneaky one but every time I try to kill it I just end up inventing more elaborate beliefs and it survives. And I really want to keep 6's implication that reading character is a rational activity and not astrology for literate people.

So: which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? A few things I'd want addressed, if you can: Does the fix depend on assuming people are basically rational, and if so what happens when they aren't (my characters frequently aren't)? Does piling up more observations actually solve the problem, or is the underdetermination still there in principle even with a lifetime of footage? And if the answer involves smuggling in assumptions about which beliefs are reasonable for someone to have -- does that mean inferring what someone wants is always partly a matter of judging them, rather than just observing them? Because honestly that last one would be great news for the novel and I want to know if I get to believe it.
