I have been following the debate between contextualists, classical invariantists, and pragmatic-encroachment theorists for several years now — it began in a graduate seminar and I have kept half an eye on it since — and I have recently realised that I have stopped expecting anyone to win. The choreography seems entirely fixed. The contextualist wheels out the bank cases and the airport cases and argues that the truth-conditions of 'knows' shift with the attributer's context. The invariantist replies that our judgements about the high-stakes versions of these cases are pragmatic infelicities, or that we systematically confuse what it would be proper to say with what is actually true. The pragmatic encroacher splits the difference by letting the subject's own practical situation alter what knowledge itself demands. Everyone grants the same intuitions about the same vignettes; each side then explains the troublesome half of the data away, and nobody concedes that a rival's explanation of those intuitions is inferior to their own. Two decades of this and the dialectic has not visibly advanced.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the three parties are not really answering one question. The contextualist is, at bottom, doing linguistics of the verb 'to know' — explaining patterns of competent assertion and denial. The classical invariantist is doing first-order epistemology of the relation between a subject and a proposition, and takes conversational behaviour to be downstream noise. The pragmatic encroacher is doing something closer to the ethics of inquiry — asking when a belief is good enough to close investigation and act on — and then encoding the answer in the knowledge relation. Each reports their results in a shared vocabulary that makes them sound like rivals when they might be colleagues in different departments. There are two less flattering versions of the same thought: that the dispute is a turf war over who owns the word 'knows' and is therefore merely verbal, or that there is a genuine disagreement buried here but one level down — about what an account of our case judgements is obliged to explain, whether the judgements themselves, the distribution of true and false utterances, or the underlying psychology — and the parties differ on that meta-question without ever stating it, which is why their arguments sail past one another.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, in fact? Diagnose it by engaging what each side actually argues rather than surveying the positions, tell me which of my hypotheses — or some fourth possibility I have not considered — best fits the observed stalemate, and given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a sharper distinction between the metaphysics and the semantics of knowledge, a particular kind of experimental data, a formal result, or simply a change of question. If your honest verdict is that no such progress is available because the disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions about what epistemology is for, argue for that instead.
