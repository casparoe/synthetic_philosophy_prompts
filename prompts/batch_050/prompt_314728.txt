So I write thrillers, and my agent keeps leaving the same brutal note on my drafts: my side characters feel "wind-up." Around the same time I fell down a rabbit hole about the sphex wasp — the one that drags her paralyzed cricket to the mouth of her burrow, pops inside to check the coast is clear, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's down there, she comes out, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in to check again, over and over, dozens of times, never once just pulling it straight in. Hofstadter calls that kind of loop sphexishness, and I had the horrible realization that half my cast does exactly this.

Then I read how Fabre, in The Hunting Wasps (the Teixeira de Mattos translation), describes the moment entomology clicked for him: "So there was more in science than the arranging of pretty Beetles in a cork box and giving them names and classifying them; there was something much finer: a close and loving study of insect life, the examination of the structure and especially the faculties of each species."

That's my problem in one sentence. I've been arranging beetles in a cork box: my characters have labels and plot-jobs, not faculties I actually test on the page.

So turn this into a procedure. I do one revision pass per chapter, about an hour each, before anything goes to my agent. Write me a checklist I can run on any scene where a character repeats a behavior, one that seperates sphexish looping — re-running the routine blind, not registering that the situation changed — from the good kind of repetition (stubbornness, ritual, trauma, habit-with-a-reason) that I want to keep. What questions do I ask, in what order, what answers mean rewrite, where does the test just fail and its judgment all the way down, and what can a test like this never catch? Then run it on three of mine: (1) a detective who re-interviews the same witness with the same questions whenever the plot stalls, (2) a mother who checks the front-door lock four times every night even after the intruder storyline resolves, and (3) a henchman who keeps showing up to threaten the hero even though it never works and his boss is livid. Fair warning: I love the lock scene, and I suspect your procedure is going to tell me its sphexish.
