I am preparing a worksheet for an undergraduate philosophy of art seminar on the transparency debate: the question whether looking at a photograph of something is a way of literally seeing that thing, or merely a way of being informed about it. I want a decision tree that sorts readers into positions on this question based on how they respond to concrete cases, not on which theoretical label they find attractive.

Please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of named views. Start with six to nine branching questions. Each question should present a concrete scenario and ask for an intuitive verdict, phrased so that someone with no background in the debate can answer it. Cases I want you to draw on include: seeing a distant ship through a telescope; watching a live closed-circuit feed of events happening in the next room; looking at a periscope or a mirror; seeing a photograph of a long-dead grandparent; looking at a heavily airbrushed or digitally altered portrait; a photograph of an actor in costume playing a fictional character, where it is unclear whether one sees the actor, the character, or neither; a real-time image produced from sensor data rather than light, such as an ultrasound or a thermal camera feed; and a painting made from life by an exceptionally reliable painter. Where two cases elicit the same verdict from every position you consider, do not waste a branch on both.

Each leaf of the tree should be labeled with a position, stated as a thesis (for example: photographs, like telescopes, are prosthetic devices through which we literally see their subjects; or: no image, however produced, ever lets us see anything). Where a position corresponds to a view actually defended in the literature, give the standard label for it in parentheses; where a combination of answers is logically available but apparently undefended, mark the leaf as unoccupied and diagnose in one sentence whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Under each occupied leaf, add one sentence stating that position's most awkward commitment -- the verdict it is forced to give about one of the cases above that most people find hard to swallow.

Two further requirements. First, make sure your tree actually separates the main contenders: if two positions in the literature give identical verdicts on all your diagnostic questions, say so explicitly and explain what further question or case would be needed to pry them apart, rather than placing them on different leaves as if the tree had distinguished them. Second, include at least one branch question about the causal condition: does it matter to your verdict whether the image was produced by a mechanism that reliably and counterfactually depends on the scene, versus by a skilled human hand? State at the end which leaves fall on each side of that branch and whether you take that condition to be doing the real work in the debate or to be a proxy for something else.
