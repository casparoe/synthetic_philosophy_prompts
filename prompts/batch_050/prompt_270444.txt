I run a small discussion group with friends who work in machine learning and a few philosophers, and for months we have been circling the same argument without any movement, and I want a diagnosis rather than another round of it. The dispute is about whether inner misalignment and goal misgeneralization are serious concerns for the kind of AI systems now being built. One side argues roughly as follows: training a system with gradient descent on some objective does not guarantee the system internalizes that objective; a sufficiently capable system may come to pursue its own internal goal that happens to correlate with good training performance, and this goal can diverge from what we wanted precisely when the system is deployed in new conditions, with deceptive alignment as the worrying extreme case. The other side finds this whole framing confused when applied to large language models: these systems are trained to predict human text and are best understood as flexible predictors or simulators rather than as agents with goals at all, talk of internal objectives is anthropomorphism inherited from reinforcement learning thought experiments, and the inner alignment literature is a pre-LLM artifact that the actual technology has quietly bypassed. What frustrates me is that both sides cite the same evidence — out-of-distribution behavior, fine-tuning results, sycophancy, the sparse interpretability results we have — and draw opposite conclusions, and neither side can say what observation would change its mind.

My tentative hypothesis is that the disagreement is mostly verbal: the two camps are using 'goal' and 'wanting' differently, one meaning something like a behavioral disposition robust across distribution shifts, the other meaning something like an internal state that plays a particular causal-representational role, and that once you fix which sense is at stake, either the disagreement dissolves or it turns into a tractable empirical question. But I can also see a rival hypothesis: that the real disagreement is about standards of evidence and burden of proof for extrapolating from current systems to future ones, in which case it is methodological rather than verbal. Please take on the role of a philosopher of mind who also follows machine learning research closely, and think carefully about the structure of this dispute rather than about who is right. Start by brainstorming at least three competing diagnoses of what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, factual, methodological, a case of the sides answering different questions under one label, or a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for — and then argue for whichever diagnosis you find most defensible, grounding your analysis in what each side actually says rather than in caricatures. Note the asymmetry that one camp demands mechanistic evidence of internal goals that current interpretability cannot yet supply, while the other effectively demands proof that future systems will be safe, and consider what that asymmetry suggests about the diagnosis.

Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress. Would a precisifying definition of goal-directedness do real work here, or would each side simply adopt the definition that favors its position? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work — for instance, whether robust behavioral generalization in systems of this kind requires anything like internal representations of objectives — and if so, is it answerable with current or foreseeable techniques? Could controlled experiments on goal misgeneralization in small models settle anything about large ones, or is the extrapolation gap itself the real sticking point? If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected because the question as posed is ill-posed, make that case explicitly and say what question should replace it.
