QUESTION: Biologists before the 1960s often explained animal behavior by saying it was "for the good of the species." Explain why this style of explanation fell out of favor, what the gene-centered alternative says, and whether selection above the level of the individual (e.g., group selection) is possible in principle and plausible in practice. Give at least one concrete example.

STUDENT ANSWER: The "good of the species" idea, associated with V. C. Wynne-Edwards, held that animals restrain their reproduction so populations don't outstrip their resources. The problem is that within any group, a mutant that doesn't restrain itself out-reproduces the restrained ones, so restraint can't be maintained by selection acting on individuals. Unless groups go extinct differentially faster than cheaters spread within them, the trait collapses. Dawkins' gene-centered view in The Selfish Gene says we should think of genes as replicators and organisms as vehicles that genes build to copy themselves; Dawkins introduced this replicator/vehicle distinction, and since only genes are copied across generations, selection properly speaking acts on genes. Altruism evolves because copies of the altruist's genes sit in relatives, so helping kin is gene-level selfishness. Fisher's argument that sex ratios settle at 50:50 because groups with biased ratios produce too few grandchildren is a classic example of group selection working. Group selection in the modern sense (multilevel selection) is possible in principle -- if groups vary in fitness and that variation is heritable -- but in practice migration between groups and within-group selection usually swamp it. So species-benefit reasoning was rightly abandoned, though selection between groups remains a live possibility for traits like reduced virulence in pathogens.
