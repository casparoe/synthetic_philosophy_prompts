I've recently had cause to think hard about mental causation. My sister is being treated for depression, and her psychiatrist framed the whole thing to us as a malfunction in serotonergic signaling to be corrected pharmacologically -- as if her despair, her ruminations, and her reasons for feeling hopeless were at best decorations on the real causal machinery. My sister pushed back: surely her thoughts do things, surely deciding to get out of bed is part of what gets her out of bed. I sided with her in the conversation, but afterwards I tried to write down what I actually think, and I want to know whether the position I ended up with is coherent before I defend it to her (or concede anything to the psychiatrist).

Here are my commitments. (1) Causal closure of the physical: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. (2) Mental realism with teeth: mental states genuinely cause behavior -- when I wave at a friend because I recognize her, the recognition is part of the causal story, not an epiphenomenal redescription of what my neurons were going to do anyway. (3) Non-reductive physicalism: mental properties are realized by, but not identical to, physical properties -- pain could be realized in an octopus or a machine without anything like our neurons, so pain isn't a neural property. (4) No systematic overdetermination: it cannot be that every case of action has two fully sufficient, independent causes running side by side, the intention and the neural event, each enough on its own every single time. (5) An interventionist picture of causation: what settles whether X caused Y is whether Y would have differed under suitable interventions on X -- if I hadn't decided to wave, I wouldn't have waved, and that's what makes the decision a cause. (6) A verdict about explanation: in psychological explanation, the mental-level cause is often the better and more genuinely causal answer than the neural-level one, precisely because it abstracts across realizers -- "she recognized a friend" explains the waving in a way no description of this particular neural firing pattern can, since a different firing pattern in a different brain would have done the same.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture on the literature. Please go through the set and identify which pairs or triples genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and in each case what would have to give to restore consistency. I'm aware of the causal exclusion argument in outline, so don't just recite it -- tell me where it actually bites this specific package, and be alert to whether (5) rescues me from it, makes it worse, or is simply talking past it. Deliver your answer as a table with four columns: Commitments (list the numbers of the theses involved); Tension (one of: genuine--severe, genuine--moderate, apparent only); Diagnosis (two to four sentences on why they clash or why the appearance of conflict dissolves); Cheapest repair (the minimal revision that resolves it, and what that revision costs me philosophically). Order the rows from most to least severe, and below the table state in one sentence which single revision, if any, would resolve the most tension at once -- even if that revision is one I'd hate to accept.
