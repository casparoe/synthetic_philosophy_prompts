I coordinate volunteers for a large urban animal shelter -- roughly 120 active volunteers, a small paid staff, and a board that has final say on removing anyone. A few times a month someone comes to me with a concern about another volunteer that cannot be checked in any straightforward way: a dog walker who seems rough with the animals when nobody is watching, a treasurer whose bookkeeping another volunteer finds suspicious, a driver who allegedly made an adopter uncomfortable on a transport run. I have no investigative powers, no camera footage in most areas, and usually nothing beyond one person's word. Yet I cannot simply shelve these reports, because some of them are probably true and the shelter's animals and finances are at stake. Every case forces the same question I recently re-encountered in a philosophy reading group that worked through Clifford's essay The Ethics of Belief and James's reply The Will to Believe: when am I even permitted to form a belief on evidence this thin, and what may I do on the basis of suspicions I am not yet entitled to hold as beliefs?

What I would like is a reusable decision procedure I can actually run on each report, grounded in that debate rather than just gesturing at it. Concretely: an ordered sequence of questions to ask about a report and about my own epistemic position, with a statement of what counts as an adequate answer to each; a principled distinction between (a) suspending judgment, (b) forming a tentative belief, and (c) acting as if a claim were true for protective purposes without believing it -- and criteria for when each is warranted; an operational version of James's conditions under which believing ahead of the evidence can be legitimate (his idea that an option must be live, forced, and momentous), including his point that some truths can only come to light if one is willing to trust first; and an operational version of Clifford's duty of inquiry -- what steps I must take to check a report before the duty is discharged, given my real constraints (a few hours per case, no legal authority, a decision the board expects within two weeks). Please mark explicitly the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to my judgment rather than pretend to settle it mechanically.

Please then run the procedure on three cases: the report that a long-serving, well-liked volunteer handles animals roughly when unobserved; a secondhand rumor of financial misconduct by the treasurer, where asking around openly would itself do damage; and a case where the report concerns behavior that is merely off-putting rather than dangerous. At least one of these applications should reach a conclusion I will not like -- I expect to be told either that I may not act on a suspicion I find credible, or that I am obliged to act on one I would rather ignore. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do: which parts of the James-Clifford dispute it leaves unresolved and where it will misfire if applied blindly.
