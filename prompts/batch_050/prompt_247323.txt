I interview guests for a mid-sized YouTube channel about big ideas in science and philosophy, and next month I've got the author of a new book coming on (keeping her name out of this so the questions don't leak). Her thesis, roughly: Darwin and Turing made the same move -- what Dennett calls a strange inversion of reasoning -- showing you can get genuine competence without comprehension anywhere in the system, and she thinks large language models are basically a third case of the same thing.

Her opening chapter leans hard on this bit from Chapter IV of the sixth edition of Darwin's On the Origin of Species, which she reads as Darwin owning up to the inversion himself:

"It has been said that I speak of natural selection as an active power or Deity; but who objects to an author speaking of the attraction of gravity as ruling the movements of the planets? Every one knows what is meant and is implied by such metaphorical expressions; and they are almost necessary for brevity. So again it is difficult to avoid personifying the word Nature; but I mean by nature, only the aggregate action and product of many natural laws, and by laws the sequence of events as ascertained by us."

The probelm is she's a very polished interviewee and tends to ride the word "metaphor" in both directions: when you press her on whether natural selection really accomplishes anything, she retreats to Darwin's own gloss (it's just aggregate law-following, no agent needed), but when she wants the analogy to Turing and LLMs to feel exciting she talks about selection designing, solving problems, finding solutions. I want 8-10 questions that make her pick a lane, with a follow-up to each keyed to the answer she'll most likely give.

A few spots I definately want covered at some point in the sequence: whether Darwin's own reading of the passage deflates her whole framing rather than supporting it; the disanalogy that in Darwin's case the *products* of the process (animals, us) often do comprehend, which doesn't obviously hold for a chatbot; what observation about an LLM would even settle whether comprehension is present, as opposed to just being asserted or denied by fiat; and the Searle-style objection that simulation of competence isn't competence. I'd also like the last question to push her on what result or finding would make her abandon the thesis.

Two more things: for each question, tell me how confident you are that it engages her actual position rather than a strawman version of it, and at the end flag the one question in the set you're least sure will land, and say why.
