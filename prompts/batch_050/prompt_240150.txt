I'm a PhD student in phil and this semester I'm TAing for an undergrad course on free will, which mostly means I grade like 90 short papers where students propose their own Frankfurt-style cases to attack the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. The quality control situation is dire. Some cases clearly work, some clearly don't, and a lot are broken in ways the students can't see (e.g. the "intervener" never actually guarantees the outcome, or there's a flicker of freedom hiding in the prior sign, or the agent only does the thing because the device does it for them).

I want to stop reinventing my objections each time, so: can you write me a grading procedure I can run on any student-proposed Frankfurt case? Not an essay about Frankfurt cases -- an actual ordered checklist where each step is a test the case passes or fails, like:

- is there a genuine mechanism that would force the action if the agent wavered, and does the case specify a prior sign the intervener can actually read in time (this is where most of them die)
- does the agent act on their own in the actual sequence, with the device never firing, or did the student sneak the device into the actual action
- is there a residual alternative possibility left in the case (flicker strategy), and if so is it robust enough to plausibly ground moral responsibility, or is it some trivial thing like the agent's left ear twitching
- does the case just assume what Frankfurt needs to prove, e.g. by describing the agent as "freely choosing" rather than showing the structure does the work

For each step say what counts as passing, what counts as failing, and how harsh to be (i.e. is it a "case is dead" failure or a "case needs patching" failure). Also tell me where the procedure runs out and I just have to make a judgment call, because I suspect the flicker-robustness question is one of those.

Then actually run the procedure on these three cases I've gotten real versions of:

1. The classic: Black will force Jones to kill Smith if Jones shows any sign of not deciding to on his own; Jones decides on his own and kills him. Student thinks this refutes PAP cleanly.
2. A student variant where the device reads Jones's brain but only intervenes *after* Jones has already begun the physical act of killing, to "make sure it completes." Student thinks the tiny tweak doesn't matter.
3. A variant where instead of a neurodevice, Black is just a really convincing mob boss who has credibly threatened Jones, and would have threatened him harder had he hesitated. Student thinks coercion cases are Frankfurt cases.

For case 3 especially I'm worried the procedure will spit out a verdict I won't want to give a student full marks for, so be honest about what it says there. Also end with what the procedure can't settle at all -- the places where whether PAP survives is a real philosophical question and not a grading one. Keep it usable in like 10 minutes per paper because I have a stack of these and a life I'd like to have.
