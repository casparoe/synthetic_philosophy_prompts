A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the outbreak of the First World War, and we need a referee. Her position: the war was overdetermined. The alliance system, the arms race, imperial rivalry, and the mobilization timetables had made a general European war so likely by 1914 that the assassination at Sarajevo was merely the trigger that happened to arrive first -- and a cause whose removal would simply have let another do the work does not deserve the title of cause. Mine: remove the assassination and there is no war that summer, and plausibly no war at all, so straightforward counterfactual dependence singles out Sarajevo no matter how much dry tinder lay around.

Please analyze the outbreak of WWI through the lens of overdetermination in the theory of causation. Specifically: (1) distinguish genuine overdetermination -- two or more independently sufficient causes both firing -- from preemption, where a backup cause is cut off by the actual one, and say which of the two better fits this case given that the would-be backup causes (a later crisis in the Balkans or elsewhere) never fired; note that my friend's reading treats the structural conditions as individually sufficient, which is itself a strong historical premise, so say what the analysis looks like if that premise is weakened. (2) Run a strict but-for counterfactual test over the candidate causes and ask whether its failure to convict the structural conditions shows they were not causes, or instead shows that counterfactual dependence is the wrong instrument for historical explanation. (3) Consider whether historians' multi-causal talk is better reconstructed along INUS lines -- each factor an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient complex -- and what that does to the verdict. (4) Adjudicate: does one of us win, or does the dispute dissolve once the right account of historical causation is made explicit? Where the causal theory is simply silent about the history, say so rather than forcing it to pronounce.
