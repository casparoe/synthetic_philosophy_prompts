Draft the joint statement that would emerge from a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two moral philosophers who have debated rescue ethics for years. The first is a Taurekian: she holds that when a rescuer can save either one stranger or five strangers — all equally at risk, all unknown to the rescuer, and saving any of them requiring the same indivisible effort — the bare fact that five is more than one gives the rescuer, as such, no moral reason to save the five. Each person, she insists, has a claim to be given a fair chance, and fairness between equally situated claimants is exhausted by something like a coin flip. The second is an aggregationist: he holds that, other things equal, one ought to save the greater number, because each person's fate matters equally and losses sum across persons, so five deaths are five times as much moral loss as one.

Structure the document as follows.

Part 1: Agreed claims. A numbered list of statements both parties can sign without reservation. Include claims about the structure of the canonical case (that the choice is genuinely forced, that whichever group is not saved will die as a result, that no facts about desert, relationships, or social contribution distinguish the parties); claims about what each side is not committed to (for instance, that the Taurekian need not deny that five deaths constitute a worse state of affairs than one, and that the aggregationist need not be a thoroughgoing utilitarian about harming); and claims about what is out of bounds in the debate (appeals to the law, to what actual bystanders would do, or to what either party finds emotionally easier).

Part 2: A disagreements table. State each remaining disagreement in a single sentence or two, phrased so that each party would accept the formulation of her or his own position. Where the two sides use the same words differently — candidates you should examine include whether a person's claim is a claim to be saved or a claim to an equal chance, and whether counting each person equally entails counting the numbers — note the divergence explicitly rather than letting it pass silently.

Part 3: Cruxes and test cases. For each crux, give a concrete case that both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the correct resolution of that case to be. Include at least the following three:

(a) The probabilistic case: the rescuer must choose between saving one person from certain death and saving five people each of whom faces an independent 20 percent risk of death. If the Taurekian's fair-chance view now recommends saving the five, has aggregation re-entered through the back door; if it does not, can the view still claim to take each person's prospect of death seriously? Each side should state its prediction for what the fair-chance view yields here.

(b) The repeated-choice case: a rescuer will face many such dilemmas over a lifetime, and a standing policy of always saving the greater number will save more lives in expectation than a coin-flip policy. Can the Taurekian remain impartial among persons while rejecting the policy, and can the aggregationist concede anything here without collapsing the disagreement?

(c) The mixed-harms case: one person will die unless saved, versus five people who will each lose a leg unless saved. Must the Taurekian flip a coin here as well, and does the aggregationist's summing of losses presuppose a commensurability of harms that his opponent need not grant? Record what each side says the case shows and what would have to be true for the case to move them.

Part 4: A closing paragraph stating whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal — that is, whether some apparent disagreement survives only because the parties attach different meanings to phrases like the numbers counting or treating persons equally — and, if so, identifying that portion precisely and explaining why it resists dissolution.

Write the whole document in the register of two philosophers who respect each other and expect to be read by partisans of both sides: no formulations that either party would reject as a caricature, no agreed claims so thin that signing them costs nothing, and no disagreement left in terms vaguer than the parties' own best statements of their views.
