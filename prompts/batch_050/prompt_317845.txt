I've been chewing on origin essentialism -- roughly the Kripke-style claim that I couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg, that my actual origins are part of what makes me me -- and I've somehow landed a year of research leave to write a short book about it for a general-but-philosophically-literate audience. The problem is I can't decide what the book should actually be *about*, because the area sprawls.

Here are the live questions as I see them, though feel free to re-carve: (1) the granularity problem -- which bits of the origin are meant to be essential? The gametes? The zygote? The conception event itself? Could I have been born a week earlier, or does that already change the relevant origin? (2) the epistemology problem -- we can't run counterfactuals, so what could possibly settle whether origins are essential rather than it being a fact about how we happen to individuate things? (3) the downstream consequences for reproductive ethics: if identity is this origin-sensitive, then embryo selection, delayed conception, donor conception etc. are all identity-affecting in a way that seems to massively strengthen non-identity-problem style reasoning, and maybe makes some common intuitions about "the child I could have had" strictly incoherent. And (4) the deflationary worry that the whole debate is verbal once you're careful about sortals.

So: which two or three of these should structure the book, and in what order? Defend the ranking -- tell me what criteria you're using (importance, tractability in a year, how much else hangs on the question, how stale the existing discussion is), which question you'd cut entirely and why it's overrated, and what would have to be true for your ranking to flip.
