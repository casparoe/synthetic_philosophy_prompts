I run a final-year module on the psychology of creativity at a conservatoire, and next term I must deliver a single ninety-minute seminar defending Hofstadter's account of creativity as variation on a theme: the view that novel ideas arise by taking an existing structure and twiddling its parameters or 'knobs', and that the apparently spontaneous leaps of genius are extended slippages of just this kind. My audience is composition students who arrive believing that creativity is inspiration, that talk of constraints is philistine, and that any theory like this one is really a theory of why they needn't bother studying. I want a teaching plan, not the seminar itself.

Approach this as a professional philosopher of mind who has also taught hostile arts audiences for years, and think carefully about the dependency structure before committing to it. Begin with a short working section in which you brainstorm at least three candidate carrying examples and at least five misconceptions this audience will bring or form, and only then write the plan, justifying your selections against the alternatives.

The plan should specify: the order in which the components must be introduced, and for each step what it presupposes about what has already landed — in particular, where the distinction belongs between twiddling a knob whose existence is given and discovering a new knob to twiddle, and how late I can afford to leave the regress this invites. Name the single carrying example the whole session turns on: it must be a creative achievement these students venerate as a leap, it must decompose into knobs they can check for themselves, and it must not be a piece of music, since that would put their own identity at stake too early; say what else it must have and must lack. Map the misconceptions — I expect 'variation means derivative', 'the theory is unfalsifiable because anything can be redescribed as a variation', and 'mediocrities twiddle, geniuses invent' — in the order they will arise, marking exactly where each should be headed off and one place where I should let a misconception form on purpose before dismantling it. Include how I should absorb a live stress-test, such as a student offering Einstein or the late Beethoven quartets as a counterexample, a closing check that would show the idea has landed rather than merely irritated them, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.

Please write in British English.
