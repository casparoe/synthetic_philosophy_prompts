I'm preparing a short position statement for a graduate epistemology workshop on self-knowledge and higher-order evidence, and before I circulate it I want to know whether the package I've assembled is actually coherent. Here is where I've landed:

(a) Knowledge requires a margin for error: where I form a belief by estimating some gradually varying quantity, I know only if I couldn't easily have been wrong in close-by cases, so safety of some stripe is a condition on knowledge.

(b) The KK principle fails. I'm persuaded by the standard anti-luminosity reasoning -- the sort that runs margin-for-error considerations across a series of insensibly gradual changes (the slowly cooling morning, the slowly dimming light) -- that no interesting condition is such that whenever it obtains one is in a position to know it obtains, and that this is precisely why knowing that one knows cannot be required for knowing.

(c) All the same, phenomenal seemings are luminous. If it seems to me that the patch is red -- seems, in the phenomenal sense, not that I'm inclined to judge it red -- then I'm in a position to know that it seems that way. This is the one place I think the anti-luminosity conclusion doesn't reach, and it's doing real work in my wider views about the epistemic role of experience.

(d) Justification is internal in the supervenience sense: whether my belief is justified is fixed entirely by factors I could become aware of on reflection, without needing to check anything about the external world or the actual reliability of my methods.

(e) Higher-order evidence always rationally constrains first-order belief: if I acquire good evidence that my judgment in some domain is biased or unreliable, I'm rationally required to lower my confidence accordingly, even when my first-order reasoning still seems to me impeccable. I deny that rationality permits the level-splitting response of holding the first-order belief while believing I'm irrational to hold it.

(f) Knowledge is the norm of assertion, and I take it that it's frequently proper for me flat-out to assert that I don't know whether such-and-such.

Some of these pair up in ways that worry me -- for instance, I've been told (a) and (c) sit awkwardly together, though I don't yet see the argument -- and I'd rather find the problems now than in the seminar. What I'd like from you: for each pair of commitments that's genuinely in tension, state the tension precisely, rate its severity relative to the others, and identify what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set. Where two commitments only appear to conflict, say so and dissolve the appearance rather than manufacturing a problem. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the overall package coherent. I have no attachment to any particular commitment over the others, so don't pull punches on any of them.
