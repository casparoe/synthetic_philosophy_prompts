I'm a second-year PhD student in political science (my dissertation is on cooperation in legislative bargaining, loosely) and I ran my own little round-robin tournament of the iterated prisoner's dilemma for a methods seminar, mostly to teach myself to code. Anyway I keep noticing something in the results that I can't find a word for in anything I've read, and it's driving me a bit nuts.

The pattern: the strategy that ends up with the highest total score across the whole tournament never actually outscores any single opponent it plays. Head to head against the friendly strategies it just ties every time, and against the nasty ones it loses by a little bit, since it eats the first defection and then matches them. So the overall winner is pairwise unbeatable-by-nobody, so to speak. And then there's the mirror image: I've read that you can engineer strategies that mathematically guarantee you finish ahead of whoever you're facing, period, no matter what they do -- and apparently when people drop those into tournaments or evolutionary simulations they do terribly. So being ahead of everyone you meet and doing well overall seem to come completely apart, and can even trade off.

Does this inversion have an established name? I've come across "evolutionarily stable" and "collectively stable" but those seem to be about resisting invasion, which is close but not quite the same thing as what I'm describing. If there's no name for it, coin one, define it precisely, and give me some boundary cases: can the inversion happen in one-shot games, or does it need repetition? What about multiplayer games? Under what conditions does pairwise dominance start tracking population-level success again?
