I’m a novelist, not a philosopher, and I’m plotting a literary thriller where a woman kills a blackmailer entirely for her own reasons, while a dormant implant in her head would have forced the same act if she’d hesitated. So she couldn’t have done otherwise, sort of, but the actual story runs through her wanting it. I keep circling Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, but I don’t want a survey and definately no reading list.

Which open questions or lines of work around Frankfurt-style cases matter most if the goal is morally serious fiction about agency? Pick your top three and rank them. For each, say why it changes what a scene should make salient: choice, blame, regret, self-deception, punishment, the feeling of being authored by one’s own motives. Also name one or two debates people treat as central that you think are overrated for this purpose, or only matter via another question.

Use criteria and make them explicit: dramatic payoff, closeness to lived moral experience, whether it settles something or just relocates the bump in the carpet, and whether an attentive general reader can feel the stakes without seminar training. If your ranking depends on a view about responsibility, say so and say what argument would reorder it. I’m especially interested in whether “couldn’t have done otherwise” should matter less to the reader than to the character, and whether that’s a cheap trick or the whole point.

Hard limit: 650 words, prioritised and defended, British spellings, no citations.
