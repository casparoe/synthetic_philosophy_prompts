so a bit of backround first: i'm not an academic or a clinician or anything, i'm a parent who started reading a lot about psychiatry after my nephew got an adhd diagnosis a few years back, and somewhere in there i fell down this rabbit hole about how diagnosing people isn't like diagnosing a car. the philosopher ian hacking calls it a looping effect -- the label changes the people it gets stuck on, they start acting and understanding themselves partly through the label, and then the label itself drifts because the people under it have changed, and round it goes. i also read about how some diagnoses exploded in the 80s and 90s and then basically faded away again, which spooked me a bit.

anyway, somehow this turned into me agreeing to give a ten-minute talk at our school district's parent forum next month. my thesis, roughly: when the district says stuff like "anxiety diagnoses among students are up 40%" and uses that to plan budgets and staffing, they're treating those numbers like temperature readings. but the numbers are partly made of the loop -- more awareness leads to more diagnosis leads to more kids seeing themselves through the category leads to more diagnosis. so my ask is deliberately modest: don't scrap anything, don't take anyone's support away, but treat diagnosis statistics as soft data, and build in periodic re-reviews of individual diagnoses instead of treating them as lifetime stamps. my working slogan is "diagnoses should come with a review date" which honestly needs workshopping.

the audience: mostly parents of diagnosed kids who fought the school for years to get accommodations and are fiercely protective of the labels, because the label is what unlocked the help. also two special-ed teachers, the district school psychologist (very much an evidence-based-assessment person), and one dad who thinks every kid has adhd now because of phones and will probably try to be on my side in the worst possible way.

prep me for the q&a. give me the objections i'll actually get from THIS crowd, in roughly the order they'll come up, phrased the way these specific people would put it rather than philosopher-speak -- i'm bracing for something like "so you're saying my daughter's anxiety is just made up?" for each objection: what i should concede immediately, what i should hold firm on, my two-minute reply, and where that reply is weakest. also tell me how to handle the phone-dad who agrees with me for reasons i hate, which objections have real force versus which are just emotional, and the question i'm hoping nobody asks.
