At the math department's board game night last week, two grad students I know, Theo and Ruth, got into an extended argument about the strategy-stealing argument for the game of Hex, and I was left holding the referee's flag without knowing enough to settle it. Both of them accept the standard version: suppose the second player had a winning strategy; then the first player could open with an arbitrary stone, thereafter pretend to be the second player following that strategy, and if the strategy ever calls for the square already occupied, just play another arbitrary stone — since an extra stone of your own color can never hurt you in Hex, the "second player" strategy would win for the first player, a contradiction. The fight was over what this argument does and doesn't establish, and how far it travels.

Theo's opening claim was that the argument shows the first player has a winning strategy in Hex, full stop. Ruth objected that it shows only that the second player cannot have a winning strategy, and that getting from there to a first-player win requires two extra ingredients the argument itself doesn't supply: that finite perfect-information games are determined, and that Hex specifically cannot end in a draw because any completely filled board gives some player a connected path — a topological fact she called the real heart of the matter. Theo conceded the determinacy point but insisted the no-draw fact was a minor lemma, not the heart. Ruth then pressed further: since the proof is non-constructive, it tells us nothing about optimal play and so, in her words, doesn't really prove anything about the game as played — it's a curiosity, not a result. Theo replied that existence proofs are perfectly good proofs throughout mathematics, though he then claimed that one could in principle extract the winning strategy from the contradiction by analyzing which assumptions were used, which Ruth called nonsense.

The final and sharpest round concerned chess. Theo argued that since the proof only uses the premise that an extra piece can never hurt, and since that premise looks true of chess, the same argument shows White has at least a draw. Ruth said the extension fails because chess allows draws, whereas the argument needs a drawless game. Theo answered that draws are no obstacle at all — the analogous conclusion for chess would simply be that Black has no winning strategy, i.e., that White doesn't lose — and that the real obstacle is zugzwang: in chess, being forced to move means an extra obligation can hurt you, so the premise fails. Ruth's last stand was that zugzwang can't be the issue because you could always just ignore your extra piece and play as if it weren't there.
