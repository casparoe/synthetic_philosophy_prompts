I want a diagnosis of a debate that seems to me permanently stuck, and I want the diagnosis itself to be committed, not another survey. The debate is the one in political philosophy about whether religious reasons may properly be offered in public political argument, especially when coercive law is at stake.

On one side, the restraint tradition descending from Rawls holds that citizens owe one another reasons drawn from shared political values, and that arguments resting solely on a comprehensive religious doctrine fail a duty of civility, at least when fundamental questions are concerned. On the other side, critics like Wolterstorff argue that restraint imposes an uneven burden: for many believers, religious reasons are not an optional garnish on secular ones but the only reasons they actually have, so demanding restraint demands that they either stay silent or speak insincerely. Audi's requirement of adequate secular reason and Habermas's proposal that religious contributions be translated into generally accessible language sit somewhere between.

What makes me suspect the debate is broken rather than merely hard is a pattern in its own history. The restraint side has steadily retreated: early strong exclusion gave way to a proviso permitting religious argument so long as public reasons are added in due course, and then to still wider versions. Each softening prompts the inclusion side to reply that the position no longer excludes anything real, since any religious argument can be retrofitted with a public-reason wrapper, while the restraint side replies that the duty was always about the reasons one takes to be sufficient, not about which words one may utter. Meanwhile the same handful of historical cases — abolitionists, the civil rights movement, Martin Luther King — keeps being recycled, with each side claiming them. Neither side seems able to specify a case that would actually discriminate between them.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not one disagreement but two running under a single heading: a normative question about the ethics of citizenship (what do I owe my fellow citizens when I justify coercion?) and a quasi-empirical question about what restraint actually does in practice (does it purify debate, or merely drive religious motivation underground where it is less answerable?). If that is right, the philosophers have been refining the first question while the second, which may be doing all the real work, goes unexamined by the armchair methods the field prefers.

So: is this dispute substantive or verbal, one question or two, and is there a hidden factual or conceptual issue underneath? Take a definite position on what kind of disagreement it is, defend that reading against the most plausible alternative, and say concretely what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction, an empirical research program, a dissolution — rather than mapping the terrain and leaving the options on the table.
