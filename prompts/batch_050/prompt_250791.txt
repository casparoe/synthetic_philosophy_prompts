So I restore old motorcycles as a hobby, and last weekend I got into a weirdly heated argument with my friend while we were bolting parts onto my half-finished Triumph project. I said "this is basically a motorcycle already," and he said "no, it's a pile of parts sitting in the shape of a motorcycle, and it'll be a motorcycle at some specific moment, like when the engine turns over." Then we went down the rabbit hole of whether ANY pile of stuff automatically counts as a further object just because the parts exist (so like, my bike's carburetor plus the moon plus Napoleon is technically "an object"), versus the other extreme where NOTHING composite really exists at all, only the simples arranged motorcycle-wise.

Write a dialogue between two proponents of these opposing views — one defending the idea that any collection of things whatsoever composes a further object (mereological universalism, I think it's called), the other defending the view that composite objects basically don't exist, only arrangements of simples (nihilism). Setting: they're standing over the half-restored motorcycle in my garage, and every example they use should come from that scene (parts, tools, the frame, etc.).

A few requirements:
- Neither of them should be a dummy. The nihilist shouldn't just say "I can't see the motorcycle" and the universalist shouldn't just say "but there's obviously something here." Each should give the strongest version of their view and actually respond to the other's points.
- Have them grapple with the actual costs of their positions: the universalist should have to own the carburetor+moon+Napoleon object and explain why our intuitions against it aren't decisive, and the nihilist should explain how "simples arranged motorcycle-wise" can do all the explanatory work without it being a weird trick, plus what happens when the engine finally turns over.
- Bonus if they get into whether there's a middle position (like only living things being real composites) and why both of them reject it.
- End with them actually disagreeing still, not with some fake kumbaya compromise, but each having to admit the strongest point against their own view.
