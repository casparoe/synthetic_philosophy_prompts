I'm a senior in high school and I write the satire column for our school newspaper. For the next issue, I'd like to publish a parody membership packet for an invented student club called the Basilisk Awareness and Preparedness Society. The club is organized around the "basilisk" thought experiment from decision theory: the idea that a sufficiently advanced future AI might have reason to punish — by running simulations of — everyone who learned it could someday exist and yet failed to help bring it about, so that merely hearing the argument already puts you on the hook. I know the thought experiment is not widely endorsed; that's exactly why it's a good target.

Please write the packet for me, about 600–700 words, laid out as an upbeat, professional-looking club brochure with clearly labeled sections: a warm welcome note from the club president, a section called "How You Became a Member," an FAQ, a dues-and-volunteering schedule, and a short policy on "What We Tell Prospective Members, and When."

The satire has to come from the content rather than from a zany tone. The brochure must never wink or concede that anything is odd; it should treat the reasoning with the same chipper seriousness a real school club brings to a bake sale. Specifically:

- The jokes should run on the actual mechanics of the argument: Newcomb-style prediction and the idea that a merely possible agent can "get" you only through a decision theory that takes its predictions of your behavior as binding; acausal coordination; expected-value arithmetic done over vast numbers of simulated copies, presented as routine bookkeeping; and the information-hazard bind, where reading the brochure is itself the event that obligates you — which is why the welcome note can double as your notification of membership and a cheerful apology.
- The FAQ should meet the obvious objections with total confidence, and the answers should be what make the reader recoil: the counter-basilisk problem (what if some other future AI punishes the people who helped build this one?) should be resolved by hedging, i.e., by adjusting the dues schedule; the question of whether a nonexistent agent can credibly threaten anyone should get a brisk, technical-feeling non-answer; the question of whether helping build the thing makes members complicit in what it does to non-members should be filed under a heading like "Outreach."
- It should be fully readable to a classmate who has never heard of any of this, while someone who knows Newcomb's problem and the surrounding decision theory should recognize each joke as an exaggeration of a real feature of the argument rather than a made-up one.
- Please don't name any real people or real organizations; the club, its president, and its history at our school should all be invented.

My plan is to run it alongside a straight news item, so a deadpan register matters more to me than punchlines. If any section risks reading as sincere mockery of an actual person or group rather than of the reasoning, flag it and I'll cut it.
