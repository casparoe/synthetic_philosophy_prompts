Something that keeps bugging me, and I was arguing about it with my brother last week. My country (Germany, but I think its similar in alot of places) runs two practices at the same time that seem to pull in opposite directions. On one side there's this whole machinery of remembering: memorial days, school trips to former concentration camps, those little stumbling stones in the sidewalks with victims' names, politicians repeating that we must never forget. Forgetting gets treated almost like a moral failing, and Holocaust denial is straight-up illegal here. On the other side, the same legal system is weirdly enthusiastic about institutionalized forgetting: criminal records get expunged after a while, juvenile offenses are wiped clean, bankruptcies fall off your record, and there's the whole right to be forgotten thing where you can get search engines to delist old stuff about you. So forgetting is sometimes a sin and sometimes a right you can claim in court.

What I want to understand is what these two practices actually presuppose about memory and why it matters. Does the "never forget" side only make sense if you think remembering is a duty we owe to the dead themselves, like some kind of debt to people who no longer exist, or would a purely forward-looking justification (remember so it never happens again) be enough to underwrite all of it -- the memorials, the criminalization of denial, the works? And on the expungement side: does that presuppose some view about personal identity, like that people aren't really the same person they were twenty years ago and shouldn't be chained to their past selves, or is it just a bet that old information misleads more than it informs?

Also: are the two practices even consistent, or do they secretly rest on contradictory theories about what remembering is FOR? And I'd like you to separate what each practice strictly requires from what's just one story you could tell about it -- like, could someone who thinks memory only has instrumental value (remember whatever prevents future harm, forget the rest) honestly endorse both practices, or would they have to give one of them up? My gut says the memorial stuff assumes something thicker than instrumentality, something about obligations to the dead, while expungement assumes the living come first. But I cant tell if thats an actual contradiction or whether one coherent theory could cover both.
