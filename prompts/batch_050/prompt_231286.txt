A colleague has been arguing that an ideal of inquiry I had always taken to be harmless is in fact incoherent, and her argument has survived a week of my attempts to break it. Her target is what she calls the completeness ideal: the claim that for every truth, there is some possible continuation of inquiry by which we could come to know it. Note how modest this sounds. It does not say we will ever finish inquiring, or that we should pursue every question, or that any truth is easy to reach — only that no truth is out of reach in principle. Her argument against it runs as follows:

(1) Assume the completeness ideal: for every truth p, inquiry could, in principle, come to yield knowledge of p.

(2) Assume, plausibly, that inquiry is not finished: there is at least one truth that nobody knows. Let p be such a truth — say, that the number of gulls within a kilometer of the harbor at noon yesterday was odd. Nobody checked, so nobody knows it.

(3) Then the conjunction q — that p, and that nobody knows p — is itself a truth.

(4) By (1), inquiry could in principle come to know q.

(5) Knowing a conjunction involves knowing its conjuncts: one cannot know that A-and-B without thereby knowing A and knowing B. So anyone who came to know q would know that p, and would know that nobody knows p.

(6) Whatever is known is true. So from the second piece of knowledge it would follow that nobody knows p.

(7) But then the supposed knower of q would both know p (by the first conjunct) and be such that nobody knows p (by the second). No one can occupy that position, so q cannot be known — contrary to (4).

(8) The only assumption about knowability was (1). Hence the completeness ideal entails that the supposition in (2) is false: if every truth could in principle come to be known, then there is no unknown truth. Everything true is already known.

(9) So the completeness ideal, if it holds at all, collapses inquiry into its own completion: an inquiry capable of learning anything is one with nothing left to learn. Since inquiry just is the activity of coming to know what one does not yet know, the ideal undermines the very activity it was meant to govern, and is therefore incoherent.

I cannot accept (9). The distance between "could come to know" and "already knows" seems to me the most ordinary distance in epistemology — it is the distance a research program exists to cross — and an argument that erases it proves too much. If forced to hold the line somewhere, I would most like to keep (5) and (6): both strike me as close to constitutive of what knowledge is, and I suspect a theory that abandons either has changed the subject.

So: where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Please identify the specific step you reject, explain why it looked innocent, and say plainly what rejecting it costs. If your verdict is that the reasoning is valid, then tell me which premise is the least costly casualty, and sketch what a repaired ideal of inquiry — one that does not immediately regenerate the argument — would have to look like. Finally, I would like your view on whether the step from (7) to (8) involves any quiet modal or inferential move that a reader might accept without noticing they had accepted it.
