Having retired from forty years as a field ornithologist, I have lately joined a philosophy reading circle, and I have rashly volunteered to prepare the handout for our session on Quine's indeterminacy of translation. What I want is a decision tree, not an essay: an instrument that carries a member from an agreed starting point to a named position, so that we discover what each of us actually thinks rather than which label we find congenial.

The agreed starting point is that the behaviour of Quine's informant, who exclaims gavagai as rabbits run past, underdetermines the choice between translating the word as rabbit, undetached rabbit part, temporal stage of a rabbit, and the rest. From there the tree should proceed through no more than nine branch questions, each phrased so that a member can answer it without knowing where it leads, and each attached to a concrete case rather than stated as an abstraction. Because my circle keeps collapsing three distinctions I want kept apart, the early nodes must separate them: first, whether the underdetermination is merely a limitation on our evidence or the absence of any fact of the matter; second, whether the argument concerns the reference of terms or the meaning of whole utterances; third, whether it afflicts only the translation of alien tongues or the understanding of one's own neighbours as well.

The leaves should include, at minimum: Quine's own position (indeterminacy all the way down, home language included); the Davidsonian response (indeterminacy is real but as benign as the choice between Fahrenheit and Celsius scales, with interpretation otherwise fixed by charity); the innatist response associated with Chomsky (natural salience and built-in constraints fix reference, so there is a fact of the matter and Quine has shown only that evidence is thin); a Platonist realism about meanings (the fact of the matter obtains whether or not any creature could discover it); a use-theoretic response (meaning is exhausted by use, so once use is fixed nothing remains indeterminate); Kripke's sceptical position on rule-following; and my own tentative view, which I call ethological instrumentalism: a signal means whatever reading best predicts the signaller's subsequent conduct, and where two readings predict the same conduct there is nothing further to be right or wrong about — though I confess I cannot tell whether this is a thesis about meaning or merely about what fieldworkers need the word meaning for. The tree must locate my view and state which, if any, of the named positions it collapses into.

Please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first set out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the positions within that space, noting every combination nobody occupies and diagnosing in a sentence whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate — I suspect the Quinean and the Kripkean will prove troublesome — rather than papering over the gap.

Finally, route the following case down your finished tree. In my fieldwork I studied a wader whose alarm cry is given only when a harrier appears over the marsh; but harriers appear only on the strong onshore gusts that carry them inland, so that in every observed instance the cry accompanied both the bird and the gust. State, for each leaf, what the cry means: harrier, gust, or harrier-or-gust.

Write in British English throughout, and append no reading list: several members flatly refuse to do homework.
