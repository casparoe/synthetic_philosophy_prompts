My partner and I have been going back and forth about whether to have a kid, and my brother (who's childfree and pretty philosophical about it) hit me with an argument I can't shake. He says: if we have the kid and their life goes badly -- chronic illness, depression, whatever -- then a real person is suffering and it's on us for rolling those dice. But if we don't have the kid, there's nobody sitting around missing out on all the birthday parties and first kisses they would've had. Non-existent people can't be deprived. So the risks only point one way: creating a life can wrong someone, not creating one can't. Therefore the responsible move is not to.

This bugs me for a couple reasons I can't articulate well. First, by that logic it seems like even a couple who could give a kid a 99%-great life shouldn't reproduce, and that feels like it proves too much. Second, he himself is glad he was born, and he'd say it'd be a tragedy if humanity went extinct -- isn't that in tension with his own argument, or is there a principled difference between being glad you exist once you do and it being good that you come to exist?

So: is his reasoning sound, and if not where exactly does it break? I'd especially like to know whether the asymmetry he's leaning on (harms count, unrealized joys don't) is actually defensible, and what it would imply for other decisions if I accepted it -- like, does it also mean I should never adopt a dog from a shelter that might get sick? Give me real philosophical engagement, not just "do what feels right."
