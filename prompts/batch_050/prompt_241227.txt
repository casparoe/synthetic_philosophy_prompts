Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of forgiveness cases, and I want to know what principle (if any) is behind them, because they kind of feel like they contradict each other.

1. A friend borrowed two grand from me, dodged my texts for a year, then showed up with what I'm convinced was a totally sincere apology and paid me back with interest. If I'd still refused to forgive him after that, I think I'd have been doing something wrong -- not just being cold or petty, actually wrong. Like, he was *owed* forgiveness at that point.

2. A drunk driver killed a kid on my street. He was genuinely wrecked with guilt, served his time, wrote the mother a long letter asking forgiveness. I think she is not obligated to forgive him, not now, not ever, and anyone who told her she should "for her own sake" is out of line. If she does it someday, it's a gift, not a debt.

3. My grandmother forgave my grandfather for years of genuinely cruel behavior even though he went to his grave insisting he'd done nothing wrong. Never apologized once. I think what she did was real forgiveness and honestly kind of heroic, not doormat behavior.

4. But my cousin keeps "forgiving" her boyfriend everytime he cheats -- no apology, no changed behavior, rinse and repeat -- and I don't think that's forgiveness at all. She's just absorbing it. So apparently I think unconditional forgiveness is possible (case 3) and also that something that looks exactly like it isn't forgiveness (case 4).

5. My brother says he's forgiven the drunk driver from case 2 "on behalf of the family." I think that's nonsense. You can't forgive a wrong that was done to somebody else; the mother alone has standing there.

6. And yet when the residents of a town near me collectively forgave a company that had poisoned their river -- after reparations and a public admission -- that seemed perfectly coherent to me, even moving. So a group can do it, but my brother can't. Not sure that distinction survives scrutiny.

7. There's a guy who wronged me, apologized, made things right, and I fully believe him -- but I never want to see him again. I think I can completely forgive him AND cut him out of my life; forgiveness isn't reconciliation. A friend of mine says if I really forgave him I'd be open to the friendship, so what I have is partial forgiveness at best.

8. A guy I know messed up badly years ago, apologized, made full restitution, and literally everyone he hurt has forgiven him. He still can't forgive himself, a decade on, and it's ruining him. Weirdly I think he's failing at something -- like self-forgiveness can be obligatory too, and he's refusing to do his job.

So: case 1 says forgiveness can be owed, case 2 says it can't. Case 3 says it can be unconditional, case 4 says apparently not. Is there one account of forgiveness that delivers all eight verdicts, or close? If it helps, my own hunch is something about what the wrongdoer has "given" the victim, but that obviously breaks case 3. Give me your best candidate principle, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop if any, and tell me whether I'm secretly using "forgive" in two different senses somewhere in there. Also: what does your account say about a case I haven't listed -- forgiving someone who's dead, where nothing they do can ever condition it?
