I am a novelist in the early stages of a book whose premise is an updated ring of Gyges: my protagonist acquires a reliable, mundane form of untraceability — nothing magical, just a confluence of anonymizing tools and circumstances that guarantees he will never be identified or held to account for certain acts. While outlining him I have had to become explicit about my own commitments on the question Glaucon presses in the Republic, and I would like to know whether my working position is coherent before I build a plot on it. Here is where I stand. First, I believe most ordinarily decent people would not become monsters under guaranteed impunity; the ring would reveal fissures and compromises, not turn an average person into Gyges wholesale. Second, I hold something like a contractualist view of morality's authority: moral norms bind us because we are mutually accountable, and their claim on any individual is grounded in the practice of holding one another to account. Third, I believe that what a person does when no one is watching is the truest index of character — that unobserved action is where the self shows itself most honestly, which is precisely why my premise interests me as a novelist. Fourth, I think Glaucon's challenge deserves a real answer, not a deflection: justice has to be shown choiceworthy for the perfectly unobserved agent, on grounds other than reputation, punishment, or the risk of exposure, and a novel that could only dramatize the costs of getting caught would have artistically conceded the argument. Fifth, as a craft matter, I do not think fiction is obliged to punish its wrongdoers on the page; an ending in which my protagonist prospers unrepentant can be the honest one.

What I cannot tell is whether these five hang together, and I would like an audit rather than a seminar. Take the commitments pairwise where it matters and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. I have my own suspicions — for instance, my first and second commitments look like they might conflict, since a contractualist grounding of morality seems to predict the collapse of decent behavior once accountability vanishes, which is what my first commitment denies — but I also suspect some of these apparent collisions dissolve on inspection, and I would rather be told which ones than have everything flagged indiscriminately.

Please order any genuine tensions by severity, and for each, state exactly what would have to give: which commitment gets revised or abandoned, and what that revision costs me both philosophically and as a matter of what my novel can honestly depict. Where a tension is merely apparent, show briefly how it dissolves rather than just asserting that it does. Finally, if you had to recommend a single revision — one change to the set that resolves the most or the deepest of the problems — tell me which it is and why, bearing in mind that I am more willing to adjust my philosophical commitments than my judgment about how ordinary people actually behave.
