I run the translation side of a tiny poetry press. Every month we publish five or six French poems in English, and the translators keep falling into the same fight: one person wants to keep the metre and rhyme even if the imagery has to be bent out of shape, while another produces a fluent English poem that keeps the images but quietly abandons the whole formal game. Reading about the translation puzzles around Le Ton beau de Marot has made me think the problem is that we have no shared way of deciding which formal features are actually doing meaningful work rather than merely being present.

Could you turn that idea into a practical procedure our translators can follow? The working principle should be something like: a formal feature counts as part of the poem’s content when changing it changes what the poem does, not just how it sounds. I want that made usable by someone who is a good translator but hasn’t read translation theory.

Please include:

- an ordered decision procedure, from first reading of the French poem to final editorial sign-off;
- the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure should stop with a no rather than allowing endless tinkering;
- a distinction between load-bearing form, replaceable formal vehicles, semantic obligations, tone and register, deliberate ambiguity, and mere stylistic preference;
- rules for when preserving the effect of a device beats reproducing the device itself, and when that move is just an excuse for rewriting;
- some kind of fidelity budget or trade-off test for cases where not everything can survive, but without pretending that a numerical score can make the judgement objective;
- instructions for producing at least three candidate versions, such as a source-close version, a function-first version and a constraint-first version, then comparing them systematically;
- the points at which judgement cannot be eliminated and the decision has to go to the senior editor;
- what the translator must disclose in a short note to readers, especially when the result has crossed the line from translation into adaptation.

The practical constraints are: the translator normally has about two hours per poem, a short note from the poet if we are lucky, and no direct access to the poet during the first pass. I make the final call, but I only want cases escalated when there is a genuine conflict of values, not whenever the translator feels unsure.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. A fourteen-line French poem in strict alexandrines with an elaborate rhyme scheme. The rhymes are skilful but mostly conventional, while the crucial event is that the final couplet shifts from vous to tu for the first time. English cannot reproduce that pronoun distinction naturally.

2. An Oulipo-style French poem that avoids the letter e, supposedly because the speaker cannot bring herself to name her absent lover, whose name contains several es. A no-e English version is possible, but it makes the diction bizarre in a way the French original apparently is not.

3. A concrete poem arranged as a narrowing spiral, with the repeated refrain becoming physically smaller as the speaker’s thoughts become more obsessive. It has to appear both in print and on an accessible website, where screen-reader users will not encounter the visual spiral.

At least one worked application should give an answer I probably won’t like, such as telling us not to publish the cleverest version under the label translation. Please use British English, and don’t turn this into an essay about translation theory; I need something my translators can actually use on deadline, occassionally under protest.
