In an introductory course on classical Chinese philosophy I was taught the School of Names' celebrated one-liner, 'a white horse is not a horse', which our textbook credited to the logician Gongsun Long. Last week a friend invoked it to settle a tedious dispute about whether a hot dog is a sandwich: if the ancients could prove that a white horse is not a horse, he argued, then membership in the narrower kind never secures membership in the broader one, so a hot dog need not count as a sandwich even under the definition of 'sandwich' I had offered. I suspect he is trading on an equivocation, but I cannot say precisely where it lies. Please reconstruct the distinct claims this slogan could be making — for instance, a claim about the non-identity of two classes, a claim about the non-identity of two concepts or meanings, a claim exploiting the ambiguity of 'is not' as between identity, predication, and class inclusion, a thesis about how compound names fix their reference differently from simple ones, or the genuinely radical claim that white horses fall outside the class of horses altogether — and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false. Then tell me which readings the traditional arguments for the slogan (such as the point that a request for a horse can be satisfied by a white horse, whereas a request for a white horse cannot be satisfied by a brown one) would actually support, and which, if any, would vindicate my friend's deployment of it in the hot dog dispute.
