Two colleagues of mine at the magazine got into a real argument over coffee and it's still bugging me, so I'm outsourcing the refereeing to you (ironically, given the topic). The fight was about whether leaning on AI chatbots is eroding our understanding in some way that's actually different from older forms of cognitive offloading. Rough transcript:

(1) Dana's opening move: offloading is offloading. Nobody says a pilot who uses autopilot or an accountant who uses Excel has "lost understanding" -- we offload to calculators, GPS, spellcheck, and notebooks all the time, and the skill that matters shifts from doing the thing to checking the thing. AI is just the next step.

(2) Marcus's reply: the difference is you can't check it. A calculator's output is auditable -- you can rerun the computation by hand if it matters. An AI summary or analysis can't be spot-checked without redoing the whole job yourself, which defeats the point of offloading. So you're not delegating a task, you're delegating the judgment, and accepting outputs on pure trust.

(3) Dana's counter: that's overstated. We accept stuff on pure trust constantly -- we trust editors, fact-checkers, colleagues, textbooks, and we can't re-derive any of that either. Social epistemic dependence is ancient and mostly fine, so why is trusting a model categorically worse than trusting a senior colleague whose reasoning you also can't fully audit?

(4) Marcus: because the colleague can explain herself when challenged, and being explainable-on-demand is part of what makes her trustworthy in the first place. Also, the colleague has skin in the game -- her reputation rides on being right. The model has neither property. And there's a second issue Dana keeps dodging: skills atrophy with disuse. Even if each individual act of offloading is rational, the cumulative effect is a newsroom full of people who can no longer tell a good argument from a plausible-sounding one, because you only learn to spot bad reasoning by doing alot of reasoning.

(5) Dana's closer: the atrophy argument proves too much -- it would indict writing itself (Plato's worry), printing, and search engines, and empirically we adapted to all of those by changing what we practice rather than by becoming uniformly dumber. Maybe we lose the ability to do first drafts and gain the ability to evaluate drafts, and the second skill is the one a journalist actually needs.

So, two questions. First, go through the five moves and say who's right at each step -- is the auditability distinction (move 2) real, does the trust-in-colleagues analogy (move 3) dissolve it, and does the atrophy point (move 4) survive the "we adapted before" response (move 5)? Second, is there something both of them are missing -- e.g., a difference between offloading the *product* of thinking versus offloading the *process*, or between individual and collective understanding? I'd like an actual verdict, not both-sides mush. Please answer in American English.
