I run a small graduate reading group in moral philosophy, and we have now spent three sessions on the aggregation problem for contractualism — the question of whether, in Scanlon's framework, an act can be justified to each person only by appeal to individual complaints, and what happens to that idea when many people's small stakes compete with one person's large stake. The standard cases are on the table: saving one stranger versus saving five strangers from the same fate; curing a vast number of people's mild headaches versus saving one person's life; John Taurek's old proposal that in a choice between saving one and saving five you should give each person an equal chance, perhaps by flipping a coin, rather than simply rescuing the larger group. We have also read Scanlon's own attempt to hold the line — his thought that smaller burdens on some cannot simply be pooled into a sum that outweighs a greater burden on another, and his suggestion that in the rescue case each member of the larger group has a complaint of the same magnitude as the one person's, so the tie is broken by the additional claim of the second, third, fourth, and fifth person rather than by any sum.

What struck me is that after three sessions nobody had moved an inch, and I am no longer sure what we are disagreeing about. The consequentialists in the group keep pressing that refusing to count numbers makes the one person's claim do all the work and treats the other four as morally weightless — that the tie-break story smuggles aggregation in through the back door while officially denying it, and that if you accept the tie-break in the five-versus-one case you owe an explanation of why it disappears in the headaches-versus-life case. The contractualists reply that pooling burdens across persons is precisely what the separateness of persons rules out, that a million headaches suffered by a million different people is not a burden anyone bears, and that the consequentialists are begging the question by describing each person in the larger group as an "additional claim" at all. When I pressed the contractualists on the coin flip, they split among themselves: some found Taurek's proposal perverse, others found it uncomfortably hard to rule out given contractualism's own premises. Meanwhile one quiet member suggested the whole dispute is really about the word "justifiable" — that the two sides agree about which outcomes are better and which acts are right, and disagree only about which vocabulary to use when saying so.

Here is what I would like from you. Diagnose this debate rather than adjudicating it. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions — one about what each individual is owed, one about what states of affairs are to be brought about — under a single heading? If substantive, locate it: do the sides differ about which cases count as data (rescue cases versus large-number small-benefit cases), about the standards a moral principle must meet, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work — for instance, whether a pairwise complaint-comparison rule can be extended to many-person cases without collapsing into aggregation or generating intransitivities — that could be settled independently of the intuitions? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the impasse persists because both sides tacitly accept that a single principle must govern all pairwise trade-offs, and that dropping that assumption changes the debate entirely. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, a formal result about complaint-based decision rules, a new case that both sides would have to take seriously, or a reframing of the question — or, if you think no progress should be expected, explain why the impasse is what this kind of disagreement looks like when both sides have correctly understood each other.
