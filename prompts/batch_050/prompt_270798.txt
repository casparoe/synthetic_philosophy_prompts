Been picking through ergodicity economics lately and I think I've either found a hole in it or I'm missing something basic. Take the coin flip game that always gets used to motivate it: you start with $100, each round heads your wealth goes up 50%, tails it drops 40%. Expected value per round is +5% (0.5 × 1.5 + 0.5 × 0.6 = 1.05), but supposedly almost every individual player bleeds out over time because the time-average growth factor is sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) ≈ 0.95, so −5% per round.

Here's my problem. If I run a simulation with 10 million players for 1000 rounds, the average wealth across players should converge to the expected value trajectory — that's just the law of large numbers, right? So the ensemble average really does grow at 5% per round. But an average is literally the total divided by N. You can't have the total wealth compounding at +5% while almost everyone in the pool is going broke. One of the two claims has to give. So which is it: does the LLN somehow fail for this game (seems impossible, the flips are independent?), or is the "typical players go broke" claim exagerated?

And a follow-up, because this is where I get stuck conceptually: if the ensemble average genuinely grows while typical individuals lose, in what sense is the ensemble average the "wrong" or "unrealistic" thing to look at? It's not a fiction, it's what actually happens to the aggregate wealth. If a pension fund pools a million people playing this game, the fund grows. Doesn't that mean the ensemble average is the right tool after all, at least whenever you can pool?
