So here's my situation. I'm a novelist -- midlist, four books, respectable but nobody's picking me for juries out of glamour -- and somehow I've ended up chairing the five-person jury for a regional first-novel prize, decent money, a fair bit of press. We've narrowed it to two finalists and we are deadlocked and the announcement is in three weeks, so I need to draft the memo that breaks the tie and I want it to actually be defensible, not just diplomatic mush.

Finalist one: a genuinely dazzling book. Funny, structurally inventive, the kind of thing that makes you jealous. The author wrote it in about seven weeks, by her own cheerful account in interviews, first draft barely touched. Finalist two: a very good book, but everyone on the jury agrees it's a half-step below -- slightly saggy middle, less original. But this author has been at it for fourteen years. Four unpublished novels in a drawer, wrote this one in 5am shifts around caring for her father through a long illness, and you can feel the cost of it on the page, in a good way.

Two of my fellow jurors are basically arguing that finalist two *deserves* the prize -- that the award should recognize the labor and the life, not just the artifact. The other two say a prize judges the book, full stop, and that rewarding struggle is both patronizing and impossible to administer -- next year everyone shows up with a suffering narrative, and anyway how would we verify the seven-weeks claim or rank heartbreak against heartbreak? There's also a wrinkle: we've just learned finalist one's novel is a heavily revised version of a manuscript that was serialized, under a different title, in a small online magazine about eight years ago. Nobody read it, but it was technically published. Our rules say "first novel" and define it as the author's first novel-length work of fiction to be published. So we may not even have the deadlock we think we have -- or we may have a worse one, because throwing out the best book on a technicality that she plausibly never noticed feels awful.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate to the jury. It needs a clear bottom line -- who gets the prize this year, or whether we disqualify finalist one, and a one-paragraph policy recommendation for future years so we're not here again. And please actually engage the strongest version of the side you come down against: if you think the prize goes to the best book, take seriously the thought that effort and circumstance aren't nothing, that a culture which only ever crowns the effortless has something wrong with it; if you think desert in the fuller sense matters, take seriously the objection that juries can't see effort, only books, and that pretending otherwise is just sentimentality with a trophy. Casual is fine, typos and all, but the reasoning has to hold up when the loser reads about it in the paper.
