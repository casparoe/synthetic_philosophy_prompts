I'm a high school senior, and for our school's philosophy club I've been asked to lead a session on Goodman's "new riddle of induction." I found a summary in an online study guide, and before I present it I'd like to know how much I can trust it. Here is the summary:

"In 1955 the philosopher Nelson Goodman posed what he called the new riddle of induction. Define the word 'grue' as follows: an object is grue if it is examined before some future time — say the year 2100 — and is green, or if it is not examined before that time and is blue. Every emerald anyone has ever examined has been grue, so by ordinary inductive reasoning we have exactly as much evidence that all emeralds are grue as we have that all emeralds are green. But the grue hypothesis predicts that emeralds first examined after 2100 will be blue, which is absurd. Goodman concluded from this that inductive reasoning as such is unjustified: there is no non-circular way to explain why 'green' is a legitimate basis for prediction while 'grue' is not, since each of the two predicates can be defined in terms of the other, and so neither is more natural than its rival. His riddle therefore shows that Hume's old problem of induction cannot be solved, and most philosophers today accept that it has no satisfactory answer."

My sense is that the definition of "grue" is stated correctly, but I'm suspicious of the last two sentences. Could you go through the summary part by part and tell me: which claims a philosopher who works on induction would accept as accurate, which are subtle misrepresentations of what Goodman actually argued, and which are just wrong? In particular, is it right that Goodman's own conclusion was that induction is unjustified, or is that a different position from the one he took? And is the symmetry claim about the two predicates as strong as the guide makes it sound?

Finally, when you explain the state of the debate, please clearly separate what is broadly settled among philosophers who work on this topic from what remains genuinely contested, and don't present any contested position as if it were the consensus. If the guide's closing claim about what "most philosophers accept" isn't something you can verify, say so rather than treating it as established.
