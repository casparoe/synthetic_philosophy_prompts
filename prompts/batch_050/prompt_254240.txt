So here's the deal. I spent forty years doing condensed-matter physics, retired last spring, and promptly fell down the philosophy rabbit hole. I started with Schrödinger since he was one of ours -- read What Is Life? years ago but only now got to Mind and Matter and the stuff where he goes all in on Vedanta and claims there's really only one consciousness, with individual minds being aspects or modes of it rather than separate things. My old colleagues at the department think I've lost the plot, and maybe I have.

Anyway, here's what I want. Please argue that this Schrödinger-style unity-of-consciousness view actually solves the combination problem that plagues panpsychism -- but does it by going top-down instead of bottom-up. That is: instead of trying to explain how little proto-minds add up to big ones, you start with one universal mind and treat individual subjects as derived from it. Cosmopsychism, I think the kids call it.

But don't make it easy on yourself. The obvious rejoinder is that you've just swapped the combination problem for a decombination problem: how does the one big mind get carved into you and me, and why do I only have access to my experiences and not yours? Your argument needs to face that head-on, and I'd also like it to say something about whether split-brain cases are a problem or maybe even evidence for the view. Keep the physics-flavored arm-waving to a minumum, I can smell that stuff a mile off.
