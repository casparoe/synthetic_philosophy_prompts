Write a Shakespearean sonnet (fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, rhymed ABAB CDCD EFEF GG) set inside Black's two-sphere universe: a world containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, alike in every intrinsic and relational respect, suspended in an otherwise featureless void. The poem is voiced by one sphere attempting to address the other, and the three quatrains should stage three escalating failures of singular reference: the first quatrain tries address by properties and finds every predicate shared; the second tries address by relations and finds each one symmetric, so that whatever holds of the addressee holds equally of the speaker; the third reaches for bare demonstratives, only to concede that either sphere could utter them with exactly the same right, so the words pick out neither. Let the volta, entering at the third quatrain or the couplet, turn from the linguistic failure to the metaphysical question the scene is built to pose: whether the spheres' being two consists in anything more than a bare thisness, a haecceity with no qualitative content, and whether Leibniz's rejoinder -- that a rational creator could have no sufficient reason to actualize such a world, since there is nothing to choose between its supposed arrangements -- dissolves the puzzle or simply declines it. The closing couplet must commit: either affirm that the spheres are two and that identity outruns discernibility, or side with Leibniz that the allegedly distinct worlds are one description counted twice -- and the final line should make that commitment cost the speaker something, since whichever side it takes, it must then say whether its attempted address ever had a target at all. The metaphysics should be accurate enough that someone who knows the debate between Black and the friends of the identity of indiscernibles can locate each quatrain in it, while a reader who knows none of it should still find a coherent, affecting poem about trying to speak to someone indistinguishable from oneself.
