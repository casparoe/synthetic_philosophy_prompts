I'm putting together a first-year unit on infinity and want to pair Thomson's lamp with Borges's The Library of Babel, since the librarians' situation looks to me like a supertask problem wearing a costume. Every book that could exist exists somewhere in the library -- including a catalog of catalogs, and the single book that supposedly ciphers and compends all the others -- so the tragedy isnt that knowledge is missing but that no search procedure can terminate. And Thomson's lamp is exactly about why "just do the search faster and faster" is no fix: accelerate through infinitely many steps and you get no coherent final state, while searching at a constant pace never finishes even with eternity to burn. Read the story against the paradox properly. Is that a fair diagnosis of the librarians' despair, and where does the story resist it -- for instance the narrator's claim that the library is unending but periodic, which would let a search succeed by luck rather than by completion, or the superstition that a Man of the Book has already read the one book that matters? Finally, does the story get something right about infinity that the lamp argument misses, like what it does to people to live inside a task they know can't be completed? I want a reading I can actually hand to students that takes both the story and the supertask paradox seriously, and is honest about where the analogy breaks down.
