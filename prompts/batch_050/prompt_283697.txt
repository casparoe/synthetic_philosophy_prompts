I'm in a grad reading group on philosophy of mind and I keep losing the same argument and it's getting annoying. We're on epiphenomenalism, and the standard objection came up: if qualia don't cause anything, then they don't cause my saying "this red looks really intense to me," so my judgments about my own experience are causally disconnected from the experience itself — zombie-me makes all the same reports. Our resident epiphenomenalist pushes back that the judgment isn't merely caused by the quale, it's partly constituted by it, or that there's a special acquaintance relation that doesn't need causation. I feel like this is cheating but I can't articulate why.

So here's the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can: epiphenomenalism can give an account of phenomenal judgment on which my judgment that I'm having a reddish experience is genuinely about that very experience, and justified by it — i.e., the paradox of phenomenal judgment is escapable without sneaking causation back in thru the back door. To sharpen it, consider a wrinkle I came up with (tell me if it changes anything): if epiphenomenalism is true, it should be metaphysically possible to "pinch off" the phenomenal properties from a brain while leaving all the physics untouched, and the person would keep reporting vivid pain exactly as before, since the physics was doing all the reporting anyway. Call the pinched-off state a ghost. My intuition is that if ghost-reports and genuine reports are physically identical, no constitutive relation can rescue the genuine ones, because the relation was supposed to hold between the experience and the judgment, and the judgment is exactly what it is either way. But maybe that's too quick.

Give me a number, decomposed. Use your own terms if mine are wrong, but roughly: (a) the probability that justified belief about X actually requires a causal connection to X — this seems to be doing all the work in the zombie intuition, but doesn't it also threaten a lot of math and ethics beliefs?; (b) the probability that a constitutive or acquaintance account can be spelled out non-circularly, given that it has to work for a theory which says qualia cause nothing at all; (c) the probability that the paradox, if it's fatal, is fatal only to epiphenomenalism and not also to interactionist dualism, or even to physicalism under some descriptions. Then combine them into your credence on the proposition, say which term you'd revise most after reading one more paper, and what that paper would have to show.
