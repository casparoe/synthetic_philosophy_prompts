I teach first-year intro logic, about 25 students, one 50-minute session. We've just covered modus ponens as a formal rule. I want an activity that makes them *feel* the problem in Carroll's tortoise dialogue -- that you can't justify a rule of inference to someone by adding more premises, because they'd still need to grant the step from those premises to the conclusion -- before I ever name it. My idea is roughly: students play some kind of justification game in pairs where one side has to extract an endorsement of an inference step the other side hasn't granted, and discovers that stacking extra conditionals into the premise-set never quite closes the gap. But I'm not sure that mechanic actually forces the regress rather than just letting students stall, so design the actual activity: concrete rules, what materials or scripts the pairs get, and a way to guarantee the regress bites even if a student tries to dodge by just agreeing to everything. The debrief should map what happened in the game onto the distinction between premises and rules of inference, say what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the paradox the game can't reproduce. Commit to one design -- don't hand me a menu of options to pick between, make the calls and defend them. Structure your answer as: The game: rules and materials (~200 words), Running it: rounds and the facilitator's moves (~200 words), The debrief (~150 words), What will go wrong and how to handle it (~100 words), What the game can't show (~100 words).
