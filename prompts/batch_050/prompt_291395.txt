For the past year I've run a small reading group — none of us has any academic training in philosophy — working through translations of early Chinese texts, and our longest-running argument has been the dispute between the Mohists and the Confucians over impartial versus graded concern. The group has hardened into two camps: a Mohist camp that takes Mozi's case for inclusive care to be fundamentally sound, and a Confucian camp that sides with Mencius's reply, including the exchange in which he presses the Mohist Yi Zhi on whether concern for strangers could have any psychological foundation at all if it does not grow out of love for one's own parents. After months of going in circles, we've agreed to stop debating orally and instead produce, in writing, the kind of joint statement two serious opponents might sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I'd like you to draft that document for us.

Here is what it needs to contain. First, a numbered agreed-claims section: statements both camps can sign about what the Mohist and Confucian positions actually demand in practice, including the caricatures neither side accepts — that Mohists forbid loving your own parents more than anyone else's, and that Confucians license ignoring everyone outside your clan. Second, a disagreements table giving the precise remaining points of difference, each formulated so that the side whose view it describes would accept the wording as fair. Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that resolving them would genuinely move at least one camp. Please include at least the following candidates and say whether each is a real crux or only an apparent one: (a) whether inclusive care, as the Mohists describe it, requires equal concern for everyone or merely non-exclusion — our group's Mohist insists the texts demand only the latter, which, if right, seems to shrink the disagreement considerably; (b) Mencius's objection to Yi Zhi that a doctrine of undifferentiated care has no root in human motivation and so can be professed but not lived; (c) whether the Confucian owes a principled account of where gradation stops, failing which the position collapses into exactly the nepotism and particularism the Mohists attacked; and (d) whether the two views diverge in conduct at all — both condemn the son who abandons his father, and both endorse extending concern beyond the family — or whether the whole dispute is about justification rather than action.

Finally, for each crux that survives as genuine, supply a case or question both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. As a starting example, consider a triage case where limited resources can either go to one's own relatives or relieve greater suffering among distant strangers: state what each side would prescribe, and have each side say whether there is any concrete situation in which its rival's prescription would be one it could not itself endorse — because if neither can produce such a situation, the practical disagreement may be smaller than the rhetoric suggests. If you conclude that part of the dispute is really about words rather than substance, name that part explicitly and explain why you think so.
