ok so my roommate is writing a psych paper and roped me into an argument about self-deception, and I built this little proof that its impossible and now neither of us can find the hole even though the conclusion has to be wrong. Here it is, numbered so we fight about it properly.

(1) Self-deception is a case of deception, just one where the deceiver and the deceived happen to be the same person. So whatever deception requires in the ordinary two-person case has to be required here too, minus one person.

(2) In ordinary deception, the deceiver doesn't believe the thing she's selling (she knows the watch is fake) and she *intentionally* gets the victim to believe it anyway.

(3) So self-deception requires: believing not-p yourself, and intentionally bringing it about that you come to believe p.

(4) But you can't intentionally make yourself believe something you currently believe is false. Belief isn't like holding your breath. To pull it off you'd have to keep your plan in mind — "I'm doing this so I'll end up accepting something I know isn't true" — and keeping that plan in mind poisons the result, since a belief you installed in yourself on purpose, for reasons you know full well aren't evidence, isn't something you can actually take to be true. (Same reason you can't win the "believe it's Tuesday for a million dollars" prize just by trying really hard.)

(5) And even if you somehow managed it, at the moment of success you'd believe p while still believing not-p, and nobody can flat-out believe a contradiction.

(6) So self-deception is impossible.

(7) But people plainly do it all the time — the smoker who's genuinely unbothered by the statistics, my uncle about his son's "borrowed" car, me every semester about how much reading I can catch up on in the last week.

(8) Contradiction. Something above has to go.

Which step do I reject, and what's the tab for rejecting it? (7) is the one thing I'm not giving up, and I'd really like to keep (2) since it seems like the one non-negotiable part of what deception even is — but tell me what I actually have to pay to keep it. Also: if the argument goes through as written, doesn't it prove too much? Like, wouldn't it also rule out plain old wishful thinking and motivated reasoning, which even my roommate admits are real? That feels like a symptom of whatever's wrong with it but I can't quite say why.
