I'm a PhD student in computer security, and my lab works on hardware random number generators, the little circuits that are supposed to turn thermal noise or shot noise into bits for cryptographic keys. The way these things get certified, both in academia and in industry standards, is essentially: run the device's output through a battery of statistical test suites (the NIST suite, Dieharder, TestU01, that sort of thing), which compute p-values for things like frequency of 0s and 1s, runs, compressibility, spectral properties, and if the stream passes enough of them the device gets stamped as producing "random" output. I've also been sitting in on a philosophy of probability reading group this term (long story, a friend roped me in) where we did algorithmic randomness -- Martin-Löf tests, Kolmogorov complexity, the invariance theorem -- and I keep noticing that the two pictures don't line up at all, and it's made me wonder what my own field's certification practice actually presupposes about what randomness *is*.

The tensions I can see: first, an ideal random source has some nonzero probability of spitting out a million zeros in a row, and that stream would fail every test in the battery, so the practice will reject outputs that a genuinely random process can produce, whilst happily accepting the digits of pi or the output of a decent PRNG with a 32-bit seed. So it seems to treat randomness as a property of the sequence, not of the process that generated it -- which is backwards from how everyone in my lab talks, since they all say the randomness is "in the physics". Second, algorithmic randomness proper is defined for infinite sequences, and the finite version via Kolmogorov complexity is only fixed up to an additive constant that depends on your choice of universal machine, so there's no machine-independent fact of the matter about whether a given finite string is random -- yet the test suites issue a definite verdict, and regulators act on it. A labmate of mine says the tests are really just measuring compressibility-relative-to-a-fixed-toolkit and everyone knows it, but that seems to undermine the whole point of certification?

So here's what I want: what conception of randomness does this certification practice actually presuppose? Does it only make sense under a product/sequence view, or can a process view (randomness as a propensity of the source) be squared with testing outputs at all, given that any output is compatible with any source? Which rival accounts -- von Mises-style frequentism, propensity views, complexity-based views -- does it quietly rule out, and is the practice even internally coherent, or is it a fudge that works for engineering reasons while being philosophically indefensible? And what would concretely have to change in the certification pipeline if we replaced the presupposed view with (a) a pure process view, where presumably you'd certify the physical model and the entropy estimates and ignore the output entirely, or (b) a strict complexity view, where verdicts are only ever machine-relative and you'd have to fix a reference machine by fiat?
