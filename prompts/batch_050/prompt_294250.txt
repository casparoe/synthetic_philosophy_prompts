A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle — the case in which a billionaire offers you an enormous sum if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make you painfully ill for a day, where the payment depends entirely on your holding the intention at midnight and not at all on whether you actually drink. We parted without resolution, and I'd like to know whether my own position is even internally coherent before I defend it again. Here is where I've landed: (1) given the prize, it would be rational to form the intention to drink before midnight; (2) once midnight has passed and the money is secured, actually drinking would be irrational, and I already know I would not drink; (3) one cannot rationally intend to do what one is confident one will not do — intending requires at least the belief that one will act; (4) rationality evaluates each choice at the time it is made, so there is no free-standing rational requirement to follow through on earlier intentions; (5) intentions are not predictions — settling on a course of action is a different mental act from forecasting one's own behavior. Which of these genuinely conflict, which only appear to, and what is the least costly revision? Please order the tensions by severity, and for each one state exactly what has to give.
