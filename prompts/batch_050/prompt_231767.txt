Yesterday evening I got into a protracted argument with a friend — an unrepentant one-boxer with sympathies for functional decision theory — and came away less certain of my own position than I'd like to admit. I'm preparing to defend my views in a reading group next week, and before I do, I want an audit of whether the position I articulated in the argument actually hangs together. Here is what I found myself defending:

(a) Causal decision theory is the correct theory of rational choice, and in the standard Newcomb problem — where the predictor merely observed me in the past and is highly but fallibly accurate — two-boxing is the rational choice. (b) A decision rule whose users are foreseeably and systematically poorer than the users of an available alternative, across the whole population of cases it governs, is thereby defective; rationality shouldn't make you predictably worse off. (c) Strict dominance reasoning is indefeasible: if one act yields at least as good an outcome as another in every causally relevant state of the world, and strictly better in some, it is irrational not to take it. (d) In the variant where the predictor's accuracy derives from having run a detailed simulation of my actual deliberative process the day before — so that the simulated me faced the identical problem and the predictor simply recorded what simulated-me did — one-boxing is clearly correct, because the content of the opaque box is, in a sense I couldn't quite pin down in the argument, "up to me" in a way it isn't in the standard case. (e) In epistemology I'm a thoroughgoing evidentialist: one should update on all available evidence, including evidence about what one's own choices indicate, but this epistemic duty has no bearing on which act is choiceworthy, since evaluating acts and evaluating beliefs are simply different projects.

My friend claimed my position is a patchwork — that I was switching decision theories mid-argument depending on which verdict I wanted, and dressing it up as nuance. I don't think that's fair, but I couldn't cleanly rebut it either. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with each other, and which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for each, say what would have to give — which commitment could be revised or refined at the lowest overall cost to the position, and what that revision would forfeit. I'd like a diagnosis, not a survey of decision theories.
