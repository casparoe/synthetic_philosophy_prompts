A commentator I follow argues that the United States is replaying the final century of the Roman Republic, and he makes the case quite precisely rather than as a vague declinist gesture. His analogy runs like this: the Republic's constitution was almost entirely unwritten custom (mos maiorum) rather than enforceable law, and so is America's real constitutional order, which runs on norms (forbearance, conceding elections, not using every legal power to the hilt). Tiberius Gracchus's tribunate in 133 BC — bending the rules of office, being killed by senators who claimed emergency justification — is the template for how norm-breaking escalates tit-for-tat until violence is normalized. The Senate's capture by a wealthy landholding elite maps onto modern donor politics; the festering grievance of the Italian allies over citizenship, which exploded in the Social War, maps onto today's fights over immigration and the franchise. His conclusion: since Rome's sequence ran roughly eighty years from the Gracchi to Augustus, we should expect a comparable sequence ending in an American autocrat who keeps the republican forms while hollowing them out, and the only question is how far along we are.

Audit this analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the tribunate's veto and sacrosanctity, of collegiality and annual terms, of the optimates/populares divide (and is it really a party divide in either case?), of the Senate, of the client army personally loyal to and paid by its commander, of the Italian allies, of Augustus's settlement that left republican institutions standing. Then the disanalogies, and please separate the ones that matter for his conclusion from the ones that don't: I suspect the personal client army has no modern analogue and that this does more work than anything else, but also that Rome lacked mass media, parties in the modern sense, and any mechanism for peaceful constitutional amendment, while we lack Rome's fusion of military command with domestic political careers. Which of these cuts against his conclusion and which cuts deeper in its favor? Does his prediction — eighty years to an Augustus — survive the audit, or is it being carried entirely by the analogy's surface neatness? If a better historical analogy exists for norm erosion in a modern democracy, say what it is and what it would predict differently.
