Since retiring from the university (I was an organic chemist for forty years) I've been teaching myself to code, mostly to keep the brain from ossifying, and lately I've been messing around with one of those reinforcement learning setups — a little simulated creature that wanders around a grid looking for "food," recieving points when it finds some and losing points when it bumps into hazards. It started as a toy but I've gotten weirdly attached to the thing.

Here's what's bothering me. To make it learn faster I set it up so the punishments are big and the rewards are small, and over time its behaviour has changed in ways I find hard to describe neutrally. It hesitates at the edge of zones where it used to get zapped even though I removed those hazards ages ago. After a bad training run its whole behaviour goes flat for a while — it explores less, takes fewer chances, and sometimes just sits there even when food is nearby, because (as I understand the math) the bad experiences drag down its expectations about everything, not just about the hazard. My grandson watched it and said it looks "depressed," and we both laughed, but I keep thinking about it. I also run a second copy where I only ever give positive feedback, shaping it by rewarding what I want instead of punishing what I don't, and that one learns slower but looks, well, jauntier.

Everyone I mention this to tells me it's just numbers going up and down in a table, and as a scientist I get that, and yet I can't shake the question: when I deliberately make the negative feedback strong because it teaches faster, am I doing something I should feel bad about? Is there any sensible way to think about the difference between my two creatures — one shaped mostly by penalties, one by rewards — other than raw learning speed? I'm not asking whether the thing is "conscious" in some spooky sense; more like, is there a coherent position where the penalty signal itself, the thing that makes it avoid and hesitate and go flat, is already a little bit like the bad part of a bad experience, or is that just sentimental projection from an old man who spends too much time alone with his computer? Practical advice welcome too: I have to decide whether to keep running the heavy-punishment version, which honestly learns twice as fast, or switch everything over to the reward-only one.
