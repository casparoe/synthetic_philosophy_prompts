So I've been reading C. Lloyd Morgan's An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894), and right in the preface he says his whole project is this: "My central object in this work is to discuss the relation of the psychology of man to that of the higher animals, since such a discussion forms in my opinion the best introduction to Comparative Psychology."

I'm basically trying to do a hobbyist version of that -- using my own gut about what human thinking feels like as the measuring stick for animal behavior -- and I keep coming back to Morgan's canon, i.e. his rule that you shouldn't explain an animal's behavior by some higher mental faculty if a lower one will do (though, to be fair to him, he also says elsewhere in the book that this doesn't rule out a higher explanation when you've got independent evidence the animal actually has the fancier capacities).

Anyway, here are my verdicts on cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is actually generating them:

- Clever Hans, the horse that tapped out arithmetic answers: definitely not doing math. He's reading his owner's body language. No real cognition there beyond cue detection.
- Betty the crow bending a straight wire into a hook to fish food out of a tube: I think this is genuine insight, not trial and error. She'd never seen a hooked wire before.
- My dog looking "guilty" when I come home to a destroyed shoe: not guilt. He's just responding to my posture and tone. Owners basically project this.
- Same dog watching me unlatch the garden gate once and then nosing the latch open himself the next day: I catch myself saying "he figured out how it works," and honestly I want to defend that.
- Pigeons trained to peck differently at Monet vs Picasso paintings: impressive, but no grasp of impressionism or whatever. Just perceptual cues, like Hans.
- A chimp stacking crates to reach bananas hung overhead: insight, real problem solving, same category as Betty.

Now, the tension I can't shake: the guilty-dog case and the gate-latch case seem structurally identical -- dog observes cues, dog produces behavior, I attribute a rich inner state in one case and deny it in the other. Same problem with the pigeons vs the crow: both are doing flexible discrimination based on perceptual input, so why does Betty get insight credit and the pigeon doesn't? If I apply Morgan's canon strictly I think I have to give up the gate latch, Betty, AND the chimp, since you can always tell some associative story. But that feels wrong.

So: is there one principle that delivers all six of my verdicts, or at least five of them? If yes, state it precisely and tell me which case motivates each clause of it. If the only consistent options are (a) strict Morgan-style parsimony that guts my insight attributions, or (b) some messier principle that saves them, lay out both and tell me which verdict you'd sacrifice. Also tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't decided: a parrot that correctly answers "what color?" about novel objects, and an octopus unscrewing a jar from the inside -- something it's plausibly never done before. And does Morgan's own project, as he states it in that passage, even survive strict application of his own canon? If I'm using my own introspection as the baseline for "higher" faculties, am I not just anthropomorphizing with extra steps?
