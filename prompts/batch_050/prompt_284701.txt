Here is the essay:

---

**The Thermostat Is Not a Policy**

Stratospheric aerosol injection -- spraying reflective particles into the upper atmosphere to bounce back a fraction of incoming sunlight -- is widely described as cheap, fast, and imperfect: cheap enough that a single mid-sized state could afford it unilaterally, fast enough to lower global average temperatures within a few years, and imperfect in that it masks warming without touching its cause, leaving ocean acidification and regional climate disruptions largely unaddressed. The philosophical debate has clustered around two objections. The first is moral hazard: if a technological sunscreen is available, governments and publics will lose their nerve on emissions cuts, and we will end up with both the aerosols and the carbon. The second is termination shock: because the particles wash out of the stratosphere within a year or two, an abrupt halt to deployment -- through war, sabotage, budget cuts, or simple loss of political will -- would release the pent-up warming in a decade rather than a century, a rate of change to which few ecosystems or food systems could adapt.

I want to suggest that the first objection is mislabelled, and that the mislabelling matters. Moral hazard, properly understood, is a problem of insurance: the party who is protected from a risk is the same party whose behaviour then becomes more reckless. But in the climate case, the parties who would choose to deploy aerosols -- present-day governments -- are not the parties exposed to the resulting risks, who are mostly future publics with no seat at the table. What we actually face is an intertemporal principal–agent problem: agents (today's electorates and their leaders) making decisions whose costs fall on principals (their successors) who cannot monitor, reward, or punish them. This is not a pedantic distinction. Insurance problems are solved by deductibles and co-payments; principal–agent problems are solved by monitoring, bonding, and pre-commitment. If we misdiagnose the disease we will prescribe the wrong cure.

Termination shock, meanwhile, is usually treated as an argument against deployment. I think it is better understood as an argument that deployment, once begun, is constitutionally transformative. A state running an aerosol programme is like a central bank defending a currency peg: exit is always possible in principle and catastrophic in practice, which means the decision to start is really a decision to bind one's successors indefinitely. The question "who decides?" therefore cannot be answered once, at the moment of first injection; it is re-posed every year thereafter, to people who never consented to the arrangement but inherit its costs.

My proposal is a continuation trust. Any state or coalition deploying stratospheric aerosols would be required to pre-fund an internationally escrowed trust large enough to finance either a gradual, multi-decade phase-out of the programme or compensation to those harmed by a sudden termination, with annual contributions indexed to how much mitigation the deployer had avoided relative to an agreed baseline. The trust converts an unbounded intergenerational liability into a bounded, priced one, and it forces the moral hazard into the open: if deployment genuinely relaxes the pressure to decarbonise, the deployer pays for that relaxation in real time.

I'd like comments on the following, in roughly this order of importance. First, the conceptual move in the third paragraph: is it true that moral hazard, strictly speaking, requires the protected party and the risk-taking party to coincide, and is my reframing as an intertemporal principal–agent problem an improvement, or does it lose something the moral hazard framing captures (for instance, the way cheap solar geoengineering might corrode political will within a single generation, not just across generations)? Second, the factual backbone: is my description of termination shock -- its timescale, its mechanism, the claim about aerosol residence time -- accurate enough to carry the argumentative weight I put on it, and where am I being glib? Third, the continuation trust: does it actually address the "who decides" problem or merely put a price on ignoring it, and what are the two or three strongest objections a critic would raise? Fourth, briefly: is the essay interesting, and which single change would most improve it?
