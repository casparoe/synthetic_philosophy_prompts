I am a PhD student in history working from colonial administrative archives, and my sources are notoriously lopsided: the records document what officials noticed, reported, and filed, while the people administered appear mostly as case numbers, petitions that survived triage, and entries in police blotters. I keep being told to "read against the grain" and to attend to "archival silences", but these are principles, not methods, and I cannot apply them consistently across thousands of files. Please turn the principle that archives are shaped by power — that what gets recorded, preserved, and indexed reflects the interests of the record-keepers, so absences are often manufactured rather than accidental — into an actual working procedure I can run on each document or file series: an ordered sequence of questions or tests to ask (about who produced the record, for what institutional purpose, what had to be excluded for the record to take this form, what traces of the excluded remain as anomalies, euphemisms, or gaps in series), with clear rules for distinguishing at least three kinds of absence (never recorded, recorded but destroyed or lost, recorded but filed elsewhere), since the inference I may draw differs in each case. Give me two worked applications: one to a routine file where the procedure yields a defensible reading, and one where it yields a reading my examiners will find uncomfortable (for instance, where treating a silence as evidence risks over-reading). Mark explicitly the points where the procedure cannot settle the matter and historical judgement must take over, and say what would most likely make the procedure misfire — that is, which kinds of archive or which assumptions about record-keeping would lead it to generate false positives for meaningful silence. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, give the procedure and reasoning after, with no preamble, and write in British English.
