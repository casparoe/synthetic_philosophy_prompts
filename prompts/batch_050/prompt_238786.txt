In our political-psychology reading group we have spent a term on moral foundations theory, and two members keep circling the same fight. One, call her the parity theorist, reads the empirical literature as showing that liberal moralities rest on a thinner basis — care and fairness, roughly — while conservative moralities draw on the full set of foundations including loyalty, authority, and sanctity, and she infers that conservative moral judgment deserves, on average, more epistemic weight: it detects morally relevant features the liberal palate simply misses. The other, the quietist, insists that no distribution of moral sentiments across the electorate could ever tell us who is right about anything; for him the theory is at most a tool for persuasion and messaging, and the parity theorist commits a dressed-up is/ought fallacy every time she speaks. Neither will concede an inch, so I have told them to produce an adversarial-collaboration document instead of another shouting match, and I would like you to draft it for them.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties can sign — for instance, about what the questionnaire and behavioral data do and do not establish, about the difference between a moral foundation as a psychological mechanism and as a justified principle, and about whether either side's position is threatened by findings that political identity shifts which foundations get endorsed; (2) a disagreements section in which each residual dispute is stated in neutral wording that both would accept as a fair statement of their own view; and (3) a cruxes section identifying the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to update. For each crux, name a concrete case or finding both agree bears on it and record each side's prediction in advance. Please include at least the following two candidates as possible tests: first, a hypothetical longitudinal study in which people induced, via sustained environmental manipulation, to rely on the so-called binding foundations report moral judgments they later disavow once the manipulation ends — does foundation-endorsing testimony under manipulation count as evidence for or against the authority of those foundations, and what does each side predict the disavowal pattern would show? Second, a thought experiment in which a society's members converge stably and reflectively on a two-foundation morality after full information and extended deliberation — would the parity theorist count this as counterevidence to her view, and what would the quietist say the convergence establishes?

If, in the course of drafting, you conclude that some part of their dispute is not really empirical at all — say, a covert disagreement about what it would even mean for a moral consideration to be genuine, or about whether detecting a feature is the same as being answerable to it — say so explicitly, mark which part, and reformulate that portion as a normative disagreement the collaboration cannot resolve by evidence. Conversely, if some claim the two treat as philosophical is actually an empirical bet, identify what observation would settle it. Do not write a verdict on who wins; the document should be one both could sign, with the remaining disagreement made as exact as possible.
