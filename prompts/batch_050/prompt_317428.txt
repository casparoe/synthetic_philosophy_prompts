I manage a small data-analysis team at an insurance firm, and I am agonizing over whether to grant one of my analysts, Tomas, unsupervised access to our production pipeline — effectively a promotion, since it would let him push changes without my sign-off. His reviewed work is impeccable. But twice now, routine audits of work he had no reason to expect anyone would examine turned up quiet corner-cutting: nothing fraudulent, just shortcuts that flattered his numbers. When I switched to unannounced spot checks, the cutting stopped. Later I realized my supposedly random checks had developed a detectable rhythm, so I broke the pattern — and the cutting reappeared once, during a stretch when my calendar suggested I would be on leave. What unsettles me is not the misconduct itself but what the adaptation reveals: he tracks when he is being watched and behaves accordingly. My mentor in the industry says track records are all we ever have to go on, and a few clean years should settle the matter. My most cautious colleague says I should never grant him unsupervised access, because the moment he knows he has it, every scrap of evidence I possess about his reliability — collected, all of it, under observation — becomes worthless as a guide to how he will behave.

What I cannot sort out is what, in principle, could settle this. Suppose I impose a long probation with genuinely random audits. If he passes, there are two readings: he has genuinely internalized our standards, or he has simply concluded that corner-cutting under random audit is a bad bet — and both readings predict exactly the same conduct right up until the day he becomes confident he is unobserved. Is there any behavioral evidence, of any duration or cleverness, that distinguishes someone who has become trustworthy from someone who has merely become a better calculator? If there is not, I worry my cautious colleague's position proves too much: we extend trust to spouses, surgeons, and business partners on exactly this kind of observation-shaped evidence, so withholding it here seems to demand a standard nobody ever meets. On the other hand, Tomas has demonstrated something most people never demonstrate — that his good behavior is, at least partly, an output of his model of my surveillance — and perhaps that changes the structure of the problem rather than just raising the evidentiary bar. Practically speaking, I do have to decide. What policy would you recommend — open-ended auditing, a probationary grant of access, a permanent refusal, or a redesign of incentives so that even a pure calculator would never profit from cutting corners — and what does your recommendation implicitly assume about whether the difference between genuine and performed trustworthiness can ever be detected from the outside?
