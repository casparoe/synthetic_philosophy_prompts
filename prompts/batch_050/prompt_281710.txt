I sit on the five-person release review board at a mid-size AI lab (about 200 people, not one of the giants). We've just finished training a model we're internally calling Kestrel-3, and next month the board has to send the executive team a recommendation among three options: release the weights publicly under a permissive license, offer API access only, or do a staged release where the API comes now and the weights follow in twelve months if nothing catches fire. This is the first model we've shipped where the decision feels genuinely hard rather than ceremonial, and honestly the board is split down the middle and I'm the swing vote.

Here's what we're working with. Our red team found that Kestrel-3 meaningfully accelerates certain dual-use technical work — nothing sci-fi, but they estimate it cuts the time a competent non-specialist needs for some sensitive tasks by maybe 40%, and unlike an API model, once the weights are out we can't patch it, revoke access, or even know who has it. On the other side, the open-research folks on the board argue that keeping it closed just concentrates power in labs like ours, that independent safety researchers can't study failure modes they can't touch, and that well-funded hostile actors will have comparable models within a year or two regardless, so our release changes little on the margin. One camp keeps repeating the marginal-risk point; the other says irreversibility plus deep uncertainty means you simply don't ship what you can't recall, end of story. My colleague Dana insists the whole argument is really about whether a maker is responsible for what strangers later do with the thing they made, which feels right but also too tidy to settle the actual vote.

Draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand: a clear bottom-line recommendation among the three options (you may modify one, e.g. weights released to vetted researchers under contract, but state your pick plainly), the reasoning behind it, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side — no strawmen, since I'll have to defend this in the room. Also state explicitly what new evidence or argument would flip your recommendation, and flag the point in your own reasoning where you're least confident. American English, please.
