so i keep getting into fights about poetry translation and i want the spec first, not another duel of examples. what would a theory of translation for formally constrained poetry actually have to do to count as adequate? give me the full requirements list: the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it can't collapse, with the case motivating each requirement, which ones are negotiable vs dealbreakers, and whether the whole list is even jointly satisfiable (if it isn't, that's a finding, not a bug in my request). then score the usual stances against it real quick -- strict literalism, sense-for-sense, domesticating into free verse, and the hofstadter le ton beau de marot approach of rebuilding the poem under the original constraints -- but keep the scoring short, the criteria are what i care about.

some cases your requirements have to cover, since these are the ones i keep tripping over:

1. a short fixed-form poem (tiny, light, rhymed, like the marot poem hofstadter built that book around) where half the literal content is clearly filler chosen to satisfy the rhyme -- if your theory says translate the content, it tells you to faithfully preserve the padding and lets you drop the thing the poem was actually for

2. a rhymed poem where the rhyme scheme keeps pairing semantically opposed words, and the argument of the poem basically IS those pairings -- a translator who keeps every line's meaning but re-rhymes randomly has destroyed something; say what, exactly

3. a poem whose acrostic spells the poet's own name, in a poem about wanting to disappear. the constraint is invisible unless pointed out. does the theory have to preserve constraints the average reader never notices, or only the ones doing audible work?

4. a sad poem in a bouncy galloping meter, so the meter makes it read half-mocking. a "faithful" translation into a sober meter is more accurate about the content and less accurate about the attitude. your spec needs a principled way to say which wins, not a shrug

things the theory can't do: collapse "translating what the poem says" and "translating what the poem does" into one fidelity score; treat every formal constraint as equally load-bearing (some are scaffolding, some are the building); or quietly assume prose-paraphrasability is the default and form is decoration, because case 2 and 3 break that.

and tell me: which single requirement on your list knocks out the most actual published translation practice? my guess is anything requiring the translator to preserve the constraint the original was composed under, since most rhymed verse gets done into blank or free verse -- but maybe you'll rank something else as the bigger killer.
