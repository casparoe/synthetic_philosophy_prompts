So my friend and I got into it last night over beers about whether thought experiments actually teach you anything about the world, or whether they're just arguments wearing a lab coat. She thinks they're all basically intuition pumps, I think some of them clearly deliver real knowledge, and we both got stuck when the other one pulled out a case. So here are my verdicts, tell me what principle (if any) is doing the work:

Galileo's thing where you tie a heavy and a light body together and show the Aristotelian view contradicts itself -- that genuinely teaches us something. No new data went in and real knowledge came out.

Stevin's chain draped over the inclined plane, where perpetual motion would follow if the two sides didn't balance -- same, that's knowledge.

Einstein imagining chasing a light beam and getting a frozen wave that Maxwell's equations don't allow -- knowledge, even though it's vaguer what exactly it establishes.

Schrödinger's cat -- this one teaches us something too, but not about cats, it's more like it pins down that a certain reading of QM has a cost nobody wants to pay. Still counts as learning something, I think.

The Chinese Room -- this proves nothing. It just takes the conclusion it's supposed to establish and wraps it in a vivid story. My friend says it's no different in kind from Galileo, and I couldnt articulate the difference, which annoyed me for hours.

Twin Earth -- convinces me completely that meanings ain't in the head, even though I'm aware it's structurally a lot like the Chinese Room one. This is where it got uncomfortable.

The old "imagine dropping a cannonball and a musket ball, obviously the cannonball hits first" style reasoning that Aristotelians actually used -- proves nothing at all, and the fact that it felt convincing to smart people for centuries is kind of the point.

And the case I keep flip-flopping on: Newton's bucket. Does the concave water surface actually establish absolute space, or did Newton just win the argument against the relationists of his day while leaving the real question open? I change my mind every time I think about it.

So: is there a principle that sorts these the way I want -- roughly, the ones where the conclusion is forced on you by the setup versus the ones where you have to already accept the conclusion to find the setup compelling? Or is that too vague and the real distinction is somewhere else, like whether the thought experiment smuggles in an empirical premise, or whether it survives being translated into a boring non-imaginary argument? I'd like the best candidate principle stated precisely, what it does with the bucket case specifically, and an honest call on which of my verdicts I'd have to give up to keep things consistent -- because I suspect Twin Earth and Chinese Room can't both come out the way I want them to.
