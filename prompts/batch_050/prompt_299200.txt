I'm a PhD student working in philosophy of logic, and two colleagues in my reading group have reached a stalemate over the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. (1) Anna argues that the thesis is an empirical conjecture about the physical world: if a physical device could compute a non-recursive function — by exploiting relativistic spacetime structure or some supertask arrangement — the thesis would simply be refuted, just as any empirical claim can be. (2) Ben replies that the thesis is a conceptual analysis of 'effective calculability' and so is not hostage to physics at all: a so-called hypercomputer would at most show that physics outruns effective procedures, not that such procedures compute more than the recursive functions, so nothing could count as a counterexample. (3) Anna counters that if the thesis were mere stipulation, the extensional equivalence of independently motivated formalisms — Turing machines, the lambda calculus, general recursion — would be a miraculous coincidence, whereas it is exactly what one would expect if they tracked a substantive fact. (4) Ben answers that the equivalence is no miracle because each formalism was designed to capture the same pre-theoretic notion, and he adds that Anna and her imagined refuters are equivocating between two readings of the thesis — one about what follows from the concept of an effective procedure, one about what physical mechanisms can do — so her 'refutation' targets a thesis different from the one he defends. Neither will concede, and I was asked to arbitrate.
