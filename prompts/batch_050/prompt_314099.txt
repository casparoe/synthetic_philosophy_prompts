I'm taking a history-and-philosophy-of-science survey course, and for our unit on reductionism I picked up a popular trade book on complexity science that devotes a chapter to presenting John Stuart Mill as the founding father of emergence. The chapter centers on Book III of Mill's A System of Logic (1843), the chapter on the composition of causes, which I have actually read, so I have some grip on the original -- but not enough confidence to grade the popularization. Here is the summary the book gives, in essence: "Long before anyone had heard of complexity theory, John Stuart Mill saw that reductionism was doomed. Mill divided the joint action of causes into two kinds. In the mechanical case, which he called homopathic, the combined effect of several causes is simply the sum of what each would produce separately, as when two forces combine into a resultant. In the chemical and biological case, which he called emergent, the combined effect is something entirely new that could not in principle be predicted from or explained by the laws governing the components -- water has properties neither hydrogen nor oxygen has, and living things are even further beyond the reach of physics. Mill thereby showed that the whole is literally more than the sum of its parts, that the laws of life can never be derived from the laws of matter, and that the mechanistic dream of explaining everything from the bottom up collapses at the level of chemistry. Modern chaos and complexity theory, with their demonstrations of principled unpredictability, are the final vindication of Mill's anti-reductionism."

Something in this smells off to me. From my own reading I recall that Mill's term for the second case was "heteropathic" rather than "emergent," and I have a vague sense that he was far more deferential to the mechanistic ideal, and far more optimistic about inductive science finding its way to higher-level laws, than this portrait suggests -- but I can't tell whether I'm misremembering or whether the book is flattening him into a convenient ancestor. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence: which parts would a careful reader of Mill's chapter accept, which are common simplifications a specialist would wince at but forgive, and which are actual misrepresentations -- for instance, the vocabulary attribution, the slide from "not deducible from component laws by us now" to "unpredictable and unexplainable in principle," and whether Mill's distinction is even a claim about what exists rather than about how laws combine? Finally, does the book's concluding move work: if we correct the summary to what Mill actually argued, does modern work on chaos, complexity, and nonlinearity vindicate him in the way claimed, vindicate only a much weaker thesis, or mostly change the subject -- and is "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" a fair gloss of anything Mill says in that chapter at all?
