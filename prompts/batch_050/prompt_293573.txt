Everyone knows Moore's paradox: there's something off about saying "It's raining, but I don't believe it," even though the sentence might well be true. The standard line is that the wierdness survives wether you say it out loud or just think it to yourself.

Build me a thought experiment where one and the same speaker can assert an omissive Moore sentence ("p, but I don't believe that p") sincerely, competently, and with zero irrationality — and where this doesn't rely on any of the usual cheat codes. Constraints: no split brains, multiple personalities, or time-slicing tricks (no "morning-me believed it, evening-me doesn't"); no merely relaying what someone else told them; the speaker has to be an ordinary person in a situation you could actually engineer in a lab or that plausibly crops up in real life; and it has to be a genuine assertion, not just reading the sentence off a card or entertaining it.

Then do the follow-through:

1. Walk through why the assertion is fine in your case, and pin down exactly which structural feature is doing the work. Is it that the speaker's evidence about their own mind comes through a different channel than their evidence about the world? Spell it out.
2. The hard part: does the paradox come roaring back if the same speaker just silently thinks the sentence instead of saying it? If your case makes the assertion fine but the private thought still defective, say what that implies about wether Moore's paradox is fundamentally a phenomenon of assertion or of belief.
3. Show that if you delete the key feature from the scenario, it collapses back into the standard paradox — i.e., prove the feature is load-bearing and not decoration.
4. Finally, steelman the person who says your case doesn't count: what's their best move for arguing the assertion is still defective after all, and does it work?

Don't just stipulate that the assertion is fine — the case has to generate that verdict from whatever's going on in it.
