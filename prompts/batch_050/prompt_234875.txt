Ok so I run a small debating society at my local library (we're all amateurs, no philosophers in the group, just people who like arguing) and there's a pattern I keep noticing that I don't have a word for, and it's driving me a bit nuts. You're an expert in both the psychology of reasoning and pragmatist philosophy — please think carefully about this one and don't just hand me the first label that comes to mind.

Here's the pattern, two recent examples. First: one of our regulars was arguing about electric cars and he kept demanding that the other guy produce, like, exact mechanisms — show me the supply chain data, show me the grid capacity math, show me the study. Fine, that's debate. But when it was his turn to make the positive case, everything was "well, obviously" and "anyone can see that" — and when I asked where HIS evidence was he looked at me like I'd missed the point. Second: my sister. She can spot a logical flaw in her boyfriend's arguments from a mile away, names the fallacies and everything, but when she explains her own side of the fight she describes it as "just thinking normally." She isn't pretending to be fair and failing. She genuinely experiences her own reasoning as not needing checking, the same way you don't check whether you're standing up.

So it's not ordinary favoritism toward your own side's conclusions — these people do weigh evidence sometimes. It's more like the *process they used* gets a permanent exemption from the standards they apply to everyone else's process. And it's not exactly hypocrisy either, because from the inside there's no double standard visible at all.

This reminded me of the very first lines of C.S. Peirce's essay "The Fixation of Belief" (1877), where he writes: "Few persons care to study logic, because everybody conceives himself to be proficient enough in the art of reasoning already. But I observe that this satisfaction is limited to one's own ratiocination, and does not extend to that of other men." Which is funny because he's basically describing my debate club 150 years early. But I'm not sure he's describing exactly my thing — he seems to be talking about confidence in your own reasoning, whereas my people additionally *enforce* standards on others, actively. Is that the same phenomenon with extra steps, or a different one that Peirce's remark doesn't quite cover?
