My friend and I have a running argument, now several dinners old, about when it is right to say that something genuinely believes something, as opposed to it merely being convenient to talk that way. Neither of us has any philosophy training; she is a veterinarian and I work in software, so we come at it from opposite directions. Over time I have collected her verdicts, and they are consistent enough that I think she is following a rule, but I cannot figure out which one. Her verdicts so far: her dog believes the leash means a walk, and can be wrong, as when he gets excited at the door and nobody is there — genuine belief. Her one-year-old niece, who cannot yet talk, believes the hidden toy is still behind the cushion — genuine belief; she was firm that language is not required. A honeybee that waggle-dances the location of nectar — yes, hesitantly, but yes. A thermostat that reacts to the room being cold — no, that is just a manner of speaking. A chess engine that plays as if it expects you to castle — no, it just runs its algorithm. And a Mars rover that reroutes itself around a rock — no, that is all pre-programmed responses, nothing like the dog being surprised. When I press her on the last two, she says the difference is that the dog, the baby, and the bee can genuinely learn and be surprised, while the artifacts are only doing what they were built to do.

The case we are now stuck on is a current AI personal assistant of the kind built on a large language model: it remembers things across conversations, will say things like "I thought your flight was Thursday, but I checked the email and it is Wednesday," corrects itself when shown it was wrong, anticipates questions, and occasionally makes confident mistakes that it then retracts. Predict her verdict and, more importantly, tell me which rule she is running. I can see at least three rules that fit every verdict she has given so far: one on which belief requires being a living, naturally evolved creature rather than a human artifact; one on which belief requires some inner conscious experience, which she presumably takes the animals and the baby to have and the machines to lack; and one on which belief is a matter of displaying flexible, world-directed behavior that can be wrong and can be corrected, regardless of what the system is made of. These three agree on all six of her past verdicts, as far as I can tell, but they seem to disagree about the assistant, which is exactly why we are stuck. State what each rule predicts about the assistant and why, give your best guess at which one is actually hers with your reasons, and tell me which additional case would best tell the rules apart — I have wondered, for instance, whether a future robot built from cultured neurons and an identical one built from silicon would force the issue. Finally, please be careful to separate what is actually settled among people who study this from what remains genuinely contested: I have the impression that even experts disagree about whether any artifact could literally believe something, and I would rather not have one side of that disagreement presented to me as the consensus. Please answer in American English.
