I'm a PhD student in philosophy (came over from a physics undergrad) and there's a situation I keep tripping over in my reading that I can't find a word for. The rough shape: you have two accounts of what the world contains -- different ontologies, apparently -- but the evidence that would settle between them isn't just missing, it's unreachable for anyone embedded in the world, because the world's own structure blocks it. Not 'our telescopes aren't good enough yet,' but 'no telescope ever could be.' I want to know if this is a recognized thing, because it feels importantly different from the usual underdetermination stuff (Duhem-Quine, curve-fitting, that whole complex), which is about evidence we happen not to have, and also different from brain-in-a-vat style skepticism, where the point is that we might be deceived about the ordinary world. Here nobody is being deceived; the question is whether there's even a real difference at stake. Sorry if this is rambly, it's been bugging me since a reading group session last semester where we all seemed to need a distinction nobody could state.

Three instances, which I'm not sure belong together. (1) In electromagnetism you can shift the potentials by a gauge transformation and nothing measurable changes -- every field, every experiment, identical. Two 'theories' differing only in gauge seem to disagree about what exists while agreeing on everything checkable in principle. (2) Regions behind an event horizon, or beyond our cosmic horizon in an accelerating universe: no signal from there can ever reach us, so claims about what's going on there are untestable from here not as a matter of technology but of spacetime geometry. (3) In Everett-style quantum mechanics, once decoherence has done its work, the interference that could in principle reveal other branches is effectively destroyed by the very interactions that make the branches distinct -- the distinguishing evidence gets consumed by the process that creates the difference.

What I want: does this configuration -- ontological disagreement where the adjudicating evidence is blocked by the physics itself rather than by our instruments -- have an established name, and how exactly does it relate to (and differ from) empirical equivalence and the standard underdetermination literature? More than the label, I want the carving: are my three cases one phenomenon or three seperate ones? Case (1) smells like there's no real difference at all, just redundancy in the description; case (2) smells like a real difference we happen to be permanently locked out of; case (3) is somewhere weird in between -- but I can't tell whether that gut ranking tracks anything principled or is just vibes. And where are the edges: does it still count if the barrier is 'merely' technological but permanently so, or only probabilistic rather than absolute, or relative to one observer rather than all possible ones? If you conclude nothing established covers this exactly, coin a term, define it properly, and give me boundary cases. Please don't just name-drop a concept and stop -- I've sat through too many reading group sessions that ended that way.
