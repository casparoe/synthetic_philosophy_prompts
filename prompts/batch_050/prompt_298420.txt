During a recent seminar debate on campaign-finance reform, a classmate brought the discussion of caps on independent political spending to a halt by declaring that 'money is speech' — as though the slogan by itself settled both the constitutional and the moral status of such caps. I suspect the slogan compresses several distinct theses, not all of them equally plausible and not all of them carrying the same regulatory implications. Please lay out, as an itemized menu, the different precise claims it could be making: for example, the claim that spending money is itself an expressive act akin to flag-burning; the claim that money is a necessary instrument or amplifier of speech, so that restricting spending functionally restricts speaking; the claim that a right to speak entails a right to devote one's own resources to being heard; the claim that influence purchased with money is normatively indistinguishable from influence earned through argument; and any further readings you find genuinely distinct rather than mere rewordings. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false; indicate which kinds of regulation it would actually undermine and which it would leave untouched — contribution limits, expenditure limits, disclosure requirements, public financing schemes; and note whether it rests on an anti-corruption rationale, an anti-distortion or equality rationale, or rejects one of those rationales outright. Then assess which of these readings my classmate's intervention would need to be true in order to shut down the debate in the way he intended, and whether those particular readings can bear that weight. Conclude by saying which reading you take the slogan's most careful defenders to actually have in mind, and whether the slogan's rhetorical effectiveness depends on sliding between that reading and weaker ones.
