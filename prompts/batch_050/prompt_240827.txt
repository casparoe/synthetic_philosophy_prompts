Standard moves for making consequentialism less demanding, as I understand the literature:

1. Satisficing: you only have to bring about a good enough outcome, not the best possible one (Slote-style).
2. Rule consequentialism: follow the code of rules whose general acceptance would have the best results, and a code anyone could realistically internalize won't demand total self-sacrifice (Hooker).
3. Scalar consequentialism: drop the categories of right and wrong action entirely and just rank outcomes as better or worse, so the question "how much am I required to sacrifice?" never gets a verdict at all (Norcross).
4. Building agent-centered prerogatives into the theory itself: agents are permitted to give extra weight to their own projects and commitments relative to the impersonal good, i.e. a hybrid view (Scheffler).
5. Biting the bullet: morality really is extremely demanding, and our intuitions to the contrary are squeamishness or self-serving bias (Singer/Unger style).
6. The institutional or cooperative turn: demandingness is really a coordination problem, so your actual duty is to do your fair share of what we would collectively owe, or to push for institutions that collectivize the burden (Murphy's line).

As far as I know, these (plus minor variants) exhuast the standard repertoire. Come up with one more strategy that is genuinely distinct — not a relabeling, special case, or mixture of the ones above. Explain how it would reduce the demands, why it isn't secretly one of the six in disguise, and whether you think it actually works or just quietly collapses into bullet-biting.
