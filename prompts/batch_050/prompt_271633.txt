I work as a software engineer, and I recently fell down a rabbit hole on decision theory after a colleague pointed out that my instinct in a probability puzzle was, by the lights of expected utility theory, irrational. The setup was the Allais paradox: most people prefer a certain million over a gamble that mostly pays a million with a small chance of paying five million and a small chance of paying nothing, yet when the certain option is removed and both choices are recast as long-shot gambles, they flip to preferring the one that pays the bigger prize. I checked my own responses and I flip too. I understand that this pair of preferences jointly violates the independence axiom, and I understand roughly why, but I keep going back and forth on whether that means my preferences are defective or whether independence is being asked to do work it shouldn't have to do.

Here is what I actually want. Before I read any more arguments for or against expected utility theory, I want a specification for what an adequate normative theory of rational choice under risk would have to deliver, written the way we would write requirements for a system: each desideratum, the case or consideration that motivates it, and an explicit flag for whether it is non-negotiable for any theory worth the name or merely a preference of one camp (for example: respecting first-order stochastic dominance; remaining well-defined when probabilities are objective, known, versus merely believed; not condemning the common Allais preferences out of hand, or alternately, diagnosing them as an error and explaining why the error is so persistent; compositionality of sequential choices, so that a plan evaluated up front does not conflict with its stepwise evaluation; some accounting of why certainty itself seems to carry weight beyond its probability). For each requirement, give the motivating case, and state plainly which competing positions that requirement rules out.

The part I most want is the dependency analysis. If some subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, if keeping independence while accommodating both Allais preference patterns while preserving transitivity simply cannot be done — say so explicitly and show where the contradiction sits, because a proof that the spec has no satisfying instance is itself the most useful possible output. Where the conflict is merely a tension rather than an impossibility, distinguish the two cases carefully. After the requirements list, and only briefly, sketch how three contenders fare against it: orthodox expected utility theory, something in the neighborhood of prospect theory or rank-dependent utility, and a view that keeps independence but reinterprets the outcomes rather than the probabilities. Do not score them exhaustively; the requirements and their motivations are the deliverable, and I would rather have ten well-argued desiderata than a tidy ranking.
