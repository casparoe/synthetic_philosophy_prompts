I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on what I provisionally call the implicit moral psychology of choice architecture. I'd like your help thinking through a cluster of puzzles that I can't get straight on my own.

Consider a government whose behavioral insights unit runs three flagship policies, all publicly defended under the single label "nudging": (1) new employees are automatically enrolled in the national pension scheme at a default contribution rate, though they may opt out or adjust the rate at any time; (2) driver's license applicants are registered as organ donors unless they actively tick a box declining; (3) tobacco products must be sold in plain packaging with graphic health warnings, though sale itself is unrestricted.

Here is what puzzles me. The official rationale for the whole program invokes two claims at once: that people reliably fail to choose as they would if fully informed, attentive, and unbiased, and that the policies leave choice intact because nothing is forbidden and opting out is costless. But policy (1) is justified by appeal to the chooser's own long-term interest as the designer infers it; policy (2) primarily benefits third parties and may not serve the chooser's interest at all; and policy (3) works not by exploiting inertia but by making an option less attractive. So I want to know:

What theory of well-being does the pension policy strictly presuppose? It seems to assume something like a laundered-preferences view — that a person's good consists in what they would choose absent bias and inattention — yet the same policy treats the opt-out form, which is precisely a possibly biased and inattentive choice, as the authoritative expression of the person's good. Can both commitments be held together, or does the practice presuppose a theory it then disavows?

Second, the whole justification of defaults rests on the empirical finding that inertia is enormously powerful — most people stay wherever the default puts them. But then what view of autonomy does the program presuppose when it counts the formal availability of opting out as sufficient for freedom? It looks as though the practice presupposes that autonomy consists in non-interference with options while its own mechanism denies that people meaningfully select among those options. Is that a coherent position, and if so, on what theory of autonomy?

Third, does the organ-donor default presuppose something different — that the state may exploit inertia for the benefit of people other than the chooser — and if so, is the single label "nudge" masking two practices with incompatible justifications, or is there one theory of legitimate state action that covers both?

Finally, tell me what would have to change in each policy if the government adopted a rival theory: an objective-list view of well-being, say, or a view on which only reflectively endorsed preferences count as authoritative. And throughout, please distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined — points where several rival theories would license exactly the same policies, so that no reconstruction can settle between them.
