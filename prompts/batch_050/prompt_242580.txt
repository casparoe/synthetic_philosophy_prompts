I'm a novelist, and I keep leaning on a friend of mine (she did a philosophy PhD before becoming an editor) for help with the emotional lives of my characters, because she has very definite opinions about what counts as what emotion. Her verdicts so far, as best I've noted them down: a woman who's terrified of a spider she fully knows is harmless -- that's real fear, she says, just irrational fear. A man who drinks six espressos and ends up with racing heart, sweaty palms, the works, but isn't thinking about anything in particular -- not fear, she says, that's just a body doing stuff, no emotion there at all. Grief for a dead parent that comes in waves over years, including long calm stretches where the person talks about the death with no tears, no tightness in the chest, nothing -- she insists it's grief the whole time, not just during the waves. A colleague who never consciously *feels* angry but whose entire career is organized around resentment of a rival -- she says that counts as anger, full stop. And an actor who works herself up on stage until her hands shake and she actually cries -- she says that can become genuine emotion, not a fake one.

Now I'm stuck on my new chapter. My protagonist has a medical condition (made up, but grounded) that completely flatlines his bodily responses -- no racing heart, no tears, no trembling, nothing, the doctors confirm it. His wife dies. He sincerely takes the loss to be terrible, he avoids places that remind him of her, his whole life quietly reorganizes around her absence, and he says he grieves. But he also says, honestly, that he feels nothing, and there's nothing bodily to feel. My question: what will my friend say -- is he grieving or not? More than the answer itself, I want to know what theory of emotion she's actually been running all along (emotions as judgments about the world, as perceptions of one's own body, as raw feelings, or some hybrid), which other theories fit her earlier verdicts just as well, and how those theories diverge on my character. And if you can, tell me what case -- something I could put to her over dinner -- would most cleanly tell the remaining candidate theories apart.

Please answer in continuous prose, the way you'd write an email -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Just talk me through it.
