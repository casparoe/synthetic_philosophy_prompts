I'm in a reading group on Hume's Treatise and we got stuck on the very first paragraph of the section "Of the infinite divisibility of space and time" (Book I, Part II). Hume writes:

"WHEREVER ideas are adequate representations of objects, the relations, contradictions and agreements of the ideas are all applicable to the objects; and this we may in general observe to be the foundation of all human knowledge. But our ideas are adequate representations of the most minute parts of extension; and thro' whatever divisions and subdivisions we may suppose these parts to be arriv'd at, they can never become inferior to some ideas, which we form. The plain consequence is, that whatever appears impossible and contradictory upon the comparison of these ideas, must be really impossible and contradictory, without any farther excuse or evasion."

So the shape of the principle seems to be: if an idea of mine adequately represents its object, then whatever I find contradictory when I inspect the idea is genuinely impossible in reality. What confused us is the load-bearing phrase "adequate representations." It feels like the principle only does work if we can tell, independently, which of our ideas are adequate -- but isn't that exactly what's under dispute in the very case Hume applies it to? His opponent presumably grants that we HAVE an idea of an indivisible point of extension but denies it's adequate, i.e. denies that the idea's internal coherence tells us anything about space itself. If adequacy has to be established before the principle applies, and establishing adequacy requires already knowing what's possible, the whole thing looks circular. But Hume presents it as "the foundation of all human knowledge," so presumably I'm misreading something. Is "adequate" doing some technical work for him (like, copied from an impression?) that would break the circle?

Also, related worry that came up over beers afterwards: does this principle overgenerate? Consider that a few decades ago a clever undergrad could have conceived, in some sense, of a proof that there's a largest prime number -- they can picture themselves writing QED at the bottom of the page -- yet what they're picturing is impossible, and not just physically. Does Hume's principle say such a person lacked an "adequate idea" of the proof (fair enough, they had no idea of its content), and if so, doesn't that make "adequate idea" just mean "idea of something possible," which again makes the conceivability test useless as a GUIDE since you'd need to know the answer in advance? Or is the notion of "appearing impossible upon comparison of ideas" narrower than I think -- maybe it only covers cases where you can survey the whole idea at once, the way he thinks we can with the smallest parts of extension? And one more small thing: what's with "without any farther excuse or evasion" -- who is he accusing of evading, and what would the evasion consist in? Any help untangling this (or pointers to what Hume means by adequate ideas elsewhere in Book I) would be much appreciated.
