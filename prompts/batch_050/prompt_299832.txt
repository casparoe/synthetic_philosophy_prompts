I'm preparing materials for a seminar on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, and I've found that my students grasp the three modes of history much better when they see them caricatured than when they read summaries. Last term I had them write fake diary entries for each temperament, which worked well, but this year I want something with more structure to it.

Write a parody of a government pamphlet from a fictional Ministry of Vital History, addressed to citizens who must be sorted into one of three licensed historical temperaments -- Monumental, Antiquarian, or Critical -- so that the nation's relationship to its past can be properly regulated. The pamphlet should follow the genre conventions of bureaucratic self-assessment documents: an eligibility quiz, descriptions of each temperament's duties and permitted activities, a schedule of mandatory festivals (the Monumental temperament gets an annual reenactment of a great deed, edited for inspirational value; the Antiquarians run the archives and are contractually forbidden from asking whether anything deserves to be discarded), fee structures, and penalties for practicing history without the correct license. The humor should come from exaggerating features Nietzsche actually identifies: the Monumental historian's habit of blurring the differences between eras so that all greatness becomes interchangeable and the present feels excused from attempting anything; the Antiquarian's piety toward everything inherited, which ends up mummifying the past and strangling whatever is new; the Critical historian's justified but dangerous urge to condemn the past, at the risk of sawing off the branch one is sitting on. A reader who knows the essay should feel the satire is fair; a reader who doesn't should still find the pamphlet funny and come away roughly understanding the three types. Keep it deadpan throughout -- no winking, no narrator who admits the whole scheme is absurd, though the bureaucratic logic should quietly collapse under its own weight (a footer about the Ministry's own founding charter being under Critical review would be a nice touch). Around 700 words, formatted like a real pamphlet with headers and a sidebar or two.
