So there's this argument that keeps flaring up in my group chat (mostly people who met through LessWrong and adjacent blogs) about the norm of assertion. One camp holds the knowledge norm: flat-out asserting p is only proper if you know p, and if it turns out you didn't know, you broke the rule of the practice even if you had decent reasons. The other camp says that's absurdly demanding, and the actual norm is something like justified belief: asserting what you have strong evidence for is fine, and a false-but-well-supported assertion can be blameless.

Write the document these two camps would produce if they ran a proper adversarial collaboration instead of rehashing the same thread for the fifth time. Format I'd like:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can actually sign. Not vacuous stuff like "assertion has some norms" — real commitments, e.g. about what both sides say when someone asserts on a hunch and gets lucky, or about whether hedging ("I'm pretty sure that...", "my best guess is...") changes the standard.

2. A disagreements table. Each residual disagreement stated in neutral wording that both camps would accept as a fair statement of their own view. Also flag any disagreements that turned out to be verbal once you cashed them out — I suspect at least one is, around the word "blameworthy."

3. The cruxes: sub-questions where settling them would actually move at least one camp. For each crux, give a concrete case or observation both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Cruxes I suspect, though replace them if you find better ones: lottery cases (is it okay to tell someone their ticket lost, based purely on the odds, before the draw is announced?); how to explain why "p, but I don't know that p" sounds off; what each camp says about the well-justified false assertion, i.e. the person who asserts carefully from strong-but-misleading evidence; and whether the norm of assertion even has to explain our practices of criticizing people after the fact, or whether that's a separate question about etiquette.

Two constraints: don't frame either side as obviously dumb, and don't adjudicate a winner at the end — the deliverable is a text both camps could sign, with the remaining disagreement made exact. Also keep it self-contained; definately no bibliography needed, this is all armchair material anyway.
