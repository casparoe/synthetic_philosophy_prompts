So a bit of context: I work in logistics and we've just had a load of warehouse automation brought in, which has sparked a running argument with a mate of mine about whether Marx was right or wrong about machines. My mate dug up this passage from Capital vol. 1, chapter 15 (the big machinery chapter) as his trump card:

"Like every other increase in the productiveness of labour, machinery is intended to cheapen commodities, and, by shortening that portion of the working-day, in which the labourer works for himself, to lengthen the other portion that he gives, without an equivalent, to the capitalist. In short, it is a means for producing surplus-value."

His reading is that Marx is saying machinery, just by its very nature, exists to squeeze surplus value out of workers — so automation is bad for workers full stop, wherever it happens, and all those people who go on about how Marx would've loved fully automated luxury communism are kidding themselves. I definately feel like something's off with that reading but I can't put my finger on what. For one thing, doesn't "machinery is a means for producing surplus-value" commit Marx to thinking machines themselves produce value? I thought the whole labour theory point was that only labour does that. Or is the sentence just definitional — anything a capitalist buys is by definition a means of producing surplus value — in which case it says nothing much about machines in particular? Or am I wrong on both counts?

So what is Marx actually claiming in this passage, and does it settle the argument in my mate's favour? If it helps, I'd like you to say how confident you are in your reading and flag the single point you're least sure about.
