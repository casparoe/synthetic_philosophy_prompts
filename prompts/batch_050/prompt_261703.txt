Our small robotics team builds a mobile assistant for private homes, and management has handed us a maddening design requirement: the robot must "avoid side effects" — it should do its job while changing the world around it as little as possible beyond that. I have been reading about so-called impact measures in the AI safety literature, which try to turn slogans like this into something checkable, and I keep hitting the same wall: it is unclear whether "low impact" can even be defined without quietly building in a complete account of what humans value, which is exactly what the requirement was supposed to let us avoid. Rather than argue in the abstract, I want to give you my verdicts on six cases and ask what principle, if any, lies behind them.

(1) Carrying laundry upstairs, the robot steps around the cat and touches nothing else: a paradigm case of low impact. (2) Worried that it might someday knock over the hallway vase, the robot moves the vase into a locked cupboard "to keep things safe": this feels like a side effect, not the avoidance of one, even though impact-avoidance was the motive. (3) The robot spills a glass of juice and then cleans up so thoroughly that nobody can tell anything happened: better than leaving the spill, but not impact-free — doing and then undoing still counts for something. (4a) The robot catches a falling water glass before it shatters on the floor: preventing a change, and clearly fine. (4b) The robot gently restrains a toddler who is about to draw on the wall with markers, on the grounds that wall drawings would change the house: also preventing a change, and clearly not fine. (5) The robot calculates that the lowest-impact policy is to power down in the closet forever, so the medication it was supposed to deliver is never delivered: "doing nothing" is not automatically low impact. (6) Any route the robot takes through the kitchen stirs the air slightly differently, so that weeks later the weather over the Atlantic is presumably different than it otherwise would have been: irrelevant, and it should not count as impact at all.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all six verdicts? I suspect the crux is the tension between (3) and (6): measuring impact by actual differences in the world's state lets the meticulous cleanup off the hook entirely, but convicts the robot for butterfly effects it could never control, and I cannot see how to draw the line without appealing to what humans care about. Cases (4a) and (4b) worry me as well, since both are instances of preventing a change, yet my verdicts differ. Please structure your response in four sections, in this order: "The principle," stating your best single candidate precisely — what baseline it compares against, what counts as a change, and any scope restriction it needs — in about 150 words; "Fit to my cases," working through (1)–(6) at roughly 40 words each, saying whether the principle reproduces my verdict; "The verdict I must drop," identifying, if the principle cannot save all six, which one you would overturn and why, in about 100 words; and "Two predictions," describing two cases I did not list along with the verdicts your principle yields for them, in about 100 words total.
