I'm drafting a novel whose narrator is an aging composer, and there's a scene where she lectures a student on what makes something a performance of her piece versus something else entirely. Before I write it I need her instincts to actually hang together, so here are the verdicts I'd give her -- tell me if any principle delivers them.

A pianist flubs a handful of notes in her sonata: still a performance of the sonata, obviously. A pianist who skips the big repeat out of laziness: still a performance of it, though she'll grumble. A jazz trio takes the sonata's main theme and reharmonizes it, changes the meter, improvises over it for twenty minutes: not a performance of the sonata anymore, it's a new piece with her theme inside it. A note-for-note cover recorded in a bedroom with a synth instead of piano: same work, mediocre performance. A student piece where the score says the performer picks the order of the five sections: all those wildly different evenings are performances of one and the same work, she's sure of that. And a string quartet arrangement of the sonata, every note kept, just reassigned across four instruments: same work. But here's the one I keep tripping on -- the jazz trio changed less of the *sound* than the quartet arrangement changes the timbre, yet she counts the arrangement and not the trio. And the aleatoric student piece can share basically zero notes between two performances and still be one work, while the trio shares plenty and isn't.

Is there a principled account of work-identity (score? sound? performance practice? something else) that gets all of these, or does she have to drop one? I'd rather know now than have a reviewer point it out. Also say what the account does with a case I haven't written yet: a DJ who plays her recording slowed down 800%, as an installation piece.
