I'm a novelist and I keep bumping into this old fight between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett about whether dreams are experiences we actually have while asleep. Malcolm's line, as I understand it, is that the waking report is all there is to a dream -- there's no further fact about what "happened" during the night, so the commonsense picture of an experience unspooling in sleep is just confused. Dennett rejects the commonsense picture too but for the opposite flavor of reason: no narrative gets played during sleep at all, the dream gets assembled at waking (his cassette idea), and he wants nothing to do with Malcolm's verificationism. So both attack the naive view from different directions, neither convinced the other, and then the debate sort of... went quiet? Nobody won, everybody moved on.

My hypothesis, as someone who writes dream sequences for a living and has a professional intrest in what makes an experience an experience: the stall isn't really about consciousness. It's that the sides disagree about what could even count as evidence for an experience at a time -- Malcolm wants waking criteria, Dennett wants a mechanism, common sense just trusts memory. But I could be totally off.

So: act as a philosopher of mind who knows this debate well and thinks carefully before answering. Start by brainstorming 3 or 4 candidate diagnoses of why it stalled, then commit to one. What kind of disagreement is this really -- verbal, a clash over standards of evidence, a dispute about which data count (first-person reports? the sleep-lab stuff where lucid dreamers signal with their eye movements? memory as the sole access route?) -- or is the question itself ill-posed? And given your diagnosis: what would progress even look like -- a distinction, an experiment, a reframing -- or should we expect none? Don't survey the positions at me, I want the structure of the impasse and an honest verdict on whether anything could settle it.
