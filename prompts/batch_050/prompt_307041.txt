I'm a PhD student working on Hume's epistemology and its bearing on legal proof, and I'd like a disciplined exercise in counterfactual interpretation. Present Hume with the modern gatecrasher problem: a thousand people attend a rodeo, only 499 paid for admission, and the organizer sues one attendee; the sole evidence that this defendant did not pay is the base rate, which puts the probability that he gatecrashed just above 0.5, thereby satisfying the civil preponderance standard. Modern courts and commentators balk at entering judgment on such naked statistical evidence alone. What would Hume say? Please argue from his own machinery — his account of probable reasoning as grounded in experienced constant conjunction, his category of "unphilosophical probability" and the ways it distorts judgment, and his method for weighing testimony against uniform experience in the discussion of miracles — whether he would take a bare frequency to license a particular judgment about this defendant, or whether his framework itself generates the modern unease. Where does the prediction become genuinely hard: does Hume possess the resources to distinguish statistical support from the individualized evidence courts seem to demand, or does his theory of belief as a lively conception force him to treat the two as differing only in degree, so that his views on belief and the passions end up explaining, rather than vindicating, our reluctance to convict?
