I am preparing a worked example for a graduate seminar on bargaining theory, and I want it built to order rather than lifted from a textbook. You are an expert in cooperative game theory; think carefully, and begin by sketching two or three candidate designs in one sentence each before committing to the one you judge cleanest.

Construct a concrete two-party negotiation — an everyday situation such as bandmates dividing touring revenue or co-founders assigning equity, not an abstract utility diagram — whose natural representation as a bargaining problem meets two conditions: (1) the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution recommend materially different divisions; (2) adding one further option to the feasible set, an option neither party would plausibly end up choosing, leaves the Nash recommendation unchanged but shifts the Kalai–Smorodinsky recommendation toward one of the parties. Constraints: the feasible set is a finite list of divisions with no randomization over outcomes, utilities are linear in the good being divided, the numbers are small whole numbers a student could verify by hand, and the scenario itself takes at most a short paragraph. Do not stipulate the verdicts; they must fall out of the two solution concepts.

Then demonstrate that the specification is met. State the feasible set and the disagreement outcome, compute both solutions before and after the extra option is introduced, and show the arithmetic. Identify exactly which structural feature does the work — I expect it to be the sensitivity of Kalai–Smorodinsky to the ideal (utopia) point and its consequent failure of independence of irrelevant alternatives, but say explicitly whether your case turns on that or on something else. Finally, give the best response available to a defender of the Nash solution who claims that the shift your case exhibits is a defect of Kalai–Smorodinsky rather than a virtue, and note what happens to the case if the added option is removed.
