I am preparing an essay for an online philosophy magazine and would like your comments before I revise. The essay is below; my specific questions follow it.

Thinking in Slow Motion: Aunt Hillary and the Parochialism of Speed

In the Ant Fugue, the dialogue in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, we meet Aunt Hillary: an ant colony that converses, jokes, and holds opinions, although no individual ant is anything more than a simple mechanism. The colony's intelligence is realized one level up, in the shifting teams and trails of ants, much as ours is realized one level down, in neurons. Most discussion of the case stalls on a question I find dull — whether a sufficiently coherent high-level description is enough for a mind. I want to press on a feature of the case that gets less attention: how slowly Aunt Hillary must think.

Signals in a colony propagate at the speed of walking ants. Plausibly, Aunt Hillary takes hours to complete a thought and days to finish a sentence. Call the natural objection the bandwidth objection: a system this slow does not think; it is merely describable as thinking. Genuine minds must outrun their environments. A being whose beliefs take a week to form always believes things about a world that no longer exists; its states may be true or false of the past, but they are never about the present, and so — the objection concludes — they are not really about anything at all.

The standard reply, which I accept, is that absolute speed is parochial. Imagine a human brain in a vat, artificially slowed a thousandfold and fed sensory input slowed to match. It would think just as we do, only measured in years rather than seconds, and no one should deny it a mind. If slowness alone does not disqualify the slowed brain, it does not disqualify the colony.

But I think the reply concedes the wrong point, and that the bandwidth objection survives in a refined form. What matters is not how fast a system thinks in absolute terms, but the ratio between the speed of its symbol-updating and the speed of the processes its symbols are meant to track. Call this the tracking ratio. The slowed brain in the vat has a tracking ratio of one: its world is slowed in lockstep with it. Aunt Hillary does not. She is a slow mind embedded in a fast world, surrounded by creatures — anteaters, philosophers — who live thousands of times faster than she does. By the time she has formed a belief about her interlocutor, her interlocutor has eaten lunch, read a book, and aged a season.

Does that matter? Not for all thought. Mathematics does not require keeping pace with anything, and neither does musing about the distant past. So I propose a distinction. Contemplative mindedness requires only internal coherence among symbols; it carries no tracking-ratio requirement. Engaged mindedness — perception, conversation, action, anything that answers to a changing world — requires a tracking ratio near one. The refined bandwidth objection then says: Aunt Hillary may be a contemplative mind, but she cannot be an engaged one. That is a surprising result, because conversation is the paradigm of engagement. The one thing the Ant Fugue depicts her doing — chatting with the Anteater — is the one thing a colony-mind could not actually do, even if it possesses beliefs, desires, and a rich inner life.

The criterion has teeth outside the thought experiment. Consider a national government. On questions where its information channels and decision procedures run faster than the phenomenon — a financial panic unfolding over weeks — it functions as an engaged mind, and we do well to describe it as believing and intending. On questions where the phenomenon outruns the procedure — a legislature deliberating, over decades, about a technology that reinvents itself every two years — the tracking ratio collapses, and the government's position is contemplative at best: a self-consistent story it tells itself, not a belief about the thing it is ostensibly about. This, I want to suggest, is not a metaphorical point. It diagnoses a real way in which large collective agents fail: not by being stupid, but by being slow relative to what they think about.

My questions:

1. Fairness to the source. For readers who know the Ant Fugue, is my compressed presentation of Aunt Hillary accurate enough to be fair, or does the essay's case rest on a distortion of the setup?

2. The weakest step. Identify the step you consider weakest. Candidates I worry about myself: (a) the claim that the vat-brain reply concedes the wrong point; (b) the move from "never about the present" to "not about anything"; (c) the assumption that conversation requires engagement rather than being sustainable as a contemplative activity under a sufficiently patient interpretation.

3. The engaged/contemplative distinction. Can you construct a case the distinction classifies wrongly? I am especially interested in borderline activities — correspondence chess played by post, monologue, prayer — where the tracking ratio is low but engagement seems present.

4. The application. Does the government diagnosis actually follow from the criterion, or have I smuggled in an empirical premise about institutional decision speed that the essay never defends?

Please be direct about what should be cut. If the central distinction collapses under your answer to question 3, I would rather know now.
