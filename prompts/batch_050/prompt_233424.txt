I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a long explainer on the reference class problem: the fact that any individual case belongs to indefinitely many overlapping classes, each with its own observed frequency, so that assigning a probability to the case seems to require a choice of class that the statistics themselves do not dictate. The piece ranges over actuarial pricing, medical prognosis, and recidivism prediction, and I want a section on proposed responses to the problem. Here are the responses I already have on file; please do not repeat any of them:

1. The narrowest-class rule (associated with Reichenbach): use the narrowest reference class for which reliable statistics exist. Its known weakness is that narrowing a class shrinks the sample that made it informative, and in the limit yields a class containing only the case itself.

2. The causal-relevance response: select the class defined by features that make a causal difference to the outcome, and dismiss classes that merely correlate as gerrymandered.

3. The fair-draw condition: a class is usable only if the case can be treated as a random draw from it; where we know the case differs from the average member, we must subdivide or adjust until the condition holds.

4. Dissolution (Venn's original stance): probability applies to classes and series only, not to single cases, so the demand for the one correct class is simply confused.

5. The subjectivist response: choosing a class is an exercise of judgment on a par with setting a prior; coherence constrains you only after the class is fixed, and rationality does not require two reasoners to converge on the same class.

6. The decision-relative response: the right class is fixed by the decision at hand and the asymmetry of the costs of error, not by anything intrinsic to the case.

My question: is there a seventh response, genuinely distinct from these six and not a rewording, special case, or hybrid of them? I would rather have one well-developed proposal than several sketches. After presenting it, please assess it honestly: what does it handle that the six above do not, where is it weakest, and if it collapses into one of the listed responses under pressure, say which one and why. Ground the discussion in a concrete case — for instance, an insurer pricing a policy for a nonsmoking endurance cyclist with a family history of heart disease — rather than keeping everything at the level of abstract principle.

Please write in American English, in roughly 600 to 800 words, and in a register suitable for a scientifically literate general readership.
