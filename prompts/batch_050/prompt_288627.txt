Act as a philosopher who has actually worked on epistemic logic and paradoxes, not just read the survey literature. Think carefully before you answer, and maybe sketch the main families of responses to yourself first so the diagnosis engages what each side actually says rather than a caricature.

Here's my situation. The surprise exam paradox has been argued about for something like seventy years. One camp treats it as a problem about backward induction, on par with the centipede game and iterated prisoner's dilemma: the student's elimination of Friday, then Thursday, and so on is just a finite-horizon rollback argument, and whatever is wrong with those is wrong here. Another camp says no, the real action is in the teacher's announcement itself: "there will be an exam and you won't know in advance which day" is a Moore-ish, possibly self-undermining utterance, so this is about the logic and pragmatics of announcing, not about game-theoretic reasoning. A third camp does dynamic epistemic logic and says both sides have the wrong notion of surprise: once you distinguish "the student doesn't know the day now" from "the student won't know the day the night before," the paradox dissolves or at least changes shape.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the three camps aren't answering the same question. They each fix on a different sense of "surprise," and each camp's solution works beautifully for its own reading while being obviously beside the point on the others' readings, so they talk past each other indefinitely. But I might be wrong -- maybe there's a single core puzzle all three are circling, and one camp is simply right.

What I want from you is a committed diagnosis, not a survey. Pick a definite position: is this a verbal disagreement, a disagreement about which formalization is faithful to the ordinary-language puzzle, or a real substantive disagreement about the legitimacy of backward induction wearing a paradox costume?
