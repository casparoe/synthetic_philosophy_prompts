A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a finite sequence of coin flips can be called random, and we ended up talking past each other. Her case rests on an analogy she kept returning to: randomness is like the fairness of a die. Fairness, she says, is a property of the die -- of the process that generates outcomes -- not of any particular roll. If a die lands six a hundred times running, the individual sixes are not "unfair outcomes"; at most, the run is evidence that the die itself is loaded. Likewise, a string of bits is a product, not a process, so asking whether the string itself is random is a category mistake: the only well-formed question is whether it issued from a random source, and if you don't know the source, the question has no answer. From this she concluded that talk of "random-looking strings" is loose talk, and that the mathematics of algorithmic randomness, whatever its merits, is really a theory of compressibility and description length, not a theory of randomness proper -- so calling an incompressible string "random" is a misleading relabeling.

I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it earns, but I couldn't say exactly where it fails. Please audit it carefully. Lay out the mapping explicitly -- what plays the role of the die, the fairness, the rolls, and the act of testing the die by inspecting its rolls -- and then identify the disanalogies. In particular, I'd like attention to these pressure points, and to any I've missed: (1) incompressibility is definable for a finite string, but only relative to a choice of universal machine and only up to an additive constant, so "random for its length" comes out fuzzy and machine-relative in a way that fairness of a die does not -- does this vindicate her, or does it show the target concept is two-place (random relative to a machine, a measure, or a class of tests) in a way the analogy simply doesn't allow for? (2) The die-testing practice she points to actually runs from product to process: we inspect rolls to learn about the die, and statisticians run tests on strings without ever touching the source -- so is her "you can only ask about the process" claim even true of her own example? (3) Test-based notions of randomness are exact for infinite sequences but degrade gracefully for finite ones (shorter strings pass fewer tests at a given level), which suggests the product-notion is not empty but asymptotic -- is that a genuine middle position, or a confusion? Finally: does her conclusion that algorithmic randomness is "just compressibility" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is it smuggled in by the analogy from the start? And if the die analogy is misleading, what would be a better one for conveying what the algorithmic tradition is and isn't claiming?

One request about calibration: please keep distinct what is settled mathematics -- the existence and properties of incompressible strings, the machine-dependence up to a constant, the behavior of effective statistical tests -- from what is genuinely contested interpretation among people who work on this, namely whether finite-sequence randomness is a coherent and worthwhile notion and whether "randomness" or "compressibility" is the right word for what the theory delivers. I'd rather not have a contested philosophical verdict presented to me as if it were the consensus of the field.
