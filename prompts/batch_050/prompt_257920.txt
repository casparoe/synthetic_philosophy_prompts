In my undergraduate history seminar, the unit on the end of the Western Roman Empire always ends the same way: half the room leaves convinced that Rome fell, catastrophically, and the other half leaves convinced that "the fall of Rome" is an anachronistic framing that historians should retire. Both halves have read overlapping material, which is what makes the split interesting to me. The catastrophists point to Gibbon's classic narrative and to the more recent archaeological case pressed by Bryan Ward-Perkins: the fifth century shows a measurable collapse in material culture across the western provinces — mass-produced pottery, roof tiles, coinage, literacy, building in stone, even the size of livestock — and the blunt political fact that by 500 the tax-collecting, army-paying Roman state in the West no longer existed. The transformationists, heirs of Peter Brown's late-antiquity school, reply that Roman law, administrative habits, aristocratic culture, and Christian institutions carried on under the successor kingdoms, that the eastern half of the empire persisted for another thousand years, and that "fall" is a value judgment projected backward by Renaissance humanists and Enlightenment historians who needed Rome to fall so that their own era could be a rebirth. A third voice, the old Pirenne thesis, relocates the real break to the seventh-century Arab conquests that shattered Mediterranean unity, which suggests the two camps may not even be arguing about the same discontinuity.

My question is not which side is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is. Is it genuinely factual — a dispute about what the archaeological and textual record shows — or a dispute about thresholds and baselines, i.e., how much continuity, and continuity of what, would count as survival? Is it largely terminological, with each side defining "Rome" and "fall" differently and talking past each other? Or is it a disagreement about the scale of analysis: the experience of a British peasant whose pottery and diet got dramatically worse versus the perspective of a senatorial family in Gaul whose world changed surprisingly little?

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually appeals to, and then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a kind of evidence that would move one camp, a reframing of the question — or an argument that the impasse is permanent because the question dissolves once "fall" is precisified. State your diagnosis in the first two sentences, and keep the whole answer under 700 words.
