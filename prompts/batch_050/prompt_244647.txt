I run community health at a mid-sized social platform (forums plus a recommendation feed), and my team keeps hitting the same wall: we flag communities that look sealed-off and dogmatic, but nobody agrees on what we're actually looking at or what to do about it. Reading around I came across a distinction, associated with the philosopher C. Thi Nguyen, between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber. Roughly as I understand it: a bubble is a community where contrary views just never arrive — the filter is passive, and if the contrary evidence did get in, people might actually update. An echo chamber is worse: the community has a trust structure that discredits outside sources in advance, so exposing members to disagreement doesn't help and can backfire, because the disagreement itself gets read as further proof that outsiders are dishonest, bought, or brainwashed.

I want this turned into a diagnostic and intervention procedure my team can actually run. Context: we review maybe a dozen flagged communities a month, we get about 45 minutes of analyst time per community, we can see posting/reply/cross-linking patterns and sample threads but can't run user surveys or experiments on users without legal review, and any intervention needs sign-off from a policy lead.

What I need:

1. A classification procedure: tests to run in order that distinguish (a) bubble, (b) echo chamber, and (c) a third category I care about a lot — communities that look sealed but whose distrust of outside sources is arguably warranted or at least reasonable given the actual track record of those sources. For each test, say what observable evidence counts as passing or failing given the data we actually have, not the data we wish we had.

2. An intervention tree for each classification: what to do, in what order, and what observable result tells us to stop, continue, or escalate. I specifically want the claim that exposure fixes bubbles but backfires on chambers operationalized into a cheap early test we can run before committing to anything — because misclassifying a chamber as a bubble and then "exposing" it presumably makes things actively worse. Tell me which direction the classification step should err in, given that asymmetry, and why.

3. Honest marking of where the procedure has to hand off to a human because the question is values/politics rather than fact. My suspicion is that category (c) is mostly like this — telling an echo chamber apart from a community with well-earned distrust seems to require judging whether the distrusted sources are actually trustworthy, and I'd rather the procedure admit that than launder my policy lead's politics into a checklist.

4. Then run the whole thing on three hypotheticals: a flat earth community; a chronic illness patient community with deep distrust of pharma and mainstream medicine, where some of that distrust has real grounding and some of it has slid into conspiracism; and a tight-knit fandom community that's totally sealed off but only about trivia. Make sure at least one comes out with a verdict I won't like — e.g., the procedure telling me to leave alone a community my instincts scream is a problem, or to intervene on one that seems harmless.

5. Finally, what can't this procedure do? Where will it systematically misfire, and what would a community do if it had read this procedure and wanted to look like the harmless kind?
