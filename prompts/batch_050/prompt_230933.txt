In Section IV, Part I of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume writes: "Adam, though his rational faculties be supposed, at the very first, entirely perfect, could not have inferred from the fluidity, and transparency of water, that it would suffocate him, or from the light and warmth of fire, that it would consume him."

Write a Shakespearean sonnet spoken by this Adam -- newly made, faculties perfect, standing at the edge of water he has never touched and fire he has never felt. The three quatrains should give us his inspection: let him reason as hard as reason can from what the senses actually deliver (fluidity, transparency, light, warmth) and let each quatrain's argument come up short in a different way, so that the reader feels why no examination of sensible qualities, however accurate, yields powers or effects. The volta at the couplet should be his hand breaking the water's surface: the first conjunction of cause and effect he has ever lived through. Use that final turn to pose, rather than answer, the question that divides Humeans from anti-Humeans about laws of nature -- after one touch, does Adam stand under a law that binds fire to burning, or has he merely logged a single instance of an event whose repetition nothing guarantees? The couplet should leave both readings live in the same image. Keep the philosophy exact: the failure must be located in what reason cannot extract from qualities, not in any defect of Adam's intellect, and a reader who knows the Humeanism debate should be able to tell from the last two lines exactly which claim the poem is refusing to settle.
