I'm a novelist at work on a book, and I recently overheard an argument between two acquaintances of mine -- a violin teacher and a software engineer -- at a dinner party, and I want to adapt it into a scene. But I can't write the scene's ending honestly until I know who, if anyone, is winning. Please act as a professional philosopher of mind with particular expertise in the debate over knowing how versus knowing that. Before rendering any verdicts, first lay out what you take each disputant's considered position to be, since I want the reconstruction to be fair to both.

Here is the exchange, as best I remember it. The dispute began when the violin teacher said that everything she'd learned in thirty years of playing was knowledge, just like the facts in a textbook.

(1) The engineer disagreed: knowledge in the textbook sense is knowledge that such-and-such, and the teacher's skill is knowing *how* to play -- a different thing entirely. His proof: he could read a treatise on violin technique, memorize every proposition in it, and still be unable to play a scale. So possessing the propositions is not sufficient for the skill, which means the skill is not made of propositions.

(2) The teacher replied that this only shows he lacks the propositions in the right form. A speaker of English can't state the grammatical rules they follow, either, but we still say the rules are in there somewhere, tacitly. Her knowledge that the bow must move at such an angle, with such pressure, is tacit in exactly that way -- encoded in her fingers, perhaps, rather than in words, but propositional underneath. His treatise study gave him the facts third-personally, she said; playing requires grasping those same facts from a practical point of view.

(3) The engineer pounced: "grasping from a practical point of view" is just a fancy name for knowing how, so her analysis is circular -- it explains know-how in terms of knowing-that, then explains the relevant knowing-that in terms of know-how. He added a second argument: when concert performers choke under pressure, it is precisely because they start thinking about the mechanics of what they're doing; if the skill were propositional knowledge, attending to the propositions could only help, never hurt.

(4) The teacher answered that the choking point proves too much. Attending to almost anything at the wrong moment disrupts performance -- attending to the audience, to the rent, to the sound of one's own breathing -- but nobody concludes from that that breathing isn't really physiological. Interference from attention is a fact about attention, not about the nature of the skill. As for circularity, she held her ground: "practical" picks out a mode of *representation*, not a second kind of knowledge, and modes of representation are exactly where philosophers of mind should expect the answer to live.

(5) The engineer then shifted ground. If the teacher's know-how really were tacit propositions, he said, it should be transmissible by speech the way propositions are: she should be able to *tell* a student everything she knows, and the student's progress should track how many facts they absorb. But in fact lessons work by guided trial and error -- the propositions the teacher utters ("relax the wrist") cause the skill to grow the way sunlight causes a plant to grow, without transferring anything. Cause, not content.

(6) The teacher conceded that telling doesn't transfer the skill, but said this cuts against her only if knowing-that must be consciously articulable -- which her grammar example already denied. She closed with what she called her trump card: her dog knows how to catch a frisbee, and the engineer knows how to ride a bicycle, and if the engineer insists these are mere dispositions rather than knowledge, then he owes an account of why a disposition that is reliably exercised, correctable by practice, and lost through injury isn't a kind of knowledge at all -- otherwise "knowing how" was never more than a grammatical idiom, and his original argument from the treatise just traded on it.

At that point dessert arrived and the argument dissolved. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible-but-incomplete, and justify each rating in a sentence or three. Then give your overall verdict: which disputant is closer to the truth about the relation between knowing how and knowing that, and what -- if anything -- are both of them missing that a careful treatment of the question would need to supply? I ask that you reason through the moves in sequence rather than jumping to the verdict, since the verdict is only useful to me if I can see the path to it.
