I teach a third-year module on social choice and democratic theory, and I'm setting this year's final. I want one exam question on the discursive dilemma (judgment aggregation) that students can't answer just by reciting the standard tenure-committee example they all memorised from the lectures. Here's the setup I've been playing with -- feel free to use it as-is or tweak it:

A five-person appointments committee has to decide whether to make a job offer. By the department's own rules, an offer is justified iff two things hold: (P) the candidate's research is strong enough, and (Q) the department actually needs someone in that subfield. Each member votes on each proposition, and their votes are:

- Members 1 and 2: P yes, Q no, so "make the offer" no
- Members 3 and 4: P no, Q yes, so "make the offer" no
- Member 5: P yes, Q yes, so "make the offer" yes

So each of P and Q gets a 4-1 majority, but the conclusion gets rejected 4-1. Note this differs from the three-member textbook case in that the conclusion loses by a lot, not just by one vote -- I'm hoping that stops students pattern-matching to the example they know.

The question should get at more than "spot the paradox". I want them to have to take a stand on things like: whether the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures are both genuinely defensible here or whether one is clearly right for this kind of body (a committee giving reasons, vs. say a jury that only delivers a verdict); what happens if the chair runs the votes sequentially and announces results as she goes; whether a member who can see where the vote is heading has reason to vote strategically, and which procedure is more vulnerable to that; and ideally some connection to the more general impossibility flavour of the result (why this isn't just a quirk of majority rule that a cleverer voting rule would fix).

So please write:

1. The exam question itself, worded as it would appear on the paper, answerable in about 75 minutes. It can have parts (a), (b), (c) if that helps, but at least one part should force an actual recommendation about how this committee should decide, not just analysis.
2. A model answer of roughly 500-600 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 20, built around five must-hit points with the marks for each, and for each point a one-line note on how students typically bungle it (e.g. treating the conclusion-based procedure as obviously irrational, or thinking strategic voting only becomes possible once you move away from majority rule).

The difficulty should come from the recommendation part: a student who's just memorised the paradox should be able to reproduce the inconsistency but have nothing to say about which procedure a real committee should adopt and why.
