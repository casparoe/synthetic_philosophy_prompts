I run the clinical protocol office for a mid-sized hospital system. Several times a month we write standing protocols for situations in which the person executing a step cannot reliably remember whether a prior step was performed: patients self-administering medication at home who forget their morning dose, agency nurses rotating through wards who cannot recall whether a repositioning or a dose was logged by the previous shift, and so on. When we write these protocols we keep running into the same structural puzzle, and I want it turned into something my staff can apply mechanically.

The puzzle, in its stripped-down form, is this. A driver on a highway will pass two consecutive exits and cannot tell them apart: arriving at an exit, he never knows whether it is the first or the second. Taking the first exit yields a mediocre outcome, taking the second yields the best outcome, and missing both is the worst. Before setting out, the best plan he can commit to is to continue past each exit he encounters with some fixed probability (the arithmetic gives two-thirds). But once he is actually sitting at an exit and reasons about where he probably is, the same calculation recommends a different probability of continuing. The plan that is best when chosen in advance is not the plan the agent endorses at the moment of action, because the planner and the agent have different beliefs about the agent's situation. Some analysts conclude the agent should simply execute the ex ante optimal plan; others conclude that rationality at the moment of action has to govern, since that is the only moment at which anything is done.

Write a decision procedure my office can run on any proposed protocol that has this shape — repeated or possibly-repeated decisions under imperfect recall — to settle three things in order: first, whether the situation should simply be restructured to restore memory (logbooks, timestamped pill caps, checklists), and what costs make that answer unavailable; second, when restructuring fails, how to compute both the ex-ante-optimal policy and the action-moment-endorsed policy for the actual case, and how to tell whether they conflict; third, a rule for which of the two the printed protocol should instruct when they do conflict, given that our agents are fallible humans who will sometimes deviate from printed instructions when the instruction strikes them as wrong on the spot. The procedure must say, at each step, what input is needed and what counts as a sufficient answer, and it must mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated — for instance, whether randomization is ever acceptable in a clinical instruction, and how to weigh execution fidelity against expected-outcome optimality.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) the two-exit driver above, as a calibration check so I can see the machinery working on known numbers; (2) a patient on a twice-daily drug where a double dose is dangerous, who at midday cannot remember the morning dose and must decide among taking a full dose now, a half dose, or skipping; and (3) a case of your own construction, chosen so the procedure outputs an answer a reasonable clinical director would dislike — for example, instructing staff to follow the printed plan against their on-the-spot judgment. Close by stating what the procedure cannot settle and which kinds of cases should bypass it entirely and go straight to a human committee.
