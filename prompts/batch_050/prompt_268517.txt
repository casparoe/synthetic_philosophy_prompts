You're a practical philosopher who's spent way too long thinking about self-binding, and you've been roped in as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration. Think carefully, and brainstorm before you draft anything.

Background — no need to look anything up, everything you need is in this prompt. Two camps have been arguing for years about "Ulysses contracts": arrangements where someone at time 1 restricts their own options at time 2, because they expect their time-2 self to want things the time-1 self endorses not giving in to (Odysseus at the mast being the cartoon version). Team Precommitment holds that this kind of binding, when done freely and with decent information, is just self-governance stretched across time — the same agent steering their own life — and that courts or relatives who block it are the ones really disrespecting autonomy. Team Later Self holds that when the time-2 self sincerely repudiates the old plan, enforcing the contract is one stage of a person coercing another stage, morally much closer to one person binding a stranger, and that "but you agreed to this earlier" doesn't settle who should win.

To keep them honest, both teams have agreed to argue over one invented case instead of their favourite slogans:

The Cassandra Clause. Dana, 34, has watched two close friends join high-demand groups and, within a year, sincerely testify that joining was the best decision of their lives — while cutting off family and signing over their savings. Dana judges that if she ever joins such a group, her own testimony at that point will be unreliable evidence about what's good for her. So she signs a contract with her brother: if she ever asks to move into a group's commune or donate more than 10% of her income to one, he is to treat the request as void for 12 months, no matter how lucid and consistent she seems. The clause even spells out that her future sincerity is exactly what she doesn't trust. Three years later, Dana joins such a group and, calmly and by all external measures competently, says it was her earlier self who was confused — anxious, suggestible, projecting. Her brother invokes the clause.

One wrinkle they also want covered: the polity-level analogue. Constitutions are often defended as a people's precommitment device (entrenchment clauses, supermajority amendment rules, rights put beyond the reach of ordinary legislation). Team Precommitment tends to like this analogy; Team Later Self tends to reach for the dead-hand objection instead.

Write the document this collaboration should actually produce:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — at least 8. Each should be substantive enough that some reasonable person could deny it, and each should be something both teams can genuinely sign, not a mushy compromise sentence.
2. A disagreements table. Each row states one residual disagreement, with each side's position phrased so that side would accept it as a fair statement of its own view. No "they just don't care about autonomy" framings.
3. Cruxes: 3–5 sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one team. For each crux, name a concrete case, observation, or argument that both teams agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each team predicts it will show.
4. Application: given the agreed claims, state where each team must land on Dana's clause and on constitutional entrenchment. Flag any point where a team's stance on the clause and its stance on constitutions sit in tension — e.g. a team that trusts Dana's time-1 judgment but not a founding generation's time-1 judgment owes an explanation, and so does a team with the reverse pairing.

Ground rules: don't adjudicate who wins; this is a joint statement, not a verdict. Don't invent authorities or citations — reason from the case and from the positions as stated. Where a disagreement turns out to be about words, say so, dissolve it, and restate whatever (if anything) is left. Start by brainstorming a rough longlist of candidate cruxes for yourself (leave the longlist out of the final document), then write the document itself.
