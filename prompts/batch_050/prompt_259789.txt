I'm a retired organic chemist who has, late in life, fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, and lately I've been reading around in this debate about the "reasons of love" -- the appraisal crowd who say love is a response to the valuable qualities you find in a person, versus the bestowal crowd who say love confers value rather than detecting it (I gather Frankfurt and Velleman are the big names here). My confusion started at my cousin's golden wedding anniversary, where the couple renewed their vows, and then continued a week later when my grandaughter explained to me how her dating app works. Here are two practices that seem to coexist in our culture without anyone noticing a tension. Practice one: wedding vows, in which people solemnly promise to keep loving each other for decades, plus the strong social norm that leaving your spouse for someone objectively "better" -- younger, funnier, kinder, richer, with all your spouse's good qualities in higher concentration -- is a betrayal, even if you haven't been dishonest and nobody's done anything wrong. Practice two: the whole courtship-and-apps culture, which treats falling in love as something that happens to you (chemistry, the spark, you can't help who you love) and treats picking a partner as a legitimate comparison exercise -- swiping, ranking, "you can do better," "don't settle."

What I want to know is what each of these practices presupposes about the reasons of love. The vows and the anti-trading-up norm seem to make sense only if love isn't just an appraisal, since an honest appraiser should upgrade to the better sample the way I'd swap out an impure reagent for a purer one, and since you can't promise to keep having an involuntary response for fifty years. But the dating culture seems to presuppose exactly the appraising, quality-detecting picture that the vows rule out. So: reconstruct the implicit theory behind each practice, tell me whether they're actually inconsistent or whether one theory of love's reasons can underwrite both (does the bestowal view handle the dating side, or does some hybrid, like the idea that love is about valuing a shared history or a relationship rather than a person, split the difference?). Also tell me what would have to change in wedding practice if the pure appraisal view turned out to be right -- should vows come with an escape clause for better matches, and would anyone get married under those terms? And be honest about where the practices are just philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where several rival theories would license exactly the behavior we see.

I'm not after a reading list, just the analysis. Please give me a clear bottom line: end your answer with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence saying which view of love's reasons, if any, the two practices jointly presuppose, or stating plainly that no single view does.
