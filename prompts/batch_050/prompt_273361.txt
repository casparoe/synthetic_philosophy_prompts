I have been teaching a graduate seminar on rational choice and strategic interaction, and this term we spent several weeks on the logic of commitment: Schelling-style precommitment, the folk result that burning one's bridges can be advantageous precisely because it forecloses options, and the less cheerful observation that when commitment devices are cheap and universally available, the advantages tend to cancel out and the costs do not. In particular, my seminar worked through the structure sometimes called a commitment race: in games like Chicken, if each player can irrevocably lock in the aggressive strategy before play, then each has reason to commit first, and if both can commit at negligible cost, the predictable outcome is mutual lock-in to strategies that produce the worst joint result, even though each party would have preferred a world in which no commitment technology existed at all.

Out of those sessions I have arrived at a position I would now like stress-tested rather than applauded. The position is this: the widespread availability of cheap, credible, and enforceable commitment technology would be net harmful for rational agents in competitive environments, and a rational agent who could unilaterally delete all such technology from the world would typically be doing everyone a favor, including her rivals. My reasoning is essentially that commitment devices transform games into races to commit first, that races to commit are winner-take-most contests over the right to be unreasonable, and that in equilibrium the rents from being first get dissipated by the cost and risk of racing, leaving everyone worse off than under mutual commitment prohibition. I take this to generalize beyond the textbook two-player cases to arms-control settings, salary negotiations conducted through take-it-or-leave-it offers posted in advance, and any domain where an agent can publicly and irreversibly rule out future flexibility.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? I would like you to distinguish several classes of potential defeater and to rank them from decisive to merely awkward. First, structural objections: is the commitment-race framing itself correct, or does it smuggle in assumptions -- about the timing of moves, about the observability and verifiability of commitments, about whether commitments can be made conditional or revocable at a price, about the number of players -- whose failure would defuse the pessimism without touching the underlying game theory? Second, counterexamples: are there well-understood classes of strategic situation, perhaps coordination-heavy or repeated ones, where cheap commitment is unambiguously welfare-improving, and does my position survive their existence or quietly collapse into a claim only about zero-sum-adjacent games? Third, the empirical and quasi-empirical question of what commitment technology actually does when it arrives: does the historical record of hostages, deposits, contracts, constitutional self-binding, and automatic retaliation systems bear out the race-to-lock-in story, or does it suggest that agents mostly fail to exploit commitment advantages and so the race never really starts? Fourth, I want you to consider the strongest version of the opposing view a careful defender of commitment would actually hold -- presumably something about how the ability to bind oneself is what makes promises, treaties, and long-term cooperation possible at all -- and ask whether my position, properly stated, is in fact consistent with that view once we distinguish commitment that forecloses options from commitment that creates them.

Please also tell me, honestly, whether my position risks anything at all or whether I have qualified it into unfalsifiability by saying "typically" and "in competitive environments," and if it has drifted toward unfalsifiability, whether that is a defect in a thesis of this kind or simply the shape such theses have. Finally, I would like the reply itself to be self-critical: state plainly which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and identify where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Write your response as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
