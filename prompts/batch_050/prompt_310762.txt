A colleague of mine in behavioral genetics talks has an argument he thinks shows the whole heritability literature is conceptually broken, and I can't find the flaw, though the conclusion seems to prove too much. (1) Heritability estimates for cognitive ability in wealthy countries run roughly 0.5 to 0.8, meaning most of the variation between individuals traces to genetic rather than environmental differences. (2) If most variation is genetic, then differences in schooling, parenting, and enrichment account for only a small share of the gaps between people. (3) A policy that only changes environments can at most eliminate the portion of inequality that environments cause. (4) So even ideal social policy could erase only a small fraction of inequality in cognitive outcomes. (5) The same reasoning applies to height, whose heritability is around 0.8, so nutrition policy could have erased only a small fraction of height inequality. (6) But average height rose substantially within a few generations as nutrition improved, and gaps between well-fed and deprived groups narrowed -- so (5)'s conclusion is false, and the parallel conclusion (4) is unearned. (7) Therefore heritability estimates must not measure what (1) says they measure; they can't be about what genes and environments actually cause. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs -- in particular, whether rejecting it requires denying that heritability is a real variance partition, or only that it licenses the move from (2) and (3) to (4). If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) through (3) than accept (7). Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
