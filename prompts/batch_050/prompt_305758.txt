I am preparing an application for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship in philosophy of physics and need a research proposal of roughly two pages. My project concerns Gödel's 1949 argument from rotating universes to the ideality of time, and I have a half-formed angle I would like you to develop into a full proposal.

The background, as I understand it: Gödel presented exact solutions of Einstein's field equations describing rotating universes in which closed timelike curves pass through every point, and he argued — not that our universe permits time travel, which he did not claim — but that the sheer possibility of such worlds under our own laws of nature shows that no objective lapse of time exists even in worlds like ours, which by all observations do not rotate. Commentators have long noted that this reasoning contains a modal step: from the claim that time cannot objectively pass in some physically possible worlds to the conclusion that it does not objectively pass in ours. My suspicion is that this step rests on a suppressed premise — roughly, that whether time objectively passes cannot be a contingent feature of a world, on a par with its rate of expansion or its net rotation — and that this premise, rather than the physics, is where the argument should be fought. As far as I can tell, existing responses either grant Gödel worlds their physics and argue that such worlds retain enough global temporal structure to count as temporal anyway, or else welcome the ideality conclusion as friendly to a block-universe picture; the modal premise itself tends to be endorsed in a sentence or dismissed in a sentence, not analyzed in its own right.

Write the proposal so that it includes: a precise statement of the central question; an explanation of why the existing responses leave that question open, without caricaturing them; the method — how I would reconstruct Gödel's compressed reasoning into explicit premises, how I would state the suppressed premise precisely enough for it to be evaluated rather than merely intuited, and which accounts of temporal passage and of natural law I would test it against, with reasons for choosing those; the possible outcomes and what each would establish, including the outcome in which Gödel's argument survives only as a conditional claim about universes that actually contain closed timelike curves, and the outcome in which its force turns out to be hostage to the metaphysics of lawhood; what would count as the project failing, for instance if the reconstruction shows Gödel never needed the premise, or if evaluating it collapses into the standard dispute between dynamic and static theories of time with no added content; a realistic statement of deliverables for the two years; and a scope note for a single researcher with one graduate course in general relativity and no ambition of producing original physics, explaining why this angle is worth pursuing rather than the more familiar questions of whether our universe in fact rotates or whether some chronology-protection principle rules Gödel worlds out of the relevant possibility space. Do not include citations or a bibliography; I will supply those myself.
