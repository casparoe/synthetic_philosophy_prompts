I teach an upper-division seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and each year I pair Ursula K. Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas with the philosophical literature on the aggregation of small harms — the choice, familiar to my students, between one person's undergoing severe and prolonged torture and an astronomical number of people's each suffering nothing worse than a dust speck in the eye. The pairing is deliberate, because I have come to suspect that the standard classroom reading of the story misdescribes its structure.

The usual reading treats Omelas as an argument against aggregation: the happiness of millions cannot simply be summed against the misery of one, and the story shows why. But notice that the case involves no aggregation on the harm side at all. There is exactly one victim, bearing a concentrated, maximal harm, against which the story sets the diffuse and largely unexamined flourishing of everyone else. Omelas is thus the mirror image of the torture-versus-dust-specks problem, not an instance of it — and I want my students to see what follows from that fact. I would like a reading of the story through the aggregation lens, done seriously and in both directions, along these lines:

First, run a redistribution test. Suppose the child's suffering could be parceled out, so that every citizen of Omelas instead bore one imperceptible increment of misery — a momentary itch, a dust speck's worth of displeasure — with the total disutility held constant. Would those who walk away still be right to walk? If your answer changes, then the story's force lies not in an anti-aggregation moral but in something about concentration itself — perhaps a deontological constraint against using one person as a means, perhaps a threshold below which harms become normatively invisible — and the reading should say which.

Second, run an inversion. Suppose the bargain were reversed: one citizen lives in unbroken ecstasy in a tower, and this somehow sustains a city in which everyone else endures chronic, low-grade, barely noticed misery. Does the story still indict? If not, what work was the direction of the asymmetry doing in the original?

Third, take the narration seriously as part of the argument. Le Guin's narrator addresses the reader directly, anticipates our incredulity, and introduces the child only after we have been made complicit in imagining the utopia. Is this literary structure doing philosophical work — seducing the reader into an aggregative frame before exposing it — and if so, does that strengthen the reading or rig its outcome? Be honest about where the story resists my lens: the child's suffering is involuntary and concealed, features the dust-specks construction conspicuously lacks, and a reading that attends only to the arithmetic will miss whatever the story is actually about. Say what, if anything, the story contributes to the aggregation debate that the bare thought experiments cannot.

Finally, a request concerning the reading's epistemic standing. Tell me what would change your verdict. If you conclude that Omelas is, at bottom, an argument about aggregation rather than about deontological side-constraints, identify the consideration or feature of the story that would persuade you otherwise; if you conclude the reverse, indicate where your reading is most exposed to counterargument.
