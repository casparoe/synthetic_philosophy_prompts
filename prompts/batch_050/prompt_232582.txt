So I was arguing with a buddy from my poli-sci days about inequality, and he deployed what he called the "knife test": fairness in dividing anything is just the cut-and-choose rule from splitting cake -- one person cuts, the other picks their slice, so the cutter has every reason to cut evenly. His claim was that Rawls basically scaled this up: design society's rules from behind a veil of ignorance where you don't know which position you'll end up in, and cut-and-choose logic does the rest, so the difference principle (inequalities only ok if they help the worst off) falls out for free, no controversial premises needed. He said anyone who rejects the difference principle must also think cutting cake unevenly is fine, which nobody does.

I'm suspicious the cake is doing a lot of hidden work here, but I can't pin down where. You're a political philosopher who's thought hard about Rawls -- take your time, brainstorm the mappings before you settle on a verdict, and give me:

1. The correspondence table. What plays the role of the cutter, the chooser, the knife, the slices, the preference for bigger slices? Be careful -- in Rawls there's arguably no separate cutter and chooser, and I want to know if that already breaks something.
2. The disanalogies that matter vs. the ones that don't. Some things I suspect: the cake is a fixed pie but the social product can grow or shrink depending on the rules; cake-cutting assumes the chooser knows what they want (bigger = better) but the veil strips knowledge of your conception of the good; cake slices are consumed once, social positions are lived for a lifetime. Are these the load-bearing ones or am I off?
3. What the analogy smuggles in. Cut-and-choose delivers something like envy-freeness -- nobody prefers someone else's slice. Does the difference principle even aim at that? If the analogy's natural output is "nobody envies" and Rawls's output is "the worst off are as well off as possible," does my friend's "falls out for free" claim collapse, since a growing pie might make an envious-but-better-off worst person the Rawlsian pick?
4. The verdict: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are priced in, or was the relaxed "it's just obvious fairness" vibe carried entirely by dessert? And if there's a better everyday analogy for what the veil actually does, give me that instead.
