I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long feature on the analogy between molecular self-replication and formal self-reference — the territory Douglas Hofstadter dubbed typogenetics, in which strands of symbols function simultaneously as instructions and as the very text that copying machinery reads, much as DNA is at once a program and a document. My editor has allotted me space for exactly three substantial threads, no more. Below are six candidate questions I have assembled; each has been raised, in some form, by people working in origins-of-life research, computation theory, or artificial life. Tell me which three I should build the piece around, in what order, and — most importantly — why.

1. Whether self-reference is genuinely necessary for robust self-replication, or merely the solution biology happened upon: could a "dumb" template-copier that nowhere encodes a description of itself count as alive in the relevant sense, and what hangs on the answer?
2. Whether the DNA–ribosome system really instantiates von Neumann's split between a constructor and a description of that constructor, or whether the fact that ribosomes are themselves encoded on DNA turns the allegedly clean architecture into a regress — and if so, whether the regress is vicious or the whole point.
3. Whether Gödel-style incompleteness has any serious biological counterpart: are there, in principle, "unbuildable" variants that a self-describing replicator cannot reach, and would that say anything deep or is it an analogy stretched past its breaking point?
4. How a purely typogenetic system — strings and string-operations, no chemistry — could ever exhibit open-ended evolution rather than settling into a fixed point or a short loop, and whether open-endedness is a property of a system's logic or only of its physics.
5. Whether mutation is better modeled as noise corrupting a message, in the information-theoretic sense, or as the machinery reinterpreting its own description — and whether the choice of model changes what even counts as an error.
6. Which direction the explanatory arrow runs: whether formal self-reference illuminates DNA, or DNA illuminates formal self-reference, and what would count as evidence either way.

For each thread you select, defend the choice on three grounds: how much of the surrounding territory it pulls in its wake, since a good feature question should make several of the others easier to explain rather than compete with them; how faithfully it can be rendered for a general reader without the kind of flattening that would embarrass me in front of the researchers I interview; and what developments over the next decade — in synthetic biology, origins-of-life chemistry, or the theory of computation — would move it up or down your ranking. Close by telling me which candidate you would cut first and what is actually wrong with it: I want to know whether it is merely less urgent than the others or whether it rests on a misunderstanding of what the DNA–Gödel analogy does and does not show.
