Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about transformative experience and ended up further apart than when we started: she thinks the notion shows that a whole class of ordinary life decisions cannot be made rationally, and I think she was leaning on a slide between two things the notion keeps apart. Next week I have to lead a reading group of non-specialists (interested amateurs, no philosophy background, one session of ninety minutes) through the concept, and I want to do better with them than I did with her. So don't explain transformative experience to me; plan how I should explain it to them.

Specifically, I want the following. First, the order of ideas: which components must be introduced before which others, and why — for instance, whether the distinction between an experience you cannot imagine in advance and an experience that changes what you value should come before or after the decision-theoretic framing, and what breaks pedagogically if I get the order wrong. Second, the carrying example: the one case that should do the bulk of the work in the room. I'd rather not use becoming a vampire, which strikes me as too fanciful to produce real disagreement, and I'm unsure between choosing to have a child and a deaf person's deciding whether to receive a cochlear implant — tell me which one carries the idea better, what properties the example must have (and which tempting properties it must lack, e.g. obvious moral stakes or a conventionally right answer), and why the other fails or distracts. Third, the misconception map: the confusions this audience will predictably form — I anticipate at least 'this is just ordinary uncertainty about outcomes', 'so all big decisions are irrational, apparently', and 'this is only about preferences changing afterwards' — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact, since my argument with my friend convinced me that the second of these is nearly impossible to dislodge once it settles. Fourth, one closing question I can put to the group that would show whether the distinction my friend blurred has actually landed — ideally a fresh case on which someone who merely parrots the material and someone who understands it would answer differently. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to flag explicitly as omitted: I assume the formal decision-theory machinery and the critiques of the original argument are too much for ninety minutes, but if one of them turns out to be load-bearing for avoiding a misconception above, say so.
