I'm a PhD student in evolutionary biology, and I keep getting into late-night arguments with a friend who's doing philosophy of mind about whether "meaning" is anything biologically special or just another thing function-talk already covers. My working hunch is that the whole original-vs-derived intentionality fight is something my field dissolved decades ago, but she's convinced I'm flattening the debate by translating it into terms where the interesting part can't even be stated. We've basically agreed to settle it by actually attempting the translation and seeing what's left over, so that's what I want from you.

Here's the dispute as I understand it: Searle thinks real, intrinsic aboutness only exists in minds, and whatever aboutness artifacts like computers or books have is entirely borrowed from the people who made or use them (the Chinese Room being his case that shuffling symbols according to rules never adds up to understanding). Dennett replies with the two-bitser: build a vending machine to accept US quarters, then ship it somewhere that a different but physically indistinguishable coin circulates, and ask what the machine "really" detects. His point is that there's no deeper fact of the matter beyond design history and environment, and that our own intentionality is like that too, just with natural selection playing the engineer.

So: restate both positions as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in -- selected-effect functions vs. causal-role functions, adaptation vs. exaptation, drift, homology, environment of evolutionary adaptedness, fitness contributions. Tell me how fluent you can assume I am (very -- don't baby me on the biology, but spell out the philosophy carefully). Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly (I'm guessing derived intentionality maps onto artifact function pretty well, though maybe the designer-isn't-selection thing already fudges it), which ones only survive with distortion, and which have no expression at all in my framework. My candidates for the untranslatable stuff are the normativity of content (how a trait can malfunction gives you error, but does it give you *misrepresentation*?), the first-person/phenomenological side of Searle's argument, and his claim that syntax alone can't be semantics. For each piece of residue, I want your honest take on whether it shows my vocabulary is missing something real or whether it shows the original claim was murkier than it looked. And don't just hand the win to either of us -- if my instinct that the two-bitser is a textbook case of function indeterminacy under environment change is missing what philosophers are actually arguing about, I want to know that too.
