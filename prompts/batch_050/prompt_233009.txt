At a department happy hour last week I watched two arguments collapse into the same one-liner. In the first, a colleague teaching philosophy of law insisted that a judge who says the defendant is guilty does not thereby make anyone guilty, whatever the institutional effect of the verdict; her interlocutor replied, as if it settled things, that saying makes it so. In the second, a graduate student defending the claim that certain slurs do not merely offend but subordinate was met with the very same sentence, this time from her own side: of course words can do that, because saying makes it so. I doubt one slogan can honestly serve both camps, since the first speaker needs it to be about judges and the second needs it to be about insults, and I suspect it means something different, or nothing precise, each time.

Here is what I would like. Take the slogan saying makes it so and lay out, as a menu rather than a single exegesis, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. At minimum, distinguish the following kinds of reading, and add any I have missed: that some utterances make themselves true simply by being uttered; that within institutions, suitably placed speakers can change what people owe, own, or are by declaring it so; that every assertion, quite generally, alters the speaker's normative standing by incurring commitments; that speech causes effects the way any physical event does, with nothing special about saying; and that the whole of social reality, including hierarchies and harms, is somehow constituted by speech rather than merely reported by it.

For each reading, I want three verdicts. First, whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, with a sentence of argument for the verdict. Second, what has to be true of the world for the reading to hold: conventions in place, authority vested in the speaker, uptake from an audience, sincerity, or whatever else is doing the work. Third, whether that reading would actually support the argumentative use I saw it put to: the philosophy-of-law exchange about verdicts, or the claim that slurs subordinate. Where a reading supports one use but not the other, say so explicitly, because that asymmetry is really my question.

Two further constraints. Keep the analysis independent of any particular theorist's terminology where possible; if a piece of jargon is unavoidable, define it in one clause and move on, since I want to reuse your menu in a handout for students who have not read the literature. And close with your best guess about which reading, or which blur of readings, competent speakers usually have in mind when they reach for the slogan, and whether the slogan's rhetorical usefulness depends on its readers hearing a stronger reading than the speaker could defend. Please write the answer in American English.
