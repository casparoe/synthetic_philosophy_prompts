Two friends of mine have been arguing for weeks about a hypothetical machine: once you're hooked up, it gives you a lifetime of perfectly convincing experiences -- success, love, adventure, whatever you'd most want -- indistinguishable from the real thing, and you forget you plugged in. Mara says she'd never enter, because what matters to her isn't how life feels from the inside but that her projects and relationships are real. Daniel says this is confused, since everything she has ever cared about reached her only as experience anyway; the machine just offers better experiences, and refusing it for the sake of "reality" is valuing a label over a life. Mara answered that in the machine her friends wouldn't actually miss her or think of her, so the love she'd feel would be one-way, and one-way love is a lesser thing even if it feels identical. Daniel replied with a case: a woman whose husband dies, but who is never told and is made to believe he's away on a long trip -- on Mara's view, the happiness she felt in those years was already defective, which Daniel finds monstrous. Mara conceded that felt happiness is worth something, but said the deception made it fragile, not valueless, and the machine is fragility perfected: nothing in it can be lost because nothing in it was ever there. Daniel's final move was to ask whether, if Mara learned tomorrow that her whole life so far had been a simulation, her accomplishments would retroactively become worthless. She said, after a pause, that they'd be worth somewhat less -- and Daniel declared victory, since any view on which your life might secretly be worth less than you think, without any difference you could ever detect, has gone wrong somewhere. She said vulnerability to bad news isn't the same as going wrong. I reported this to a third friend who refused to pick sides, so I'm asking you: go through the exchange point by point, say who is right about each specific claim, and identify what -- if anything -- both of them are failing to see.
