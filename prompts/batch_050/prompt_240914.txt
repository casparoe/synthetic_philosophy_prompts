My colleague in the cog sci department and I have this running game where I throw philosophy of mind thought experiments at her and she has to give a verdict, no hedging allowed. She swears she has no theory, she's just going case by case, but I'm teaching phil mind next semester and I'm determined to reverse-engineer whatever she's actually running. Her verdicts so far: Swampman — the atom-for-atom duplicate of me that a freak lightning strike assembles out of swamp muck, zero causal history — fully minded, pains, beliefs, memories (well, apparent memories), the works. Blockhead, the giant lookup-table contraption that passes any Turing test you like by searching a precomputed list of every possible conversation: not minded, and she was pretty dismissive about it, called it a jukebox. The China brain — a billion people with radios each playing the role of one neuron for an hour, perfectly replicating the neuron-level goings-on in someone with a splitting headache: she says there's a genuine headache being felt there, everytime, even if it's nobody's headache in particular. Gradual replacement, where my neurons get swapped for functionally equivalent silicon chips one at a time with all behavior and organization preserved: conscious the whole way through, and she said the chips don't even need to be silicon, they could be "beer cans and string if you could get the causal links right." And a plain old thermostat: nothing it is like to be one, obviously.

Next week I'm going to spring this one on her: the exact same China brain setup, neuron-level organization perfectly preserved, except it runs slow — each radio message gets sent once a day instead of instantaneously, so what would be a ten-second headache in a normal brain gets stretched across decades, with all the little causal dependencies between the "neurons" intact, just massively decelerated. Is the headache still being felt, somewhere in those decades? What I want from you: first, lay out at least two rules or theories her track record so far is consistent with (I'd be surprised if it pinned down just one), and say what each one predicts about the slow-motion case — they should diverge on it, otherwise I've picked a boring case. Then give me your actual prediction of her verdict and why, and finally name the further thought experiment that would settle which rule she's really running. One wrinkle: in her published work she makes sympathetic noises about embodiment and dynamic interaction with the environment, so if you think that matters, tell me how it fits with the China brain verdict, because to me those two pull in different directions. Please answer in American English, say how confident you are in your prediction, and single out the part of your reconstruction you're least sure of.
