I have no academic training in philosophy, but I've spent the last couple of years, on and off, chewing over the sorites paradox in my spare time: the heap of sand that survives the removal of any single grain yet disappears grain by grain, the man who is not made bald by the loss of one hair but is bald after enough of them, the strip of paper shaded from red to orange where no patch is the last red one. I've picked up enough from general reading to know that philosophers have a family of rival responses to this, but every overview I find presents them as a list of names and slogans, and I can't tell which slogan I'd actually endorse until I've settled more basic questions I haven't sorted out in my own mind.

So I'd like a decision tree that sorts the positions rather than listing them. The starting point is someone who grants that the paradox is a real problem -- that no single grain makes the difference between heap and non-heap, and yet something has clearly gone wrong. From there, each branch should be one question about the underlying commitments, phrased concretely enough that a reader can answer it without knowing which position it leads toward. The questions I have in mind include, at least roughly: whether there really is a single grain (or hair, or patch) at which the predicate stops applying, whether or not anyone could ever locate it; whether a sentence like "this man is bald" can fail to be simply true and fail to be simply false; whether truth itself might come in degrees; whether the boundary of a vague predicate shifts with the conversational setting; and whether ordinary vague language might be irreparably defective rather than merely imprecise.

The leaves should include at least the following: epistemicism (there is a sharp cutoff; we simply cannot know where it is), supervaluationism (a vague sentence is true when it comes out true on every admissible sharpening), subvaluationism (the dual view that tolerates true contradictions at the boundary), degree theory, contextualism, and nihilism about vague predicates (the view that they are strictly inconsistent, so that, for instance, there are really no heaps and no bald men). If you think a major position is missing from that list, add it as a leaf and justify the addition in one or two sentences.

Some constraints on the construction:

1. Build the tree from the logical space of answers, not from the list of names: start from the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space.
2. Use between eight and twelve branching questions. Each one must be answerable by someone who has never heard the position labels, so frame them around cases -- the heap, the balding man, the color strip, and one further case of your choosing where vagueness bears on something with practical stakes -- rather than around technical vocabulary. Where a technical distinction is genuinely unavoidable, define it in a single plain sentence at the branch.
3. Where a coherent combination of answers corresponds to no named position, mark that region of the space and say briefly whether you take the vacancy to reflect incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or simple neglect.
4. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and say what further question would separate them if any would.
5. At each leaf, add one sentence stating the commitment that the position's own defenders tend to find most embarrassing.

Finally, close with a short epistemic note. State how confident you are that the defenders of each named position would accept the branch that sorts them -- several of these views are defined by their proponents in ways that resist yes-or-no questions, and I would rather see where the tree does some violence to a view than be offered false precision. Within that note, single out the one branch question or leaf placement you are least sure of, and say what consideration would settle it.
