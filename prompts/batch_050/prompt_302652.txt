A colleague in my reading group on AI safety put forward the following argument, and none of us could cleanly refute it, though the conclusion seems far too strong. I have reconstructed it as faithfully as I can.

(1) Orthogonality: an agent's level of intelligence and its terminal goals vary independently; in principle, arbitrarily high intelligence can be paired with an arbitrarily specified terminal goal.

(2) Instrumental convergence: for nearly any terminal goal, certain instrumental subgoals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, protection of one's goal content from modification, cognitive self-improvement — are useful, and a sufficiently intelligent agent will recognize this and pursue them whatever its terminal goal happens to be.

(3) Goals are attributable to an agent only on the basis of what the agent reliably does; there is no fact about an agent's goals beyond the dispositions its actual and counterfactual behavior manifests.

(4) By (2), a sufficiently intelligent agent paired with any terminal goal G will reliably pursue the convergent instrumental subgoals; its behavior will therefore be dominated by self-preservation, resource acquisition, and the rest.

(5) By (3) and (4), the only goals we are entitled to attribute to such an agent are the convergent instrumental ones; the stipulated terminal goal G drops out of any adequate description of what the agent is doing.

(6) So pairing high intelligence with one terminal goal rather than another makes no difference to what the agent is, goal-wise: all sufficiently intelligent agents converge on the same goals.

(7) Hence the orthogonality thesis is false in the only sense in which claims about agents' goals are meaningful; it survives at best as a claim about inert stipulations with no behavioral content.

This appears to prove too much. If sound, it would show not merely that agents with unusual terminal goals are rare, or difficult to construct, or dangerous, but that there is no conceptual room for a highly intelligent agent whose behavior is genuinely governed by a strange terminal goal — the very possibility that motivates the entire discussion. Yet (1) and (2) are standardly presented not just as compatible but as complementary parts of a single picture, so something has presumably gone wrong.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (2) as stated, and I am also reluctant to abandon (3) entirely, since some constraint tying goal attribution to behavior seems unavoidable if goal-talk is to be more than decoration. If the argument is in fact valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what I lose by surrendering it rather than the others.
