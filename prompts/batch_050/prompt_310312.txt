I've been chewing on something about the word "if" for a while and I want to know whether my instincts hang together or not. I don't have any training in logic or philosophy, this is just from paying attention to how me and people around me actually talk.

So here's my question: is there one rule for when an "if" sentence is okay to say that covers all of the cases below? Because my gut verdicts feel like they might not fit together, and I'd rather find that out from someone than keep having arguments with my brother about it.

1. We had leftovers go missing from the fridge. Two suspects: my sister and my dad. I'd happily say "if my sister didn't eat them, my dad did." But I would NOT say "if my sister hadn't eaten them, my dad would've." Those seem like the same thought to my brother but they don't to me at all. The first one is about what's true right now, the second one is about what would happen in some made-up version of events and I have no idea about that.

2. Last month I flipped a light switch and the lamp came on. "If I hadn't flipped the switch, the lamp wouldn't have come on" — obviously true, I think. But two weeks ago my roommate flipped a second switch at the exact same moment as a joke, and then "if I hadn't flipped my switch, the lamp would've come on anyway" seems just as true. Same sentence shape, opposite answers. I'm pretty confident in both verdicts but I can't explain the difference except by pointing at the situation.

3. "If I'd gone to bed early last night, I'd feel fine this morning" — sure. "If I'd gone to bed early AND had three espressos at midnight, I'd feel fine this morning" — no. So apparently you can't just throw extra conditions into the front part of an if-sentence and keep the conclusion. That seems like a real rule but it also seems like it breaks the if-sentences I learned in math class where you can always assume more stuff.

4. "If it doesn't rain Saturday, the picnic's on" — totally normal. "If it didn't rain Saturday, we'd have the picnic" — this one sounds off to me, like I've already given up on the weather, even though Saturday hasn't happened yet and neither of us knows anything. My verdict: it's not false exactly but you shouldn't say it. I don't know if "shouldn't say it but still true" is even a coherent category.

5. "If I hurry, I'll catch the bus" and "if I hurry, I might still miss it" — honestly I think both can be fine to say in the same breath, but then I feel dumb because that seems like saying something and taking it back at the same time.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts? State it in plain words, tell me which case each part of it is doing the work for, and be honest if there's a verdict of mine it can't save -- I'd rather drop one of my instincts than adopt some crazy complicated rule with a special clause for every case. Also tell me what your principle says about two cases I didn't mention: "if I hadn't forgotten my keys, I wouldn't be locked out right now" (mixing a past if with a right-now conclusion) and "if their goalie hadn't gotten hurt, we might have won."

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- like, yes there's one rule / no there isn't -- then get into the reasoning. No intro paragraph, no restating my cases back at me.
