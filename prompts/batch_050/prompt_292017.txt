im a novelist working on a book set in the ruins of a palace civilization, and i keep arguing with my brother-in-law (an archaeologist) about whether societies like that actually collapse or just change into something poorer and quieter. his favorite example is the maya -- the cities emptied, the inscriptions stopped, but the people never went anywhere, they just moved back to the hills and kept speaking mayan languages. i want a poem for my epigraph that takes his side seriously without quite handing him the win.

write me a sonnet. the octave should sit fully inside the "collapse" picture as it feels from the ground -- the last scribe leaving the palace, the last dated stone, fields going back to scrub -- and the volta should be the moment the reader realizes the archive ending isnt the same as the people ending, that a kingdom can collapse the way a wave collapses, i.e. back into water. the closing couplet then has to concede the cost honestly: something real was lost, even if almost nobody died of it, and i dont want the poem pretending the transformation story makes that loss disappear. no rhyming "doom" with "tomb", and it should be accurate enough that my brother in law cant nitpick it over christmas dinner.
