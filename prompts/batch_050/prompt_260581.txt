My girlfriend and I have been stuck on the same argument for almost a year, except it's a philosophy argument, not a real one. It started when she asked why I love her and I gave the honest list -- she's funny, she's sharper than me, she's the calmest person I've ever seen in a crisis -- and she said that answer actually bothered her, because if those are the reasons, then someone funnier and sharper showing up would be a reason to trade up, and if she stopped being those things my love would be, what, rationally unsupported? So I tried the other answer: no reasons, I just love you, full stop. She said that was somehow worse, because then it seems totally arbitrary, like a coin flip that happened to land on her, and the same flip could have landed on literally anyone. So apparently both available answers are bad, which is at least something we agree on.

Since then I've learned this is a real dispute, roughly the appraisal vs. bestowal debate about love's reasons (Velleman and Kolodny on one side, Frankfurt on the other, very roughly), and we've basically been reenacting it badly. Here's what I'd like: the document the two camps would produce if they did an adversarial collaboration instead of arguing past each other for fifty years. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can actually sign -- e.g. things about whether "because I just do" and "because of your qualities" are each inadequate as complete answers, about the trading-up and fungibility worries, about whether love needs to be responsive to the beloved's changes. Then a table of the remaining genuine disagreements with these columns exactly: Disagreement | Appraisal-side formulation (worded so that side would sign it) | Bestowal-side formulation (same standard) | Underlying crux | A case, thought experiment, or consideration both sides accept as bearing on that crux | What each side predicts or says about that case. Use cases like: loving someone through dementia, the funnier stranger at the party, a parent asked why they love their kid, and love that persists after betrayal. And if some of the disagreement turns out to be verbal -- like the two sides meaning different things by "reason" -- call that out in its own row rather than papering over it. Don't declare a winner; I just want to bring the table home and figure out which row our fight actually lives in.
