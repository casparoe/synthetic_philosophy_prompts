I facilitate a public-history night in my hometown, which is arguing over a plaque for the 1911 depot fire that helped end our rail economy; some residents blame the dry lightning, some the bankrupt railroad, some the town council’s canceled water tower, and some the insurance investigators. Design a 70-minute activity for about 18 adults, mixed ages and no philosophy background, that makes them feel why an event can be overdetermined without making explanation impossible: several conditions each look sufficient, some are merely preempted backups, and choosing one cause for a plaque is partly explanatory and partly political. Give rules, roles, a small token or budget mechanic, and a sequence in which participants first confidently rank causes, then discover that removing any single favored cause still lets the town burn in at least one plausible timeline, then must decide what the plaque may responsibly say. Include a debrief mapping their moves to difference-making, INUS-style insufficiency, preempted versus joint causes, and pragmatic causal selection; say what will predictably go wrong, how to keep it from becoming pure relativism, and which feature of real historical blame the game cannot reproduce.
