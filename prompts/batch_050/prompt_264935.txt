I work as a research assistant at a foreign-policy think tank, and over a couple of years of reading memos and op-eds and sitting in on debates, I have noticed a recurring pattern in how disputes unfold once a historical precedent gets invoked. I do not have a name for it, and I want to know whether one exists.

Three instances, because the abstract statement does not quite capture it. First: a working group is arguing about whether to arm some small country against a larger neighbor, and the discussion is granular -- troop dispositions, alliance commitments, production timelines. Then someone says the neighbor's leader is replaying 1938, and from that moment the debate is no longer about the country in question. It becomes a debate about 1938: whether appeasement was rational given rearmament schedules, whether the analogy's defender has read the revisionist historians. People who know nothing about the present case suddenly feel licensed to weigh in, because they know the old one. Second: a different precedent is invoked, and both sides claim it. One participant says we must not repeat Vietnam, meaning we must not intervene; another says the real lesson of Vietnam is that we left too soon and our credibility collapsed. The invocation changes nobody's position; it just gives each side a heavier stick. Third, offered as what I take to be a boundary case: a scholar argues that a current great-power rivalry fits the Athens-and-Sparta pattern, and a critic replies that naming the pattern makes leaders expect the war and thereby makes the war more likely. I think this third phenomenon is something else -- the invocation acting on the world rather than on the debate -- and I mention it only to mark an edge.

What the first two instances share, as best I can isolate it: the precedent does not function as evidence for or against a policy. It functions as a relocation device. It moves the dispute onto terrain where the participants already have well-stocked arsenals of claims, and where expertise about the actual situation at hand confers no advantage. The debate becomes cheaper to join and harder to settle at the same time.

My question is whether this has a name. It is adjacent to framing effects and anchoring, but those are claims about how individuals process information, and what I am describing is a claim about what happens to the structure of a collective argument. It is adjacent to the literature on analogical reasoning in decision-making, but that literature largely treats an analogy as an argument to be evaluated for fit, whereas in my cases the analogy seems to have stopped functioning as an argument at all -- in the second instance it does not even constrain anyone's conclusion. If no existing term fits, coin one, with a definition and clear boundary cases. I should add that I will not be satisfied with a bare label: I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are. In particular, distinguish it from merely being persuaded by an analogy, and from strategic bad-faith deployment of precedents -- the relocation seems to happen even when the invocation is perfectly sincere.

Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order. First, "Candidate concepts" (about 200 words): the nearest established notions, one per short paragraph, stating how each would try to cover my description. Second, "Mismatches" (about 200 words): where my description departs from each candidate. Third, "Verdict" (about 150 words): the best-fitting concept, or, if none fits, a coined term with a precise definition. Fourth, "Boundary cases" (about 100 words): cases the term should and should not cover, including a ruling on my third, self-fulfilling instance.
