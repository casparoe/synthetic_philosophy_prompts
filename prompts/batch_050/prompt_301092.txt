I want your credence on a proposition my colleague and I have been circling for months, with the reasoning decomposed rather than a bare number.

The proposition: it is morally worse to adopt a policy of rapid resource depletion rather than a conservation policy, even in the case where the choice determines which future people will exist. Specify the case as follows. Under conservation, the people who will exist a few centuries from now live very well; under depletion, an entirely different set of individuals exists, because the different policy shifts patterns of partnership and conception, and their lives are markedly worse but still comfortably worth living. No one who ever exists, under either policy, would have existed under the other, so no one can say they would have been better off had the alternative been chosen.

My credence that choosing depletion in this case is morally worse, and wrong for that reason, is about 0.9. My colleague's is about 0.35. Her view is that wrongness always bottoms out in a complaint some particular person could in principle register, and that where no existing or future person has such a complaint, the lingering intuition of wrongness is a cognitive illusion: we mistake a comparison of states of affairs for a wrong done to someone. I think she has the burden of proof backwards.

Please give your own credence as a number in the very first sentence, then the derivation, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Decompose at least the following sub-questions, assigning each a rough probability with a line of justification:

1. Whether a purely person-affecting constraint — an outcome is morally worse only if worse for some person who does or will exist — is correct, rather than merely one ingredient in a pluralist view.
2. Whether, if that constraint fails, there is a coherent principle that ranks the two policies' outcomes without dragging in consequences its defenders would balk at (for example, committing us to preferring vast populations at barely positive welfare over smaller flourishing ones).
3. Whether the depletion intuition is trustworthy in the first place — that is, whether it survives once you control for the way different-people cases are described, since intuitions here are notoriously sensitive to whether the setup is framed as harming a group or as merely selecting which group exists.
4. Whether the dispute even has a determinate answer, as opposed to being a standoff between two internally consistent moral outlooks that disagree about what comparisons of outcomes so much as mean.

Then show how your sub-probabilities combine into the headline number (if your combination is not a simple product or weighted sum, say what it is and why), identify the single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and name the argument or consideration that would move that term the most. Finally, given my 0.9 and her 0.35, tell me which sub-question you judge the disagreement most likely lives in, and whether either of us is making an identifiable mistake rather than just weighting things differently.
