I am a novelist, and I have agreed to contribute a short statement of my working creed to a symposium on the moral life of fiction. Before I send it off, I would like the statement audited for internal consistency. Here is what I currently believe:

1. Rousseau's central charge against the theater in his Letter to d'Alembert is substantially correct: staged suffering lets an audience rehearse fine feelings at no personal cost, and spectators leave persuaded that their tears have already acquitted them of the duties those tears merely rehearsed. The emotion is genuine, but it substitutes for the action it ought to have prompted.

2. The novel is a morally serious form for precisely the opposite reason: sustained imaginative residence in a consciousness unlike one's own enlarges the sympathies, and this enlargement is the best justification I can offer for my own practice.

3. The usual way of squaring 1 and 2 — the theater corrupts because it is public, passive, and over in an evening, whereas reading is solitary, active, and slow, so the novel escapes the mechanism Rousseau described — seems to me sound.

4. Whether a work improves or corrupts its readers has no bearing on its literary merit; aesthetic value is autonomous from moral effect.

5. A novel that flatters its readers' standing sympathies — that invites them to pity the safely distant or the decorously suffering and to feel the better of themselves for it — is, to that extent, an artistically worse novel.

Please go through these and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to. I would rather not be told that everything is in tension with everything; if a pair is actually consistent, I would like to hear that, with the reason. For each real tension, rank it by severity and identify the least costly revision — which thesis would have to be weakened or given up, and what that concession would cost the statement as a whole. Finally, if I may change only one item before the symposium, which change would leave me the most coherent creed?
