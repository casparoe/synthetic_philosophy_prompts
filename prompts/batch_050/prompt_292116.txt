Here's my situation. I help run an interdisciplinary seminar (philosophers, linguists, a couple of cognitive scientists) and we've spent the term on metaphor, and the argument has gone absolutely nowhere in a way I find genuinely puzzling. On one side you've got the Davidsonians: following Davidson's line in What Metaphors Mean, they insist a metaphor means only what its words literally mean, and that the interesting stuff a metaphor does — making you see one thing as another, jolting you into noticing something — is an effect on the hearer, not a further propositional content the sentence encodes. On the other side, the conceptual-metaphor people (Lakoff & Johnson and followers) say metaphor isn't a decorative use of words at all but a basic structure of thought: we understand abstract domains through systematic mappings from concrete ones, and the linguistic metaphors are just surface evidence of these deep mappings.

What's weird is that both camps seem to concede the other's central observation when pressed. The Davidsonians happily agree that metaphors are cognitively powerful and pervasive; the Lakoffians happily agree that the literal meaning of a metaphorical sentence is often false or absurd and that grasping the metaphor isn't like decoding a hidden proposition. And yet each side insists the other is badly wrong, and after a whole term nobody has budged.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conviction — is that the two camps are answering different questions under one label: Davidson is doing semantics (what, if anything, does the sentence say), while Lakoff & Johnson are doing cognitive psychology (how do we conceptualise abstract domains), and the apparent contradiction dissolves once you keep the questions apart. But maybe I'm being too conciliatory and there is a real point of disagreement hiding somewhere — e.g. over whether literal meaning is even psychologically primary, or over whether metaphorical "content" can be paraphrased.
