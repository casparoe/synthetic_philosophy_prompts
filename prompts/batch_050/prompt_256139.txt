So I write for a general-audience magazine that covers science and big ideas, and I'm starting research on a long-form piece about whether suffering and happiness can actually be weighed against each other -- like, is there a single scale where enough pleasure compensates for a given amount of pain, or does some suffering just never get "bought off" no matter how much good you pile on the other side? I keep running into this in different guises: animal welfare people arguing about whether cage-free campaigns trade off fairly against reducing the worst factory farming practices, health economists putting a number on a year of chronic pain versus a year of ordinary life, effective altruists debating whether preventing torture-level suffering should lexically outrank almost everything else. Someone pointed me at the "lexical threshold" idea -- that below some intensity suffering can be offset by goods, but past the threshold nothing compensates -- and it seems like the whole debate turns on whether pain and pleasure are even commensurable in the first place.

Here's my ask. My editor has given me roughly 5,000 words and about six weeks, and the readers are smart but not philosophers -- they've heard of utilitarianism, they haven't heard of population ethics. Which open questions in this commensurability debate should the piece actually center on, and in what order? I'm not after a survey of the literature, I'm after a defended agenda for the article. For each question you put on the list, tell me: why it's load-bearing for the practical disputes above (or why it isn't and it's just intra-philosophy turf war), whether a journalist can make it vivid without a math degree or whether it collapses into mush outside the seminar room, and what I'd find out during reporting that should move it up or down the list. Also flag which questions seem important but you think I should skip anyway -- the ones that are famous in the field but wouldn't change what any of my example cases look like. I'd rather have three questions argued hard than eight gestured at. And be honest about where your ranking is a judgment call rather than something the debate itself dictates, because I'll have to defend the structure of this piece in a pitch meeting and "the philosopher I consulted said so" won't cut it.
