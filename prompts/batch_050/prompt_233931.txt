I'm a type theorist who works daily in a proof assistant (mostly Agda and Lean), and I'm fluent in the usual vocabulary of natural deduction: introduction and elimination rules, hypothetical judgments, derivable versus admissible rules, the Curry–Howard reading of implication as function space, and the object-language/metalanguage distinction.

I've been rereading Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895), and I keep feeling that the dialogue is really a type-error report delivered by a very patient tortoise. Here is the passage I want to work from — not the famous line about what is worth writing down, but the moment the regress actually starts:

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

Here is what I want. First, the translation, as faithful as you can make it. Restate the Tortoise's argument in my vocabulary: Achilles's successive additions of C, D, E as premises versus modus ponens (or →-elimination, or plain function application) as an inference rule; whether the regress is best rendered as an attempt to internalize an admissible rule as a derivable one, or as a confusion of object-language implication with the metalanguage consequence relation, or something else. Be precise: in a proof assistant, what exactly would each of the Tortoise's demands correspond to? Is there any sense in which one could "write down" →-elimination as just another term in the context, the way the Tortoise keeps asking Achilles to enter each hypothetical in his note-book?

Second, the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. The Tortoise's move turns on an epistemic attitude — "if I failed to see its truth" — and on a notion of being logically forced to accept a conclusion. As far as I can tell, neither has a type-theoretic counterpart: a derivation either applies the elimination rule or it doesn't, and the system has no slot for an agent who possesses the function and the argument but declines to apply them. Tell me which parts of the dialogue translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression at all — and for each item of residue, say whether it marks a genuine gap in the type-theoretic framework (something a theory of inference needs that the formalism lacks) or a confusion on the Tortoise's part that the formalism rightly refuses to express.

Finally, the verdict. Does restating the puzzle in type-theoretic terms dissolve it — the Tortoise's request is simply ill-typed, and Carroll has shown only that rules are not premises — or does it merely relocate the regress one level up, since the question of what justifies the elimination rules themselves is one the object language cannot even ask? And does the passage support reading the puzzle as being about the justification of deduction at all, or is it better read as a point about the difference between a rule of inference and a premise, with the epistemology of acceptance doing all the argumentative work? If you think my "type-error report" reading is itself an overreach, say so and say why.
