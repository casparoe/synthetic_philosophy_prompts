In the US, tipping your waiter 18-20% is treated as close to a moral obligation -- stiffing a server makes you a jerk, full stop. But nobody tips the McDonald's cashier, the flight attendant, the nurse, or the person who fixes your car, even though some of them make less money and work harder than the average waiter. Meanwhile in Japan tipping is actively rude, and in Germany you just round up a bit. And visitors are expected to follow whichever system they're in, no excuses.

So here's what I want to know: what theory of fairness or desert does the American tipping norm actually presuppose? Is it secretly a theory about which wages are the customer's responsibility vs the employer's? A theory that only face-to-face service counts as labor you can be grateful for? A status thing where tipping marks certain jobs as "servant" jobs? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and tell me which parts of the practice it really needs vs. which parts would also make sense under rival theories (e.g. tipping as insurance against bad service, or as a gift, or as a tax the restaurant industry externalizes onto customers).

Also: if we swapped the presupposed theory for one that says all low-wage labor deserves equal treatment, does that mean tip everyone or tip no one? And does the "visitors must follow local norms" rule commit us to saying conventions generate real moral obligations, or just that politeness does? Be specific about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined -- I suspect most of it is, honestly.
