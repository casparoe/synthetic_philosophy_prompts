I serve on the admissions committee of a selective graduate program, and I need a memo to circulate before our vote next month. Here is the situation. For the past six years, a data-science team at the university has run a model that takes the same application files we receive and predicts, candidate by candidate, whether our committee will ultimately admit or deny — issuing its predictions before we meet. Its accuracy has been about 96% overall, including cycles in which we revised our rubric. Until now the predictions were filed away as a retrospective audit. This year the dean has proposed that we receive the model's predicted decisions alongside the files, as an efficiency measure, observing that since the model nearly always turns out to be right about us, treating its outputs as our default would spare hundreds of deliberation hours.

The committee is split three ways. One faction wants the predictions treated as presumptive verdicts, departed from only for a stated reason. A second wants us never to see them. A member of this camp invoked the old fable in which a servant, told that Death will come for him in Damascus, flees to Samarra — only to learn that Death, expecting precisely that flight, had arranged to meet him there. Her argument, as I understand it, is that if the model is genuinely reliable about us, then any deliberate attempt to defy it is something it has already predicted, so "choosing to prove it wrong" is incoherent; and if we can defy it, its track record is irrelevant to what we ought to do. Either way, she concludes, the predictions can give us no reason to conform. A third faction answers that the track record was compiled while we deliberated in ignorance of the predictions, so it records what we do when we decide for ourselves — which makes it excellent evidence of what a full, honest deliberation would conclude, and ignoring it would be perverse.

Please draft the memo. It should recommend one policy (or a precisely specified hybrid) with an actionable bottom line the dean can implement; set out the reasoning, including whether the fable actually supports the second faction's conclusion and what it would take for the model's reliability to count for or against any particular admission decision; and give an honest statement of the strongest argument on the losing side, in terms its proponents would accept.
