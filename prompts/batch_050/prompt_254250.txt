I'm putting together a problem set for a small seminar on the foundations of probability (mixed group of phil and math students), and one week is on Bertrand's paradox and what it does to the principle of indifference. My stock of examples is the usual one: the random-chord-in-a-circle problem, where the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle comes out as 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on whether you randomize over endpoints, distance from the center, or midpoint position; the cube factory, where a machine makes cubes with edge somewhere between 0 and 2 meters and being uniform over edge length, face area, and volume gives 1/2, 1/4, and 1/8 for the probability that the edge is under a meter; and von Mises' wine-water case, where being uniform over the wine-to-water ratio versus the water-to-wine ratio yields inconsistent probabilities. I want the students to build their own Bertrand-style scenario, but I obviously need one myself first to demo what I'm after, and I don't want to just recycle one of these three.

So: construct a new scenario with the same structure -- a single well-described physical-ish situation, a well-formed question about it, and two or more parameterizations of the unknown quantity that each look natural but where a uniform distribution over each gives different numerical answers to the same question. Work out the actual numbers so I can see they really diverge. Two constraints to keep you honest: first, nothing geometric -- no chords, circles, sticks, darts, points on surfaces; the cube factory is geometric enough that I want to leave that territory behind. Second, it shouldn't be the wine-water trick in disguise, where you just take one quantity and then be uniform over its reciprocal or its square instead; the two parameterizations need to be genuinely different measurable features of the situation, the way edge length and face area are different facts about a cube, not the same fact re-expressed. A realistic setting helps -- a factory, a lab measurement, a queue, something my students could picture -- rather than pure abstracta.

Finally, two follow-ups so this doubles as exam prep: tell me which of your parameterizations you actually find most compelling and why (gut verdict is fine, but defend it), and say whether you think the Jaynes-style move -- pick the distribution invariant under the natural symmetry group of the problem, the way the chord problem arguably singles out the midpoint-radius method -- would resolve your case or whether your scenario resists that treatment. If it resists, is that a mark against my scenario or against the Jaynes strategy? I'd rather you be honest about this last part than tidy.
