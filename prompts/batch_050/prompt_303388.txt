Historians often debate whether they can — or should — judge past actors by the moral and intellectual standards of their own time. Write an essay that evaluates this debate using the following test, and treat the test itself as part of what's under scrutiny. Imagine a historian assessing some past practice (say, a legal institution or a system of social hierarchy) under three standards in turn: (a) the standards widely accepted among the practice's contemporaries, (b) the standards of the historian's own time, and (c) the standards the historian's own society would plausibly endorse two centuries hence, extrapolated from current trajectories of moral opinion. Call any judgment that survives all three a "stable verdict," and ask whether stable verdicts are the only ones a historian is entitled to make, or whether the test itself smuggles in questionable assumptions — for instance, that moral opinion trends in a discoverable direction, that a historian's present is no less parochial than the past it studies, or that verdicts failing the test are merely projections rather than knowledge. Your essay should weigh the strongest considerations on both sides — including the worry that refusing present-tense judgment falsifies the past by excusing what contemporaries themselves condemned, and the worry that embracing it turns history into moralized anachronism — but it must reach and defend a verdict: should historians aim at stable verdicts, judge openly from their own time, or pursue some third option you define? Write the essay in American English.
