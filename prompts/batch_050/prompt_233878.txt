The exam question was:

"Self-deception is often said to face two puzzles: the static puzzle and the dynamic puzzle. Explain both puzzles. Then explain how an intentionalist account (e.g., Davidson's) and a deflationary account (e.g., Mele's) each try to avoid or dissolve them. Which approach do you find more promising, and why?"

Here's the student's answer:

"Self-deception seems paradoxical for two reasons. The static puzzle is that the self-deceiver apparently believes both p and not-p at the same time: a jealous husband believes his wife is faithful, yet also believes she is cheating. But holding flatly contradictory beliefs seems psychologically impossible, or at least deeply irrational in a way that needs explaining. The dynamic puzzle concerns how the deception gets going. For me to deceive you, I have to know the truth and intend to get you to believe something false. If I try this on myself, I already know the truth, so the project of deceiving myself looks doomed from the start -- I'll see right through my own trick.

Intentionalists like Davidson respond to the static puzzle by partitioning the mind: the contradictory beliefs live in different, partially isolated compartments, so the person never holds them side by side in a single coherent perspective. Since the two beliefs are kept apart, the static puzzle dissolves, and this automatically resolves the dynamic puzzle as well, because the partition is exactly what keeps the deceiving part's knowledge from reaching the deceived part.

Deflationists like Mele take a different route. Mele argues that self-deception requires an intention to deceive oneself, but he partitions the mind so that the intention never has to be consciously confronted. Biased treatment of evidence -- selectively attending to welcome data, scrutinizing unwelcome data more harshly -- does the work inside one partition while the other holds the comforting belief. On his view the static puzzle never really arises, since the self-deceiver only genuinely believes p (the comforting belief) and merely suspects or fears not-p; and the dynamic puzzle is dissolved because nothing like an intentional act of self-deception ever occurs.

I find the deflationary approach more promising. Since motivated reasoning and confirmation bias are ubiquitous features of ordinary cognition, Mele's account shows that self-deception is continuous with everyday irrationality rather than a mysterious special case. And because partitioning requires us to posit homunculi inside the head, which is obviously unscientific, Davidson's view should be rejected outright."
