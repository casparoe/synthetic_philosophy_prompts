I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on Hobbes and the rationality of cooperation, and I'd like you to run a counterfactual for a dissertation chapter. Consider two technologically mature civilizations that have come to know of one another but are separated such that no causal contact is or ever will be possible -- say, each lies permanently outside the other's future light cone. Each can nonetheless build detailed models of the other, and each correctly believes the other to be a capable reasoner modeling it in turn. Suppose each could divert resources to an activity that benefits only the other civilization (stipulate some channel that transmits value without information or matter; treat it as a philosopher's stipulation, not physics), where joint cooperation leaves both better off but cooperating alone is a net cost. Some contemporary decision theorists hold that rational agents in this position should cooperate anyway, on the grounds that two sufficiently similar reasoners facing symmetric problems arrive at correlated conclusions even without causal influence -- so-called acausal trade.

My question: what would Hobbes make of this? Please reconstruct his likely verdict through his actual machinery rather than gesturing at it. In particular: Does the impossibility of enforcement settle the matter, given his insistence that covenants without a common power to overawe the parties cannot oblige? Does his reply to the Foole -- that performing one's part first is not contrary to reason -- extend to a case where no third party will ever know, where reputation is impossible, and where the counterparty is not a potential conqueror but a permanently unreachable mind? Would he classify reasoning from simulation-symmetry as prudence or as a confusion about causation? And if his framework turns out to license cooperation here, does that expose a tension between his instrumentalism about reason and his official account of the state of nature, or does it draw on a resource already implicit in his view?
