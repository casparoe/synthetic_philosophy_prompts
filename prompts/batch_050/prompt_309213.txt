Nearly fifty years after David Lewis's triviality results, philosophers still cannot agree on whether indicative conditionals have truth-evaluable contents whose probability equals the corresponding conditional probability. Lewis showed that, given modest assumptions about how probabilities distribute across partitions, no proposition can play that role in general. Since then the field has split into camps that, as far as I can tell, mostly restate themselves: those who conclude that indicative conditionals simply do not express propositions and are instead devices for conditional assertion or supposition; those who deny that "if" is a connective at all, treating it as a restrictor on modals and quantifiers so that the triviality proof never gets a grip; and those who keep propositionalism and revise something else -- the logic, the semantics (dynamic or context-sensitive), or the assumption that conditional probability was the right target. My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the sides are answering different questions under one heading: one camp is doing the pragmatics of conditional utterances, another the compositional semantics of conditional sentences, and "does the conditional have a probability?" means something different to each. Diagnose the impasse: is that hypothesis right, or is there a substantive shared question with a hidden empirical or formal disagreement underneath? I want a committed verdict with reasons, grounded in what each position actually claims, plus a definite answer on what would count as progress -- a theorem, a distinction, an experimental result about ordinary judgments -- or an argument that nothing will settle it because the question is ill-posed.
