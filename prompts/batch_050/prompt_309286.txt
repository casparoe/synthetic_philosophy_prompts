I am a novelist at work on a book whose structure is deliberately self-enclosing: in the outer frame, an author writes a character; in the inner frame, that character turns out to be writing the author. When I describe the project to friends, I reach for the standard comparison — Escher's Drawing Hands, each hand sketching the other into existence — and I borrow the vocabulary of tangled hierarchies to claim that in my novel the levels of author and character are genuinely collapsed, so that neither is the real one. A friend who writes literary criticism pushes back: the lithograph, she says, depends on an invisible third level — Escher himself, at his desk, drawing both hands — and my novel likewise has an inviolate level, namely me; the tangle is an illusion staged from above.

Audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of each hand, of the act of drawing, of the paper, of the artist's wrist outside the frame, in both the lithograph and my nested novel. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks matter for my intended use rather than being merely incidental. Does a drawn hand draw in any sense at all, the way my inner character might be said to write? Does the difference between a static image and a temporally extended text — where the reader encounters the levels sequentially and can be made to lose track of which encloses which — strengthen or weaken the claim of tangle? Finally, say whether my claim of genuine level-collapse survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether my friend's point — that every fictional tangle is authored from an untouched level, and that the two hands only appear to draw each other because a third hand drew both — dissolves it. If there is a better analogy for what such a novel actually achieves, name it and explain why it fits.
