I TA for an undergrad philosophy of social science seminar, roughly 18 students, and we're doing two weeks on collective intentionality -- Searle-style we-intentions, Bratman on shared intention, Gilbert on joint commitment. The problem is they all nod along to the I-intention vs. we-intention distinction like it's obviously real, and I don't think they actually feel it. They treat it as a labeling choice. Meanwhile I have this running argument with one of the keener students who takes the deflationary line: a so-called we-intention is just an individual intention plus mutual beliefs plus some coordination, and the "irreducibility" people are mistaking the warm feeling of smooth cooperation for a distinct kind of mental state. I don't want to settle that for them in lecture, I want them to hit it themselves.

So: design me one 75-minute in-class activity (no lecture up front, don't let the students hear any of the theorists' names until the debrief) that makes the deflationary hypothesis genuinely hard to dismiss -- i.e., something where participants first cooperate under purely individual incentives and parallel reasoning, and then are put in a situation where that machinery predictably breaks down and something has to shift in how they relate to each other for the task to get done at all. I want rules, materials, a timeline, and a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money. The second phase should be designed so that the "just individual intentions plus beliefs" strategy fails in a way the participants can feel in the moment, not just see afterwards on a whiteboard -- so please think carefully about what structural feature of the task forces that failure (incomplete information about each other's sub-plans? the need to be able to count on the other person even when defecting would be rational? pick whichever and commit to it).

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto the pieces of the conceptual distinction (mutual responsiveness, meshing sub-plans, commitment you can rely on vs. mere prediction of the other person), and structure it so the students can actually stage the deflationist vs. irreducibilist argument using what just happened to them as evidence -- my skeptic student should be able to point at a moment and say "that was just coordination," and someone else should be able to point at the same moment and say "no, something changed there," and both should have to get specific. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I suspect some groups will brute-force the second phase with signaling or a leader, which would quietly prove the deflationist's point and ruin the exercise -- how do I rule that out by the rules rather than by policing them?). Finally, one honest note on what the activity can't reproduce: my guess is it can demonstrate that joint activity has a different structure than parallel individual activity, but it can't settle whether participants actually had a different kind of intention, since that's introspective -- tell me if you think that's the right limitation to flag or if the real gap is somewhere else.
