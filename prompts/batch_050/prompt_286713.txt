I work on a small law-reform commission in a common-law country, and our job is to review draft regulatory bills before they go to parliament. Pretty much every bill has some criminal offenses in it, and every drafter's instinct is to make them strict liability because it makes prosecution easier. Our commission keeps pushing back ad hoc, with no consistent standard, and the drafters have started (fairly) complaining that they can't predict when we'll object.

So I want a reusable procedure we can run on any proposed offense -- something a reviewer can apply in an afternoon without being a criminal law theorist -- for deciding whether the offense should (a) require full mens rea as to each element, (b) be strict liability but with a due-diligence / reasonable-mistake defense, or (c) be strict or near-absolute liability with no fault element at all. Base it on whatever the philosophy and doctrine of criminal responsibility actually has to say: the presumption of mens rea, the distinction between truly criminal and merely regulatory offenses, the difference between strict and absolute liability, why conduct/circumstance/result elements might be treated differently within the same offense, and the evidential arguments (who is best placed to know the relevant facts and prove a negative).

Structure it as an ordered set of tests: which questions to ask first, what answers rule out option (c) immediately, which considerations are tiebreakers (max penalty, stigma, whether it's a licensing-type activity people opt into, whether the "harm" is a wrong in itself or a regulatory line-drawing, what a reasonable person in the defendant's position could realistically have done). Be explicit about where the procedure bottoms out in a judgment call and just say "this is a values question, escalate it" rather than pretending there's a test.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical offenses:
1. A food-safety offense: selling a packaged product containing an undeclared allergen.
2. An environmental offense: "a person commits an offense if a pollutant enters a waterway from land they occupy," drafted with no fault language.
3. A possession offense: carrying an object that in fact meets the statutory definition of a prohibited blade, where the defendant reasonably believed it was ordinary camping gear.

At least one of these should be a case where the procedure spits out an answer our commission probably won't like politically -- flag that honestly rather than bending the procedure.

Please write the whole thing in American English. And at the very end, give the machine-readable bottom line for the three hypotheticals as \answer{1=..., 2=..., 3=...} using the labels mens-rea, strict-with-defense, or near-absolute.
