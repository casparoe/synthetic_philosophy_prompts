Two friends of mine, Lena and Owen, got into a surprisingly heated thing after a local accident and now I’m stuck as referee. A reservoir used an amphibious weed-clearing robot, nicknamed Pickerel, that runs at night with one human monitor watching 14 machines. The monitor can veto, but a veto freezes the controls for about 8 seconds while the robot may keep drifting. One foggy night its sonar said “soft bank,” camera said “possible hard object,” and the learned policy ranked bank higher becuase false stops had caused angry complaints all summer. It clipped an unlit kayak; the paddler swerved into a boom and got hurt. The monitor never got an alert because the risk score sat just under the ping threshold.

Lena’s side, roughly: this is exactly what people mean by a responsibility gap. The monitor didn’t have time or a decent picture, the engineer didn’t foresee this exact fog/algae/kayak combo, the manager didn’t intend anything, and the paddler made a bad call too. If we punish whoever is nearest, that’s moral luck with paperwork.

Owen: the “gap” is manufactured. The firm chose 14-to-1 monitoring, the 8-second veto lag, and a threshold tuned so alerts stay rare. You don’t get to move all the real decisions upstream to design meetings and then act shocked that nobody downstream had a clean moment of control.

Lena: then every crash becomes “negligent design” until automation is basically strict liability. Perfect safety is impossible, so companies will either hide the model or never deploy anything that beats human drivers overall.

Owen: no, only unreasonable tradeoffs count. Ask what an extra monitor, slower veto, or lower threshold would’ve cost against predictable harms. Also separate blame from payment: maybe no employee deserves prison, but the org can still owe compensation, changes, and an apology.

Lena: an org can’t be sorry. Victims need a someone, not a press release and a fine spread across shareholders; otherwise desert and apology still fall into the gap even if liability doesn’t.

Owen: she’s smuggling in the idea that responsibility must be individual, backward-looking, and courtroom-shaped. There’s answerability, accountability, liability, role-responsibility; the kayaker’s unlighted boat matters without erasing upstream duties.

Please adjudicate move by move, including which premises each is sneaking in. I especially want you to use a distinction I made up but seems useful: “responsibility latency” = the gap between the last realistic intervention point and the moment harm gets locked in, and who was allowed to control that clock. Say who is more right overall, what both miss, and whether your answer changes if the robot was still statistically safer than the human crews it replaced.
