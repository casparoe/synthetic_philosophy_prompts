I wrote down my working position on lying vs misleading for a blog post, kind of fast, and I haven't checked it for internal friction. Tell me if it hangs together. Here's the set:

1. Lying is genuinely worse than merely misleading, other things equal. It's not just a lawyer's technicality -- there's a real moral line there.
2. What makes lying wrong is that it abuses the practice of assertion: when you flat-out say that p, you stake your credibility on p, and lying spends the trust that the whole practice runs on. That's the source of the wrongness.
3. Misleading works differently -- the usual Gricean story about implicature. You say something literally true, the hearer does the inferential work, and that's exactly why it's morally cheaper: you never staked anything, and if challenged you can honestly say you never claimed it.
4. Case verdict: a doctor who tells a patient "most people with this diagnosis see real improvement within a year," knowing this patient's subtype basically never improves and intending him to walk out hopeful, does something just as wrong as if she'd flat-out lied. The technical deniability doesn't move me at all, it's worse then a white lie.
5. Silence and omission, where you have no special duty to inform, are often fine. Buyers can do their own due diligence, and hearers bear some responsibility for the inferences they choose to run.
6. Bluffing in poker and misdirection in bargaining are permissible even though they deliberately aim at producing false beliefs.
7. My own capstone idea, the two-track view: the wrongness of deception has two independent components, a trust-spending component tied to what you assert and a manipulation component tied to the false belief you engineer. Misleading usually only carries the second, but it can max that one out.
