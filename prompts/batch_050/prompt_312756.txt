I am a PhD candidate in political science, TAing an undergraduate seminar on conflict and decision-making (roughly 18 students, one 75-minute session). My students have all absorbed the pop version of the dollar auction — "escalation is irrational, people are dumb" — and I want to dislodge that. Design an in-class auction game with token stakes (no real money) that meets three conditions. First, the full rules, including the trap, are explained to everyone in advance, and escalation should still occur, so that students experience firsthand that transparency does not immunize them. Second, the session should include a comparison condition (e.g., a variant in which the second-highest bidder pays nothing) that lets participants feel the difference between genuinely competitive escalation — where each bid is locally rational but the joint trajectory is ruinous — and mere sunk-cost chasing, since these are two distinct mechanisms that get lumped together under "escalation of commitment." Third, provide a debrief that maps particular moments of the game onto the escalation literature and onto real cases (arms races, bidding wars, staying in failing interventions), tells me what will predictably go wrong the first two times I run it and how to respond, and is explicit about which features of real-world escalation — face-saving, audience costs, identity stakes — the game cannot reproduce and I must supply through discussion.
