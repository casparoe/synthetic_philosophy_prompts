I work in programming-language semantics; my day-to-day vocabulary is denotational and compositional: expressions have denotations in a domain, contexts are functions from free variables to values, meaning is a homomorphism from syntax to semantics, connectives are fixed operations on denotations, and entailment is something like inclusion of models. I have a decent working knowledge of the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism as philosophers usually state it, but I want to see how much of it survives being recast in the vocabulary I actually think in.

Please do the following. First, restate the expressivist's account of normative discourse as a semantic proposal in my terms: what is the analogue of a denotation for a normative sentence if it is not a truth condition, what is the analogue of assertion, and how does the compositional machinery get set up. Second, reconstruct the Frege–Geach argument itself as a claim about compositional semantics: the same sentence occurring unembedded in one place and embedded under negation or in the antecedent of a conditional in another; the requirement that it make the same semantic contribution in both positions; and the fact that the modus ponens inference from the conditional and its antecedent to the consequent would be a mere equivocation if the embedded occurrence meant something different. State what, in my vocabulary, the expressivist is being accused of failing to provide. Third, and this is the part I care most about, give me a residue report: which components of the problem translate cleanly (for instance, whether the equivocation worry is really just the observation that a semantics assigning different denotations to free and bound occurrences of an expression breaks substitution-based accounts of validity), which components you had to distort to fit, and which have no expression at all in a framework where sentences denote and inference is model inclusion. In particular, I suspect that the notions of accepting a normative claim, of disagreement between two speakers, and of the difference between expressing an attitude and reporting that one has it may not have counterparts in denotational semantics, and I want to know whether that is right. Finally, tell me what to conclude from the residue: does what fails to translate show that my framework is missing something the philosophers are tracking (and if so, what would have to be added -- a theory of force, a speech-act layer, something else), or does it show that part of the Frege–Geach debate is an artifact of a vocabulary that treats attitudes and truth conditions as rival candidates for the same job, a rivalry that disappears once the semantic and pragmatic layers are separated the way a language designer would separate them?
