I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last year reading moral psychology and anthropology with the same impatient hope I once brought to noisy data: that a few variables might explain many outcomes. The honor, dignity, and victimhood schema keeps recurring in my notes, but I distrust my first reaction to it, namely that dignity is obviously the adult in the room. So here are my verdicts on invented cases, stated as plainly as I can. In a university department, a senior researcher publicly mocks a junior colleague’s accent; the junior colleague answers with a controlled public rebuke and then lets the matter drop: admirable. The same junior colleague demands a formal investigation, a public apology, and the senior researcher’s removal from a committee over the same remark: excessive. A student reports a professor’s repeated humiliations to a faculty integrity office after private requests fail: proper, and the fact that others rally around the student does not by itself make it coercive. An employee overhears two strangers making a crude joke about another group and posts the audio online, naming them, before checking context: wrong, even if the joke was ugly. A close-knit immigrant community settles a business cheat by ostracizing the cheat until restitution is made, without appealing to the state: acceptable where courts are inaccessible, but dangerous once it becomes hereditary stigma. A fraternity brother punches a man for insulting his sister and calls it a matter of honor: bad, though less bad to me when it is a single proportionate rebuke to a deliberate public degradation than when it is revenge for a trivial slight. A hospital tells staff to absorb patients’ racist abuse with professionalism rather than complain: callous, not dignified. A campus group treats every uncomfortable idea as harm and demands institutional protection as proof of innocence: I recoil, yet I notice this is nearly the same grammar I approved when the student sought third-party help against repeated humiliation.

What principle, if any, is running under these verdicts? I would like it stated precisely enough to be falsified, with the clause or clauses motivated by particular cases: perhaps some compound of severity, publicity, repetition, power asymmetry, availability of fair procedure, and the risk that third-party enforcement becomes status competition. Please say which of my judgments the best principle saves, which it convicts, and whether the apparent tension between the integrity-office case and the uncomfortable-idea case is a real inconsistency or only a resemblance. Then test the principle on cases I have not decided: a whistleblower who leaks documents to journalists because regulators are captured; a small scientific field in which reputational shunning is the only effective sanction against fabricated data; a child bullied online whose school’s restorative process keeps failing; and a scientist who responds to a condescending review by writing a devastating public reply that is accurate but career-ending. Finally, I want the normative question faced rather than dissolved: if honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures are ideal types rather than census categories, is any of them morally superior as a default for institutions, or only better for different error costs, and what evidence would change your ranking? If my request rests on a confused premise — for instance, if these labels are too sociologically loose to bear the weight I am putting on them, or if my cases smuggle in the conclusion — say so plainly and rebuild the question rather than politely answering a muddled one.
