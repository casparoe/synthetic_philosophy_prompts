I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a column that started as a light piece about why readers mourn fictional characters and has turned, as these things do, into a metaphysics problem. The sticking point is a contrast my editor flagged. On the one hand, it seems plainly true that Sherlock Holmes does not exist, and a natural reading of that sentence takes the name to fail to refer to anything. On the other hand, there is a whole class of sentences that seem plainly true and not merely true-within-the-stories: that Holmes is more famous than any actual detective, that he was created by Conan Doyle rather than by Wilkie Collins, that he appears on a pub sign in London. A pretense theorist or paraphrase theorist will try to handle these without granting the name a referent; a realist about fictional characters will say Holmes is an abstract artifact, brought into being by the writing of the stories, and that the first class of sentences uses the name in one way and the second class in another. I don't want the column to just recite both positions, so I'm asking you to do something my sources keep refusing to do: commit to a number.

Precisely, give me your credence in the following proposition: the correct semantics for English treats at least some sentences containing "Sherlock Holmes" as literally true in virtue of the name referring to an abstract, created fictional character, rather than treating all apparently true Holmes-sentences as either true-in-fiction, pretense, or paraphrasable away without a referent. Then decompose the credence rather than defending it in a lump. The sub-questions I can see are: the probability that negative existentials like "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" can be made sense of while the name still refers (say, via a context shift or a distinction between what exists and what there is); the probability that abstract artifacts are a legitimate ontological category at all, given the usual queerness worries about created abstracta; and the probability that the external sentences (the fame and authorship claims) genuinely resist pretense-theoretic or paraphrastic treatment, which is where I suspect the real action is. If you think I've carved the problem wrongly, substitute your own sub-questions, but assign a rough probability to each with a line of justification, show how they combine into your final number, and identify which single sub-question the number is most sensitive to.

For calibration: my own credence is around 0.6, and a philosopher I interviewed put hers at 0.1 on the grounds that creationism about fictional characters is "metaphysics by press release." Tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in, and what argument or case, on either side, would move your number the most. One more thing: this column format rewards confidence, but I'd rather you be honest with me than quotable. If the proposition as I've stated it is ambiguous in a way that prevents any credence from being well-defined, or if the decomposition I've sketched smuggles in a disputed assumption, say so plainly and tell me how the question should have been posed.
