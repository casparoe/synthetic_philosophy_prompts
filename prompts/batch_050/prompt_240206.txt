Longtime LW/ACX reader here, so I’ve absorbed the usual costly-signaling frame maybe a little too deeply. I was recently at an engagement party where everyone agreed that the ring had to be expensive enough to “mean something,” but also that buying love with money was crass, and that spending too much could itself be a warning sign about the guy. That seems philosophically weird to me. If the practice is a costly signal, what exactly does it presuppose: that affection is otherwise cheap to fake, that recipients can infer sincerity from willingness to incur a loss, that the loss has to be socially visible, or that the couple are using an irrational custom as a kind of commitment technology? And why would an expense count as evidence of devotion rather than evidence that the buyer is wealthy, status-seeking, bad with money, or simply good at following scripts?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of the engagement-ring norm in terms of costly signaling and the handicap principle, but distinguish a signal from an actual commitment device, a screening test, a positional/status good, and a coordination convention. I’m especially interested in the epistemic part: if both partners know that the ring is supposed to signal sincerity, can it still do that job once everyone knows the equilibrium? Also discuss why supposedly efficient gifts like cash or an index-fund contribution often feel less meaningful, why lab-grown diamonds threaten the custom even when they look identical, and whether the norm really requires waste rather than merely requiring some cost that scales with resources. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories could explain it equally well? Finally, say what would have to change in the ritual if we replaced the signaling theory with either a pure commitment-device theory or a pure social-convention theory. I’m definately not asking for etiquette advice or a history of diamond marketing.
