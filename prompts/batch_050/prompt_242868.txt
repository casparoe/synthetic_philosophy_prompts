I'm taking a seminar on the legal and moral philosophy of mass atrocity, and I'm stuck on what the legal category of genocide actually commits us to. The practice, as I understand it: international law singles out genocide as uniquely grave, graver than other mass crimes; it requires that perpetrators aimed at destroying, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group — so a commander who foreseeably wipes out a group as a side effect of conquest, without aiming at its destruction, is generally held to fall outside the crime; and it deliberately excludes political and social groups, so a regime that murders far larger numbers of its own political opponents has committed crimes against humanity but not genocide. Meanwhile governments and press treat the word itself as carrying special weight, sometimes hesitating to apply it precisely because of what the label would seem to demand.

What theory of the wrongness of mass killing does this category presuppose? Candidates I can see: that human groups are bearers of value beyond their members, so destroying a group is a loss additional to the deaths; that selecting victims by identity is a distinct dignity wrong, akin to discrimination, however many die; or that the intent to erase a people is morally worse than equal or greater killing done for territory or power. Which of these does the practice strictly presuppose, and which merely fit it comfortably? Is it coherent to rank the extermination of a smaller protected group above a vastly larger political massacre — does the special-intent requirement quietly assume that motive dominates outcome in a way the rest of criminal law rejects? And if atrocity law were rebuilt on a straightforwardly individualist, harm-based view, what concretely would change, and would anything worth keeping be lost? If you think the category presupposes no single theory, or that I've misdescribed the law somewhere, please say so plainly rather than working around it.
