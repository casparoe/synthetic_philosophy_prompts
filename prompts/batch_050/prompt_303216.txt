I serve on an independent review commission empaneled to advise the ministry that runs our national social-benefits agency. Six years ago the agency replaced its published eligibility regulations with a machine-learning system. The rules that now decide who is entitled to what exist nowhere as text: they are encoded in the model's parameters, the agency itself cannot state them in full, and they shift whenever the model is retrained, which has happened without notice eleven times. The agency also used the system to recalculate past entitlements and issue clawback demands to roughly 40,000 households, some of whom relied in good faith on written benefit confirmations the agency had sent them. The commission must now recommend one of three courses: (a) uphold the clawbacks as valid applications of duly authorized rules; (b) void them on the ground that a regime of secret, unstable, partly retroactive rules never achieved the status of law at all, so the clawbacks were baseless from the start; or (c) a middle course, such as voiding only the retroactive recalculations while letting prospective determinations stand pending reform.

Draft the commission's decision memo, in American English, recommending one of these courses. The memo needs a clear bottom line the commissioners can vote on, followed by the reasoning. It should engage the conceptual dispute directly rather than name-dropping it: one camp on the commission holds, in a Fullerian spirit, that legality has internal conditions -- publicity, prospectivity, clarity, stability, consistency, congruence between announced rule and administration -- and that a system failing them badly enough produces not bad law but no law; the other camp holds, in a Hartian spirit, that validity is a separate question from merit, that these rules were validly made under the legislature's delegation and the rule of recognition however shoddy they are, and that the honest course is to call them law and condemn or repeal them rather than pretend they never existed.

The memo should do at least the following: explain what practically turns on the choice of framing, since the two camps appear to converge on condemning the system but diverge on whether clawbacks are void from the outset or valid until repealed, with different consequences for restitution; grapple with the hardest feature of the case, which is that officials administering a rule of recognition seemingly cannot converge on, or even state, the rules they are supposed to be recognizing, and assess whether this is a novel failure mode the classic positions don't anticipate or just an extreme instance of unclarity; address the worry that the voiding option is itself retroactive in effect, unsettling settled payments, and therefore sits uneasily with the very ideals invoked to justify it; and give the strongest version of the losing camp's position, including the Hartian charge that the Fullerian framing lets adjudicators disguise a moral veto as a conceptual finding, or conversely the Fullerian charge that the Hartian framing ratifies governance by oracle while pretending only to be describing it. Roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words. Reason from the positions themselves; do not pad the memo with citations or a bibliography.
