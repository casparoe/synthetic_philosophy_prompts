The question on the assignment was:

"Some philosophers object to hedonism on the grounds that pleasures are phenomenally too diverse to constitute a single kind of feeling. State this objection (sometimes called the heterogeneity problem), explain the 'attitudinal' response to it, and assess whether that response succeeds. (~250 words)"

Here's the student's answer:

"The heterogeneity problem is that the things we call pleasures have nothing in common, feeling-wise. The pleasure of eating chocolate, of finally getting a proof, of a warm bath, and of hearing that a friend is okay after surgery all feel completely different from each other. So pleasure can't be one kind of sensation, which is a problem for hedonism, the view that pleasure is the only thing that's good in itself.

A well-known reply, associated with Mill, is to drop the idea that pleasure is a feeling at all. On the attitudinal view, pleasure isn't a sensation but an attitude of the subject toward some state of affairs — being pleased about something, or in other words getting what you want. Attitudes don't need to share a common feel, so the diversity of the feelings doesn't matter anymore: what unifies all pleasures is just that the subject's desire is satisfied.

The reply works, as far as it goes, and since it dissolves the heterogeneity problem it shows that hedonism is defensible overall. There's still a worry about worthless pleasures (the sadist's enjoyment etc.), but that's a seperate objection."
