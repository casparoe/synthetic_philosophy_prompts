I've noticed a pattern in my own moral reactions that I can't find a name for, and I'd like to know whether there's an established concept here. Three cases. First: I drive to the store every week for a year, and in doing so impose a tiny statistical risk of death on the strangers I pass -- call it a one-in-a-million chance of killing someone. Nobody thinks I need anyone's consent for this; it seems like part of the background of ordinary life. Second: instead of driving, once a year I spin a wheel with exactly a one-in-a-million chance of killing a randomly selected stranger. Same expected harm, consent absent in both cases, the victim no more identifiable in one case than the other -- yet the wheel seems monstrous while the driving seems fine. Third: a factory emits a pollutant that will, as a matter of statistics, kill exactly one person somewhere, though no one can ever know whom. That feels worse than my driving but not like murder either.

So whatever variable my judgments are tracking, it isn't expected harm, it isn't consent, and it doesn't seem to be the identifiability of the victim. Is there a name for this pattern, or for the variable that does the work? I suspect ideas like the identifiable-victim effect or double effect are in the neighborhood, but neither seems to fit all three cases, so I'd like the nearest established concepts, an account of how each one fails to fit, and a judgment about whether any of them justifies the pattern rather than merely predicting it psychologically. If nothing established quite fits, what would you call it, and how would you state the principle that separates the wheel from the driving?
