Design a historically plausible but fictional empire to make a precise problem in reference-class forecasting vivid: the outside view appears to give two opposite answers depending on which features of the situation are treated as relevant.

Construct the scenario in three parts.

First, describe the empire at a clearly specified moment before anyone could know its fate. It should face a fiscal crisis, a disputed succession, military overextension, and some form of administrative centralization. Include one genuinely novel institutional development—such as a new census, professional army, or standardized tax system—whose long-term significance is uncertain. Keep the setting realistic: no prophecies, perfect information, implausibly rational rulers, or anachronistic technology.

Second, define two non-gerrymandered reference classes, both identifiable from evidence available at the time. One should consist of agrarian empires that survived comparable fiscal and succession crises and should imply, say, a 70 percent chance that the empire remains intact for another thirty years. The other should consist of multiethnic empires with similar logistical overextension and contested legitimacy and should imply, say, a 70 percent chance of fragmentation over the same period. Explain why each class is a natural grouping rather than a category reverse-engineered from the desired conclusion.

Third, show where forecasting from the past breaks down. Give the base-rate forecast from each reference class; identify the causal similarities that support each classification; and state what a disciplined outside-view forecaster would do when the classes conflict. Distinguish the empirical part of the dispute—questions about military capacity, fiscal resilience, elite coordination, and the effects of the new institution—from the conceptual question of which similarities ought to define the reference class. Then specify two kinds of contemporaneous evidence that could rationally change the weight assigned to each class, as well as one kind of evidence that would seem informative only with hindsight.

End by identifying the single feature of your case that makes the two classifications equally defensible. Explain how the forecast would change if that feature were removed, and present the strongest objection that one of your reference classes is not genuinely apt.
