I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long essay about why intelligent, well-informed people so often fail to converge: forecasters who have digested the same polling data, scientists who have read the same studies, analysts who have watched the same markets. As background reporting for the essay, I want to get clear on a theoretical contrast.

In game theory and the economics of information, the standard modeling choice is the common prior assumption: rational agents are modeled as if they began with identical prior beliefs and came to differ only because they received different private information. Under this assumption, results such as Aumann's agreement theorem suggest that genuinely rational agents cannot agree to disagree — if their estimates become common knowledge, those estimates must coincide.

Now consider a weaker alternative, which I'll call the common evidence assumption: agents need not share priors at all, but they must agree about what the total body of evidence consists in — which observations were made, which experiments were run, what the raw data say — while remaining free to weigh and interpret that evidence differently.

Please compare the common prior assumption with the common evidence assumption as foundations for understanding expert disagreement. In particular, address the following:

1. Does the impossibility of agreeing to disagree survive if we retain common knowledge of rationality but drop common priors in favor of the common evidence assumption? What does your answer reveal about which assumption is actually doing the work in the agreement theorem?
2. Is the common evidence assumption even coherent as stated? One might argue that agreeing about what the evidence is — how to describe it, individuate it, and count it — already presupposes a shared interpretive framework, and thereby smuggles something like a common prior back in through the side door. Assess this objection.
3. Which assumption offers the better model of persistent real-world disagreement among experts, and is realism even the right standard by which to judge them, given that both are avowed idealizations?
4. What does each assumption imply about when deference is rational? That is, when should learning that an informed peer disagrees with you move your own beliefs, and does the answer change if you and your peer merely share evidence rather than priors?
