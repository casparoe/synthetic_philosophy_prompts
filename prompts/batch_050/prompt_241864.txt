So in history class we're doing this unit on how historians actually work, and my teacher keeps hammering that the real evidence is primary sources, like actual documents from the time — letters, tax records, court files, stuff that got saved in archives. Oral traditions and stories people pass down get treated as kind of sketchy, like a last resort when theres nothing written. But then it hit me: the stuff that gets written down and kept is mostly produced by governments, churches, rich people, basically whoever had scribes and storage space. Like, we "know" a lot about medieval peasants but its all filtered through monks and tax collectors, the peasants themselves are basically silent. Same with enslaved people, women, colonized peoples — tons of documents ABOUT them, almost none BY them.

So my question is: what is this whole practice of treating the archive as the gold standard actually assuming? Does it assume that writing is somehow more truthful than memory? That the past is only knowable through whatever powerful people bothered to record? Does it quietly assume the people who show up in the documents are representative of everyone who doesn't? And is this even consistent with itself — because the same textbook admits (not in exactly these words) that history gets written by the winners, so why does the method then turn around and trust the winners' filing cabinets?

Also I'm not sure if the assumption is about evidence (documents are just more reliable) or about value (the recorded lives are the ones that mattered), or if the practice could work under either one. If we dropped whatever the archive method is built on, what would history even look like — would oral history count the same, and what are you supposed to do about groups who left almost no records at all, just say nothing about them?
