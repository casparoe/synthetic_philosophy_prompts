At a food company where I serve on the ethics committee, we are considering replacing some imported plant protein with protein from a facility that would rear and kill roughly twenty billion insects each year, mostly as larvae. One consultant argues that the decision is analogous to buying fire insurance: just as a prudent homeowner protects against a low-probability but catastrophic house fire, we should protect against the low-probability but morally enormous possibility that these insects can suffer. Even if the probability of insect sentience were only one in a thousand, he says, the number of animals involved would make precaution overwhelmingly rational; therefore, the project should not proceed unless insect sentience has been ruled out. Another consultant objects that fire insurance works because the hazard, financial loss, premium, and compensation are all comparatively well defined, whereas insect welfare involves disputed probabilities, uncertain intensity, and no meaningful way to compensate the victims.

Please audit the fire-insurance analogy. Set out the intended correspondence explicitly: what plays the roles of the fire, the probability estimate, the premium, the insured loss, the insurer, and the decision to purchase coverage? Then identify where the correspondence breaks down. In particular, distinguish uncertainty that any insect species is sentient from uncertainty about which life stages are sentient, how intensely they can suffer, and how their experiences compare across enormous populations. Explain whether a tiny probability can legitimately be multiplied by vast numbers of animals, and whether that style of reasoning leads to fanaticism—the idea that arbitrarily improbable harms must dominate whenever the stakes are made sufficiently large. Also consider whether uncertainty across insect species and production facilities is more like correlated risk than independently insurable events.

Finally, assess what conclusion the analogy can actually support. Does it justify the first consultant’s near-absolute prohibition, a weaker presumption in favor of plant protein, investment in humane rearing and slaughter methods, research into insect sentience, or merely giving insects some nonzero but discounted weight in a broader cost-benefit analysis? If insurance is not the best analogy, propose a better one and explain what it changes about the decision.
