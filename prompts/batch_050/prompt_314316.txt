I help run a neighborhood tool-lending shed -- volunteer thing, about 200 households, you borrow a drill or a ladder and bring it back. Until recently it ran on a notebook on a string: write your name, write what you took. This year stuff started going missing. Not tons, but enough that we're restocking out of pocket, and at the last meeting the group split into two camps and it got heated.

Camp one: lockbox, keycards, deposits on anything over 50 bucks, late fees, a camera on the door. Their pitch is that people are people, a few jerks ruin it for everyone, and once word gets out that nobody checks, the jerks multiply. My uncle's version, delivered at me over Thanksgiving: trust is great, verify first. He says this is America, the default assumption is that everyones working an angle, and honestly so is city hall.

Camp two: the exact opposite. Add a bench and a coffee thermos, put up photos of stuff people built with borrowed tools, keep the notebook. Their pitch is that the moment you treat people like suspects they start acting like suspects, and the honest members -- who are the entire point -- quietly drift away because it stops feeling like a community. My roommate, who's from Denmark, is adamant that back home people just trust each other and it works, that they barely even double-check what the tax office tells them, and that Americans basically get the theft rate they design for.

Here's my problem: both camps sound right and they can't both be. If people respond to being trusted by becoming trustworthy, then the cameras don't just catch jerks, they manufacture them. But if trustworthiness is a fixed trait, then camp two is volunteering to get robbed and calling it community.

Also nagging at me: I half-remember reading about a daycare that started fining parents for late pickup and lateness went UP, like the fine converted "imposing on the nice teachers" into a paid service. If that's a real result it seems like ammunition for camp two. But does it generalize, or does it only apply to obligations that were social to begin with?

And the Denmark thing cuts both ways, right? Their trust sits on top of a whole country that mostly works -- courts, benefits, all of it. Can a 200-household shed in a mid-sized American city just decide to be Denmark? Or is my roommate exporting the attitude without the machinery that makes the attitude safe?

So: who's being more reasonable here? When you extend trust to strangers, is that a prediction about how they'll behave, or is it more like a bet you're partly making true by placing it? And what should I actually propose at next month's meeting? I'm genuinely torn, so don't just split the difference and say "a little of both" unless you can explain why the two mechanisms wouldn't cancel each other out.
