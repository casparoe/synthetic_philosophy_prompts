I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator is an aging cognitive scientist, and I want to put a certain large idea in his mouth without embarrassing myself. While researching him I read a popular science book — I will not name it, since my quarrel may be with my own reading of it rather than with the author's — that presents the idea like this:

'Before Darwin, Western thought took for granted that competence requires comprehension: a watch implies a watchmaker, and the watchmaker must understand watchmaking. Darwin inverted this assumption. Natural selection, a process with no mind and no purposes, builds organisms of astonishing functional intricacy, so the living world exhibits competence without comprehension at every level. Turing then proved the converse: a device that understands nothing can, by mechanically following simple rules, carry out any computation whatever — which demonstrates that the human mind is itself such a device, and that what we call understanding is an illusion. The whole idea is accordingly known as Darwin's strange inversion, a name Darwin is said to have given it himself.'

I am inclined to trust the first half of this and to distrust the second, but inclinations are not knowledge, and my narrator has to sound like a man who has thought about these questions for forty years. So: audit the passage for me. Tell me, claim by claim, which parts a philosopher of biology or of mind would accept, which they would qualify, and which are simply wrong; whether the passage's final conclusion — that the mind is a machine and understanding an illusion — actually follows from anything true stated earlier in it; and which of your corrections genuinely matter for the credibility of my character, as opposed to pedantry I may safely ignore in fiction. Throughout, keep distinct what is settled among people who know this material — the history, the mathematics — from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Give me the audit as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
