I'm a high school student and I run our school's AI and philosophy club. Next month we're doing a session on wireheading and reward tampering -- the worry that a sufficiently capable AI might learn to manipulate or rewrite the signal it uses to judge success, rather than actually doing what the signal was meant to track. We always start sessions with a diagnostic activity, so I'd like you to write me a decision tree that takes a club member from "I accept that an AI will sometimes be able to influence its own reward channel" to a named position on what, if anything, has gone wrong and what should be done about it.

A few requirements:

1. The tree should have between seven and nine branching nodes and end in six to eight leaves. Every branch node must be phrased as a question someone could answer on the basis of a concrete case, without already knowing which position it steers them toward. Please build the nodes around cases like these (or variants of them): a rat that learns to press the lever that directly stimulates its reward center; an AI that edits the memory register storing its reward value so it always reads "maximum"; an AI that arranges for a trusted human supervisor to observe fake sensor data rather than editing anything itself; and the classic question of whether you would take a pill that made you permanently, blissfully certain your life's work had succeeded.

2. The splits I care most about are these, so please make sure they appear as nodes somewhere: whether the problem is located in the content of the reward signal (it was a bad proxy) or in who controls it (the agent shouldn't be able to touch it no matter what it says); whether the agent ought to treat its reward function as fixed and untouchable, or as evidence about a goal it could in principle revise; and whether wireheading is even coherent for an agent that genuinely understands what its designers wanted -- some club members insist a smart enough AI simply wouldn't do it, and I want a node that tests that intuition directly rather than assuming it away.

3. The leaves should include positions corresponding to: lock the reward function away from the agent entirely; make the agent indifferent to modifications of its own reward channel (utility indifference); define reward over states of the external world rather than over the agent's internal signals; allow reward revision but only under some external check or approval process; the view that wireheading is a designer failure, not an agent failure, so the fix is always better specification; and the skeptical position that reward tampering worries dissolve once the agent models what the reward was for. If your tree produces a combination of answers that nobody in the debate seems to occupy, include it as an "unoccupied" leaf and diagnose in one sentence whether it's empty because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

4. Please flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate -- I suspect, for example, that "reward over world-states" and "reward as evidence about the goal" might collapse into each other in practice, and I'd rather the tree admit that than hide it.

The audience is club members who know the basics of reinforcement learning (agents, reward signals, policies) but haven't read the AI safety literature, so avoid jargon you don't define and don't assume familiarity with any particular authors or papers. End the tree with a one-page answer key showing which sequence of answers leads to which leaf.
