I'm a journalist working on a long-form piece about "choosing without knowing who you are," and I keep noticing a structural rhyme between two ideas from very different literatures. In Rawls's original position, parties choose principles of justice behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing their place in society, their talents, or their conception of the good. In updateless decision theory, an agent is advised to act according to the policy it would have wanted to commit to before learning which of its possible instances or situations it actually occupies. Both seem to say: reason well by deliberately discarding knowledge of which person you turned out to be.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher with real expertise in both social contract theory and formal decision theory. Think carefully, and begin by sketching the correspondence between the two devices before evaluating anything. Then compare them on the points where I suspect the rhyme hides important differences:

First, what exactly is being withheld in each case, and from whom — Rawls strips knowledge of social position and of one's ends, while the updateless agent seems to keep its values and lose only information about its location. Does that difference do real work?

Second, the rational engine differs: Rawls famously denies the parties knowledge of probabilities, pushing toward maximin-style reasoning and the difference principle, whereas the updateless agent typically retains a prior over instances and maximizes expected value. Is this an accidental feature of each theory or a deep one, and could either framework survive adopting the other's engine?

Third, the purpose differs: the veil is a device of representation meant to model fairness between distinct people, while updatelessness is meant to solve problems in instrumental rationality. Can an updateless-style construction do the normative work Rawls needs, or does something essential to justice get lost when the "parties" become instances of a single agent's policy?

Where does the comparison genuinely illuminate, and where does it mislead?
