I sit on our state bar's special committee on AI licensure (don't ask how I got roped into this). A legal-tech company, ClearLease, has petitioned for its AI system to receive a limited license to practice — it would only handle uncontested residential eviction filings and form responses, no courtroom appearences. We have to issue a written opinion to the full bar next month, and I got voluntold to draft it.

The honest-to-god strongest argument for granting the license is the corporate-person analogy: the law already treats corporations as persons — they sue, get sued, hold property, appear in court through agents — and nobody thinks a corporation is conscious or has a soul. Personhood, on this view, is just a bundle of legal incidents you hand out when it's useful, so refusing the AI on purely metaphysical grounds is confused.

The strongest argument against, as I see it: a corporation's personhood is ultimately cashed out in human accountability — officers, directors, shareholders you can depose, fine, or jail. ClearLease offers a liability insurance fund instead, which covers the money but not the finding-someone-answerable part. And there's a wrinkle nobody on the commitee has a good answer for: the system is retrained roughly every quarter and keeps no persistent memory between client matters, so the "entity" we'd be disciplining in year two arguably isn't the one that erred in year one. Corporate personhood assumes a persisting bearer of obligations, and it's not obvious we have one here.

Act as a legal theorist who knows the philosophy-of-personhood literature cold. Think carefully through the strongest version of both sides before settling on a position, then draft the committee's opinion: a clear recommendation — grant, deny, or grant with conditions you spell out concretely (time frames, renewal requirements, supervision duties, whatever you think actually works) — with the reasoning behind it. Whichever way you come down, the opinion has to take the corporate-person analogy seriously and say where it holds up and where it breaks, because half our members think it's decisive. Lead with the bottom-line recommendation in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating the question back at me.
