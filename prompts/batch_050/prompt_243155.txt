After a long argument with a friend about whether entrenched social norms can be arbitrary without being irrational, I drafted the following take-home question for an upper-level undergraduate course on social ontology. I am now grading the responses and would like an independent assessment of one student’s answer.

Exam question: In Convention (1969), David Lewis argues, in effect, that a convention is a behavioral regularity in a recurrent situation sustained by expectations and conditional preferences, where some alternative regularity could have done the coordinating work instead, and where the relevant facts are common knowledge. This is a paraphrase supplied to students, not a quotation. Using this account, explain how a norm can originate and persist without anyone designing it and without being the uniquely best available solution. Then apply the account to the case below. Distinguish (i) a convention’s being arbitrary in Lewis’s sense from its being irrational, and (ii) the conditions under which a population would rationally switch to a different equilibrium from the conditions under which it merely complains about the current one.

Case for application: In a large city, cyclists keep left on shared paths. All regular users would now prefer keeping right, because a new bridge has made right-hand exits shorter, but each cyclist keeps left because they expect others to do so and fear collisions. Most also believe, falsely, that most others strongly prefer left. A phone app could coordinate a switch on an announced date if enough users opted in, but the threshold for safe switching is not common knowledge.

Student’s answer: Lewis’s point is that conventions solve coordination problems. Keeping left is a convention because everyone conforms, everyone expects conformity, and each prefers to conform so long as others do. Since a mirror-image right-hand rule would coordinate equally well, the left rule is arbitrary, though not irrational: given the expectations in place, unilateral deviation is costly. It persists through a feedback loop in which action produces evidence for the expectations that sustain it. No one needs to have designed it; salience, precedent, or chance can fix one equilibrium, and repetition turns it into common knowledge. Lewis would say the cyclists are stuck in a bad equilibrium: because every individual’s preference is conditional on others, unilateral deviation is pointless, and only a coordinated switch is rational. The false belief that others prefer left is irrelevant, however, because Lewis’s common-knowledge condition requires only that each person believes the others will conform, not that anyone knows others’ preferences. If the app made the switch date and threshold public, Lewis’s view implies the population would switch at once, since the right rule is strictly better; failure to switch would show that the regularity was never a genuine convention but a coerced norm. Lewis’s account therefore predicts that inefficient conventions are unstable once publicized, because rational agents gravitate to the Pareto-superior equilibrium.

Please grade this answer out of 100 using this rubric: accuracy about Lewis’s account, 40; application to the cyclist case, 30; handling of the two requested distinctions, 20; clarity and organization, 10. Give the grade first, then provide specific comments that justify it. Identify which claims are correct, which are mistaken, and which are merely unsupported, quoting or closely paraphrasing the student’s own wording where necessary. Pay particular attention to any conflation of common knowledge with weaker mutual belief, any misattribution to Lewis of a view about efficiency or instability, and any inference about the app case that does not follow. Do not add citations or a reading list; the point is to assess the answer as written.
