I am a novelist, and for thirty years one reader has seen every book before anyone else: my editor. We have never discussed philosophy, but across a dozen manuscripts she has developed unshakeable instincts about which of the loves I depict are genuine and which are counterfeit, and I have learned to trust those instincts more than my own. I now want to understand what rule she is running — because I have a new manuscript, and I honestly cannot tell what she will say about it.

Her verdicts so far, as I reconstruct them from our correspondence:

1. In an early novel, a man marries a woman for her wit and vivacity; a stroke in middle age takes both. The love persists, transfigured, sustained by the decades they have accumulated. She called it the truest thing I had written.

2. In another, a young man adores a celebrated actress he has never met. He would, as he puts it, do anything for her — but what he wants is to possess her; her actual suffering leaves him unmoved, and when he has one chance to spare her a public humiliation at some cost to his fantasy, he declines. My editor: not love, whatever the character himself believes — infatuation wearing love's clothes.

3. A woman discovers, mid-marriage, that the quality she most prized in her husband — his courage — is one he does not possess; her belief was an error, perhaps a self-serving one. She stays, and the love I portrayed was tender and real. My editor found it wholly credible: love, she said, is not falsified by having been founded on a mistake.

4. An aging husband cares for his wife through dementia, forty years of shared life behind them. He does everything a loving husband would do — but the narration makes plain that he keeps a meticulous inner ledger of his sacrifices, resents her, and cherishes chiefly his own image as a man who does not abandon. My editor: this is not love, whatever else it is; duty and vanity impersonating it. The forty years, she insisted, change nothing.

5. A mercenary courtship: a young man marries an heiress for her fortune. Twenty years on, the fortune is gone, and the manuscript shows a man who would give his life for his wife and could not say when that became true. My editor: whatever its origins, it is love now.

A philosopher acquaintance tells me her instincts recapitulate a venerable dispute about the reasons for love: whether genuine love is a response to the beloved's valuable qualities; or a response to the person rather than to any of their properties; or a matter of wholeheartedly caring for the beloved's good for the beloved's own sake, so that love bestows value rather than finding it; or a response to the shared history of a relationship rather than to anything about the beloved at all. He named Frankfurt, Velleman, and Kolodny; I have not read them.

Now the new manuscript. A retired schoolteacher — childless, twice widowed — takes it upon herself to nurse a young woman in her building through a long convalescence. They are strangers beforehand. The girl is sullen, unremarkable, occasionally cruel, and delirious through most of her illness; they exchange no confidences, and she will remember almost none of it. When she recovers, she leaves the city without thanks and without ever quite learning who tended her. The teacher expects nothing, gains nothing, keeps no ledger. I have tried to portray her devotion as wholly unambivalent: she cares for the girl's good for the girl's own sake, and reflecting on it at the end, she would do it all again.

What will my editor say? I want more than a guess. Tell me which conception of love her five verdicts seem to instantiate; which rival conceptions fit the same record just as well, and what each of them predicts about the schoolteacher; which earlier verdict is the closest analog to the new case and why it fails to settle it; and your best prediction, with reasons. If her record genuinely underdetermines this case, tell me so plainly — and name the further case, some sixth manuscript I might one day write, whose verdict would best discriminate among the rival rules.
