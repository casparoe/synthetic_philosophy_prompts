Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about Boltzmann brains, and we discovered that our disagreement was structural rather than factual. Let me lay out the setup precisely. Take the proposition P: I am a Boltzmann brain — that is, my current experiences, including my apparent memories of a coherent personal past in a lawful low-entropy universe, belong to a spontaneously fluctuated observer rather than to an ordinary observer embedded in a real history. My friend grants that P is probably false, but she insists that rational credence in P is all-or-nothing: it must sit near zero or near one, with no stable intermediate value. Her reasoning is that the credence decomposes into a cosmological term (does the actual universe, under the correct physics and the correct measure over cosmological configurations, produce fluctuated observers with my exact experiences in numbers vastly exceeding ordinary observers?) and an epistemic term (conditional on that cosmological fact obtaining, ought I to conclude that I am one of the fluctuated observers?). She claims the epistemic term is effectively one, because the standard way of shrinking it — pointing out that believing P undermines the reliability of the very evidence and reasoning that led you to believe it, and declaring the inference self-defeating — is illegitimate. A brain-in-a-vat hypothesis is equally destructive of the evidence adduced for believing it, she says, yet no one thinks that observation dissolves the skeptical problem; you do not get to dismiss a hypothesis merely because it predicts its own unbelievability. So on her view the entire question collapses into the cosmology term, and any credence strictly between zero and one betrays confusion.

I think she is wrong, though my reasons are murkier than hers. My intuition is that the self-undermining move has genuine force against P specifically, because a Boltzmann brain's apparent memories and inductive track record are not systematically misleading in the way a vat-dweller's might be — they are random noise. A hypothesis that renders one's evidence random, rather than merely false, strikes me as rationally inadmissible in a stronger way: the inference to it consumes its own premises, whereas the vat hypothesis is at least coherent all the way through. But I cannot presently say whether that is a principled disanalogy or a verbal maneuver, and my friend pressed me on exactly this point for an hour. For the record, my credence in P is about 0.03, which she regards as a symptom of muddled thinking, since on her view I should either commit to the cosmology being BB-dominated (and go to ~1) or not (and go to ~0).

Here is what I would like from you.

First, give me your own credence in P — treating your experiences as those of a typical early-twenty-first-century human — decomposed into at least the following sub-questions: the probability that our universe's cosmology yields BB dominance; the probability, conditional on BB dominance, that the relevant anthropic inference (reason as if sampled from observers with your experiences) is valid; the probability that the self-undermining objection blocks that inference even granting both of the foregoing; and any further terms you think belong — for instance, whether P even has a determinate truth value, or whether the first-person pronoun picks out anything unique across a plenitude of phenomenally identical observers. Give a number and one or two lines of justification for each term, then show how they combine into your final credence.

Second, adjudicate the structural dispute: is my friend's all-or-nothing claim correct, and if not, where exactly does it fail? In particular, is the disanalogy I sketched — random noise versus systematic deception — a defensible ground for treating P as incoherent in a way the vat hypothesis is not?

Third, tell me which term in your decomposition your final number is most sensitive to, and what empirical or philosophical evidence would shift it the most.
