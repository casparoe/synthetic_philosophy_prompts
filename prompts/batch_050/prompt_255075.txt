I'm drafting a thesis chapter on expressivist semantics for normative language, and before I wade any further into the literature I want to fix, in advance, what I'm entitled to demand of a theory in this space. The recurring frustration is that each author seems to treat their own favorite achievement as the criterion of adequacy, so "solves the problem" often just means "solves the problem I started with." Write me the desiderata: what any adequate expressivist account of normative talk must deliver in order to count as having addressed the Frege–Geach challenge rather than merely relocated it. For each requirement, give the phenomenon or example that motivates it -- embedding under negation and the antecedents of conditionals, the apparent validity of arguments built out of normative and descriptive premises, the sense in which two speakers who disagree about whether lying is wrong are contradicting each other rather than merely clashing in attitude, the felt asymmetry between sincerely uttering a normative sentence and reporting that one has a certain attitude, the behavior of normative vocabulary in questions, embeddings under "believes," and so on -- and explain why a theory that fails it would be giving up something real rather than merely disappointing someone's taste.

I also want your view on the structure of the list itself, which matters more to me than any single item. Which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable, in the sense that a theory failing them has changed the subject from normative language to something else? Which are negotiable -- demands that a sophisticated expressivist can plausibly explain away as artifacts of the realist picture we're being asked to give up -- and what does the explaining-away cost? And do you suspect any pair (or triple) of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied? If, for instance, full compositionality of normative content and the thesis that normative sentences express non-representational states turn out to be in tension, or if getting moral modus ponens to come out valid in the logician's sense conflicts with the expressivist's diagnosis of why it feels valid, I want that flagged explicitly, because a proof that the desiderata list is unsatisfiable would be just as valuable to my chapter as a theory that meets it.

Finally, and more briefly than the above, score the main contenders against your list: a simple emotivism of the old sort, a prescriptivist view in Hare's style, a plan- or hyperplan-based expressivism in Gibbard's style, Blackburn-style quasi-realism, and a hybrid view on which normative utterances both express attitudes and assert descriptive contents. For each, say which desideratum it most clearly fails or strains, and which desideratum does the most discriminatory work in separating the views from one another. I'm less interested in your verdict on which view wins than in whether the desiderata, once stated carefully, make the disagreement between the views tractable -- or reveal it to be partly about what we're entitled to demand in the first place.
