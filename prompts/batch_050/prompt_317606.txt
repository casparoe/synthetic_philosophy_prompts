I've been arguing with a friend about when it's okay to tie your own hands, and I realized I don't have a consistent view. It started from the Odyssey, where Circe tells Odysseus how to get past the Sirens (Book 12, in A.T. Murray's translation):

"But do thou row past them, and anoint the ears of thy comrades with sweet wax, which thou hast kneaded, lest any of the rest may hear. But if thou thyself hast a will to listen, let them bind thee in the swift ship hand and foot upright in the step of the mast, and let the ropes be made fast at the ends to the mast itself, that with delight thou mayest listen to the voice of the two Sirens. And if thou shalt implore and bid thy comrades to loose thee, then let them bind thee with yet more bonds."

So the crew is told in advance to treat Odysseus's future pleas as null and void, and to tighten the ropes if anything. Here are my verdicts on cases, and I want to know what principle I'm running on:

- Odysseus's setup itself: clearly fine, actually kind of admirable.
- My roommate has an app that charges her $5 to a charity she hates every time she opens Instagram before 6pm: fine.
- A guy with a gambling problem puts himself on the casino's self-exclusion list, which he can't get off of for a year even if he begs: fine.
- A woman with recurrent depression signs a directive authorizing hospitalization during future episodes even if she refuses it at the time: I think fine, but I'm noticeably less sure than about the gambler.
- Someone in the early stages of dementia signs a directive refusing all life-prolonging treatment for the version of themselves that won't recognize their kids anymore: this feels wrong to me, even though I can't see how the structure differs from the depression case.
- A legislature passes a law declaring it can never be repealed by any future majority: feels illegitimate, though again, why is it worse than the casino list?
- After three horrible breakups, a guy gets a procedure that permanently dulls his capacity to fall in love: feels the worst of all of them, worse than anything above.

My tentative principle is that self-binding is legit when it protects your settled values from temporary distortion, and illegit when it overrules what the future you will genuinely want -- but the dementia case and the legislature case both seem to break that, and the breakup guy breaks it in a third way I can't articulate. Fix my principle or replace it, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and say what it predicts for one case I haven't listed, like a country constitutionally entrenching its own term limits.
