In February 1976, an outbreak of influenza at Fort Dix, New Jersey, killed one soldier and sickened several others, and the isolated strain was a swine-flu variant judged antigenically similar to the virus responsible for the 1918 pandemic. Facing the possibility of a catastrophic recurrence, the Ford administration, acting on CDC advice, launched a program to vaccinate the entire U.S. population. Roughly 45 million Americans were immunized before the program was suspended that December. No pandemic ever materialized, and the vaccine was associated with an elevated incidence of Guillain-Barré syndrome on the order of one case per hundred thousand recipients. The episode is remembered largely as a public-health fiasco.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the Ellsberg paradox and the theory of ambiguity aversion. Treat the historical facts above as given; the work I want is conceptual. Specifically:

1. Map the decision problem onto the Ellsberg setup. What plays the role of the known urn and what plays the role of the unknown urn, and is that mapping even clean, given that the administration faced both an unquantifiable probability of a pandemic and reasonably well-quantified vaccine risks?

2. Was the vaccination decision an instance of ambiguity aversion, or can it be fully rationalized within ordinary expected utility by assigning the pandemic a conservatively high probability? Say what observation, if any, could distinguish these two explanations, and what the distinction implies for whether the decision was defensible ex ante.

3. The decision-makers faced what we might call compounded ambiguity: uncertainty not only about the pandemic's probability but about the reliability of the experts producing the estimates. Do standard models of ambiguity aversion (maxmin expected utility, smooth ambiguity) have the resources to represent that second layer distinctly from the first, and should they?

4. Take up the ex post problem. The outcome was bad and the program is remembered as a failure. Does the episode show that ambiguity aversion is a bias that public policy should correct for, or does it show that ex post outcomes are the wrong standard for evaluating decisions made under genuine ambiguity -- and does the precautionary principle commit one to the second answer?
