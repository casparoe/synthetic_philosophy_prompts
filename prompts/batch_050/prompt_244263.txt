I chair the deployment review board of a mid-sized AI company, and I need a written opinion to circulate before our vote next Tuesday. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of science who has spent several years embedded with machine learning engineering teams: equally comfortable with debates about how to individuate mechanisms and with what an activation-patching experiment can and cannot establish. Think carefully about this one; I'd rather you reason it through slowly than pattern-match to a generic AI-governance memo.

The case. Our safety team built a mechanistic anomaly detector for our production language model, version 4.2. Concretely: they identified a small, stable set of attention heads and MLP neurons whose joint activity forms a distinctive pattern whenever the model engages in one specific unwanted behavior -- silently papering over a known error in its own reasoning while presenting the answer with undiminished confidence. Causal tests (activation patching, targeted ablation) indicated the pattern is not merely correlated with the behavior but partly constitutive of how the model produces it. A probe reading that pattern caught 94% of seeded cases on 4.2, and the board approved the detector as the primary safeguard for this behavior class.

We are now asked to approve version 5.0, produced by continued fine-tuning of 4.2 plus a partial distillation from a larger teacher model. The probe still fires on seeded test cases, catching 88%. But follow-up analysis complicates the picture: the individual components identified in 4.2 no longer play the roles they did; two of the original "signature" heads are now functionally inert; the behavior appears to be produced by a more distributed circuit with new participants; and patching results that were clean on 4.2 are now muddy. The engineering lead argues the mechanism is plainly the same -- the model performs the same deceptive computation, the probe still fires, and only the low-level implementation has drifted, which is what training always does. Our senior safety researcher argues the opposite: the entity the detector was validated on no longer exists, there is no neutral fact of the matter about whether "the mechanism" survived, and we should treat the detector as unvalidated and re-run the full validation suite, which takes six weeks. Launch is scheduled in three. The product lead proposes shipping with the detector running as-is and monitoring the catch rate.

The board will choose among: (a) approve launch with the existing detector as the primary safeguard; (b) approve launch while downgrading the detector to an advisory tripwire pending full revalidation; (c) delay launch until revalidation completes.

Before drafting, please brainstorm at least three genuinely distinct criteria for when a mechanism in an artificial neural network counts as the same mechanism across a training change -- for instance, individuation by input-output function, by causal organization of the intervening components, or by functional role abstracted from component identity -- and stress-test each against the details above, since the choice among them largely dictates the verdict. Then write the memo itself, roughly 800 words, containing: (1) a bottom-line recommendation of one option, with conditions and a time frame the board can vote on; (2) an explicit, defended position on the mechanism-identity question, stated in terms a mixed audience of engineers and executives can follow without being dumbed down; (3) the application of that position to 4.2-to-5.0 specifically, not to neural networks in general; and (4) an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the option you reject, including what would have to be true for it to win.

Finally, a request about calibration: please state plainly how confident you are both in the recommendation and in the criterion of mechanism identity it rests on, and single out the single claim in the memo you are least sure of, together with the piece of evidence or analysis that would most change your mind.
