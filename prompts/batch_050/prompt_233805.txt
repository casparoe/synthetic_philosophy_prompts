I'm a program officer at a small grantmaking foundation. We get around 200 proposals a year, I can fund maybe 35 of them, and I'm the one who builds the ranked list that our board signs off on. My decisions get challenged a couple times a year by applicants or board members, so I have to be able to defend them, but I also only have a few hours per proposal at most.

Here's my problem. I keep running into what I've heard philosophers call spectrum arguments, and I don't fully trust my own comparisons anymore. Like: proposal A looks a hair better than B, B a hair better than C, and so on down the line, each step totally reasonable, and then I notice I've ranked A over P even though if you'd shown me just those two side by side I'd have picked P instantly. Or I do pairwise comparisons across criteria -- impact, cost, risk, equity -- and I catch myself in loops, preferring X to Y, Y to Z, and Z back to X. I've read just enough decision theory to know intransitive preferences can get you money-pumped and that there's a whole literature on whether "better than" even has to be transitive (all the stuff around mere addition and sequences of tiny improvements that supposedly lead somewhere absurd), but I have no idea how to turn any of that into something I can actually do on a Tuesday afternoon.

So: act as a decision theorist who also understands how real grantmaking works, not just the seminar-room version. Think carefully about what the philosophy here genuinely implies for practice and what's just clever puzzle-mongering. Then write me a procedure I can run on my ranked list each cycle -- the checks to run in order, how to detect a suspicious chain of pairwise judgments versus a legitimate one, when a tiny difference between adjacent proposals should be treated as a tie or a coin flip instead of a ranking, what to do when I find an intransitive loop (do I average, drop a criterion, force a choice, what?), and at what point the procedure should stop and kick the decision back to me as a judgment call. Be honest about which steps are real safeguards and which are just rituals that make me feel rigorous.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: proposal A beats proposal B by a small margin on each of my five criteria, but my gut says B is the better project overall. Second: the funding cutoff problem -- proposal 35 and proposal 36 are nearly indistinguishable, but 35 gets fully funded and 36 gets nothing, and the same logic chains all the way down to proposals I know are weak. Third, give me a case where the procedure spits out an answer I won't like -- e.g., it forces me to fund the safe, boring proposal over the exciting one I already half-promised to a board member -- and walk me through whether I should swallow it or override, and what overriding consistently would do to the whole system.

Please write it as continuous prose, like a long email from a colleague -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I know that's annoying for a "procedure" but I want to actually read it, not skim it.
