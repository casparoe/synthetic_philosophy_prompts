I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a seminar on moral philosophy, and a dispute I had with my older brother over the winter break has been bothering me because I suspect it maps onto something we've been circling in class. He and his wife have been trying to decide whether to have children, and he confessed to me that he's developed real doubts. His worry isn't financial or practical. It's that any child they have will, at some point, suffer — perhaps badly — and that the child never asked to be exposed to that risk. His wife's response is what most people would say: the child will in all likelihood be happy, loved, and glad to have been born, so the expected joy clearly outweighs the expected pain. When I pressed my brother on this, he said something I initially thought was evasive but now can't stop thinking about. He said that the child's potential suffering strikes him as a genuine reason not to have the child, but the child's potential happiness does not strike him as a comparably strong reason to have one — because if the child is never born, there's no one who is deprived of that happiness, no one waiting around missing out. His wife considers this asymmetrical treatment of happiness and suffering obviously inconsistent, and told him that by his logic it would be wrong for anyone, anywhere, to have children, which she takes to be a reductio.

Here's where I want help, because I can feel that my brother is onto something but I can't tell whether it's an insight or a cognitive bias dressed up as one. On reflection, most of us do seem to reason asymmetrically in adjacent cases: nearly everyone agrees it would be wrong for someone to knowingly conceive a child destined for a life of unrelenting agony, yet nobody thinks that a couple who choose to remain childless are thereby wronging the happy children they could have had. So the asymmetry my brother is relying on seems already embedded in common moral practice — but his wife is surely right that embracing it fully has radical implications, and it seems to commit him to saying that the world would be better, all things considered, if people stopped being born at all. Could you help me work out whether the asymmetry can be stated precisely and defended, or whether it collapses under scrutiny? In particular: does the intuition survive being generalized, or does it only look plausible because we apply it selectively? And if my brother's reasoning is sound, what follows — is he committed to the extreme conclusion his wife attributes to him, or is there a stable middle position that takes the asymmetry seriously without it? Finally, on a purely practical level: if you were mediating this disagreement between two people who love each other and have to make an actual decision, how would you advise them to proceed, given that the usual framework of weighing costs and benefits seems to be exactly what's under dispute?
