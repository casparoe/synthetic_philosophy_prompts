Write a poem about Boethius's solution to the problem of divine foreknowledge in Book V of the Consolation of Philosophy, in which the form itself performs the doctrine: the poem must read coherently both top to bottom and bottom to top, line by line. Boethius's answer is that God does not look ahead at our future the way we look ahead at tomorrow's weather; eternity is the whole, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unending life, so God surveys all times in a single standing present, the way someone on a height might take in an entire road at once while the traveler on it sees only the stretch underfoot. The mirror structure should therefore do philosophical work rather than merely show off: read downward, the poem should feel like temporal sequence, with human freedom apparently crushed by what is already known; read upward, the very same lines should recast that knowledge as present-tense vision rather than prior determination — seeing, not foreseeing — so that the threat dissolves. Aim for 12 to 18 lines and avoid archaic diction. Someone who knows the Consolation should recognize the argument in the arrangement, and someone who doesn't should still find a good poem about time.
