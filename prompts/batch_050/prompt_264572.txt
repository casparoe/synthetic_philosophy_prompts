I should like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher — someone steeped in twentieth-century ethics and the metaphysics of personal identity, but also a practised and patient reader of literary fiction. I am a retired molecular biologist who has come to philosophy late and with great enthusiasm, and I am preparing a paper for our local philosophy circle. Please think carefully about what follows; I would suggest beginning by setting out, briefly, the two or three strongest considerations on either side before committing yourself to a reading, and only then writing the piece proper.

My problem is this. I have been working through Rawls's A Theory of Justice and Parfit's Reasons and Persons, and I keep returning to Rawls's complaint that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons — that it takes the perfectly respectable principle by which one individual balances losses and gains within a single life and extends it, illegitimately, to the balancing of lives across a whole society. Parfit's reply, as I understand it, is that the objection quietly presupposes a non-reductionist view of what a person is: if what matters in survival is psychological continuity rather than some further deep fact of identity, then the boundaries between lives are less morally weighty than Rawls requires, and aggregation across persons ceases to look like the obvious sin it first appeared.

It has struck me, on a second reading, that Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go is almost engineered to test this dispute, and I want you to read it through that lens. Consider: the donors are clones, raised to give up their organs by instalments until they complete — so that one separate person's good is aggregated away piecemeal, not all at once, which seems to dramatise Rawls's point with unusual precision. Hailsham's gallery, where the children's art is collected as evidence that they possess souls, reads like an institutional answer to the rationale that consumes them, yet the kindness of the regime does nothing to touch the separateness objection, which is about the structure of the justification rather than the quality of the treatment. The rumour of deferrals, and Kathy and Tommy's hope in it, strikes me as the novel's way of asking whether any humane revision could rescue an aggregative scheme from within. And then there is the matter that troubles me most as a former geneticist: the donors are copies, and they hunt for their possibles as though the original held the key to what they are. Parfit would say genetic sameness is not what matters — psychological continuity is — and I cannot decide whether the novel quietly vindicates him on that point whilst vindicating Rawls on the larger one, or whether its humanism rests on something else entirely, perhaps a Kantian thought about treating persons merely as means.

Please address, in an organised fashion: whether the novel vindicates Rawls against Parfit, or Parfit against Rawls, or neither; what to make of the characters' complete failure to rebel, given that Rawls's objection is structural rather than a complaint about the victims' feelings; and where the work resists the reading — for I am aware that the donations take place almost entirely offstage, that the book is no policy satire, and that donation may function above all as a figure for the universal human fate, which we all meet without rebellion. An honest reading must say what the philosophical interpretation misses. I know the novel well, so no plot summary, and I have no need of citations or a bibliography. Finally, do tell me what the novel adds to this debate that the arguments alone do not — what a work of fiction can show about the separateness of persons that A Theory of Justice and Reasons and Persons, for all their power, cannot.
