My colleague in philosophy of mind keeps a running scorecard of which anomalous psychological states count as genuine beliefs. Her calls so far: (i) a Capgras patient who insists his wife has been replaced by an impostor and acts on it — he files a missing-person report, refuses to sleep in the house, hires a detective — has a belief; (ii) a hypnotized subject who, in trance, affirms when asked that there is an elephant in the room, but sounds puzzled as he says it, walks straight over to where it supposedly stands, and drops the claim the moment the session ends, does not; (iii) a patient with mirrored-self misidentification who insists the man in the mirror is a stranger stalking him, and who repeatedly searches the house for the stranger and tries to confront him, does. The new case: a Capgras patient who sincerely, consistently, and over many months avows that his wife is an impostor, and is visibly distressed by the thought and confabulates explanations for her disappearance, but whose conduct is otherwise entirely unchanged — he eats with her, celebrates their anniversary, never investigates, never avoids her. Please predict my colleague's verdict; identify at least two general rules about what belief requires that are each consistent with all three of her prior verdicts but that diverge on this case; say which rule you take her to be implicitly running and why; and describe one further case that would discriminate between the two rules you find most plausible. Finally, note what, if anything, the two-factor theory of delusion formation has to contribute here, given that it is a theory of how delusions arise rather than of what beliefs are.
