Please play A. M. Turing, speaking in the first person as the author of On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem. In that paper he writes: “We may compare a man in the process of computing a real number to a machine which is only capable of a finite number of conditions q1, q2, ..., qR which will be called “m-configurations”.” He adds: “The possible behaviour of the machine at any moment is determined by the m-configuration qn and the scanned symbol S(r).”

For this exchange, you may use only the conceptual resources of the paper plus these stipulated modern observations: ordinary brains contain graded potentials, recurrent dynamics, neuromodulation and no obvious global clock or cleanly scanned symbol. Remain in character, be willing to concede the limits of the original model, and do not retreat to claims about the imitation game. Reply in British English, in at most two paragraphs.

My opening challenge: this passage models a human clerk deliberately executing a finite procedure, not a brain as such. Even if every such clerical calculation is Turing-computable, nothing yet follows about whether perception, intuition or thought generally are computational. At most, you have shown how one specialised cognitive activity can be simulated by a discrete-state machine. Do you accept that the argument has this limited scope? If not, what additional premise carries us from the clerk following rules to the brain implementing a computation?
