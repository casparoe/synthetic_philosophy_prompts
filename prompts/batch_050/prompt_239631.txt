Every film publication I know of has the same unwritten rule: the reviewer has to have actually seen the movie. Doesn't matter if your best friend has flawless, time-tested taste and swears the new one is a masterpiece — you can't file a review on the basis of her say-so, and if you did it would be a scandal. What's odd is that the same outlets will happily report secondhand that the film runs 2h10 or that it won a prize at Cannes, and readers happily pick what to watch off aggregated scores, i.e. other people's verdicts. So the taboo is weirdly specific: testimony about the facts of the artwork is fine, testimony about its quality is not, at least for the critic. What does this practice presuppose about aesthetic judgment — does it only make sense if forming a proper aesthetic opinion requires first-hand acquaintance rather than just acquiring a true belief? Which rival views of aesthetic knowledge would it rule out, and is the practice even internally consistent, given that deferring is fine for audiences but fraudulent for critics?
