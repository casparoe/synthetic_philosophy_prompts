I’ve recently ended up helping a local emergency-response charity draft its triage policy, which is annoyingly making all this much less theoretical. In a flood, for example, one team might be able either to reach one stranded person on the east side of town or five people on the west side, but not both. Our draft says to prioritise the larger number when the parties are otherwise similar.

A friend who did some philosophy says Taurek’s argument about whether the numbers should count makes that rule hard to justify. He summarised the article like this:

Taurek’s point is that suffering does not literally add up across people. Five people in danger do not jointly constitute one person facing five times as much danger, and there is no extra collective person who experiences the five losses. Each of the six people has an equal claim to be rescued, so it is morally permissible to save the one rather than the five. Indeed, the fairest procedure is to give the one and the five an equal chance, perhaps by tossing a coin. Counting the numbers means treating people as units in an impersonal calculation, so any policy that routinely sends resources to the larger group fails to respect persons separately. Taurek therefore has to say that you should toss a coin even if the one is your own child and the five are strangers.

How fair is that summary? Please separate what Taurek is centrally arguing from what is a popular overstatement or an extra conclusion. In particular, does “suffering doesn’t add up” mean that five deaths are no worse than one in every relevant sense? Does equal moral status really require an equal chance of rescue? And would Taurek’s position actually forbid a general policy of saving the greater number, or only deny that the number by itself settles every rescue choice?

I’m less interested in the standard utilitarian reply than in what a sympathetic reader of Taurek would accept here, because if the summary is right I may need to rethink our commitee’s draft policy.
