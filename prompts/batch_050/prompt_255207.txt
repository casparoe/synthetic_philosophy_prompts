In preparation for an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of assertion, a colleague sent me an op-ed from a general-audience magazine that attempts to bring Harry Frankfurt's account of bullshit to bear on large language models. The relevant paragraph reads as follows:

"Frankfurt's insight is that bullshit is not the same as lying. The liar knows the truth and deliberately says the opposite of it, while the bullshitter simply speaks without bothering to check whether what he says is true. That is why bullshit, unlike the lie, is always false, and why Frankfurt concludes that lying, as the more deliberate and intentional act, is the more serious offense against truth. Applied to artificial intelligence, the implication is straightforward. A language model has no beliefs, so it cannot know the truth; and since it cannot know the truth, it cannot intend to deceive anyone about it; hence it cannot lie. Its false outputs — so-called hallucinations — are therefore bullshit in Frankfurt's strict technical sense rather than mere errors, and calling them 'errors' is a euphemism that obscures what these systems are actually doing."

I have read Frankfurt's essay, though not recently, and my recollection is that the op-ed is broadly on the right track but goes wrong somewhere in its treatment of the lying–bullshit asymmetry; I cannot reconstruct the point with any precision. Please audit the paragraph claim by claim. Which parts would a careful reader of Frankfurt accept as accurate? Which misrepresent his view, and in what way — conflation of two notions he keeps apart, inversion of a comparison he draws, overstatement of a modest thesis? And does the final inference about hallucinations follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate? In particular, I would like to know whether classifying a hallucination as bullshit in Frankfurt's strict sense requires attributing to the model attitudes or aims that it plausibly lacks, whether the op-ed's implicit assumption that every false output must be either a lie or bullshit is exhaustive, and what the most defensible classification of hallucination would be if the op-ed's conclusion fails. Please keep the entire response to no more than 700 words.
