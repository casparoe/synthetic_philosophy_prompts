On a philosophy podcast I listen to, one of the hosts gave this thumbnail sketch of the literature on higher-order evidence: "Higher-order evidence is evidence about the quality of your own thinking -- learning that you were sleep-deprived when you formed a belief, or that a respected colleague who saw the same evidence reached the opposite conclusion. The consensus view is that this is just more evidence, to be weighed like any other: if you discover that someone just as smart and informed as you disagrees, rationality requires you to meet them halfway, since from the inside your own reasoning and theirs look identical and you have no non-circular way to prefer your own. The uncomfortable consequence is that you can end up believing something while also believing that you shouldn't believe it -- philosophers call this epistemic akrasia, by analogy with weakness of will, and like its practical cousin it is straightforwardly irrational, a failure of rational coherence that the correct theory should rule out."

This seemed mostly sensible to me, but two things nagged: the move from "your reasoning looks identical from the inside" to "split the difference" felt faster than it should, and the claim that epistemic akrasia is *straightforwardly* irrational seemed in tension with the fact that the summary itself seemed to describe situations where you can't avoid it. Could you audit this summary for me? Which parts are broadly accepted, which present a contested position as settled consensus, and where, if anywhere, does it conflate distinct theses or distinct kinds of case? I'd also like to know whether the concluding verdict on epistemic akrasia follows from the earlier claims -- or whether a proponent of the "weigh it like any other evidence" picture might actually be pushed toward *permitting* akratic states in exactly the cases the host describes.
