I'm preparing a short "position statement" for a graduate reading group on political liberalism, and before I circulate it I'd like a stress-test. We just finished the later Rawls, and I've tried to write down where I actually stand on religion in public life rather than what I think I'm supposed to say. Here are my five commitments, roughly in order of how confident I am in them:

(1) I accept the duty of civility: when constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice are at stake, citizens and especially public officials should justify their positions in terms of shared political values -- freedom, equality, fair terms of cooperation -- rather than by appeal to their own comprehensive religious or philosophical doctrines. (2) I accept Rawls's later proviso: it's fine to introduce religious reasons into public political discussion, provided that in due course one also offers the public reasons that support the same conclusion. (3) I think the abolitionists and Martin Luther King Jr. are models of democratic citizenship, full stop -- their explicitly Biblical framing of their political claims was not merely excusable given their circumstances but something we should celebrate and imitate today. (4) I don't think secular citizens owe anyone an analogous restraint: someone arguing from secular humanism or utilitarianism is working from premises that are simply reasonable, not from a "comprehensive doctrine," so the duty in (1) and the proviso in (2) apply asymmetrically -- religious arguments need supplementing, secular ones don't. (5) I take the idea of an overlapping consensus seriously, which I understand to mean the state must never ask religious believers to dilute or moderate their convictions; the restraint Rawls asks for concerns only what may be offered as public justification, not what anyone may believe or preach.

My sense is that this is a coherent, recognizably Rawlsian package, but a friend skimmed it and said I can't have all five at once -- though she wouldn't say why. So: go through the set pairwise and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only look like they conflict but actually dissolve once you're careful about what Rawls is and isn't restricting. Rank any real tensions by severity, and for each one tell me precisely what would have to give -- which thesis gets revised, and what the revision costs me. If one single amendment repairs the most damage, identify it. I'd also like to know whether any of my five, taken individually, misstates the view I think I'm endorsing, since that would be an embarrassing thing to discover in front of the group.
