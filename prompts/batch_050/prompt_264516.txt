ok so I just got out of a genuinely three-hour argument with my friend Marcus about the "equality of what" debate and I'm still annoyed, so I want an outside diagnosis. The setup: I'm loosely a capabilities person, he's a resourcist in the rough Dworkinian mold, and we both spent a while dunking on welfare as a metric (expensive tastes problem, adaptive preferences, the whole greatest hits) before realizing we actually disagree with each other way more than with the welfarists. And the frustrating thing is we kept circling. He'd say the state can't be in the buisness of judging which conceptions of the good are worth supporting, so it has to hand out stuff and let people make of it what they will, and I'd say a wheelchair user and an able-bodied person with identical bundles of stuff are obviously not equal in the relevant sense, and he'd say the capability view smuggles in a controversial ranking of which functionings matter, and around we went. We both knew all the standard cases -- expensive tastes, disability compensation, the person who turns resources into misery through bad choices, the cheerful ascetic -- and neither of us moved.

Here's my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a verdict: the debate has been going for fifty years partly because the three sides aren't actually answering the same question. Welfarists are answering "what ultimately matters for a person's life going well," resourcists are answering "what can a neutral state legitimately distribute without taking sides on the good life," and capability theorists are answering "what does someone need to stand as an equal in society." If that's right, the stall isn't because the question is hard -- it's because there are three different questions wearing one label, and arguments that are decisive within one question look like non sequiturs to the other camps. Marcus thinks this is way too charitable and that there is a real substantive disagreement underneath, about whether justice should be sensitive to things beyond a person's control but indifferent to things traceable to their choices, and that all the currency talk is just a proxy war over the responsibility cut.

So: who's right about the structure here? Diagnose the disagreement -- is it verbal, is it three questions under one heading, is it really a disguised fight about responsibility, or is it bedrock intuition-pumping where each side just finds their own cases vivid? Use what the positions actually say and how the standard cases land differently depending on which question you think is being asked. And then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress -- a distinction that needs drawing, a case both sides are wrong about, a decision to just split the question -- or whether I should expect to still be having this argument with Marcus in 2065, and why.
