I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long essay about the 1966 University of Texas tower shooting. Quick recap in case the details are fuzzy: Charles Whitman killed his mother and wife the night before, then shot dozens of people from the tower, killing 14 more. In the note he left he complained about overwhelming violent impulses he couldn't understand or control and asked that his brain be examined after his death. The autopsy found a small tumor pressing against his amygdala, though experts then and now disagree about whether it actually caused anything.

Here's what I need from you, as background analysis for the essay. I want to run this case through Peter Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes from Freedom and Resentment -- the idea that our practices of blame and resentment are woven into ordinary interpersonal relationships, that we switch to the detached "objective attitude" (treating someone as an object of treatment or management rather than a participant) mainly in special cases, and that these attitudes don't need metaphysical free will to be justified. Please address:

1. How Whitman's own letter reads in Strawson's terms -- is he, before the fact, asking us to take the objective attitude toward him? Is that even something a person can coherently request about themselves within Strawson's picture, or does the letter show him still stuck in the participant stance (guilt, confusion, self-blame)?
2. What exactly the tumor discovery should change, on Strawson's view. It's tempting to say it shifts Whitman from "wicked agent" to "unfortunate patient," but Strawson is careful that excusing works in lots of ordinary, non-pathological ways too ("he didn't know," "he couldn't help it"). Does a brain tumor do different philosophical work than an ordinary excuse, or is it just one more entry on the same continuum?
3. The awkward bit: we can't verify the tumor caused the violence. So does the reactive shift depend on an empirical hypothesis that might be false? And if it does, what happens to Strawson's claim that our practices are not hostage to theoretical discoveries? This seems like a live tension for his whole approach, since neuroscience keeps finding "tumors" of one kind or another (or at least people claim it does).
4. Finally, run it backwards: does this episode confirm Strawson's framework, strain it, or show it was never really built for cases where the excusing fact is a contested piece of neurobiology rather than something we can see in ordinary social life? I want your honest assessment, not a defense.

Please be concrete about the case details I've given and don't invent new ones. End with a final line starting "Verdict:" saying, in one sentence, whether you think the Whitman case ultimately supports or undermines Strawson's account.
