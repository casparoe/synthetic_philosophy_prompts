So I write a magazine column on science and ideas, and I'm working on a longform piece about whether technology is ever really "neutral" — the usual knife-is-just-a-tool debate, but I want to actually plant a flag instead of doing both-sides mush. Before I commit, I want to stress-test my own position, because I have a sneaking suspicion I've assembled it from books that hate each other. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(a) Langdon Winner is basically right that artifacts can have politics — the design of a thing can settle political questions by itself, like his example of the low overpasses on Long Island keeping buses (and thus poor and Black New Yorkers) off the beaches, whether or not Robert Moses personally intended every bit of it. (b) But I'm also a card-carrying social constructivist about technology — I think the SCOT people are right that what an artifact means and does is fixed by the relevant social groups interpreting and using it, not by anything inside the artifact; the same bike meant different things to different groups and got redesigned until closure. (c) I think strong technological determinism is just false: technologies don't unfold by their own inner logic, history could have gone otherwise, and "the printing press caused X" talk is lazy. (d) I think Winner's stronger claim also holds — some technologies are inherently political, not just contingently so; his case is nuclear power, which he thinks demands centralized, tightly policed, basically authoritarian-adjacent management no matter what society builds it. (e) And I think the designer's intentions don't settle anything — politics can get baked in by accident, or downstream, or by whoever maintains the thing.

Are (a)–(e) a coherent package or a playlist of greatest hits that can't be played at once? Please do an actual audit, not a tour: for each pair that's genuinely in tension, say how bad it is and what exactly has to give, and for pairs that only *look* incompatible, show me why the tension dissolves rather than just waving at it. Rank the real tensions by severity — I suspect some of these are fatal and some are fine and I genuinely don't know which is which. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that makes the set consistent, what it costs me rhetorically in the piece, and whether there's a second-place revision that's pricier but might be worth it anyway. One thing I care about: don't let me keep a claim just because it's famous — if (d) is the problem, say so even though it's the line everyone rememebrs.
