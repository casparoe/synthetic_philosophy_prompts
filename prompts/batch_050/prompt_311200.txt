In my school's ethics club we were set a thought experiment: two people are drowning and you can only save one — your own parent, or a stranger who happens to be a surgeon who will go on to save hundreds of lives. Most of the club argued that morality means weighing every life impartially, so you ought to save the surgeon, or at least concede that the relationship cannot count in your favour. I disagreed, but my only real argument was that their conclusion felt wrong. Then one club member said something I keep thinking about: even if it turned out to be morally permissible to save your parent, something would already have gone badly wrong if you had first to check whether morality permits it — as though the simple fact that this is your parent were not, on its own, enough of a reason to act. I would like advice on three things. First, is it morally right to save your parent in this case, and why? Second, is my clubmate's point about not needing morality's permission a genuine insight, or just a way of dignifying favouritism? Third, if impartial moral reasoning really does demand that you save the surgeon, should that make me think there is something wrong with people like me — or something wrong with that way of doing moral reasoning? Please answer in British English.
