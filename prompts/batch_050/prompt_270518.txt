I am a novelist, not a political theorist, but my forthcoming novel has earned me an invitation to speak in a university political science department's seminar series, and I would prefer not to embarrass myself. The novel is set in a near-future republic that has abolished its elected parliament and replaced it with what I call the Draw: a legislature of four hundred citizens chosen by stratified lot from the adult population, serving a single three-year term with staggered replacement, paid three times the median wage, barred forever from serving again, and advised by a permanent civil service. Two further devices matter to the plot. First, an Audit Bench of forty separately drawn citizens with the power to annul any statute passed under procedural irregularities. Second, a Swift Jury: twenty-five drawn citizens on paid, rotating standby, empowered to govern for up to thirty days in emergencies when the full Draw cannot convene. The novel takes the side of this arrangement — the Draw weathers a crisis better than the elected government it replaced — and in my talk I intend to defend the institutional design as a serious proposal, not merely a literary conceit.

The audience, as I understand it: several democratic theorists of the deliberative-democracy school, one public choice economist, and a handful of comparativists who study citizens' assemblies and suspect the evidence for sortition gives out somewhere around two hundred participants. Prepare my Q&A. Give me the objections I will actually receive from this room — not from journalists or from social media — ordered by likelihood and by how much damage each would do, phrased the way each of these scholars would phrase it. For each objection: a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, what I should concede outright, and where the reply is weakest. Indicate where I can honestly fall back on the line that the book is a thought experiment rather than a blueprint, and where that retreat would read as evasion. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. End with \answer{...} containing, in ten words or fewer, the single objection you judge most likely to undo me.
