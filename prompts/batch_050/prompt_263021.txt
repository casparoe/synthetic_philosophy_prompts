Ok so I keep thinking about the self-torturer puzzle (the Quinn one with the dial) because it maps so cleanly onto stuff I see at work. My understanding of the setup: you've got a device on you with settings 0 to 1000. Going up one notch pays you some money, and the pain difference between any two adjacent settings is so small you can't consciously feel it. So at every single step, taking the deal looks obviously rational. But by setting 1000 you're in agony and would pay more than you ever earned to be put back to 0.

We get this exact shape at work with gradual migrations — every PR is a small local improvement, no reviewer ever has grounds to block one, and six months later the architecture is a disaster and everyones standing around asking how we got here. So part of me wants to say the self-torturer made no mistake at any step and the problem lives somewhere else, in the framework that evaluates choices one at a time.

My actual question: give me your credence, a real number, that rational preferences can be intransitive — i.e. that an agent can rationally prefer n+1 over n at every step while rationally preferring the start over the end. And decompose it: how much of your number rides on whether a difference too small to notice can ground a rational preference at all, how much on whether rationality should be assessed move-by-move or over whole sequences, and whether any stopping rule for the agent exists that isn't just a sorites line-drawing hack. Which of those sub-questions is your number most sensitive to — like, if you flipped your view on one of them, which flip moves the final number most?
