I retired three years ago after a career as an experimental biologist, and I have spent my retirement catching up on the philosophy I never had time for. Our local reading circle has just finished Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon — the novel, though some of us read the original short story years ago — and I have agreed to lead the discussion. I would like to read it as a sustained answer to, or perhaps a sustained objection against, a particular way of answering the question what intelligence is. Here is what I want the reading to address.

First, measurement. As a scientist I was struck that the novel operationalizes intelligence exactly the way psychometrics does: maze races against a mouse, IQ scores, inkblot tests. Charlie's intelligence is treated throughout as a single scalar quantity that an operation can triple and that the so-called Algernon-Gordon effect can then subtract away again, like a volume of fluid. Does the novel actually believe this? Peak-intelligence Charlie masters languages overnight and outthinks his own professors, which fits intelligence as raw processing capacity. Yet the novel keeps insisting that genius Charlie remains emotionally a child, and the most devastating scenes — the bakery coworkers who petition to get rid of him once he becomes clever, the diner scene where he catches himself laughing at the disabled busboy and is horrified — turn on capacities the tests never touch. So is the book smuggling in a distinction between measured intelligence and something like wisdom or maturity? And is that distinction coherent, or does it merely relabel everything the tests miss as not really intelligence, in a way that would make the psychometrician's position unfalsifiable? I would like that charge taken seriously, not just asserted.

Second, identity and moral standing. Pre-operative Charlie is treated by the scientists as raw material, and the novel pairs him relentlessly with Algernon — fellow subject, fellow experiment, right down to the final misspelled request that someone leave flowers on the mouse's grave. Does the novel presuppose that a person's claim on our consideration is independent of intelligence, or does it dramatize the opposite, showing that everyone around Charlie values him in proportion to his score? And is the genius Charlie even the same person as the man who wanted to become smart so that people would like him? If intelligence can be gained and lost while the person persists, it looks like an accidental property; the novel's pathos seems to rely on its being far more than that.

Third, I want resistance. The science is fantasy, and perhaps the book is really about dignity rather than about intelligence at all. Tell me honestly where my reading forces the text.

Finally: the first-person progress reports show what it is like, from the inside, to gain and lose the very capacity by which one understands one's own situation. What, if anything, does that add to the philosophy of intelligence that argument alone cannot deliver?
