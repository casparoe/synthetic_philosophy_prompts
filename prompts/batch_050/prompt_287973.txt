I'm a PhD student in political theory, and a colleague walked me into an argument last week that I keep failing to escape. (1) When a decision turns on an empirical question -- which policy will in fact produce a given outcome -- it should be made by whoever is most reliable at answering that question, other things equal. (2) We accept (1) everywhere else: we don't put drug approvals, bridge designs, or surgical techniques to a general vote, and we think it would be reckless to do so. (3) Most questions contested in ordinary elections -- the effects of tax structures, sentencing policy, central bank mandates -- are empirical in just the same way. (4) Voters are demonstrably unreliable on these questions, and the errors are systematic rather than random, so aggregation theorems that require uncorrelated mistakes don't rescue the majority. (5) So, other things equal, electoral weight should be assigned by demonstrated competence, not distributed equally. (6) Nothing in (1)–(5) depends on how unequal the underlying competence turns out to be; the conclusion follows even if the competent group is large and shifting. (7) But then universal equal suffrage is at best an approximation we tolerate, not something with any principled defense -- and my colleague says this "refutes democracy from within its own standards of rationality." I strongly suspect the move from (4) to (5), or else something smuggled into "other things equal" in (1), but I can't say precisely what's wrong, and every objection I've tried -- consent, public reason, the symbolic value of equality -- seems to just deny (5) rather than show a step is invalid. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you elsewhere -- in particular, can you keep (2)?
