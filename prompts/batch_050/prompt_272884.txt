You are a professional philosopher specializing in practical rationality and decision theory. I'd like your help thinking through a strange opportunity I've been offered, and I'd like you to reason carefully from the structure of the situation rather than pattern-matching it to a famous puzzle.

Here's my situation. A small private foundation runs what they call a "sincerity fellowship." The deal is this: on a date we agree on, I spend a day with their assessors -- interviews, physiological monitoring, the works -- and if they certify that I genuinely intend, on that day, to spend the entire following year living in a remote cabin they've chosen, with no phone, no internet, and no visitors, they transfer 400,000 euros to my account that same evening. The contract is explicit and they've shown me past winners who never went: the moment I'm certified, the money is unconditionally mine, and whether I actually spend the year in the cabin is legally and reputationally irrelevant. They don't want me to go; they want to study people who can sincerely commit without any enforcement.

My problem is that I'm pretty sure I can't lose by trying, and yet I can't seem to actually do the thing. When I imagine the assessment day, part of me is already thinking about the evening afterward, about how I'd never really go to that cabin -- and I suspect the assessors will detect exactly that. I could genuinely go if I had to; if the money required going, I'd sign today. But forming the real intention now, knowing I'll be free to drop it an hour after I'm paid, feels like trying to jump while knowing there's a net. My brother says I'm overthinking: just want the money, and the intention will follow. My partner says the fact that I can't simply choose to intend shows something deep about what intentions are.

Before answering, brainstorm briefly and privately about what makes this case hard -- whether the difficulty is psychological, logical, or about what rationality even demands of me. Then give me your considered advice as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: your verdict on whether a rational person in my position could form the intention, why the money itself can't seem to do the motivating work, whether my brother or my partner has the better diagnosis, what strategy (if any) you'd actually recommend I try on assessment day, and what my predicament reveals about the relationship between intending and expecting to act.
