I'm a journalist covering physics-and-ideas type stories, and I'm working on a piece about whether time really "flows." A physicist I interviewed emailed me afterwards with an argument he says settles it, and I can't find the hole but the conclusion is nuts. Here's his argument roughly as he gave it:

(1) Special relativity says two observers moving relative to each other disagree about which distant events are happening "right now," and neither one is wrong.
(2) If presentism were true -- i.e. only the present exists -- there'd have to be an observer-independent fact about what exists right now. Relativity says there isn't one.
(3) So presentism is false and eternalism is true: past and future events exist just as much as present ones.
(4) If the past exists just as much as the present, then events in it are real in exactly the same sense as current events.
(5) My grandmother's being alive in 1962 is an event in the past.
(6) So my grandmother is alive, full stop -- she's just alive "over there" in 1962, the way Paris is "over there" across the Atlantic.
(7) Therefore death is an illusion, grief is a category error, and every obituary I've ever written is false. Nobody has ever died.

He admits (7) sounds flip but says it's the honest consequence of the physics, and my editor of course loves it. But it seems to prove way too much. If (6) works, then by the same symmetry future events are also already real, so the heat death of the universe is "over there" too, and whatever I do next week is already sitting there waiting, so nothing I do matters either. I'd like to tell him where this goes wrong. My hunch is the slip happens somewhere between (4) and (6) but I can't articulate why, and I also don't want to concede (1)-(3) too fast if those are doing work they shouldn't be. Which step should I push on, why did it look fine, and what does each possible fix cost me -- especially if I want to keep the physics intact and still get to say my grandmother died?
