ok so my roommate read a bunch of Dennett over the summer and now he's got this argument he thinks shows that Darwinism eats itself, and its been bugging me for like two weeks. He starts from this passage in chapter IV of On the Origin of Species, where Darwin is comparing natural selection to a human breeder:

"As man can produce and certainly has produced a great result by his methodical and unconscious means of selection, what may not nature effect? Man can act only on external and visible characters: nature cares nothing for appearances, except in so far as they may be useful to any being. She can act on every internal organ, on every shade of constitutional difference, on the whole machinery of life. Man selects only for his own good; Nature only for that of the being which she tends."

Then he argues:

1. If a mindless process reliably produces everything a comprehending, goal-directed agent would produce, then appealing to comprehension in explaining those products is idle — you'd be positing a cause that adds nothing.
2. Per the passage, natural selection is exactly such a process: it does everything an intelligent breeder does — selects for a good, attends to every shade of difference, works on the whole machinery of life — only better, and without any mind behind it.
3. So comprehension is explanatorily idle wherever selection can do the work.
4. Human brains, and the reasoning they produce, are among the things selection built. So scientific reasoning — Darwin's, Dennett's, my roommate's — falls under (3) too.
5. If comprehension is idle everywhere, then nobody's theory is accepted because anyone comprehended the reasons for it; it's accepted (when it is) because selection happened to build machines disposed to say that sort of thing.
6. But then Darwin's or Dennett's asserting the theory gives us no comprehension-based reason to believe it.
7. So the whole "competence without comprehension" picture is either false or self-defeating: if it's true, no one could ever comprehend that it's true, or have a genuine reason to accept it.

The conclusion seems insane to me — if this argument worked it would prove way too much, since basically any naturalistic theory of mind would self-destruct the same way and we'd have to conclude that no scientific reasoning is ever trustworthy. But I genuinly cant find the bad step. Every individual move looks small and reasonable, and (2) is honestly just paraphrasing Darwin.

For what it's worth, the two things I'd most like to keep are (a) that natural selection really does explain apparent design without a designer, and (b) that my reasons for believing things can be actual reasons rather than just dispositions installed by selection.
