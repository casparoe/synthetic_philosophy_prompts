I belong to a philosophy reading group that recently worked through the chapter of John Stuart Mill's An Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy in which Mill explains how each of us comes to believe that other people have minds. His reasoning, as we understood it, runs roughly as follows: in my own case I experience a uniform sequence in which certain states of my body are followed by feelings, and those feelings in turn produce outward expressions and actions; when I observe other human beings, I perceive only the bodily states and the outward behavior, never the feelings themselves; but because their bodies are like mine, and because their outward acts resemble the acts that in my own case I know from experience to be caused by feelings, I infer that they too have feelings like mine. Two members of the group, Marta and Jonas, ended up in a sustained dispute about whether this argument succeeds, and they asked me to referee. I honestly could not settle it, so I am bringing it here. The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went like this.

(1) Marta opened by arguing that Mill's inference is an induction from a single case that cannot even in principle be checked or extended: the correlation between outward behavior and inner feeling is observed in exactly one instance, namely one's own, and no one can ever observe another person's feelings to confirm the generalization. An analogy whose entire evidential base is one case, she said, is not a weak induction but no induction at all, so if this is the best we have, the belief in other minds is simply unjustified. (2) Jonas replied that this misreads the argument's logic: Mill's inference is causal rather than enumerative, resting on the uniformity of nature — like antecedents have like consequents — which is the same principle behind everyday inferences to unobserved cases, such as expecting this fire to burn or assuming that other people's bodies digest food much as ours do. If the single-case objection defeats Mill, it defeats most of our causal reasoning about the unobserved, and so it proves too much. (3) Marta answered that the causal reading inherits the same defect, since a law connecting bodies with feelings could only ever be established where both relata are observable, and that remains exactly one case; ordinary causal generalizations rest on many instances or at least could gather more, whereas this one is confined to a sample of one as a matter of principle. Worse, ordinary inductions are correctable by failed predictions, but nothing could ever count as a failed prediction here, because the predicted feelings are unobservable — so the analogy is uniquely weak, not ordinarily weak. (4) Jonas made two points in response: first, that Marta conflates establishing a general law with applying the uniformity principle, and that her standard would make solipsism the rational default position, which is absurd, especially since the conclusion only ever needed probability rather than certainty; second, that Mill's argument explains something her critique cannot, namely our graded confidence — near-certainty about fellow humans, real but lesser confidence about apes and dogs, hesitation about insects — which tracks bodily and behavioral similarity just as an analogical inference predicts it should, and a merely failed argument would not explain that pattern. (5) Marta's final move was to say that the gradedness is precisely the problem: an argument whose force fades with similarity can never say where minds end, whether at the octopus or at a sufficiently sophisticated artificial system, and a criterion that falls silent exactly in the hard cases is no criterion at all. There the argument stalled, with each convinced the other had missed the point.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, with a brief justification for your verdict. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing — for instance, whether they share an assumption about what an adequate treatment of other minds would have to deliver, and whether that assumption is itself questionable. Finally, give an overall verdict: who is more nearly right about Mill's argument as it stands, and is the disagreement perhaps better understood as the two of them demanding different things from the argument rather than one of them simply erring about its merits?
