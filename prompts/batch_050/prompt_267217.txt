I chair the model risk committee of a mid-sized insurer that underwrites novel medical device designs, and I've been a reader of rationalist blogs and forums for long enough that I thought I understood the philosophy of priors. Then it landed on my desk in actuarial form. For genuinely new device categories we sometimes have no field failure data at all, and our underwriting rules still require a default probability that the true long-run failure rate θ exceeds the contractual threshold that triggers premium surcharges. Two analysts, given the same total absence of data, produced wildly different defaults. Analyst A put a flat distribution on θ over [0, 1], reasoning that complete ignorance means every failure rate is equally likely. Analyst B put a flat distribution on log θ, reasoning that we are exactly as ignorant of whether the rate is one-in-a-hundred versus one-in-a-thousand as we are of whether it is one-in-ten versus one-in-a-hundred, and that an "ignorance prior" should be indifferent across orders of magnitude. For the thresholds that matter to us, these two distributions imply surcharge decisions that disagree in both directions. Both analysts are competent, both can gesture at Bertrand's old paradox about chords in a circle, and neither can refute the other.

The committee has fractured into three camps and I need to bring a written opinion to next month's meeting. The first camp says we should simply legislate a convention: flat on θ, full stop, on the grounds that the failure rate is the "natural" quantity the contract is written in, and that any consistent convention is better than case-by-case wrangling. The second camp says the whole episode shows that any single parametrization is arbitrary, and wants our policy to require robustness instead: an underwriting conclusion is only certifiable if it survives across a declared family of plausible ignorance priors, with models whose decisions flip being sent back or priced as uninsurable. The third camp wants us to adopt a formal rule as the standing standard — Jeffreys priors, reference priors, maximum entropy, something with a derivation — so that the choice is made once, in general, rather than ad hoc per submission. Each camp can see the others' weaknesses and none will budge.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting: a clear recommendation for one of these three policies (or a principled hybrid), stated as an actionable bottom line our analysts could actually implement — i.e., what the policy concretely requires of the next submission like this one. The memo should take seriously the strongest objection to whichever policy you recommend: if you side with convention, you must answer the charge that the convention is unprincipled and merely hides the arbitrariness; if you side with robustness, you must answer the charge that "a family of plausible priors" just reintroduces the same underdetermination one level up, and that the policy may make sparse-data underwriting impossible; if you side with a formal rule, you must answer the charge that Jeffreys and maximum-entropy constructions depend on a choice of data model or constraints, which is arguably the same choice wearing a derivation. Finally, the memo should take a position on what the Bertrand problem actually shows: that the very idea of representing ignorance by a probability distribution is incoherent, or that "ignorance" is only ever well defined relative to a parametrization and a decision problem — and what follows for us if the latter. Aim for something a committee of non-philosophers can vote on.
