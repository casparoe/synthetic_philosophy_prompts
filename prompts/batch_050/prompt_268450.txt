I write historical fiction, and I have been brooding over a chapter of my own drafting in which a sixteenth-century character has begun, against my intentions, to read like a tolerant liberal born before his time — a sure sign, on Herbert Butterfield's diagnosis of the Whig interpretation of history, that I have started arranging the past as a progress toward us, sorting the dead into those who helped build the present and those who stood in its way. The corrective I instinctively reach for, however, tempts me in the opposite direction: a declension narrative, in which the past possessed some integrity — of belief, of community, of craft — that the present has squandered, and the story runs downhill toward us rather than up. I would like to understand whether these are genuinely two temptations or a single one wearing two expressions.

Please compare the Whig interpretation with the declension narrative. Are they the same error with the sign reversed — both treating the past as a trajectory whose meaning is fixed by the present — or do they rest on different presuppositions about time, agency, and value? Consider, in particular, whether the Whig storyteller must believe in moral progress while the declensionist need only believe in loss; whether one temptation flatters the reader and the other flatters the writer; and where exactly the line runs between explaining how the present in fact came about, which any narrative of the past must do, and writing as though the past had been aiming at us all along. Finally, as a matter of the novelist's craft rather than the historian's conscience: which of the two is harder to escape in a genre that must make long-dead characters matter to living readers, and what habits of composition would amount to genuine resistance rather than to the opposite temptation in disguise?
