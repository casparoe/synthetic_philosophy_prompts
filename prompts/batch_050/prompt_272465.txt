I am a retired experimental physicist who, over the past two years, has been working my way through philosophy with the same doggedness I once brought to the laboratory. Lately I have been preoccupied with semantic anti-realism in the tradition associated with Dummett: the view that truth cannot outrun what could, in principle, be known or warranted. I confess the motivation appeals to me. A physical quantity to which no possible measurement could ever be sensitive strikes me as suspect, and I am inclined to extend the same suspicion to truths that no possible inquiry could ever reach.

But I have also been working through Fitch's knowability result, and as far as I can tell it is a wrecking ball aimed at exactly this position. Before I abandon the view, I want to see whether a serious defender can save it. That is where you come in.

Please play a committed, sophisticated defender of the knowability principle — sharp, not a strawman. You may concede that the principle needs restriction or reformulation, and you may grant small technical points, but you must never concede the core claim that truth is epistemically constrained and that radically verification-transcendent truth is unintelligible. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say "stop," and you may ask me at most one clarifying question per reply if you need it. Keep every reply to a single paragraph of at most 180 words.

Here is my opening move. Take your principle: every truth is knowable. Now consider some truth p that, as it happens, nobody knows. Your principle then implies that it is knowable that p is true and nobody knows it. But knowledge is factive and distributes over a conjunction: anyone who knew that conjunction would have to know p while also knowing that nobody knows p, which is impossible. So the conjunction is unknowable, contradicting your principle, and the only remaining option — that there are no unknown truths — is absurd. As a physicist I read this as a no-go theorem, not a mere puzzle. So tell me: which step of the derivation do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? And if your answer is to restrict the principle, explain why the restricted version is still strong enough to do the work you originally wanted — ruling out verification-transcendent truth — rather than a retreat that leaves the realist everything he wanted.
