I spent forty years as an experimental physicist and, since retiring, have been reading my way into philosophy of science. I now co-run a small seminar for fellow retired scientists and engineers — people with strong technical instincts but no philosophical training — and I have agreed to lead our session on Kuhnian incommensurability. I understand the material well enough; what I want from you is a plan for teaching it, not another exposition of it.

My audience will arrive with a picture I would like disrupted carefully rather than merely contradicted: that science is cumulative, that later theories straightforwardly contain earlier ones as limiting cases, and that talk of scientists "living in different worlds" is poetic overreach. Please lay out the session as follows.

First, the dependency structure. In what order must the components be introduced — I am thinking of exemplars, the theory-ladenness of observation, normal versus revolutionary science, and the claim that key terms change meaning across a revolution — and why does that order matter? Which of these cannot be understood before another is already in place, and which ordering mistake do people typically make when presenting this material?

Second, the carrying example. I need one historical case to bear most of the weight. Candidates I have considered are the phlogiston-to-oxygen transition, the shift in the meaning of "mass" between Newtonian and relativistic mechanics, and the Ptolemaic-to-Copernican change in what counts as a planet. Recommend one and justify it: what must the example have (a term my audience already uses confidently, so the meaning-shift is felt rather than asserted?) and what must it lack (contemporary controversy, for instance, or mathematics beyond my audience)? Say also why you would reject the other two.

Third, the misconception map. I expect at least the following to surface: that incommensurability means total untranslatability and therefore no rational comparison between paradigms; that Kuhn is claiming theory choice is mere mob psychology or fashion; that meaning-change is just scientists being sloppy with words; and that the whole thesis is refuted by the obvious instrumental success of modern science. For each, tell me where in the sequence it will predictably arise, whether to head it off preemptively or let it be voiced first, and what specifically to say at that point — including the distinction between incomparability and non-cumulativeness, which I find is where even careful readers slip.

Fourth, a closing check for understanding. Give me one question or short exercise — not a request to recite the thesis — whose answer would show that the seminar has grasped why rational comparison can survive meaning-change, rather than merely having absorbed the slogan.

Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to say I am leaving out: the later refinements around taxonomic categories, the comparison with Feyerabend, the strong social-constructivist readings, or whatever else you judge will muddy rather than deepen the first pass.
