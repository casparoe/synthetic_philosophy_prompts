So about six months ago I found out that when most people say they "picture" a beach they apparently mean it literally, and I don't. Classic aphantasia situation -- I assumed "counting sheep" was a metaphor until my partner looked at me like I was an alien. Anyway, I'm a TA for an upper-level philosophy of mind / cognitive science seminar and I get to write one question for the final this year, and obviously I want it on the imagery debate (Kosslyn vs. Pylyshyn).

Here's my problem: every exam question I've seen on this topic is basically "describe the debate, who won?" and students just recite the picture metaphor vs. propositions thing and mention the mental scanning and rotation experiments. I want something they can't answer from the lecture summary. Students will have ~75 minutes.

My idea, roughly, but improve it: the question should put aphantasia to work. Something like -- people with aphantasia report having no conscious imagery at all, yet many can still solve rotation-type and "which is darker, the green of grass or the green of a pine tree" type problems, often just slower. What does that do to the debate? Does it vindicate Pylyshyn's line that what matters is propositional/tacit knowledge rather than any picture-like format? Does Kosslyn have a response (e.g., imagery as a dissociable component, or subpersonal depictive processing without conscious experience)? Or does the case actually fail to discriminate between the two, and if so, what WOULD discriminate -- what would an experiment have to show for the format question (depictive vs. propositional representations) to actually be settled rather than just showing that some imagery-ish processing is going on?

Please write:

1. The actual exam question, phrased the way it would appear on the paper. It should force students to engage the tacit knowledge / cognitive penetrability objection properly, not just describe it, and it should make clear that "aphantasia proves Kosslyn wrong" and "aphantasia is irrelevant" are both too easy.
2. A model answer of about 600-700 words -- what you'd want a genuinely good student to write.
3. A marking sceme out of 20, with maybe five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and what seperates an A answer from a B answer in terms of specific moves (e.g., does the student notice that the debate can't be settled by behavioral data alone without some bridging claim about format, vs. just asserting which side aphantasia supports).
4. One line per must-hit point on how students typically get it wrong, so my co-TAs and I mark consistently.

One constraint: don't make the question depend on any specific paper or experiment by name -- students should be able to answer it from the ideas, since half of them won't remeber which study showed what anyway.
