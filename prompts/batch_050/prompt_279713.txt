Construct a thought experiment that tests Boethius’s solution to the problem of divine foreknowledge by separating temporal order from explanatory order. Use an ordinary protagonist facing a morally significant choice—for example, whether to report a colleague’s misconduct—and two infallible representations of what she will do:

1. a document produced yesterday whose content would remain fixed even if she were to choose otherwise; and  
2. an eternal view of the whole timeline, not located before the choice, such that had she chosen otherwise, the view would have had a different content.

Neither representation may causally influence her, coerce her, or merely amount to an extremely reliable prediction. Make the eternal view’s mode of access intelligible through an analogy without reducing it to a belief that mysteriously adjusts itself after the fact, and explicitly flag where the analogy breaks down.

After presenting the scenario in no more than 300 words, give Boethius’s verdicts on three questions: whether the choice is necessary simpliciter; whether it is necessary only conditionally, given that it is timelessly seen; and whether the divine seeing causes the choice. Distinguish necessity simpliciter—her lacking any relevant power to do otherwise—from conditional necessity—the impossibility of both seeing her act and her not performing it. Then contrast these verdicts with those appropriate to yesterday’s fixed document.

Finally, identify which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work, especially any appeal to counterfactual dependence; explain whether the distinction survives if divine knowledge is instead located in the past; and present the strongest objection that Boethius has merely replaced problematic foreknowledge with an equally choice-fixing timeless actuality. If these specifications depend on an incoherent conception of timeless access, say so directly and revise the task rather than politely completing an impossible one.
