You are a poet with serious training in analytic philosophy. Write a villanelle — nineteen lines, two repeating refrains, the usual ABA scheme — that enacts Gilbert Ryle's critique of Cartesian dualism rather than merely describing it. The poem's narrative spine should be his famous example: a visitor who is shown the colleges, libraries, museums, and playing fields of a university and then asks where the university itself is, mistaking it for one more item on the tour rather than the way all those buildings are organized and used. The visitor of the poem is instead touring a human life — the habits, the skilled performances, the hesitations and recoveries, the public conduct — and keeps asking where the mind is hidden behind or inside all of it. Let the villanelle's two refrains do the philosophical work: the same demand for a hidden occupant should keep returning, each recurrence slightly more strained, so that the poem's form itself performs the mistake of treating a person's doings as evidence of a further, occult thing that does them. In the closing quatrain, do not refute dualism with an argument; let the question quietly lose its grip, the way a question does when one finally sees how the pieces were already in view. Before composing, spend a few sentences brainstorming which concrete images of intelligent conduct (playing chess, telling a joke, catching a mistake mid-sentence) best resist being read as mere outward signs of an inner theater. Someone who knows Ryle should recognize the argument's exact shape; someone who doesn't should simply get a good poem.
