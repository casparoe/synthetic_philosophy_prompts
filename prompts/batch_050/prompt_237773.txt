I've been going around in circles on consequentialist cluelessness while drafting a blog post and I need a consistency check on my own position before I commit to it in public. Here's what I currently hold:

(a) Plain act consequentialism: an act is right iff no available alternative has better total consequences, impartially weighed.

(b) Under uncertainty, the way to live by (a) is to maximize expected value.

(c) Cluelessness is real: pretty much everything I do ramifies into the far future (who meets whom, which kids get born, tiny shifts in how history goes), those effects swamp the near-term ones in scale, and I can't even assign them a sign.

(d) Despite (c), the everyday verdicts stand: donating to a well-vetted charity beats a beach vacation, not lying beats lying, and driving to the shops is morally fine even though it reroutes traffic and thereby eventually changes who gets conceived.

(e) A wrongness verdict that no one could ever in principle have evidence for isn't a real obligation. Morality has to be usable by the creatures it's for.

(f) If a consequence is genuinely unpredictable in sign — no information either way — it can't turn an otherwise good act into a wrong one. Symmetric ignorance cancels itself out.

Which of these are actually in tension and which only look like it? Rank the real tensions by how much damage they do, and for each one say what would have to give and roughly what that costs — I'm honestly not attached to any particular piece, so don't spare any of them. If some pair only conflicts because I'm conflating two levels of theory (or running a decision rule against a standard of rightness), say that explicitly rather than treating it as a genuine problem. And tell me straight out if the framing itself is off — e.g. if (c) as I've stated it is stronger than the actual cluelessness worry, or if (f) is quietly doing more work than it can justify — I'd rather hear that than get a polite reshuffle of the list.
