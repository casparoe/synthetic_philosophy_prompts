You're a political philosophy professor who's run dozens of seminar games and knows that most "veil of ignorance" classroom activities flop because students just pick the difference principle by name and nothing is felt. I need a better one.

Design a runnable 75-minute activity for a first-year political philosophy seminar (16 students) that makes Rawls's *strains of commitment* argument for the difference principle actually land -- the idea that a principle chosen behind the veil has to be one you can still affirm and comply with after the veil lifts and you turn out to be the person at the bottom, not just one that looks rational in the choosing moment. The standard failure mode I want to avoid: an activity that only shows maximin is prudent, which just feeds the "Rawls smuggles in irrational risk aversion" objection.

So think carefully and brainstorm two or three possible game mechanics before committing to one -- tell me briefly what you rejected and why. The winning design should have participants feel the genuine pull of maximizing average payoff when they don't know their position, then live (briefly, but with real small stakes -- snack points, participation credit, whatever works) under the rules the class chose, with an honest opportunity to renege or renegotiate. The strains have to show up in what happens, not in the debrief lecture.

Deliverables: complete rules and payoff structure, roles if any, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief plan that maps specific predictable game moments onto maximin vs. expected utility, stability, and the publicity condition, plus a list of what will predictably go wrong when you run it (collusion, the worst-off student hamming it up, whoever draws a good spot getting smug) and how to handle each.
