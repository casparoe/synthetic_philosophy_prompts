I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine (long features, coffee-table maths, that sort of thing), and I'm drafting a piece on why you can't just build a "fair" algorithm. It's built around the cluster of impossibility results showing that when two groups have different base rates, you generally can't have calibration within groups AND equal false positive rates AND equal false negative rates at once, short of perfect prediction. My editor reckons readers will glaze over unless each result is anchored to how much it should actually worry a practioner, so here's what I need.

The running example for the piece: a mid-size lender deploys a default-risk scorer. Its two applicant groups genuinely default at different rates (say 8% vs 15%), the model is decently predictive in both groups, and the regulator cares about both calibration and error-rate parity.

Three things:

1. Rate the following results on two scales I've invented for the piece: "headline risk" (0-10, how awful it sounds to a lay reader when it bites) and "wiggle room" (0-10, how much a competent team can design around it, where 10 = loads of room). Two or three sentences of justification per number, please -- the numbers matter less than the reasoning.
(a) calibration vs equalised odds: can't both hold when base rates differ;
(b) equal false positive rates vs equal false negative rates: conflict with each other unless predictions are perfect or base rates match;
(c) fairness gerrymandering: passing a fairness check on every subgroup you've thought to test guarantees nothing about intersections of those subgroups or ones you didn't check.

2. My editor has proposed what he calls a "compromise metric": enforce calibration on the top-scoring half of applicants and equalised odds on the bottom half, his theory being that each constraint bites hardest in a different part of the distribution. Rate this dodge 0-10 on "does it actually escape the theorems" and tell me precisely where it breaks. I suspect it doesn't escape at all, but I want the exact mechanism, not a vibes-based dismissal.

3. Which of the three results should I lead the piece with for educated non-specialists, and which should I bury or cut entirely? A paragraph on the ordering.

Two ground rules: don't tell me the answer is "pick whichever metric matches your values" -- every reader has heard that by now -- and do flag where the theorems' assumptions (fixed base rates, equally informative scores across groups) are doing the heavy lifting, since that's where practitioners push back.
