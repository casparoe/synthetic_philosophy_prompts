I defend the constitution view of material objects: the statue and the lump of clay that constitute it are two numerically distinct things that coincide — they share all their parts and occupy exactly the same region, but differ in their persistence conditions, since the lump survives being squashed and the statue does not. I take this to beat the alternatives: it avoids the perdurantist's temporal parts, the eliminativist's denial that statues exist, and Burke-style dominance sortals, which strike me as saying that whether a lump constitutes a statue depends on which sortal happens to be dominant, something I find arbitrary.

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure. What would have to be true for it to be false? Work through the obvious pressure points — the grounding problem (what, given that statue and lump share every part and every spatiotemporal and categorical property, grounds their alleged modal and normative differences?), the counting problem (how many things are on the pedestal, and what principled answer can the view give?), and the worry that constitution without coincidence collapses into identity — but also go further: which results in neighboring areas, such as the logic of parthood, the semantics of sortal terms, or general theories of persistence, would vindicate or undermine it? For each candidate defeater, tell me whether a committed constitution theorist would concede it as evidence against the view or dismiss it as a mere restatement of the view's commitments, and whether that dismissiveness is principled or defensive. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly whether the view, as its defenders now formulate it (with, e.g., the claim that constitution is an irreducible, sui generis relation), still risks anything at all — and if it has been insulated against refutation, whether that is a vice for a metaphysical position of this kind or simply what mature metaphysics looks like.
