I spent about thirty years as an entomologist (mostly on Diptera population dynamics, if that means anything to you) and since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole, as one does. The argument that won't leave me alone is the scale argument for taking insect welfare seriously: there are on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any moment, so even if the probability that they're sentient is fairly low, the expected amount of suffering in insects could swamp everything else we worry about. I find this deeply unsettling, partly because I ran experiments on fruit flies for decades without losing a wink of sleep over it.

I've been collecting the standard replies people give for why the scale argument might not work, and I think I have most of them: (1) the fanaticism worry -- expected value reasoning goes haywire with tiny probabilities and enormous stakes, so we shouldn't let the arithmetic push us around; (2) the deep uncertainty reply -- the "probability of sentience" isn't an ordinary credence grounded in frequencies or models, so the expected value calculation isn't just small, it's ill-posed; (3) the attack on the premise -- the behavioral evidence (nociception with motivational trade-offs, associative learning, that play behavior in bees) arguably puts the probability well above "tiny," so the real dispute is about evidence, not arithmetic; (4) the dimness discount -- even if insects have valenced experience, it may be so faint or fragmentary that badness doesn't scale linearly with headcount, so multiplying by 10^18 is a mistake; (5) sign uncertainty -- if wild insects are sentient their short, mostly-miserable lives might be net bad, so more insects could be worse, and we're clueless about which direction interventions push; and (6) threshold views about aggregation -- on some views lots of tiny pains never add up to one big bad thing, no matter how many there are.

Here's what I want: one more reply to the scale argument that is genuinely different from these six. Not a rewording of one of them, not a special case (e.g. "sign uncertainty but for farmed crickets"), and not just two of them stapled together. It can draw on biology, decision theory, or philosophy of mind -- as a retired scientist I can follow technical material, so don't dumb it down. And after giving it, tell me honestly whether you think it's any good, and which of the six it's closest to if it had to sit next to one of them.
