I am a PhD student in machine learning working on scalable oversight, and I am trying to get clear on what exactly HCH (Humans Consulting HCH), as it figures in the iterated amplification literature, is supposed to be an idealization of. The concept seems to be asked to play two roles at once. On one reading, it is a stand-in for what a human would conclude given arbitrarily much time and cognitive labor — a limit of deliberation, useful as a conceptual target for alignment. On the other reading, it is defined operationally as a recursion of ordinary, time-limited humans, each answering a small question in isolation, with no view of the computation as a whole.

These two characterizations appear to pull apart, and I would like a careful explanation of whether and how they can be reconciled. Three points in particular puzzle me.

First, the deliberative reading. A single human reasoning for a very long time retains memory, revises earlier judgments in light of later ones, and gradually builds understanding. No node in the HCH tree can do any of these things locally; whatever reflection occurs must be encoded in the structure of the decomposition itself. Is the idea that global recursion substitutes for local memory — that the way questions are factored plays the functional role that re-reading one's own notes plays in ordinary deliberation? If so, what precisely has to be true of the decomposition for that substitution to go through, and is that a condition we could ever verify from inside the tree, given that every verification is itself just another node?

Second, the status of the factored cognition assumption. Is the claim that hard questions can be decomposed into sub-questions whose answers compose into a correct answer to the whole an empirical hypothesis about cognition, an engineering desideratum, or simply a definitional feature of the idealization? Consider questions whose difficulty seems to consist precisely in seeing how the parts hang together — gestalt-heavy problems in, say, philosophy or mathematics. If the compositionality assumption fails for such questions, does HCH merely become unsafe for them, or does it fail to be well-defined for them — is there still a fact of the matter about what the limit computes?

Third, where the alignment is supposed to live. The intuitive appeal of the construction is that the root human's values are inherited by the entire tree. But each node sees only its own question, so fidelity of value-transmission depends entirely on each node decomposing its question in a way faithful to the intent behind it — and that faithfulness is itself judged, if at all, only by other nodes of the same kind. Is there a regress here, and if so, where is it supposed to bottom out? In the root human's unaided judgment at the leaves?

Please treat this as a question about the idealization itself rather than about the training schemes (iterated amplification, debate) that take HCH as a target — though I would welcome, in closing, a remark on whether the idealization, once clarified along the lines above, is even the right kind of thing to serve as such a target, or whether its role is better understood as a thought experiment about the structure of deliberation.
