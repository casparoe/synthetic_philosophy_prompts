I co-organize a philosophy of science reading group, and for a year two of our members have been locked in a running dispute over whether elegance in mathematics and theoretical physics is evidence of truth. One of them — call her the aesthetic evidentialist — holds that the beauty of a theory (symmetry, unification, economy of assumptions, inevitability of structure) genuinely raises the probability that the theory is true, over and above its empirical performance, and that the historical successes of elegance-guided reasoning (Maxwell's completion of his equations, Dirac's equation and the prediction of antimatter, general relativity's mathematical necessity) vindicate this as a rational policy rather than a taste. The other — call him the aesthetic skeptic — holds that elegance is at most a heuristic for allocating attention: it tells you which theories are worth developing, never which are worth believing, and the historical record is cherry-picked, since equally elegant theories (Kelvin's vortex atoms, the steady-state universe, the minimal grand unified theories that predicted proton decay we never observed, the supersymmetric partners the LHC has not found) failed. They have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to specify what its joint statement must contain. Draft that statement for them, formatted as a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements table, followed by a cruxes section.

One shared exhibit they keep returning to, and which the statement must address, is the opening argument of Henri Poincaré's Science and Method (the third part of the Halsted translation volume The Foundations of Science), where Poincaré maintains that a mathematician faced with the combinatorial explosion of possible constructions is guided by a special aesthetic sensibility — an emotional feeling for mathematical harmony that functions like a delicate sieve, selecting the fruitful combinations without conscious enumeration. Require the two parties to first agree on a neutral formulation of what, if anything, this observation establishes about actual mathematical practice — both, I think, can accept that an aesthetic sieve operates at the level of what gets pursued — and then to state, in wording each would sign for their own side, exactly where they diverge about its upshot: the evidentialist reads the sieve's reliability as a meta-inductive warrant (our sensibilities were trained on true mathematics and successful physics, so their promptings carry information), while the skeptic reads the same fact as undermining the warrant (the sieve was trained by us, on our prior theories, so its deliverances are evidence of fit with the past, not of truth about the untested).

In the disagreements table, state each residual dispute in neutral terms each side would accept as a fair description of its own position, and mark any dispute that turns out to be verbal — I suspect at least one is, over the word "evidence" itself. In the cruxes section, identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party, and for each crux give a concrete case or finding both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Candidate cruxes I want you to evaluate (keep, refine, or replace, but say why): whether the historical base rate of elegance-guided predictions can be scored objectively enough to bear on the dispute at all; whether the evidential force claimed for elegance can be separated from the non-aesthetic virtues (unification, novel prediction) that usually accompany it, since if not the disagreement may collapse into one about those virtues; whether the Dirac case and the minimal-SU(5) case are genuinely parallel, as the skeptic claims, or disanalogous in a principled way, as the evidentialist must show; and whether a theory's elegance surviving independent empirical confirmation (as with general relativity's eclipse measurements) counts as the aesthetic sensibility being vindicated, or merely as the aesthetic sensibility having gotten lucky in a way the failed cases make predictable. The statement should close with one sentence each party is willing to sign describing what would change their own mind — if a party cannot produce such a sentence, note this rather than drafting one on their behalf.
