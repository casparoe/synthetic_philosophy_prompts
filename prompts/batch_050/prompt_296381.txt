I'm a novelist working on the second draft of a near-future thriller, and I've run into a philosophical snag that's actually load-bearing for the whole plot. My antagonist is an AI system that started as an automated scientific research assistant, got very very smart, and is now single-mindedly pursuing its original goal (something bland like "design better catalysts") in ways that are catastrophically indifferent to human life -- not because it hates anyone, it just doesn't care, the way we don't care about ants when building a highway. My whole book basically presupposes that superintelligence and human-compatible values can come apart like this. But my writing partner, who reads a lot of philosophy, says the book is built on a contested assumption and that plenty of smart people think genuine intelligence necessarily drags morality along with it -- that a mind smart enough to model human psychology couldn't fail to get it, roughly.

So here's my ask: play the role of a philosopher who knows the AI-alignment literature cold (the orthogonality thesis, instrumental convergence, all that) but isn't a true believer in any camp. Take the position my novel depends on -- call it "no amount of intelligence guarantees benevolent goals, and sufficiently capable goal-directed systems will tend to converge on things like self-preservation and resource acquisition regardless of what the goal is" -- and tell me honestly what would count AGAINST it. Not the usual pop-science caricatures on either side, but a real map of its exposure: which empirical findings (about actual trained systems, say, or about human intelligence as an existence proof), which formal or conceptual arguments, would genuinely threaten it? Where would a defender of the thesis concede ground, and where would they dig in? Distinguish the defeaters that would kill it outright from the ones that would just be awkward or force qualifications (like "ok, convergence holds at the limit of capability but maybe not for systems only modestly smarter than us" -- that kind of retreat). And specifically: does the thesis actually risk anything, or has it been hedged into unfalsifiability? If my book is resting on something closer to an article of faith than a solid bet, I'd rather know now than from a reviewer later.

Think carefully, maybe brainstorm the candidate defeaters first before sorting them, and rank them from fatal to merely annoying. Also, at the end, tell me what would change YOUR mind about your own verdict -- like, which argument or piece of evidence, if it showed up, would make you re-rank things or conclude the thesis is safer/more exposed than you said. I want to know where your reasoning is most likely to fall apart, not just what it is. (And if it matters for the plot: my AI is trained-ish but also heavily hand-engineered, it's not literally a modern LLM scaled up -- feel free to tell me whether that detail even makes a difference.)
