I'm part of a graduate reading group currently working through the Rawls–Harsanyi exchange on choice behind the veil of ignorance, and at last week's meeting a mathematically inclined friend announced that a large part of that debate rests on a bookkeeping error. His claim was that maximin and minimax regret — two rules that are usually presented as rivals for decision under genuine ignorance — are in fact the same rule described twice, so that any dispute over which of them a rational agent in the original position would follow is empty. His argument strikes me as proving far too much, but I have been unable to locate the exact misstep. It ran as follows:

(1) If two decision rules rank the available options identically in every possible choice problem, then the question of which one is the rational rule is merely verbal. (2) Utility is measurable only up to a positive affine transformation: no rational decision rule may change its recommendation when all payoffs are multiplied by a positive constant or have a constant added to them, since such operations only redescribe the same underlying preferences in different units. (3) The regret table is constructed from the original payoff table by subtracting, within each state of nature, the best payoff achievable in that state — and subtracting a constant is an affine transformation with positive slope. (4) By (2) and (3), the regret table is simply a redescription of the original choice problem, so no rational rule can recommend one action on the payoff table and a different action on the regret table. (5) On the regret table, the best attainable payoff in every state is zero, and an action's maximum regret is exactly the negative of its minimum entry; minimax regret applied to the original problem is therefore nothing other than maximin applied to the regret table. (6) By (4), maximin applied to the regret table must agree with maximin applied to the original table; hence minimax regret and maximin always recommend the same action. (7) So at least one limb of the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute — the question of which rule rational agents would endorse under true ignorance — is no real dispute at all, and the textbook tables purporting to show the two rules coming apart must contain an arithmetical slip somewhere.

I cannot accept (7), since the divergence between these rules is routinely exhibited with three-line examples that I have checked myself, and I would be sorry to give up (2), which I had taken to be uncontroversial orthodoxy about utility. Which step should I reject, why does that step look so innocuous at first glance, and what exactly does it cost to reject it — or, alternatively, to reject one of its neighbours instead? A compact numerical example (two states, two or three actions) showing where the reasoning binds and where it breaks would be especially helpful. Please write your answer in British English.
