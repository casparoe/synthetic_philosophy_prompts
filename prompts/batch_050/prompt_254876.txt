I'm drafting a short essay for an undergraduate aesthetics seminar I help teach, and I'd like your comments before I submit it to the seminar organizer. The essay tries to stake out a position in the debate over why originals seem to matter more than perfect forgeries, but it does so through a somewhat unusual route: a case of what I call "self-forgery," where an artist repaints and then destroys her own earlier work. Please read the essay below and then give me detailed feedback. I'm especially interested in three things: (1) whether the self-forgery case actually does the argumentative work I claim it does, or whether it can be accommodated just as easily by the rival views I criticize; (2) what the strongest objection to my positive account would be, particularly from someone who thinks all aesthetic value must be perceptible in principle; and (3) any structural or expository problems — places where the argument is unclear, moves too fast, or relies on intuitions I haven't earned. Please respond in American English, and don't hesitate to be blunt: I'd rather hear that the central case fails now than after submission.

Here is the essay:

Why do we care whether a painting is an original? A perfect forgery looks identical, so the difference cannot be seen. One influential answer, associated with Nelson Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic arts, holds that even an undetectable difference matters because it might someday become detectable: the possibility of comparison keeps the forgery from counting as the same work. A rival, deflationary answer holds that our preference for originals is mere snobbery or superstition, a confusion of market value with aesthetic value. I want to defend a third view, which I call the causal-intimacy account. On this view, an original is valuable because it is the physical residue of the artist's decisions: the very surface that the artist's hand, guided by the intentions we admire, actually touched and shaped. Looking at an original is an attenuated form of contact with a historical act of making, and forgeries, however perfect, lack the relevant causal lineage.

The standard test cases — the famous twentieth-century forgeries of Vermeer, say — do not discriminate between my view and Goodman's, since both predict that the forgery loses value once exposed. So consider instead a case of self-forgery. An elderly painter, dissatisfied with the celebrated canvas she produced at twenty-five, repaints it from memory, corrects what she now sees as its flaws, and then destroys the earlier canvas. The new painting is causally continuous with an artist — indeed, with the same artist — and it may even be perceptually superior. Yet I submit that something has been lost, and my account explains what: the intentions we admired were those of a twenty-five-year-old, and the new surface bears the trace of a different, later agency. Neither Goodman's view nor the deflationary one seems to capture this, since no deception has occurred and no detection is in prospect. The loss is real even though nothing false has been represented to anyone.

Two objections deserve mention. First, the account seems to predict that restoration gradually erodes value as original material is replaced, and it is not obvious that this is a prediction rather than a reductio. Second, the account appears to imply that purely digital artworks, for which there is no unique material residue, can have no originals at all — or else that the notion of causal intimacy must be generalized from matter to history, in which case it is unclear whether the account remains distinctive or collapses into the familiar claim that an artwork's historical context partly determines its aesthetic character. I leave both objections unresolved here, but I flag them because I suspect the second one is fatal.
