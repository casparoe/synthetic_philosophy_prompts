I spent forty years as a research chemist before retiring, and since then I have been working my way through the philosophical literature on rational choice. A fellow member of my reading group, a retired engineer, has produced the following argument, and I cannot find a comfortable place to stop it, though its conclusion strikes me as absurd. I should like to know where it goes wrong.

(1) Under conditions of genuine ignorance — where the agent has no defensible probability distribution over states of the world, not merely an imprecise one — expected-utility reasoning requires the agent to fabricate probabilities.

(2) A fabricated probability is arbitrary, and rational choice cannot rest on arbitrary inputs; two equally rational agents would assign different numbers and reach different conclusions, so the method settles nothing.

(3) Hence, under genuine ignorance, rational choice may draw only on the structure of the outcomes themselves, not on any assignment of likelihoods.

(4) Of the decision rules that use only outcome structure, maximin alone guarantees the best available worst case; every rival rule — expected utility under an imposed uniform prior, minimax regret, and the rest — knowingly permits an avoidable catastrophe in at least some circumstances.

(5) Therefore, under genuine ignorance, maximin is the uniquely rational decision rule.

(6) We are under genuine ignorance concerning most real decisions: no defensible distribution exists over the worst possibilities of crossing the road, undergoing elective surgery, drinking the tap water, or taking any vaccine whose rare side effects are not yet fully charted.

(7) Therefore a rational agent should decline to perform any action whose worst possible outcome is catastrophic.

(8) But every such everyday action has a catastrophic worst case, so a rational agent should be effectively paralysed — and the rest of us, who act daily, are simply irrational.

I should mention what I would most like to keep: I find Rawls's case persuasive that maximin is sometimes the rational rule, particularly where the worst outcome is grave and a satisfactory security floor exists, so I would rather not rescue the argument by concluding that maximin is never defensible. Equally, I am not prepared to accept step (8). Please identify which premise or step I should reject, explain why that step appeared sound on first reading, and then price the alternatives: what Harsanyi's insistence that rational agents must always form priors has to concede here, what the defender of minimax regret loses if the argument is stopped at that point instead, and what a Rawlsian must say about the reach of genuine ignorance in ordinary life.
