I'm a third-year philosophy graduate student with a solid background in mathematical logic (comfortable with computability theory at the level of a first course) but no training in measure theory beyond the basics. I've become absorbed by the cluster of questions around algorithmic randomness -- in particular the old worry about whether the notion can be made sense of for finite sequences, given that the classical machinery (Martin-Löf tests, prefix-free Kolmogorov complexity, martingales) delivers its cleanest results only for infinite sequences, while any actual sequence we encounter -- a stretch of coin flips, a quantum device's output, a dataset -- is finite. I have roughly two years to devote to a dissertation and need to pick a problem soon, and my advisor keeps telling me that the difference between a good and a bad thesis here is mostly about which question you choose, not how clever you are.

So here is what I'd like from you: a defended agenda. Identify what you take to be the three or four most important open questions in or around the foundations of algorithmic randomness -- these could include whether finite-sequence randomness can be given a non-arbitrary grounding despite the machine-dependence of Kolmogorov complexity up to an additive constant, what the relationship should be between algorithmic randomness and physical indeterminism (does quantum randomness need the classical notion, replace it, or bypass it?), whether the various characterizations of randomness converging on the same class of infinite sequences is evidence that we've found a natural concept or an artifact of shared assumptions, or others you think I'm omitting. For each, explain: why it matters and what depends on its resolution (i.e., which other questions in philosophy of probability, statistical practice, or the interpretation of physics are downstream of it); how tractable it is for someone with my toolkit, as opposed to someone with a serious measure-theoretic or computational learning theory background; and what specific developments over the next few years -- a new theorem, a conceptual argument, an application -- would move it up or down the ranking.

Please be willing to be opinionated: tell me which question you would actually advise someone in my position to spend two years on, and equally importantly, which popular-sounding question in this area you think is a trap -- either because it's essentially settled in disguise, because progress on it wouldn't change anyone's mind, or because the interesting version of it requires mathematics I can't acquire in time. I'd rather get a contested ordering with real reasoning than a balanced survey.
