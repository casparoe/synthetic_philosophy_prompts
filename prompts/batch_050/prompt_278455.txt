I direct a small philosophy-of-AI research group, and we are preparing an application to a funder that supports foundational (non-commercial) work on machine learning interpretability. The application requires a two-page research proposal, and I would like you to write it for us.

Our half-formed idea is this. Interpretability researchers routinely describe their methods as uncovering what a neural network "represents" — in individual neurons, in directions in activation space, or in the latent variables recovered by sparse dictionary methods. Yet the same trained network typically admits many competing decompositions of its internal states: different methods, and sometimes different runs of one method, carve up activation space differently, and practitioners disagree about whether one carving is correct or whether all of them are merely convenient lenses. When the disagreement is discussed at all, it tends to be treated either as a methodological nuisance (use whichever decomposition serves your downstream purpose) or as a crude realism-versus-instrumentalism standoff imported wholesale from the general philosophy of science. Our suspicion is that both framings are dead ends, and that the question — does the network genuinely contain the representations our methods attribute to it, and could there be a fact of the matter about which decomposition is right? — becomes tractable only when recast as a question about explanatory indispensability. Roughly: a posit counts as a representation in the network, rather than as an artifact of the analyst's tools, only if it is indispensable to the best causal explanation of the network's behavior, where "causal explanation" is spelled out in terms of interventions on internal states (activation patching, feature ablation, interchange-style manipulations) rather than post-hoc interpretive appeal.

The proposal should do the following:

- State the research question precisely, in a form that could in principle receive a yes-or-no answer for a given network and a given method, not just a general attitude toward the debate.
- Explain why the existing positions leave the question open: why task-relative instrumentalism dodges rather than answers it; why naive realism founders on the apparent existence of empirically equivalent decompositions; and why it matters whether that equivalence is strict or only approximate — i.e., whether non-uniqueness refutes realism or merely underdetermines it.
- Lay out the approach: philosophical analysis anchored in two or three live disputes from actual practice (for instance, whether features are best understood as directions, as something sparser, or as an artifact of assuming that superposition is a real phenomenon to be explained), asking in each case what evidence would settle the dispute and whether the indispensability criterion settles it.
- Justify this approach over the obvious alternatives: purely formal identifiability results, purely empirical benchmarking of interpretability tools, and a general taxonomy of realism positions.
- Say what a successful result looks like (a criterion plus worked verdicts on the chosen disputes) and what would count as the project failing (for example, the criterion turning out to be extensionally empty, or every chosen dispute proving to be a straightforwardly empirical question that philosophy cannot touch).
- Present a realistic twelve-month work plan for a three-person team, with intermediate deliverables.

Do not include citations or a bibliography; we will supply those ourselves.
