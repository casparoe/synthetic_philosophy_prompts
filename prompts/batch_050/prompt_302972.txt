I am a novelist at work on a book that moves between imagined futures, and I have fallen into the habit of testing my sketches on a friend who teaches moral philosophy: I describe two possible worlds, and she tells me which she judges better. Her answers so far have been consistent, but I cannot tell what principle is driving them. First: a world of one million people, all flourishing, against the same world with a second million added whose lives are good though less rich than the first million's — she preferred the larger world, without hesitation. Second: ten million flourishing against ten billion whose lives are only barely worth living, where the sheer numbers make the total welfare of the second world far greater — she firmly preferred the small world. Third: ten million flourishing against a hundred million living solidly good lives, well above the barely-worth-living level but well below flourishing — she preferred the hundred million, again on grounds of total. Now, for the novel's final section, I need a fourth verdict I can trust before I ask her: a world of ten million flourishing, unchanged, but sharing the planet with a further billion on the frontier whose lives are all only barely worth living, against the same world without the frontier billion at all. What will she say, and why? Please set out the candidate principles her first three verdicts are consistent with, identify which of her earlier cases is the closest analog to this one, and tell me what further pair of worlds would discriminate between the principles if her verdict here does not settle it.
