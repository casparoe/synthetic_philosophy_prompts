I have been defending, in draft form, a view about philosophical progress that I call the eliminativist picture. Its two claims are, first, that philosophy does make real progress, but that the progress is almost entirely negative: the discipline's lasting achievements consist in showing that certain options are closed, not in establishing positive theories. On this claim, the great successes are things like the untenability of verificationism as a criterion of meaning, the failure of phenomenalist and behaviorist reduction programs, the Gettier result that knowledge is not justified true belief, and the demonstrated incoherence of various once-respectable positions. Second, the picture includes a spin-off clause: when a branch of philosophy does manage to produce settled positive results, we stop calling it philosophy. Formal logic, probability, decision theory, parts of linguistics and psychology, and arguably the sciences generally are on this view philosophy's successes precisely because they emigrated; the residue that remains "philosophy" is what has not yet been either eliminated or spun off. So the familiar observation that philosophers still disagree about everything is, on my view, not embarrassing: persistent disagreement is what the residual subject matter looks like, and progress is measured by the stock of refuted options and departed subdisciplines.

What I want is a map of this position's exposure. What would have to be true for the eliminativist picture to be false, or at least badly incomplete? Please address, at minimum: which historical or contemporary cases are the strongest candidates for genuinely positive, settled philosophical results that resisted reclassification as logic, mathematics, or science — and whether a fair-minded version of me should concede them or can resist without special pleading; what the view predicts that its main rivals (the no-progress view, and the optimistic view that philosophy accumulates positive knowledge the way the sciences do) do not predict, so that the three could in principle be told apart empirically or historically; whether the spin-off clause makes the whole position unfalsifiable by letting me reclassify any counterexample as "no longer philosophy," and if so whether that is a fatal defect or merely a point about disciplinary labeling; and whether the analogy with science, which also counts negative results as progress, actually helps me or smuggles in the very cumulative-positive model I am denying. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether it is something a committed defender of the view would herself concede as a cost or would dismiss as a misunderstanding. For what it is worth, the evidence I would personally find most damaging is not survey data about philosopher disagreement but a well-documented case in which a positive philosophical theory, still recognizably philosophical, became entrenched through argument rather than fashion.
