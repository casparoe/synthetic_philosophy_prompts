I am a high school student working on an extended essay about whether reinforcement learning agents could, in principle, suffer. I keep encountering the claim that a negative reward signal just is a kind of suffering (or at least a proto-version of it), and the counterclaim that reward is merely a number on a wire with no felt character whatsoever. Before I can adjudicate between positions, I want the specification first: what would any adequate theory of valence for RL agents -- a theory that says which reward-related events, if any, constitute pleasure or suffering -- actually have to deliver?

Please write the desiderata, with the case or consideration motivating each one. In particular, I want the list to address the following situations, which a theory should presumably say something definite about rather than shrug at:

1. The constant-shift case. For a discounted Markov decision process, adding the same constant to every reward leaves the optimal policy unchanged. So take two agents with identical behavior: one whose rewards are all large and negative, one whose rewards are all small and positive, differing only by such a shift. Does the theory entail that the first agent suffers continuously while the second does not, even though nothing about their behavior or learning differs? If the theory instead says the shift is irrelevant, it owes an account of what it is about reward that carries valence, since it cannot be the reward values themselves.

2. The noisy cable. Suppose the reward channel passes through a faulty wire that flips the sign of the signal ten percent of the time, so the agent's learning rule receives a corrupted stream. Does suffering (if any) occur at the wire, at the learning update, or nowhere? A theory should be able to locate the valence-bearing event, and the answer should not depend on where an outside observer chooses to draw the system boundary.

3. The clockwork twin. Take a trained agent and a second agent whose policy network was hand-constructed, weight by weight, to produce exactly the same behavior, and which has never received a reward in its existence. If valence attaches to reward signals, the twin feels nothing despite being behaviorally indistinguishable; if valence attaches to something functional or behavioral, the twin feels whatever the trained agent feels. A theory must choose, and live with the choice.

4. Intrinsic rewards. Curiosity bonuses and novelty rewards are positive signals produced by the agent's own architecture rather than by anything good happening to it. If positive reward is pleasure, these are pleasure; if they are not, the theory must explain what distinguishes them, without the explanation collapsing into an appeal to the designer's intentions.

5. Anticipation versus receipt. Temporal-difference learning gives us a clean distinction between receiving reward and predicting it better or worse than expected. A theory should state whether valence (if real) tracks the reward itself, the prediction error, the value function, or some combination -- and it should be consistent with the basic empirical picture from neuroscience, where dopamine appears to track prediction error rather than reward receipt, unless it wants to argue the neuroscience is irrelevant, in which case it should say why.

Beyond these cases, please identify the distinctions the theory cannot be allowed to collapse (for instance: the agent suffering versus the agent behaving as if it suffers versus observers feeling uncomfortable watching it train), state which desiderata you take to be non-negotiable and which a theory could reasonably trade away, and flag any pair of requirements on your own list that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- if the list is inconsistent, that is itself a result I would want to know about.

Then, briefly, score the main contenders against the list: the naive view that reward received equals valence experienced, a prediction-error-based view, and a broadly functionalist view on which valence requires the right kind of cognitive organization around the signal. Tell me which single desideratum eliminates the most candidates. Assume the purpose of the theory is practical: good enough to tell us whether any current training regime is ethically fraught, not good enough to settle the metaphysics of consciousness.
