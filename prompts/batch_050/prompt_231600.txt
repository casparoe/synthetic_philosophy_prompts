So I was re-reading some old threads about "the map is not the territory" and it got me thinking about an edge case for that whole way of talking, and I want to run it by you through a specific philosophical lens. Take the Rodney King case: in 1991 a bystander, George Holliday, filmed LAPD officers beating King, the video was all over the news, and then in 1992 the jury in Simi Valley acquitted the officers anyway, and LA burned. The part that fascinates me is that the defense's whole strategy apparently revolved around the video itself -- slowing it down, freezing frames, going through it frame by frame to argue that each individual movement by King justified the next blow. So you have this weird situation where everyone agrees the jury spent weeks staring at the evidence, and the conviction still didn't happen.

Analyze this through Kendall Walton's transparency thesis -- the idea that photographs (and by extension video) are transparent, that when we look at a photo of something we literally see the thing itself, the way we see through a telescope or a mirror, rather than merely seeing a representation of it. Walk me through what the thesis predicts here: on Walton's view, did the jury literally see the beating? Does that make the acquittal more or less mysterious? Then get into the hard part -- what happens to transparency when you manipulate the medium. If I see the beating "through" the video, do I still see it through a frame-by-frame breakdown, or does freezing and decomposing the footage convert seeing into something more like inference or measurement? Is there a principled line between looking through a recording and analyzing a recording, and does the slowing-down cross it?

Finally run the analysis in reverse: what does this episode do to Walton's view? One read is that it's a problem for transparency -- if photographs really put us in perceptual contact with events, how could twelve people watch the beating and walk away unconvinced? Another read is that the case actually vindicates the thesis in a perverse way, because the defense's need to dismantle the video suggests they accepted that raw footage functions like direct perception and had to break it down into something the jury could re-interpret. Tell me which read you find more plausible and why, and be honest about where the transparency framework just doesn't have the tools to answer -- e.g. whether "seeing" the beating was ever really the epistemically load-bearing thing, as opposed to interpreting it, and wether that distinction even survives Walton's account.
