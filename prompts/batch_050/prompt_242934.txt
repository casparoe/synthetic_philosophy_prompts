A close friend of mine has had chronic lower-back pain for years, with scans showing nothing that would explain its severity. Her physiotherapist keeps reaching for the same metaphor: pain is your body's burglar alarm. The point was partly reassuring -- pain is an output the nervous system generates as a warning, not a direct readout of tissue damage, so her pain was real even though nothing was "on fire." But the metaphor kept being stretched to do more argumentative work. Her pain, the physiotherapist said, is like an alarm that has become oversensitive and now goes off when a leaf brushes the window: the alarm itself is malfunctioning, there is no burglar, and the goal of treatment is to recalibrate the system rather than to hunt for damage. A skeptic in my family took the metaphor in the opposite direction: if the alarm is misfiring, then nothing is actually wrong with her, so her disability claim is suspect -- you don't get compensated for a faulty sensor. Same analogy, two very different conclusions about whether her pain is "real," what it's for, and what (if anything) anyone owes her.

I recently came across a condition that seems to complicate the whole picture: pain asymbolia, where patients with certain brain lesions (and some patients after cingulotomy) say they still feel pain -- they can locate it, describe its intensity, tell it from an itch -- but report that it no longer bothers them and they no longer avoid it. This struck me as a serious problem for the alarm metaphor, because the entire point of an alarm is that it is intrinsically demanding: an alarm you can calmly ignore while finishing your tea is failing at the one thing alarms exist to do. If the phenomenal feel of pain can come apart from its badness and its motivational grip, then which part is "the alarm"? Is the badness of pain a feature built into the signal itself, or a separate response the signal normally triggers? And if it's the latter, does that vindicate or undermine the physiotherapist's use of the metaphor?

Please audit this analogy carefully. Give me an explicit mapping (what plays the role of the sensor, the control unit, the siren, the homeowner, the burglar, the recalibration), then identify where the mapping breaks down: features a real alarm has that pain lacks (a designer, a spec sheet, a distinction between malfunction and proper sensitivity, a decidable fact of the matter about whether an intruder is present), and features pain has that alarms lack. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions the metaphor was being used to support -- the physiotherapist's "recalibrate, don't hunt for damage" prescription and my relative's "misfiring alarm means no real problem" skepticism -- from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, say whether pain asymbolia should push us toward a different picture altogether (perhaps pain as a signal plus a separate evaluative response), and if so, what a better analogy would be, or whether the alarm metaphor can be patched to survive.
