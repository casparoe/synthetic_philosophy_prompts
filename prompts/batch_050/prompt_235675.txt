Exam question: Deflationary theories of truth are often introduced by way of the equivalence schema: "p" is true if and only if p. Explain what is supposedly "deflated" about truth on such views. Distinguish at least two deflationary positions (for example, the redundancy theory, the prosentential theory, or minimalism), making clear how they differ rather than merely listing them. Finally, assess whether a deflationist can accommodate the claim that truth is a norm of assertion — that asserting something commits one to its truth — or whether that claim creates a serious problem for deflationism.

Student's answer: Deflationary theories hold that truth has no substantive nature waiting to be discovered. The predicate "is true" does not pick out a deep property such as correspondence with facts or coherence with a belief system; instead, its whole function is captured by the equivalence schema, which licenses moving from a sentence to its truth-attribution and back. To say that "snow is white" is true is to say no more than that snow is white, though the predicate is practically useful because it lets us endorse claims we cannot or do not wish to state individually, as when we say that everything the witness said is true.

On the redundancy theory, associated with Frank Ramsey, the phrase "is true" is strictly redundant: the claim that it is true that p says exactly what p says, in different words. Ramsey also developed the prosentential theory, later refined by Grover, Camp, and Belnap, on which "that is true" functions as a prosentence — a device that stands in for a whole sentence the way a pronoun stands in for a noun — so the predicate is not even a genuine predicate but a piece of anaphoric machinery. Horwich's minimalism goes further than either of these in one direction: it holds that truth is not a property at all, since the only facts about truth are the non-paradoxical instances of the equivalence schema, and there is nothing for a property to be over and above those instances. Minimalism thus differs from the prosentential theory by eliminating truth-ascription entirely rather than merely reanalyzing it.

On the norm of assertion: at first glance deflationism seems threatened, because if truth is thin, it is unclear how it could be the thing assertion aims at. But the appearance dissolves. To say assertion aims at truth is just to say that in asserting that p, one commits to p; the equivalence schema guarantees that this commitment can be redescribed as a commitment to truth. Since the deflationist can translate any talk of aiming at truth into talk of the claims themselves, the norm is fully preserved, and there is no residual problem. Deflationism therefore accommodates the normative claim without remainder, and critics who press the objection are simply assuming the substantive conception of truth they were supposed to be arguing for.

End of the student's answer.
