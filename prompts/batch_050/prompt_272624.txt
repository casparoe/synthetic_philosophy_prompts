After forty years as an experimental chemist, I have in retirement been reading philosophy, and lately the ethics of communication — in particular the debate over whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading. I have settled on a set of commitments, and before I write them up for a reading group I would like an audit of whether they hang together. I am not after a survey of the literature. I want to know which of my commitments pull against one another, which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection, and, where the tensions are genuine, how severe each is and what would have to give. If a single revision would restore consistency, please identify it and state plainly what it costs.

My commitments are these:

1. Lying is, as such, morally worse than misleading someone to the same conclusion. I do not mean merely that lies tend to turn out worse in practice. I mean there is a genuine default presumption against lying that does not attach to misleading, even though particular circumstances can override it.

2. The wrongness of deception consists in the harm done to the deceived: the false belief itself, and the compromised agency of a person who then acts on it. Where no one is deceived, there is no distinctive wrong of this kind.

3. I accept the broadly Gricean picture of conversation. What a speaker literally says is fixed by linguistic convention; conversational implicatures arise because hearers assume the speaker is observing a cooperative principle and its associated maxims — truthfulness, relevance, informativeness, and so on — and reason from that assumption to what the speaker must have meant.

4. Producing a bad outcome is morally worse, and harder to justify, than merely allowing it to come about; we bear greater responsibility for what we do than for what we fail to prevent. Applied to communication: the liar produces the hearer's false belief, whereas the misleading speaker merely fails to prevent the hearer from drawing a false conclusion on the hearer's own initiative.

5. For all that, I hold that a researcher who reports only the true results favourable to a drug, knowing the funder will conclude the drug works, wrongs the funder almost as gravely as one who fabricates the data outright. I saw enough of this in industry to be quite certain of it.

6. I take bald-faced lies to be genuine lies, and wrong as lies. A defendant in a show trial who makes a false confession, knowing no one present will believe a word of it and indifferent to whether anyone does, still lies. Lying therefore requires neither successful deception nor the intention to deceive.

Some of these strike me as uneasy neighbours — (1) and (5) in particular, and perhaps (2) and (6) — but I cannot tell which of the apparent conflicts are real and which would evaporate under scrutiny, and I suspect the true fault lines may lie elsewhere entirely. Please proceed as follows: first, identify every genuine tension in the set, ordered by severity, and for each one state exactly which commitments are incompatible and why; second, identify the apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection, and show why they dissolve; third, for each genuine tension, say what the cheapest way out would be; and finally, tell me which single revision to the set as a whole restores consistency at the lowest overall cost, and what I would be giving up.
