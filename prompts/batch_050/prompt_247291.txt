(1) A scientific theory is a tool for generating predictions, and its worth is exhausted by how accurate those predictions are. (2) You can only test an assumption by testing what follows from it, so the realism of assumptions cant be checked except through predictive success. (3) So, as Friedman says in the 1953 methodology essay, unrealistic assumptions are no objection to a theory that predicts well. (4) A curve-fitting routine with enough free parameters can be tuned to fit any data set you currently have, and re-tuned whenever new data comes in, so it predicts just as well as any theory you care to name. (5) Therefore a well-maintained curve fit is exactly as good a scientific theory as Newtonian mechanics, and preferring mechanics is an aesthetic choice, not a scientific one. (6) But that can't be right -- we'd still trust mechanics on the next unobserved case and nobody would trust the curve fit. (7) So one of (1)-(4) has to go. My instinct is that (4) is sneaky, since "predicts well so far, with maintenance" isn't "predicts well," but my student says the re-tuning clause is doing honest work and now I'm not sure. Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost someone who wants to keep the Friedman line?
