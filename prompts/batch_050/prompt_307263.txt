I teach an upper-level jurisprudence seminar, and I am setting this year's final exam on the rule of law. In previous years I have asked students to compare Fuller and Raz on the morality of legality, and what I got back was competent but memorized: tidy recitals of Fuller's eight desiderata, a dutiful paragraph on the grudge-informer debate, and the safe conclusion that theorists "differ on how much moral content legality has." This year I want the question built around a scenario they cannot have prepared for, one that forces them to say what the rule of law is actually for rather than who said what about it.

Here is the scenario I have in mind. Imagine a state, call it Meritia, whose legal code satisfies all eight of Fuller's desiderata to the letter: its laws are general, publicly promulgated, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, practicable, stable over time, and applied in perfect congruence with their text. Enforcement, however, is fully automated. Citizens receive penalty assessments from an algorithmic system that offers no hearing, no opportunity to contest or explain oneself, no reasons beyond the statutory citation, and no avenue of appeal — though its application of the code is provably error-free, indeed far more reliable than any human judiciary. Meritia is also thoroughly authoritarian: the content of its code includes speech restrictions and collective punishments, all impeccably clear and prospective. The question should require students to determine which of Fuller, Raz, and Waldron would count Meritia as possessing the rule of law, where each would locate its failure or success, and why. In particular, I want students to confront the fact that Raz, who treats the rule of law as an instrumental virtue of legal systems — the way sharpness is a virtue of a knife, usable for good ends or bad — seemingly must say Meritia scores near-perfectly, and to decide whether that consequence is a reductio of his view or a bullet he knowingly bites. They should also have to reckon with whether Fuller's scattered remarks about reciprocity between ruler and subject give him resources to condemn Meritia that his eight desiderata alone do not, and whether Waldron's insistence on the procedural and adjudicative dimensions of legality — courts, argument, the dignity of being heard — does better at capturing what is missing, and at what cost to the supposed neutrality of the ideal.

Please deliver four things: (1) the exam question as it would appear on the paper, answerable in ninety minutes and worth twenty marks; (2) a model answer of roughly six hundred words, written at the level of a strong top-of-class student; (3) a marking scheme organized around five must-hit points, stating the marks each is worth (totalling twenty) and appending to each a one-line note on the specific way weaker candidates typically get it wrong — for instance, attributing Waldron's proceduralism to Fuller, or assuming Fuller claims legality guarantees substantive justice; (4) a paragraph stating precisely what separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of moves made, not polish. End your response with a final line beginning "Crux:" stating in one sentence the single issue on which the question turns.
