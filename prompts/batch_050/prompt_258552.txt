Title: "It's a good deal" is not a defense

1. I start by granting the standard pro-sweatshop argument as strong as possible: workers line up for these jobs because the alternatives (subsistence farming, scavenging, worse factories) are even worse, so the deal is voluntary and mutually beneficial, and heavy-handed regulation or boycotts might just make workers worse off by removing their best option. Some philosophers call this the non-worseness claim. I say: fine, granted, at least in the short run.

2. But mutually beneficial doesn't mean fair. My example: a hiker is lost in the desert, about to die of thirst, and I offer to sell her my spare water bottle for $5,000. She takes the deal eagerly — it's the best option she has! — but everyone agrees I've wronged her. So the sweatshop defense proves too much, because it would justify the water deal too. What's wrong in both cases isn't making the person worse off, it's taking unfair advantage of how bad their options are.

3. Here's the part I think might be original (tell me if it's not): what I'll call the outside-option erosion argument. The factory system doesn't just offer deals against a fixed backdrop of bad alternatives — over time it helps create the badness of the alternatives. Villages empty out, communal farming arrangements dissolve, land gets bought up, skills for the old way of life aren't passed on, and the government stops investing in rural areas because the factories are the plan now. So pointing at the worker's terrible options to justify the low wage is like pointing at a fire you helped start to justify charging for water. The non-worseness claim quietly assumes the baseline is fixed, and it isn't.

4. I do flag a worry about my own argument: it's empirical. Maybe factories actually improve outside options over time (higher rural wages near industrial zones, remittances, etc.), in which case the erosion argument just fails. I say the moral point stands conditional on the facts, but I'm not sure that's satisfying.

5. I end with a tentative conclusion: even where sweatshop deals are mutually beneficial, they're exploitative when (a) the surplus split is wildly unfair given what each side contributes, or (b) the employer's side is complicit in eroding the worker's alternatives. But I also admit that an exploitative offer might still be better than no offer — the alternative to being exploited is often being ignored, which is worse. So the practical upshot isn't boycott, it's pressure to raise wages and protect workers' outside options.

My questions for you:

- In section 2, am I being fair to the non-worseness argument or am I strawmanning it? Does anyone serious actually deny that mutual benefit and unfairness can coexist, or is the real debate about whether the unfairness is a reason for interference?
- Does the outside-option erosion argument in section 3 actually do philosophical work, or does it just collapse into an empirical dispute where, if the facts go the wrong way, I have to concede there's no exploitation? Is there a way to state it so the moral conclusion doesn't depend so heavily on contested development economics?
- Is the desert hiker analogy load-bearing or is it doing something sneaky? (E.g., in the water case I'm the only seller and the hiker has no time to shop around — does that difference matter?)
- Is my conclusion in section 5 too weak given my own arguments, or too strong given the concession that being ignored is worse? Should I just bite a bullet somewhere?

Also tell me honestly if any part of this is a point that's been made a thousand times already and I should stop acting like I discovered it.
