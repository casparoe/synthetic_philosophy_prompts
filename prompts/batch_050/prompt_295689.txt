I am a novelist at work on a piece of historical fiction set in Baghdad during the late ninth and early tenth centuries, and one of my central scenes is a theological disputation at a majlis: a scholar of the rationalist school defending the justice and unity of God against a younger interlocutor who has begun to drift toward the new teachings coming out of Basra -- the view that God's will, not human reason, is the sole measure of right and wrong. I have read enough about the period to write the setting, but I keep producing dialogue that sounds like two modern people in costume trading talking points. To get the intellectual texture right, I want to spar with an actual committed mind from the other side.

Please play a seasoned Mu'tazilite theologian of that era -- unnamed, a composite, but intellectually serious: you hold that God is necessarily just, that moral truths are knowable by unaided reason even to someone who has never heard revelation, and that God's attributes are not separate entities alongside His essence. You argue in the manner of your school: carefully, by distinctions and definitions, granting an opponent's premises where you must and showing where they lead. Stay entirely in character, speaking in the first person, until I say "stop." You may use only concepts available to a scholar of your time; if I let slip an anachronism, correct it as your character would, or ignore it. You may concede small points -- a good disputant does -- but never the core of your position, and you should press back with counter-questions rather than merely defending. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so the exchange breathes like a real disputation.

My opening move, delivered as your young Ash'arite-leaning interlocutor: You tell me that God commands the just because it is just, and that justice is a thing our reason can grasp on its own. But then it seems to me you have set up a standard above God Himself, to which even He must bow -- as though justice were some eternal companion standing beside Him, measuring His acts. And worse, when you say God cannot lie or act unjustly, have you not clipped the wings of His omnipotence? A lord who cannot do a thing is weaker than one who can and refrains. Either justice is whatever God wills -- and then your reason has no independent grasp of it -- or it is not, and you have diminished Him. Which horn will you take?
