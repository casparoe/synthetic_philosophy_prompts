I serve on the grants panel of a mid-sized private foundation that funds work at the intersection of AI safety and philosophy of science. We have a proposal in front of us asking for roughly two years of funding for a mechanistic interpretability project on a mid-scale language model: the team wants to identify the model's internal "features," map the circuits connecting them, and ultimately certify that certain dangerous concepts (they give deception and resource acquisition as examples) are or are not represented in the network. One of our external referees wrote a scathing review arguing the project rests on a category mistake. Her position, roughly, is that interpretability methods -- sparse autoencoders, probing, activation patching -- produce descriptions that are useful to researchers but do not carve the network at any joints it actually has; the "features" are projections of our own conceptual scheme onto a giant lookup of correlations, so asking whether the model "really represents" deception is like asking whether a river delta "really represents" the fingers of a hand. She concludes the proposal should be rejected as philosophically confused, not merely risky.

Our program officer is inclined to fund anyway. His counter-memo argues that the referee is applying a standard of realism that nothing in science meets: genes, tectonic plates, and phonemes are all, on some deflationary views, "just useful descriptions," yet the discovery of their causal roles transformed their fields. He thinks the right test is whether the putative representations do causal and predictive work -- whether intervening on a feature changes behavior in systematic ways, whether the same features show up across training runs -- not whether they satisfy some metaphysician's criterion for being really there. Both reviewers seem to me to be talking past each other, and I suspect the truth is that there are several grades of realism about representations (bare usefulness, causal-role realism, something closer to the model having its own ontology) and the proposal needs some grades but not others for its safety claims to go through.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the panel before our vote: it should recommend one of fund, reject, or fund with conditions; it should adjudicate the dispute between the two referees by working out which grade of realism about neural representations the proposal's safety claims actually require, and whether current interpretability methods could plausibly deliver it; and it must give the strongest version of the losing side rather than a strawman, since both referees will read the outcome. If you recommend conditions, make them conditions the research team could actually act on and we could actually check at review time. End the memo with a final line of exactly this form: \answer{fund}, \answer{reject}, or \answer{fund with conditions}.
