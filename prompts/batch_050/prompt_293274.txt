So I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend Sam about whether wild animal suffering is something we should actually care about, and at one point I brought up that footnote in Bentham's Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation where he points out a full-grown horse or dog is far more rational and aware than a newborn infant, so whatever line we draw between "counts" and "doesn't count" can't rest on reason or speech. Sam said it was a cheap trick, I definately got defensive, and it went badly. Anyway, next month I'm leading our local philosophy reading group (a nurse, two software guys, a retired teacher, no one with philosophy training) and I want to do a session on wild animal suffering that builds on the move in that footnote. I don't want the session itself, I want a plan: what already has to be in their heads before the Bentham comparison lands as a serious challenge instead of a gotcha, the order to introduce the pieces (what sentience is and isn't, the nature-is-fine intuition, the scale of it), the one concrete case that should carry the evening -- I'm tempted by a fawn starving in a hard winter but tell me if there's a better pick and what the case needs to avoid -- the objections they'll raise in the order they'll raise them (I'm betting "nature's just like that" first, then "predators would starve too," then "we can't fix it anyway so why think about it") and where in the sequence each should be absorbed, what I should openly skip at this level and say so out loud, and a way to tell at the end whether they've actually grasped the footnote's point or just feel bad about Bambi.
