Ok so here's one for you. In his 1878 paper "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (published in Popular Science Monthly), C.S. Peirce goes after philosophers who use the "a priori method" — basically settling their beliefs by what seems agreeable to reason rather than by anything that would force agreement across inquirers. He ends the section with this jab:

"In contenting themselves with fixing their own opinions by a method which would lead another man to a different result, they betray their feeble hold of the conception of what truth is"

Write me a deadpan op-ed, 450 words max (the Peirce passage above doesn't count toward the limit), in the voice of a proud professional philosopher of exactly the stripe Peirce is attacking, responding to this passage as if it were a compliment he's finally recieving. He should lean all the way in: a method that would lead another man to a different result is, he argues, a feature — proof of intellectual pluralism, respect for individual temperament, freedom from the tyranny of convergence. Every claim he makes has to actually follow from the a priori method as Peirce describes it (fixing belief by what's agreeable to reason, checking opinions for consistency with one's own system, treating dispute as permanent rather than as something inquiry is supposed to end). He can brag about tenure, about never having changed his mind in print, about his school producing as many "truths" as it has members. No winking at the reader, and dont let him concede anything real to Peirce. The humor should come from him earnestly defending the exact thing Peirce meant as an insult — and by the last paragraph, without him noticing, the reader should be able to see precisely why Peirce thought a "feeble hold of the conception of what truth is" was a devastating charge, and why Peirce wanted truth tied to what investigation would ultimately force everyone to agree on. Stay in character the whole way through.
