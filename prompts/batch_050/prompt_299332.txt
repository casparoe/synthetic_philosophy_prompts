Next month I am giving the retirement toast for my former dissertation advisor, a learning theorist who spent four decades proving limits: impossibility results in the spirit of the no-free-lunch theorems, showing that no learning algorithm outperforms every other when performance is averaged over all possible data-generating processes, and that every successful induction therefore leans on prior assumptions about which worlds we actually inhabit. The oddity of the evening, which I want the speech to confront rather than hide, is that roughly half the guests are her former students who now run spectacularly successful applied machine-learning teams — people who generalize from data every day and get rich doing it — while the other half are family, old friends, and her bridge club, who know her as the person who warned them for years never to trust a trend line.

In Section I of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, David Hume describes a certain kind of thinker this way: "proceeding from particular instances to general principles, they still push on their enquiries to principles more general, and rest not satisfied till they arrive at those original principles, by which, in every science, all human curiosity must be bounded." (You may treat this passage as not counting toward the speech's length.)

Please write a toast of about five minutes, built around this passage. The speech should do the following:

- Open by presenting the passage, and take a position on whether it describes my advisor's career as a triumph, a cautionary tale, or some third thing — a theorist whose life's work was locating exactly where Hume's boundary lies for machines that learn, rather than for minds.
- Use the passage to structure the toast's central tension: Hume's relentless generalizer sounds like a hero of inquiry, yet the no-free-lunch results suggest the boundary she mapped is real — that there is no assumption-free route from data to prediction, for gradient descent any more than for the human habit of expecting the future to resemble the past. The speech should make the mixed audience feel both halves of this: the theorists should hear the theorem stated correctly, and the bridge club should understand why it matters that "it works in practice" is not the same as "it had to work."
- Address the successful practitioners in the room directly, without either mocking them for ignoring the theory or flattering them into thinking the theory was wrong. The honest line — that their successes are bets on the world's actual regularities rather than refutations of her theorems — should land as the toast's tribute to her, not as a concession extracted from her.
- Include exactly one joke about induction that both halves of the room can laugh at, and end on an image of what it means to spend a career mapping a boundary that everyone else profits from sailing up to.

Do not let the speech pretend that the no-free-lunch theorems give practical algorithm-selection advice they do not give, that they prove induction is irrational, or that Hume himself resolved the problem of induction. If the toast gestures at how we live with an unjustified-in-principle practice that works, it should do so with a light touch rather than a lecture.
