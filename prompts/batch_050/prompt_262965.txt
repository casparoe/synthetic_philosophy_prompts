I was listening to a debate about AI regulation the other day and one of the guests leaned hard on Dennett's line about chatbots: that LLMs are counterfeit people, and counterfeit people are dangerous in basically the same way counterfeit money is dangerous. The idea, as I remember it, is that fake currency doesn't just harm the person you pass it to -- it corrodes trust in the whole monetary system, which is why we treat counterfeiting as way more serious than ordinary theft, and why (Dennett says) these systems should be treated as a serious threat to the fabric of society and heavily restricted, not shrugged at. My roommate thinks this is the single best argument she's heard on the topic and keeps deploying it; my other friend thinks it's a slick metaphor doing all the argumentative work.

I'm sympathetic to both of them at different moments, so what I want is a proper audit of the money analogy rather than a thumbs up or down. Some things that strike me as worth sorting out: money has value only because we all trust it, so fakes are pure poison, but is language or testimony like that, or does it have sources of value that survive being faked? Counterfeit bills are designed to pass as real ones, whereas plenty of chatbots announce what they are -- does disclosure dissolve the analogy or just move it to the undisclosed cases? Forgers can't accidentally produce real currency, but could a "fake person" accidentally produce real understanding, real conversation, real comfort, and does that possibility have any analogue in the money case? And money is deliberately standardized so that authenticity is a checkable binary, while personhood seems to come in degrees and kinds. Feel free to raise breaks I haven't thought of.

Structure the answer like this, roughly: **The mapping** -- a correspondence table or list, about 200 words, matching each element of the counterfeit-currency case (the currency, the counterfeiter, the duped user, the trust system, the legal response) to its supposed analogue in the chatbot case. **Where it breaks** -- about 400 words, the disanalogies, ranked by how much damage they do to the argument specifically (not just to the metaphor's neatness). **What it smuggles in** -- about 200 words, any assumptions the analogy quietly imports about what persons are or what we want from each other. **Does the conclusion survive** -- about 200 words, whether the heavy-restriction conclusion still stands once the breaks are priced in. **A better analogy?** -- about 100 words, offer one if you've got it.
