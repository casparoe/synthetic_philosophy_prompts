A colleague of mine has an argument that seems to prove that no one has ever been justified in believing anything. I take it to prove too much, since the conclusion would cover the beliefs the argument itself relies on -- including its own premises. Here it is as he gave it to me:

(1) A belief is justified for a person only if it is either supported by reasons the person has or is non-inferentially justified.

(2) One belief can support another only if it is itself justified: an unjustified premise cannot transmit to a conclusion a warrant it does not possess, any more than an unfunded check can fund a payment.

(3) There are no non-inferentially justified beliefs. For suppose some belief were justified without support from other beliefs. Its justification would have to come either from something with propositional content or from something without it. If from something with propositional content -- a seeming, say -- then since that content can be false, one needs justification for taking it to be true, which reinstates inferential support. If from something without propositional content -- raw sensation -- then it cannot stand in any evidential relation to the belief at all: a claim no more follows from a sensation than from a sneeze. Either way, non-inferential justification names nothing real.

(4) So justification always rests on further justified beliefs, and hence on a chain that terminates in the unjustified (excluded by (2)), circles back on itself, or runs on without end.

(5) A circle confers nothing: if a belief's justification traces back to that very belief, the detour through intermediaries is just a longer way of saying the belief holds because it holds.

(6) An endless chain confers nothing on finite creatures: we have only finitely many beliefs, and a chain that exists only in the abstract, not as reasons the believer actually has, is not her justification but at most a description of a justification someone else -- or no one -- might possess.

(7) Therefore no belief is ever justified for anyone.

The conclusion cannot be right, yet each premise looks defensible in isolation. Tell me which premise or step I should press on, and what pressing it costs. In particular: why did that premise look fine -- what true principle is it a plausible-looking distortion of? Then work through the three standard responses to the regress -- foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism -- and say what each must commit to, or give up, in order to reject the step you have indicted, rather than merely asserting the conclusion is false. I would like to keep both of the following if possible: that ordinary perceptual beliefs can be justified, and that the regress is a genuine problem rather than a verbal muddle. If I cannot keep both, tell me which is cheaper to drop and why. Finally, state what would change your verdict: what repair of the premise you indict would show the argument is sound after all, or at which point your own diagnosis is most likely to break down.
