I am a novelist with a couple of decades of practice, and for the past several years I have also run an advanced fiction workshop. Lately I have been reading Whitehead on education, and I keep having the suspicion that his theory is, in disguise, a theory of how a writer learns the craft. His three-stage rhythm of learning — the stage of romance, where the student first apprehends a subject in its vivid, unexamined wholeness; the stage of precision, where that first apprehension is subjected to exact technique and disciplined detail; and the stage of generalization, where the precise skills are reabsorbed into a living, flexible command — seems to map onto something I know intimately: the way a writer moves from the intoxicated first draft, through the grinding work of revision and technical mastery, to the point where technique disappears into voice. And his complaint about inert ideas — knowledge received but never put to work — sounds to me exactly like what I tell students who can recite every rule of craft and still write dead pages.

Here is what I would like. Take the core of Whitehead's position — the three-stage rhythm, the attack on inert ideas, his insistence that education must keep knowledge connected to its live application, and his claim that the rhythm recurs at every level of scale rather than happening once — and restate it systematically in the vocabulary I actually work in: drafting and revision, scene and summary, showing versus telling, character arc, voice, workshop critique, the difference between rules learned from manuals and instincts earned at the desk. Translate each component one at a time, and for each give me an honest residue report: what translates cleanly into craft terms, what you had to distort or pad to make it fit, and what, if anything, has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

Then the part I care most about: for each piece of residue, tell me whether it points to something my workshop vocabulary is missing — a distinction I should be making with my students but have no word for — or whether it points to something in Whitehead's account that is less precise than it appears, a place where the generality of his philosophy papers over a problem that working writers actually have to solve. I do not want the translation to flatter either side. If Whitehead is really just saying that bad teaching produces bad writers, I want to hear that said plainly; and if he is identifying a structural rhythm that my craft talk only gestures at, I want that shown plainly too.
