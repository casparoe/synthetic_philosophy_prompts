So I'm a novelist, and at our writers' group last night two friends of mine got into a real fight over a scene in Marta's draft. The scene: her character Ingrid drives forty minutes home on the freeway while brooding about a fight with her sister, arrives, and genuinely cannot remember a single moment of the drive. Marta wrote that Ingrid "saw nothing of the road." Dan said that's bad phenomenology: Ingrid obviously *experienced* the road the whole time -- the taillights, the curves, all of it was there in her experience, it just never got into memory or report, and if it hadn't been experienced she'd have crashed. He cited Ned Block's 1995 paper about a confusion over a function of consciousness, the bit where experience (phenomenal consciousness) can supposedly float free of the information being available for reasoning and reporting (access consciousness).

Marta pushed back: if a state can't be reported, remembered, reasoned about, or used to guide anything, then calling it conscious is just an empty compliment -- "experienced but totally inaccessible" is a distinction without a difference, and anyway the whole point of the scene is the eerie blankness, which Dan's reading ruins.

Dan said by her logic blindsight patients and people deep in flow states are zombies, and that she's confusing consciousness itself with the later mechanisms that grab it. Marta said that's exactly the move that makes his view untestable: any evidence she could offer against "inaccessible experience" is by definition inaccessible, so his position can't lose, and a position that can't lose isn't worth having in a scene or in a philosophy paper. Dan's last shot was that untestable isn't the same as false, and that she was letting a dramaturgical preference masquerade as an argument about the mind. They both turned to me and I had nothing.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move -- who's right about what, what each is missing, and who comes out ahead overall (and separately, whether any of this should actually change how Marta writes the scene, or whether that's a different question entirely).

After the prose verdict, give me a table with these columns: Move (short label of the claim or counterclaim), Speaker (Dan or Marta), Verdict (clearly right / mostly right / toss-up / mostly wrong / clearly wrong), Why (one sentence). Then a final JSON object with fields "overall_winner" (Dan, Marta, or neither), "biggest_thing_both_missed" (string), and "should_change_the_scene" (boolean).
