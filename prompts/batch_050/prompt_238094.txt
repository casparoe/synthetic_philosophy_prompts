So I keep running into "is this still the same thing" questions and I want to know what I actually believe, so here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I'd like the principle underneath them if there is one.

Case 1: the ship of Theseus thing, gradual plank-by-plank replacement over years as each plank rots. Same ship the whole time, obviously yes.

Case 2: same ship, but the old planks get kept in a warehouse and years later someone reassembles them into a ship of the same design. That warehouse ship is NOT the original. It's a replica made of original parts, which is a different thing.

Case 3: Star Trek teleportation. You get scanned, your body is vaporized, and a perfect copy is assembled at the other end from new matter. My gut says you die. The person who steps out is a copy who thinks he's you. I realize this bothers people but it's where I land.

Case 4: my favorite band has replaced every member gradually over 30 years, one at a time, each new guy learning from the others. Still the same band. But if all five members had quit simultaneously and five strangers took over the name the next day, that would be a new band trading on the old name.

Case 5: a river is the same river for millennia even though none of the water is the same. Yes, same river.

Case 6: my chess club dissolves, then twenty years later some new students found a club with the same name and same constitution. Different club.

Case 7: an amoeba splits in two. I honestly don't think either half is the original amoeba. The original is gone, there are two new ones.

Case 8: I lose a fingernail, gain and lose cells constantly, my personality changes -- still me, obviously.

OK so here's my problem. Cases 1 and 4 say gradual total replacement preserves identity. But case 3 is basically that same structure compressed in time -- matter destroyed, functionally identical replacement -- and I say death. Is the only difference the timescale, or the continuous functioning? And if it's continuous functioning, does that mean case 6's club would have survived if it had just gone dormant for twenty years instead of formally dissolving? I want to say no, the dormancy one is still a new club, but I'm not sure I can defend that.

So: is there one coherent principle about what persistence over time requires that captures most of these verdicts? Does it end up looking like endurantism or perdurantism or something else, and which of my verdicts does it force me to give up -- I suspect at least one has to go. Also tell me what it predicts for a case I didn't list: a bronze statue melted down and recast from the same bronze into an identical statue a year later. Same statue or not?
