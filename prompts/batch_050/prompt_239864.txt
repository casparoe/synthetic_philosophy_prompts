I am a novelist in the early stages of a book whose antagonist -- though I resist the word -- is the founder of a planned community, a man who genuinely loves the people whose lives he arranges. I keep returning to The Truman Show as the great dramatization of this figure, and I would like a careful reading of the film through the philosophy of paternalism and nudging. Christof does not imprison Truman so much as architect him: the staged trauma that makes him fear water, the travel posters warning of disaster, the actors who steer him home, the career conveniently at hand. On Thaler and Sunstein's picture, a nudge is supposed to be cheap to avoid and, on the more scrupulous versions, in principle transparent; Christof's interventions are neither, yet his justification -- the world outside is worse, and I have given him a good life -- is exactly the justification paternalists offer. So: is Christof a choice architect who has simply abandoned the safeguards, or does the film show that the safeguards are what does the moral work, such that without them you have left paternalism for something else entirely? I am also interested in a wrinkle I have not seen discussed in the literature, and which my novel turns on: Truman was nudged from birth, so Seahaven's architecture is not an interference with preferences he already had -- it is the medium in which his preferences were formed. Call this, for lack of a better term, constitutive nudging. Does the standard critique of nudges -- that they bypass rational agency -- even apply when there was never a pre-architected agent to bypass, or does it apply with double force? Where does the film take a stand on this (I think of Truman's dawning suspicion, and of what the audience is invited to feel about Christof's tenderness at the end), where does it stay honest about the cost of the open door, and where does it cheat by making the outside world symbolically benign? Finally, the craftsman's question: what does the film know about benevolent control that the philosophy papers do not -- something, I suspect, about how it feels from the inside to discover that one's choices were curated -- and how might a novelist dramatize a Christof who is neither monster nor fool?
