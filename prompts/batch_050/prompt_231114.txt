Two people in my reading group have been going at it for months and I want to force them to produce something. Both are property dualists -- they agree phenomenal properties are real and not reducible to anything physical -- but one is a committed epiphenomenalist (brain processes cause experiences, experiences cause nothing, not even our beliefs about experiences) and the other is an interactionist who thinks epiphenomenalism refutes itself through the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if qualia are causally inert, then our judgments and reports about them aren't explained by them, so an epiphenomenalist's own arguments, which all start from what experience is like, would be just as likely to be produced by a zombie with no experiences at all, and then in what sense does anyone know anything about thier own consciousness.

Write the joint statement these two could actually sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. I want it in continuous prose, like a letter -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables -- but it should cover, in order: first the claims both can put their names to (I'd guess these include that causal closure of the physical is the real engine behind epiphenomenalism, that the zombie twin who makes identical reports is a consequence both sides accept rather than a gotcha, and that neither side gets to just assume a causal theory of knowledge); then the disagreements, each phrased carefully enough that the person holding it would accept the wording of their own position; then the cruxes. I suspect the cruxes include whether a judgment about an experience has to be caused by that very experience to count as knowledge, and whether the epiphenomenalist's common-cause reply -- the same brain state produces both the experience and the judgment, so the judgment reliably tracks the experience without being caused by it -- actually secures reliability or still leaves the judgment's aboutness unexplained. For each crux, give at least one concrete case or thought experiment both would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side says about it. If part of their fight turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part.
