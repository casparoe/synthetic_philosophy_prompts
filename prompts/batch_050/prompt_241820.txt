I retired three years ago after a career in geology (petrology, mostly) and have been filling the gap with philosophy, the way you do. Our little reading group did the extended mind material last month — the Clark and Chalmers paper with poor Otto and his notebook — and I made the mistake of trying to defend it to my son-in-law over Christmas. He cornered me with what follows, and I still can't find the weak joint. Here it is as I've written it up.

(1) What makes something part of a cognitive process is the work it does — storing, retrieving, and transforming information the agent relies on — not where it happens to sit. Skin and skull are a biological accident, not a principled boundary.
(2) So if something outside the head does that work continuously, reliably, and automatically, the way Otto's notebook does for his memory, then it is literally part of the mind.
(3) The notebook qualifies, so it's part of Otto's mind, and its entries are among his beliefs.
(4) But lots of other things qualify by the same test. My phone's contact list is my memory for numbers. I ask my wife about our appointments automatically and trust her answer exactly the way Otto trusts the notebook. And a search engine is continuously available, generally reliable, and consulted more or less automatically by everyone with a smartphone.
(5) So by (1) and (2), my wife's memory of our calendar is part of my mind, and the contents of a search engine's index are among the beliefs of everyone carrying a phone.
(6) But then my mind contains billions of claims I've never entertained, some flatly contradicting others; its boundaries shift whenever a server goes down or my wife forgets something; and "my mind" no longer picks out anything a science of the mind could actually study.
(7) So (1) and (2) prove too much: they don't extend the mind, they dissolve it.

My son-in-law thinks this refutes the extended mind outright. My own suspicion, which I can't back up, is that the rot is somewhere in the move from "does the work" to "is part of," i.e. in (1)-(2), and that the original paper has more resources than I've given it credit for — but maybe I'm wrong and the trouble only starts at (4), with my "same test" claim. Where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? Which single premise or step should I surrender, why did it look so plausible, and what does surrendering it cost the extended-mind side — and on the other hand, what would it cost a critic to accept (1)-(3) and try to fence off (4) somehow? I'd like to keep (1) if I can, since that's the bit that felt obviously right to me as a scientist, but tell me honestly if that's the expensive one.
