Two colleagues of mine, a historian of science and an economist, got into a running argument at a department reception that never got resolved, and I'd like a referee. It started over the familiar cases of simultaneous discovery: Newton and Leibniz both developing the calculus, Darwin and Wallace both arriving at natural selection. The economist took the inevitabilist line: when the same idea turns up independently in two places at once, the obvious lesson is that the idea was ripe and someone was bound to get it; the cult of the lone genius is mostly a myth, and the bitter priority dispute between Newton and Leibniz was two men fighting over credit for something history would have produced regardless. The historian pushed back that this is hindsight doing the work: we only ever hear about the cases where discovery happened, so "it was inevitable" is unfalsifiable; and the fact that we got two calculuses rather than one cuts the other way, since Leibniz's notation proved so much more usable than Newton's that which man invented it changed the mathematics that followed. The economist replied that this actually concedes his point: if the notation mattered but the inventor didn't, then the idea is the real causal agent and the person is fungible, which is exactly what inevitabilism says. The historian's last move was to press on timing: Darwin sat on his theory for roughly two decades and only published because Wallace's letter forced his hand, so the timing of discovery is clearly contingent on personality and circumstance, and if timing is contingent then the subsequent history of biology is contingent too, because a theory arriving twenty years earlier or later lands in a different world.

Neither would give an inch, and I genuinely couldn't settle it. Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification. Then tell me what both parties are missing — for instance, whether they're both equivocating on what "inevitable" means, or conflating the question of whether a discovery was bound to happen with the distinct question of whether it matters who made it. End with an overall verdict on who is more right, or an explanation of why no such verdict is available.
