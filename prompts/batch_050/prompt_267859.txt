I'm designing a diagnostic exercise for a seminar on moral disagreement, and I'd like a decision tree that sorts respondents by their actual stance on compromise between conflicting value systems — cases where each party does something it takes to be wrong in exchange for the other side doing likewise: legislators trading votes on bills each finds objectionable, a devout parent funding an adult child's religiously forbidden project in return for the child forgoing something the parent condemns, or a government granting concessions to an unjust faction in exchange for disarmament. The tree must lead to at least these leaves: that such trades are categorically forbidden because one may never do what one believes wrong; that they are permissible but only ever regretful second-best, never moral improvements; that they can be genuine gains from trade when each party concedes on what it weights less; that they are good for a further reason — expressing respect or solidarity across disagreement; that they are incoherent because the relevant values are incommensurable and there is nothing to exchange; and that they corrupt the parties, making each complicit in the other's wrongdoing rather than reducing wrongdoing overall. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the leaf labels: phrase every node as a question someone could answer without knowing which position it leads toward, use concrete cases rather than abstract principles at at least three nodes, and note any combination of answers no one occupies, with a sentence on whether it is empty because incoherent or merely undefended. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and for each leaf give one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
