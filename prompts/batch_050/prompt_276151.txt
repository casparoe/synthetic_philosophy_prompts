So my department's reading group on the philosophy of love keeps circling the same fight and I want to understand why. The appraisal people (Kolodny, maybe Jollimore) say love has to be grounded in the beloved's qualities, otherwise it's arbitrary and can't justify the special treatment love involves. The bestowal people (Frankfurt, Velleman in a different way) say that gets it backwards -- love isn't a response to value, it's a source of value, and the appraisal view can't explain constancy when qualities change or why we don't "trade up" to a better-qualified person. Every meeting someone rediscovers the trading-up objection, someone rediscovers the arbitrariness objection, and both sides agree these objections are old and neither side moves. My hypothesis is that this isn't really one disagreement: the appraisal camp is answering a question about what justifies love's demands (which is why they focus on reasons and rationality), while the bestowal camp is answering a question about the phenomenology and psychology of attachment (which is why trading-up feels decisive to them), and the two questions just keep getting run together under "the reasons for love." Is that right, or is there a genuinely shared question both sides are failing to settle? If they are talking past each other, say what each question actually is and whether both are legitimate. If they're not, identify the real crux -- is it a disagreement about what a reason is, about the metaphysics of value, about which cases count as data? And either way: what would progress look like -- a distinction, a new test case, or just accepting the impasse?
