I have no philosophical training, but I have been reading about classical Indian epistemology on my own, and I keep getting stuck on one point. In the Nyāya Sūtra of Gautama (Book I, Chapter 1, sūtra 6), comparison (upamāna) is listed as a genuine means of knowledge in its own right: roughly, it is the knowledge one gains of an unfamiliar thing through its similarity to something already well known — as when someone who has never seen a gavaya is told it resembles a cow and later recognizes one on that basis. My trouble is that this seems to give me nothing I could not already get from the other three means of knowledge Gautama admits: perception acquaints me with cows, testimony conveys the comparison, and inference does the rest. So the fourfold list looks padded to me. Please argue the opposite case: that knowledge by comparison cannot be reduced to perception, inference, and testimony working in combination, and that any attempt to reduce it either leaves some of the knowledge unaccounted for or smuggles comparison back in under another name. Engage with the actual structure of the example rather than merely asserting that the tradition must have had its reasons.
