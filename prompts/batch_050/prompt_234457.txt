Write a Shakespearean sonnet about Lewis's triviality result for indicative conditionals. Use the octave to build the intuitive picture: it seems natural that your credence in "if it rains, the picnic is off" should simply be your conditional credence that the picnic is off given rain, as though the conditional named a proposition whose probability tracks that ratio. Let the volta be the moment the picture collapses: Lewis showed that if this equation is to hold not just for your current credences but across every revision of them, then no non-trivial proposition can play the role of the conditional, and your belief states degenerate. Use the final couplet to state precisely what the result forces on us — either the equation fails, or the indicative conditional is not a proposition at all — without softening it into "conditionals are mysterious." The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of language would recognize the actual argument (including why the requirement that the equation survive conditionalization is what drives the collapse) and graceful enough that a non-specialist would still find it a good poem. Maximum 16 lines, and do not append any prose explanation.
