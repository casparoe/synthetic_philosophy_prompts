ok, question about criminal responsibility and psychopathy. In a lot of legal systems, someone who genuinely cannot understand that killing is wrong -- because of psychosis, severe intellectual disability, etc. -- may be excused or treated as less culpable. But a psychopathic defendant usually isn’t. If he can calmly explain that murder is illegal, that other people consider it immoral, and that getting caught will have consequences, courts generally treat him as knowing what he was doing. Yet the same diagnosis can then count against him at sentencing: lack of empathy, absence of remorse, manipulativeness, risk of reoffending, even though those traits are often presented as relatively stable features of the person rather than choices he made.

What theory of moral knowledge does this practice presuppose? Does it assume that moral knowledge is just thin propositional knowledge -- knowing the rule, its source, and the likely penalties -- or is there some coherent sense in which the defendant “knows it is wrong” without morally understanding why it matters? Reconstruct the implicit theory rather than just saying courts want to punish him.

Also, where is the practice underdetermined: which parts strictly require that theory, and which could instead be explained by deterrence, incapacitation, or ordinary retributive judgments? And how should we handle the apparent inconsistency? If affective indifference means the person cannot be moved by moral reasons in the normal way, why doesn’t that mitigate culpability? But if it does not undermine responsibility, why should remorselessness make the sentence worse? Does aggravating punishment for psychopathy quietly presuppose responsibility for one’s character, or is it really just a prediction of future dangerousness? Finally, what would criminal law have to change if we accepted a thicker, affective account of moral knowledge -- and would excusing psychopaths on that basis risk excusing anyone who committed a crime because they simply didn’t care enough about the victem?
