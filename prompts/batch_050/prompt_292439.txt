I am a third-year undergraduate double-majoring in philosophy and biology, and I have been asked to give a short toast at an unusual dinner in a few weeks. I would like your help writing it.

The occasion is the retirement of my philosophy of mind professor, Dr. Mara Whitfield, after thirty-one years. The department is combining her retirement dinner with what the invitation only half-jokingly calls a wake for Hillary: a leafcutter ant colony the department has kept in a glass vitrine in the basement for well over a decade, named by generations of students after Aunt Hillary, the conscious ant colony in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. Hofstadter's colony could carry on a conversation; the individual ants had no more idea what the colony was saying than neurons have of what a brain is thinking. Dr. Whitfield built her career on taking that thought experiment literally. Her view, in her own slogan, is that the mind is not in the ants but in the traffic — and that we refuse to count a colony as a subject only because its thoughts are too slow, too dispersed, and too alien for us to talk back to.

Three weeks ago the colony's queen died. Without her, no new workers will be produced, and the colony is slowly winding down; by the evening of the dinner it may be gone entirely. Hence the combined event.

The audience is the problem. Seated at the front tables will be: several of her functionalist colleagues, who will nod along to colony-as-person talk; her oldest philosophical opponent, a biological naturalist who regards the entire framing as a category mistake; one committed panpsychist graduate student; her wife and two adult children, who have endured thirty years of jokes about being the second great love of her life; and Jim, the animal-care technician who maintained the colony, routinely culled surplus males, and once destroyed a fungus-stricken sub-colony. Jim told me privately that he is dreading the evening, because if the colony was a person, he is not sure what that makes him. The twist I hope the toast can use is that Dr. Whitfield's view actually acquits him: on it, individual ants are cells, and it is the panpsychist's view, not hers, that indicts him.

Two further constraints. First, the real Hillary never did anything conversational; she farmed fungus, foraged, and defended herself. Hofstadter's colony talked, and the toast cannot quietly borrow that drama — whatever is moving in the claim must rest on the colony's organization alone. Second, when a student asked Dr. Whitfield at our final seminar this semester whether she believes the actual colony downstairs is conscious, she declined to answer, saying roughly that the honest position is that we do not know what would settle the question, and that making that difficulty vivid was the point of her work. A toast asserting that the colony was a person would contradict her in front of everyone and raise ghastly questions about fifteen years in a basement; a toast saying it was only ants would undo her at her own retirement dinner.

Please write a toast of about four minutes — five hundred spoken words or so — warm rather than technical, with no vocabulary a family member would need explained, that threads all of this: honoring her thesis without asserting it, saying something true and kind to Jim, acknowledging the difference between a colony that talks and one that merely organizes itself, and ending on the family rather than the ants. After the toast, add two or three sentences telling me the single claim you were most careful not to make.
