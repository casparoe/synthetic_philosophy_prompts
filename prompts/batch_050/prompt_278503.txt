I teach a graduate seminar on epistemic rationality and want to use the 2007–08 financial crisis as a test case for what I call the presumption of independence: when several apparently distinct assessors reach the same conclusion, we provisionally treat their agreement as stronger evidence than any single judgment, unless we have reason to suspect a shared source of error. Before the crisis, many lenders, ratings agencies, investors, and regulators converged on the view that mortgage-backed securities were relatively safe. Their reasoning often assumed that borrower defaults would not become highly correlated across regions, that diversification and tranching would contain localized losses, and that the number and variety of institutions endorsing the products supplied further confirmation. Yet these judgments drew on overlapping historical data, similar modeling conventions, common assumptions about housing prices, and institutional incentives that discouraged dissent.

Analyze the episode through this framework, keeping the analysis bidirectional. First, distinguish at least three possible independence claims: causal independence among borrower defaults, conditional independence among forecasts given the available evidence, and epistemic independence among the institutions issuing the judgments. Which of these would have justified confidence in securitization, and which were actually warranted? Map the theoretical notions onto the case concretely—identify the relevant estimators, evidence, shared background assumptions, and common causes of error—and say what observable facts should have defeated the presumption before the collapse. Then use the episode to test the framework itself: was the error an irrational failure to recognize obvious dependence, a normally reliable heuristic operating under unusually strong model correlation, or evidence that independence should never be presumed but must instead be positively established? Guard against hindsight bias by separating what was reasonably knowable from what became clear only afterward. Conclude by considering which institutional arrangements—genuinely diverse data, independent modeling methods, adversarial review, or structural separation between issuers and evaluators—would make agreement evidentially meaningful again.
