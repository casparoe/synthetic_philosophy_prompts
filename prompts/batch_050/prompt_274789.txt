I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am finishing a profile of a decision scientist who has spent her career on bounded rationality — how people actually choose when they cannot optimize, and when declining to optimize is itself the reasonable thing to do. Over several interviews I put vignettes to her and asked a single question: was this decided well? From my notes:

1. An emergency physician, treating a crashing patient, uses the first airway technique she judges workable rather than pausing to rank every alternative. Verdict: unimpeachable.
2. A buyer in a hot housing market, where good houses sell within days, signs on the first house meeting every item on her written checklist and cancels her remaining viewings. Verdict: sensible.
3. A small-business owner hires the first candidate who passes her work-sample test, from applicants who arrived one at a time over several weeks, with the vacancy costing her money weekly and rejected candidates impossible to recall. Verdict: fine.
4. A diner keeps a waiter standing while she reads the entire menu twice and ranks every dish, then orders what she would have ordered anyway. Verdict: badly calibrated.
5. A man buys the first used car he test-drives even though it lacks two items on his own list of requirements; he keeps the list unchanged and is unhappy with the car for years. Verdict: an error — settling, not satisficing.
6. A job-seeker who, after four months in which every offer fell short of her target salary, accepts one somewhat below it. Verdict: correct; she added that a standard which never bends in light of what a search turns up is not a standard but a fantasy.

For our final interview I intend to put one further case to her, and I want to anticipate her answer in the piece. The director of a nonprofit must choose a payroll-software vendor on a five-year contract. The second of three scheduled demonstrations meets every item on her written requirements list, under budget, and the vendor's quote is good for sixty days. Signing at once would spare her roughly fifteen hours of further evaluation and give her closure. Meanwhile the board's procurement policy in any case holds a three-week window open during which other vendors may submit bids at no cost to her; her assistant would screen any late bids against the requirements list and forward only those that pass, so her total cost of waiting is three more weeks on the current, serviceable system and at most an hour or two of reading. A compliant late bid could be substantially better or substantially cheaper.

Please do the following. First, predict her verdict on this case, and state your confidence. Second, set out the two or three distinct rules her earlier verdicts are each consistent with — satisficing as a stopping rule in its own right, in Herbert Simon's sense; the search-theoretic rejoinder associated with George Stigler, on which "good enough" is merely the point where further search stops paying; and any other serious candidate, such as the view that a heuristic is rational only relative to the structure of its environment — and say what each predicts here and why. Third, tell me which of the six earlier verdicts does the most work in fixing your prediction, and which is the closest analog to the new case. Fourth, if her track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so plainly, and state what each possible verdict would commit her to — including whether her tolerance of the revised salary standard in case 6 marks her as a satisficer with adaptive aspirations or as a bounded optimizer by another name. Fifth, describe the single follow-up vignette that would most cheaply settle which rule she is actually running. Finally, a question of fairness to my subject: would it be accurate, in print, to describe her as a satisficer in Simon's sense, or would that misdescribe her position?
