I have to circulate a short position note for a philosophy of biology reading group, and I want an internal consistency audit before people start poking holes in it. Dont give me a general survey of theories of biological individuality.

Here is the package I am tempted to defend:

1. There is no sharp line between an organism and a mere collectivity, but a system counts as an organism only when the interests of its parts are aligned enough for selection to act mainly on the persistence and reproduction of the whole.

2. Genetic sameness is neither necessary nor sufficient. A human chimera can be one organism, while a genetically uniform clonal colony is not automatically one organism.

3. Functional integration by itself is not enough. Otherwise any stable ecosystem with nutrient cycling and mutually dependent species would count as an organism.

4. A honeybee colony is a genuine superorganism. Worker sterility, colony-level temperature regulation, division of labor, and reproductive swarming align most adaptation with the colony’s persistence and reproduction.

5. A Portuguese man o’ war is not a single organism. It is a colony of zooids, even though the zooids are genetically identical, develop from one founding embryo, are highly specialized, and generally cannot survive independently.

6. Most holobionts are ecological partnerships rather than organisms, because microbial members can be replaced and the host and microbial lineages retain partly opposed reproductive interests.

7. Imagine an invented braid lichen containing a fungus, an alga, and a bacterium. In nature, all three are normally dispersed together in the same reproductive fragment, and the fragment has drought resistance that none of the partners has alone. Each lineage can still be separated in a laboratory and reproduce independently. I would call the braid lichen one organism because the common fragment gives selection a whole-system unit to act on.

8. The same system can count as an organism for one explanatory purpose and as a population or community for another. This kind of pluralism does not make the labels arbitrary.

9. Replacement of parts does not by itself threaten individuality. An organism can persist through continual turnover of cells, microbes, or other components as long as its organization persists.

Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? Please rank the tensions by severity, explain what each side of a tension presupposes, and identify the smallest revision that would resolve it. If I could revise only one commitment, which change would make the overall position most coherent, and what would my repaired view imply about the man o’ war and the braid lichen? Answer in American English.
