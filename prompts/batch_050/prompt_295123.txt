So I was rereading Book I of Aristotle's Politics (in the Rackham translation) and got stuck on this bit where he's in the middle of arguing that slaves are basically living tools:

"And every assistant is as it were a tool that serves for several tools; for if every tool could perform its own work when ordered, or by seeing what to do in advance, like the statues of Daedalus in the story, or the tripods of Hephaestus which the poet says 'enter self-moved the company divine,' —if thus shuttles wove and quills played harps of themselves, master-craftsmen would have no need of assistants and masters no need of slaves."

Write an essay about what this passage does to Aristotle's infamous doctrine of natural slavery. Here's the tension I want explored. On one reading, the passage is a quiet bombshell: if the whole point of a slave is to be an instrument that gets the household's work done, and self-acting tools would make slaves unnecessary, then slavery looks like a purely instrumental arrangement that better technology would dissolve — which sits very oddly with the claim, made a few paragraphs later, that some people are slaves by nature because they can apprehend reason without possessing it. Nature doesn't stop making natural slaves just because the shuttle weaves itself. On the other reading, the passage changes nothing: the natural slave is defined by a psychological deficiency, not by his economic function, so the automation fantasy is a throwaway remark about convenience, and the doctrine stands or falls on whether such deficient humans exist (Aristotle thinks they do; we think he's wrong, or maybe that he's describing people brutalized by slavery itself). You can also consider a third option if you find it more defensible: that the passage reveals Aristotle himself half-sensing the arbitrariness of the institution, like when he admits some people think slavery exists only by convention and force.

Take the passage seriously as evidence — what work is it doing in the argument, why is it phrased as a counterfactual, and does Aristotle seem to notice the problem it creates? Weigh the readings against each other, but I want a real verdict at the end, not a shrug: does the self-moving tools passage undermine the natural slavery doctrine, or is it compatible with it? Defend whichever answer you land on.

Some ground rules: aim for somewhere around 700-900 words (the quoted passage doesn't count toward that), and write it as continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just paragraphs like you'd write in a long email to a friend. Don't bother with citations or a bibliography, just engage with the text itself.
