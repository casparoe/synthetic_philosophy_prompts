Last night a friend and I spent the better part of three hours arguing about whether photographs let us literally see the things they depict, rather than merely showing us images of them, and we parted without resolution. She defended what she called a transparency thesis: when I look at a photograph of my late grandfather, whom I never met, I am genuinely seeing him, much as I see myself in a mirror or a bird through binoculars, because the image depends counterfactually on the scene in a mechanical way that no one's beliefs or intentions mediate. I found myself mostly agreeing, but when we started trading cases, my own verdicts turned out to be a mess, and I'd like to know whether any single principle lies beneath them.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them. Mirror: clearly seeing. Telescope and microscope: clearly seeing. A live closed-circuit feed of the building's lobby: I want to say I'm seeing the lobby. A photograph of my grandfather: yes, I'm seeing him, in the only sense that matters here. A competent oil portrait painted from life: no, that's depiction only, even if it's more accurate than a bad photo. A photorealistic pencil drawing: still no. A fully AI-generated image produced from a text description of a scene that actually exists: no. An ultrasound image during a pregnancy, despite the false colors and heavy computation: yes, the parents really do see the baby. A photograph that was retouched in the darkroom: still seeing, at least the unretouched parts. And then the pair that keeps me up: I think a fifty-year-old photograph still lets me see the long-demolished street it shows, but when my friend asked whether a surveillance feed delayed by thirty seconds still counts as seeing the hallway live, I found myself wanting to say no — and I cannot for the life of me justify why thirty years of temporal distance is harmless while thirty seconds breaks the connection, especially given that the photograph's causal chain is, if anything, more attenuated.

So: is there one principle that delivers all of these verdicts, or close? My suspicion is that something like mechanical counterfactual dependence on the scene, independent of anyone's intentional states, does the work for the painting, drawing, and AI cases, and maybe for the ultrasound; but I don't see how it adjudicates the delayed feed, or whether the principle needs a clause about the kind of information transmitted — say, whether the image carries egocentric spatial information about where the object was relative to the camera, as it seemed to us a mirror does and a description never does. Please state the best candidate principle (or two rivals, if the field genuinely splits) precisely, tell me which of my verdicts each version forces me to abandon, and say what the surviving account predicts for three cases we never discussed: a photograph of a mirror reflection, a camera-trap photograph taken with no human involvement whatsoever, and a real-time video feed from a rover on Mars. If no coherent principle saves more than most of my verdicts, I'd rather hear that than a gerrymandered one that saves them all.
