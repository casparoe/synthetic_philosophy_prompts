I run a small philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a philosopher of religion defending the view that testimony can rationally justify believing a miracle. She thinks Hume's argument in Section X of the Enquiry ("Of Miracles") simply fails, but she's slippery about saying exactly where. I want to pin her down using this passage from Part I, which my whole line of attack turns on:

"Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony."

Give me exactly eight interview questions as a numbered list, each a sentence or two with a follow-up keyed to the answer she'll most likely give, no intro and no closing. The spine I have in mind: if the only reason we trust witnesses at all is the experienced match between reports and facts, doesn't believing a miracle on testimony saw off the branch the believer is sitting on? She'll surely answer that testimony has corrected experience plenty of times (Hume's own Indian prince and the frost, anomalies in science that forced revisions), so press her on the difference between overturning a generalization by repeatable observation and by a one-off report, and on whether her view contains any principled rule for deciding which extraordinary testimonies to accept and which to bin. End with a question about what evidence would actually change her mind.
