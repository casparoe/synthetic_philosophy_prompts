I am a TA grading undergraduate essays for a course on the ethics of collective wrongdoing, and I would like your help assessing one student's answer. Please assign a grade out of 100 and provide the specific point-by-point comments that justify it: identify what the answer gets right, where it goes wrong, and where it says something defensible but underdeveloped. I want the comments concrete enough to hand back to the student, so please tie each criticism to the particular step in the answer rather than offering a general impression.

Essay question:

Individual participation in collective wrongdoing often appears to make no difference to the outcome: the harm would have occurred anyway, at the same scale, had the individual abstained. Explain why this "no-difference" observation generates a problem for holding such individuals complicit. Then set out two strategies for grounding complicity despite it — one that grounds complicity in participation in jointly intentional activity, and one that grounds it in an agent's contribution to the wrongful features of an outcome or structure regardless of difference-making. For each strategy, state one type of case it handles well and one it struggles with.

Student's answer:

The no-difference problem arises because collective harms are typically overdetermined: each participant's contribution is individually sufficient for the harm, so no one can be singled out as its cause. If complicity requires causing wrongdoing, then no individual participant is complicit, which seems wrong — we do want to say that each member of a firing squad is complicit in the execution even though any one member's shot would have killed the victim regardless.

The participatory strategy responds by locating complicity not in causation but in shared intention. An agent is complicit when she intentionally takes part in a jointly intentional activity that is wrongful, as when she does her part in a plan she shares with others. This handles coordinated wrongdoing well: members of a conspiracy or a mob acting on a common purpose are complicit in the group's deeds because of what they jointly intend, not because of what each causes. It struggles, however, with uncoordinated cases — millions of consumers each buying cheap clothes contribute to sweatshop labor without sharing any intention, and the strategy seems to let them all off the hook.

The contributory strategy instead grounds complicity in whether the agent's action contributes to the wrongful features of the outcome. Contribution here is measured counterfactually: the consumer's purchase is complicit because, had she not bought the garment, the sweatshop system would have been marginally less supported. This handles diffuse, uncoordinated wrongdoing well, since each purchase does make a tiny counterfactual difference. It struggles with cases where the counterfactual contribution is zero — for instance, when a supplier would have made up any shortfall in demand — so it cannot fully escape the no-difference problem it was meant to solve.

On balance I find the participatory strategy more defensible, since complicity is fundamentally a matter of the agent's relation to a wrong rather than of tiny causal effects, and the consumer cases can perhaps be handled by widening what counts as intentional participation.
