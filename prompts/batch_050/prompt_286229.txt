I coordinate the contested-claims desk for a mid-sized public library system. Patrons submit challenges to factual claims we make or platform -- in exhibits, in program descriptions, in the recommended-reading lists we publish -- and my office decides which challenges get a formal review (staff time, consultation with an outside expert, a written response posted to the patron) and which get a brief form-letter reply declining to engage. We receive roughly thirty challenges a month. Budget covers about five formal reviews; the head of programs signs off on each one. Some challenges concern safety (water quality, vaccine statements in a health display); some are sincere but strange; some are repeated filings by the same individuals aimed at particular staff; and experience shows that both errors -- dismissing a genuine doubt, and dignifying a challenge that dissolves the terms of any possible inquiry -- cost us public trust.

I want a decision procedure my two assistants can run on an incoming challenge in about twenty minutes, grounded in the picture of doubt Wittgenstein develops in On Certainty. I have in mind in particular the remark at §105, where he says that all testing, confirmation, and disconfirmation of a hypothesis already takes place within a system, and that this system is not a doubtful starting point for our arguments but rather the element in which arguments have their life -- the medium without which there is no arguing at all. Take that remark seriously as a design constraint. The procedure should be a sequence of questions, in order, each with what counts as a sufficient answer, built roughly around these ideas: whether the challenger accepts the practices and instruments by which the disputed claim could in principle be checked; whether the doubt is offered on grounds, and whether those grounds are themselves more certain than what they are meant to undermine; what background propositions the doubt must exempt from doubt in order to be statable at all; and whether a formal review is the kind of thing that could settle the matter for this challenger. Specify where in the sequence the procedure terminates in a form-letter response, where it terminates in a formal review, and where it hands the case to me because the question is not about the claim but about the challenger.

Then run the procedure on three recent cases and show the working at each step: a patron who challenges our water-safety display, accepting the relevant testing agencies' methods but disputing whether they were properly applied to our municipal supply; a patron who challenges the globe in our children's room, holding that all photographic and testimonial evidence for the Earth's shape is fabricated, and who has filed nine previous challenges; and a patron who asks, in writing, how the library can be certain that the staff member who checked out their book existed at all, citing a philosophy podcast. Make one of these a case where the procedure's verdict will annoy someone it would be politically easier to please -- either by recommending a formal review of a challenge my board would rather dismiss, or by declining one a loud constituency wants honored -- and say so explicitly.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which distinctions from On Certainty had to survive the translation into a checklist and which I am losing, which recurring challenges it will misclassify, and at which steps my assistants will be exercising judgment that the procedure merely disguises as a rule.
