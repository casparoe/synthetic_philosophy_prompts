You are an expert historian and historiographical methodologist advising an interdisciplinary research institute. Think carefully about the causal and normative distinctions involved before presenting your final answer; begin by brainstorming the main ways that environmental explanations in history can go wrong, then organize your conclusions into a practical form.

Our institute reviews thirty to forty humanities grant proposals each year that assign major historical influence to non-human forces such as pathogens, climatic shifts, volcanic eruptions, crop failures, floods, and changing ecosystems. The review board has recently adopted the following principle, which it calls distributed causal agency:

Non-human processes can function as indispensable conditions, triggers, accelerants, constraints, or selective pressures, but calling them historical actors is justified only when the proposal specifies the mechanisms through which their effects entered human practices, institutions, beliefs, and decisions. A strong proposal must neither treat environmental change as an irresistible command nor portray human beings as perfectly autonomous agents merely inconvenienced by nature. It should identify relevant human choices and social structures without reducing every outcome to politics, culture, or ideas. Because climate, disease, and geological events have often been invoked to justify racial, civilizational, or geographical hierarchies, the principle also requires an explicit audit of any claim that a population’s character, intelligence, political capacity, or moral worth follows from its environment.

Please turn this principle into a decision procedure that a competent reviewer can apply to a standard ten-page proposal in under ninety minutes, without specialist knowledge of the relevant period or access to external research. The procedure should include:

1. An ordered sequence of questions, beginning with quick screening questions and moving to more demanding causal tests.
2. For each question, what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as a warning sign, and what counts as an automatic failure.
3. A method for distinguishing whether the non-human factor is being presented as background condition, trigger, amplifier, constraint, selective pressure, or direct agent, including guidance for cases where it occupies more than one role.
4. A counterfactual test that helps reviewers ask whether the proposed causal factor was necessary, sufficient, merely probabilistic, or rhetorically decorative.
5. A mediation test requiring the applicant to identify plausible institutions, technologies, beliefs, practices, or decision-makers through which the environmental influence operated.
6. An alternatives test that requires serious consideration of at least one human-centered explanation and one different non-human explanation, without allowing the demand for alternatives to become an excuse for rejecting all causal reasoning.
7. A moral and political risk audit for claims that environmental conditions supposedly produced durable traits in a population, with distinctions among empirical risk, speculative overreach, and intrinsically hierarchical reasoning.
8. Clear decision categories: approve, approve with required revisions, send for specialist review, or reject. State which questions may stop the review immediately and which deficiencies must instead be weighed cumulatively.
9. Explicit points at which the procedure hands the decision to human judgment because the issue concerns values or disciplinary standards rather than factual adequacy.
10. A short statement of what the procedure cannot detect, especially where specialized empirical knowledge is indispensable.

Then apply the procedure in detail to the following three hypothetical proposals:

- A volcanic eruption produces several years of cold summers and poor harvests. The applicant argues that the eruption caused a distant kingdom’s revolution and asks reviewers to treat the volcano as the revolution’s principal historical actor.
- A recurring epidemic leads a large city to create quarantine offices, records of mortality, and eventually permanent public-health institutions. The applicant argues both that the epidemic transformed governance and that prior legal ideas and administrative rivalries shaped the form of that transformation.
- A prolonged regional drought coincides with migration from a rural society. The applicant argues that the drought proves that the society was inherently fragile and that its members lacked the adaptive capacity possessed by neighboring peoples.

For each application, show the procedure’s reasoning step by step and state the resulting decision category. At least one application should produce a decision that the hypothetical applicant would strongly dislike even though parts of the project are intellectually promising. Conclude with a concise one-page checklist that reviewers could copy into an evaluation form.
