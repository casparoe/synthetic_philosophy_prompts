I run a small nonprofit — a network of three food pantries in a mid-sized city — and I have no background in philosophy or mathematics, though I keep our books, so I suppose I think in expected values more than most people. Something that happened at our spring fundraiser last year has been nagging at me ever since, and at this year's dinner I have to address it publicly. I'd like you to write the speech.

Last year, during the dessert course, a man I didn't know handed me an envelope. Inside was a signed letter pledging $12 million to our organization — enough to run all three pantries in perpetuity — on two conditions: that I mention him by name in my remarks that evening and toast the certain success of his longevity research foundation, and that I never afterward publicly express doubt about it. I did not give the toast. He left before coffee. Since then I've learned a little about him: he is real, he is wealthy, though almost certainly not $12 million wealthy, and his foundation appears to consist of a website and a post office box. My honest estimate is that the odds of the pledge ever being honored were somewhere between one in a thousand and one in a hundred thousand, and I cannot narrow it further.

Here is what bothers me. I did the arithmetic on a napkin that night. Even at one in ten thousand, the expected value of giving the toast was $1,200 — more than I ask most of our donors for — and the cost was ninety seconds of personal embarrassment. By my own bookkeeping logic, declining was the single most expensive decision I have ever made: over a thousand dollars of expected meals, forfeited. And yet I have never for one moment felt I did anything wrong. I feel that I declined to be conned, and that a policy of doing the arithmetic on such offers would ruin me, because anyone can always name a bigger number, and if I pay up whenever the number is big enough I will spend the rest of my life being auctioned off to whoever lies most extravagantly. My friend Carol, who teaches statistics at the community college, tells me philosophers have a name for this predicament — something about a mugging — and that serious people disagree about what to do.

The man has written to me again. The offer stands, doubled: $24 million, same conditions. This time he will be in the room.

Write the five-to-seven-minute speech I will give at the dinner. The audience is mixed: our regular donors and volunteers, who know nothing about any of this; my board treasurer, who has seen the letter and thinks I'm a fool for hesitating; and Carol, who will be at table six checking my reasoning. The speech must do several things at once. It must explain to the whole room, without jargon, what the offer is and why the arithmetic tempts me. It must accept or decline the offer out loud, by name. And it must give a reason for the decision that I could actually defend — one that explains why this case is or isn't different from buying a lottery ticket, or from the everyday long shots, like grant applications and cold calls to foundations, that are simply part of my job. If there is a principled line between the long shots I should take and the ones I shouldn't, I want the speech to draw it in plain words and to hold up when Carol looks it over afterward. What I do not want is a speech that hides behind eloquence and dodges the decision; the room will know if I dodge.

End the speech itself with the toast or the refusal, and then, as the very last line of your response, state the decision in the form: Verdict: accept or Verdict: decline.
