so last week our reading group did Descartes' Discourse on Method and afterwards two friends of mine, Sofia and Marcus, got into an arguement at the pub that's still going in the group chat. it started over this passage from Part One (in the old Veitch translation):

"And besides these, I know of no other qualities that contribute to the perfection of the mind; and as to the reason or sense, inasmuch as it is that alone which constitutes us men, and distinguishes us from the brutes, I am disposed to believe that it is to be found complete in each individual; and on this point to adopt the common opinion of philosophers, who say that the difference of greater and less holds only among the accidents, and not among the forms or natures of individuals of the same species."

Relevant background: in Part Five Descartes gives his two famous tests for telling a real human from a machine built to imitate one. Roughly: first, no animal or machine could string words together so as to give an apt reply to whatever is said in its presence, the way even the dimmest person can; and second, reason works in every situation whereas each part of a machine is built for one particular action, so a machine may beat us at some tasks but will inevitably collapse at others.

Sofia's opening move: take the Part One passage seriously and there's no half-reason in Descartes — it's a form, you have it whole or not at all. So his Part Five language test is all-or-nothing too. A modern chatbot strings words together and replies aptly to basically anything, which even clever animals never did, so by Descartes' own criterion it has the form, full stop, and since reason comes complete in each individual there's no such thing as it having a bit of a mind.

Marcus: the Part One passage is about how reason is distributed among things that already have the form of man — equal shares for peasants and professors. It says nothing about how you detect the form, so you can't use it to upgrade a test into a definition. And the second test is the one that matters for artifacts: a chatbot's apparent generality is just an enormous pile of particular dispositions trained on text, exactly the picture Descartes gives of a machine — lots of special arrangements, no universal instrument. It can talk about everything but can't act in the general run of life's situations, which is what his test was about.

Sofia: two problems. First, the failure profile is the reverse of his prediction — Descartes said a machine would shine at narrow tasks and fall over at open-ended conversation, and what we got falls over at counting letters while chatting fluently about grief. His empirical bet about what mechanism can do just lost. Second, if every behavioral success gets redescribed as mere disposition of parts, the tests are unfalsifiable and his conclusion about animals was never a discovery, just dogma with a test-shaped costume.

Marcus: no, the costume point cuts at you — his whole framework has an immaterial soul doing the work, so the tests were only ever signs, and signs can misfire. Also his brute-animal claims being empirically wrong (signing apes, tool-using crows, that whole literature) shows the tests were bad even on their own terms, which your chatbot arguement inherits.

I stayed out of it, partly because I genuinely can't tell who's winning. Can you go through the exchange point by point and say who's right about each specific claim — the form-vs-accident reading, whether the tests are definitions or signs, the reversed failure profile, the unfalsifiability charge, and the soul point — rather than just giving an overall winner? And is there something about how the Part One passage and the Part Five tests fit together that both of them are missing?
