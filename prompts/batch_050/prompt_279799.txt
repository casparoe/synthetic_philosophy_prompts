Since retiring from a career in molecular biology I have been working through moral philosophy with a small reading group, and at our last meeting one member closed a discussion of anti-natalism with the line better never to have been, offered as a complete argument that prospective parents wrong their children simply by creating them, however good those children's lives might turn out to be. I suspect that sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and I would like them separated. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan might be making — for instance, a pessimistic empirical claim about how much suffering ordinary lives actually contain, a comparative claim that never existing can be better for someone than existing, and a structural claim that the badness of pain and the goodness of pleasure are not symmetric in a way that is meant to settle the question without any pessimism at all. For each reading, say whether it amounts to a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something false or incoherent, and whether it would in fact support the use my colleague made of it. Then commit: tell me which single reading you take to be the strongest version of the slogan, whether on that reading it really delivers the conclusion that procreation is always a harm, and where you judge the argument to be most vulnerable.
