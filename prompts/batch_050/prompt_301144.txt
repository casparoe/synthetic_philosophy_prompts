I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a magazine piece on Robert Brandom's inferentialism — the view, roughly, that an expression's meaning is its role in reasoning: what follows from claims involving it, and what one must establish to be entitled to those claims. A section of my draft contains an argument that I now suspect proves too much, and I would like a diagnosis before it goes to my editor.

(1) On inferentialism, an expression's meaning just is its inferential role — the full set of inferences in which it figures.
(2) No two speakers endorse exactly the same inferences involving any given word. I infer from "raven" that ravens are birds; a young child does not; an ornithologist draws consequences neither of us could.
(3) So "raven" has a different inferential role — and therefore a different meaning — in every mouth.
(4) Genuine disagreement requires that one party affirm the very content the other denies; if your "raven" and mine differ in meaning, we have never actually disagreed about ravens, nor about anything else.
(5) People plainly do disagree, and communicate, all the time.
(6) Therefore inferentialism is false — and since any use-based theory individuates meaning by speakers' actual inferential habits, every theory in that family falls with it.

What worries me is that (6) appears to refute not only Brandom but any account on which meaning depends on use, and even to make meaning essentially private — which feels like more than this argument should be able to buy. Yet each premise looked harmless when I wrote it down. Please tell me which premise or inference you would reject, explain why it looked innocent, and say what rejecting it costs — both for me and for the inferentialist — compared with rejecting one of its neighbors. If forced to choose, I would rather keep (4) and (5) and let (1) take the strain, but I want to know what I am paying either way. Please write your answer in American English.
