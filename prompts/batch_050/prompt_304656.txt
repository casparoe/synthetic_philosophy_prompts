I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my desk, like every desk I know of, publishes generational journalism at a steady clip: pieces about how Gen Z approaches work, what Millennials did to homeownership, whether Boomers will ever retire. The practice has some very specific conventions that I've started to find philosophically strange. We treat the boundaries as fixed facts -- Pew's cutoffs get cited the way you'd cite census figures -- even though the people who draw them admit they're conventions. We explain cohort differences almost exclusively by "formative events" (9/11, the financial crisis, smartphones), which assumes a particular window of youth in which a person's outlook gets set for life. And we freely ascribe dispositions to cohorts -- entitlement, pragmatism, risk-aversion -- while in the same publications running pieces that rightly criticize racial or gender generalizations of exactly the same logical shape.

Demographers keep telling us the whole thing is rotten: that measured "generation gaps" mostly dissolve into age effects (everyone is different at 25) and period effects (everyone changed when smartphones arrived), and that a generation, unlike a birth year, is not something data can detect but something the analyst imposes. Yet the practice survives this criticism completely unscathed, which suggests it's serving a purpose that has nothing to do with empirical accuracy.

So my question: what ontology of social kinds does generational talk actually presuppose? Does it require generations to be something like natural kinds -- real joints in the social population with essences and boundaries -- or could a sophisticated user of the practice get by treating them as useful fictions, statistical clusters, or self-fulfilling labels that people grow into once they're named? Does the "formative events" convention presuppose a specific, and contestable, picture of how identity and values develop? And is there an asymmetry problem: does the practice presuppose that generalizing about 70 million people by birth year is legitimate in a way that generalizing by other shared traits is not, and if so, on what grounds?

Finally, the practical question for my own work: what would journalism that dropped the presupposed ontology actually look like? If I reported cohort differences strictly as age and period effects, or treated generational identity as something manufactured by the labeling itself rather than discovered, which of our standard story forms would survive, and which turn out to be artifacts of the theory rather than reports of anything in the world?
