At a lab meeting I recently sat in on, two researchers, Elena and Sam, got into an extended disagreement about how to supervise a language model being fine-tuned to give investment advice to retail clients. I followed most of it but couldn't settle who won, and I'd like a careful adjudication.

Elena's opening position was that supervision should target outcomes only: whether the advice the model gives is actually sound. Her argument was that human reviewers cannot reliably evaluate the intermediate reasoning of a system that may soon reason better than they do, so rewarding steps that *look* good to reviewers just trains the model to produce reasoning that is legible and persuasive to humans rather than reasoning that is correct. Outcome rewards, by contrast, leave the model free to discover whatever internal process actually works, even if that process is alien to us.

Sam replied that outcome-only supervision has a fatal blind spot: a model can reach a correct answer for the wrong reasons, and if luck and flawed heuristics are rewarded whenever they happen to work, you are training exactly those heuristics. Worse, outcomes are often only checkable long after the fact — the quality of investment advice only reveals itself over years — so in many domains there is no timely outcome signal to train on at all. Process supervision, he said, gives a denser signal and is the only way to keep the stated reasoning honest rather than decorative.

Elena answered that this cuts both ways: outcome rewards at least anchor the model to reality, whereas process rewards anchor it to the reviewers' prejudices. A model trained to please step-checkers will learn to produce confident, well-structured reasoning that conceals its errors — a subtler failure than a visible wrong answer, and harder to catch. If outcomes are too slow or noisy to measure, she said, the right response is to invest in better outcome measurement, not to retreat to supervising appearances.

Sam's final move was to concede that process supervision is gameable but deny that outcome supervision escapes the problem: a model rewarded on judged outcomes will learn to produce outcomes that *look* correct to evaluators — telling clients what they want to hear, or giving answers whose flaws only an expert would notice. And in domains with no ground truth at reasonable cost, he argued, judging the quality of the reasoning is simply the only option available; dismissing it wholesale amounts to dismissing most of how humans supervise one another, since we routinely evaluate arguments whose conclusions we cannot independently verify.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each of the four moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Then give an overall verdict on whose position is stronger, and identify anything important that both Elena and Sam are missing.
