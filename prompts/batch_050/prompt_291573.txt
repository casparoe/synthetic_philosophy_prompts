I'm preparing a recorded interview for a graduate seminar series on causation. My guest is the author of a new book defending a criterion she calls intervention preservation: two systems count as running the same mechanism just in case there is a mapping between their component variables such that every surgical intervention on one system — setting a variable to a value while severing its usual upstream influences — produces, under the mapping, the same qualitative downstream effects in the other. Her stated application is anomaly detection: a monitor learns the mechanism of a healthy process and flags a process as anomalous when the learned intervention profile no longer transfers to it. She is quick on her feet, and her favorite defensive move is to say that apparent counterexamples merely chose the wrong variables or the wrong grain of description.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, ordered so that this grain-of-description defense becomes progressively more expensive to deploy. At a minimum, I want the sequence to include:

- One question on the triviality worry: given free choice of variables and grain, can intervention preservation be satisfied by any two systems with matching input-output behavior, so that 'same mechanism' quietly collapses into 'same function'? Follow up for when she replies that grain is not chosen but fixed by the interventionist practice itself.
- One question on realization: a faithful real-time simulation of a furnace versus the furnace. If the criterion certifies them as the same mechanism, has 'mechanism' lost the causal bite that was supposed to distinguish it from a mere model; if it does not, where exactly in the criterion does the disqualification enter?
- One question about feedback: her illustrations all use acyclic graphs, but physiological and industrial processes are shot through with loops. How does the criterion handle an intervention on a variable that changes the very upstream influences the intervention was defined as breaking?
- One question on absential structure: a mechanism whose normal operation depends on something not happening — a valve that stays shut, a transcription factor that stays suppressed. Are absences component variables? If not, what does the criterion say about a second system that reproduces the same intervention profile without anything corresponding to the blockage?
- One question built around a masked-failure case I will describe to her on air: a process whose internal causal structure has silently reorganized — a supply line rerouted through a redundant path after a partial failure — while every measurable output stays normal. Her detector sees no anomaly; her criterion presumably registers a different mechanism. Which should the engineer trust, and is she really offering an epistemic tool or a metaphysical definition?
- One question constructed as a sorites: a mechanism in which one component per week is replaced by a functional substitute from a different causal family. Intervention preservation never fails between consecutive weeks, but after two years it fails between the endpoints. Since sameness cannot be non-transitive, where did the original mechanism go — and what does her answer imply for a detector that only ever compares today with yesterday?
- A closing question asking what would count as evidence against intervention preservation itself: what would a pair of systems have to be like for her to concede that sameness of mechanism outruns sameness of intervention profiles?

For each question, give the answer you most expect her to give and one follow-up keyed to it, and flag any question where you think she has no good answer so that I know where to linger.
