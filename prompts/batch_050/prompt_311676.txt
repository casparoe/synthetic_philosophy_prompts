I am taking a seminar in normative ethics, and for a term paper on supererogation I need to know whether my judgments about particular cases can be brought under a single principle. My verdicts are as follows.

(1) A passerby who wades into a shallow pond to rescue a drowning child, ruining an expensive coat in the process, has done nothing beyond his duty; walking on would be blameworthy.

(2) An office worker who donates the price of such a coat to a charity that will, with high reliability, use it to save a child's life has acted supererogatorily: admirable, but not required, and not blameworthy for refraining.

(3) A soldier who throws himself onto a grenade to shield his comrades acts supererogatorily, even though the act was fully within his power; so whatever keeps it from being a duty, it is not that he could not have done it.

(4) A parent who takes a second job for years to pay for a child's medical treatment is doing her duty, not exceeding it — even though the total sacrifice far exceeds the soldier's.

(5) Offering one's train seat to an exhausted stranger is merely decent: neither required nor supererogatory, and its omission is at most mildly criticizable.

(6) There is a genuine, non-conventional line between duty and what lies beyond it, yet no threshold of cost such that a marginal increase in what an act costs the agent flips it from obligatory to optional.

What principle, if any, captures all six? Please state the best one or two candidates precisely, identify which verdicts motivate which clauses, and tell me which of my verdicts the simplest adequate principle would force me to abandon. I would also like the principle's verdicts on cases I have not listed: a donor who pays for a cure for one named, identifiable stranger; a rescuer who would have to take a year of unpaid leave; and a case in which fifty bystanders could each save the child single-handedly. Finally, if no single principle accommodates the whole set, say which pair of verdicts is doing the damage and what each possible resolution costs.
