my ancient history prof has a favorite line: societies don't die, they go bankrupt. collapse, he says, is like corporate bankruptcy -- it feels like the end of the world from inside, it's brutal for some people, but most bankruptcies end in restructuring, not liquidation: the assets get sold off, the workers get hired by the successor firm, and life goes on under a new name. he leans on this to argue that the Late Bronze Age "collapse" and the fall of Rome were mostly transformations, and that we should stop treating collapse as the worst-case scenario for modern states -- it's a reorganization, not an extinction.

i want to know how much weight this analogy can actually bear. audit it for me: what maps onto what (who are the creditors, who are the shareholders, is there anything like a bankruptcy court or a legal floor that protects people during the process, and what's "liquidation" for a civilization?), where the mapping breaks (can a society's losses be distributed the way debts can? is there anyone to pick up the pieces if there's no successor firm?), and whether the "collapse isn't the worst case" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. also stress-test it against cases where the analogy seems to strain: the Classic Maya, and Easter Island -- restructuring or liquidation?

give me your answer as a table with these columns: element of the bankruptcy analogy | proposed societal counterpart | does the mapping hold (yes / partial / no) | what this costs or saves the prof's conclusion. then a short bottom-line verdict after the table.
