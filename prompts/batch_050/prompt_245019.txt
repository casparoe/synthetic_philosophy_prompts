I am a grants officer at a mid-sized charitable foundation. Every quarter, our committee distributes a fixed budget across roughly a dozen proposals, most of which benefit identifiable groups that differ greatly in how well off they are: homeless outreach in our own city, a deworming programme abroad, a subsidy for university scholarships, a village water scheme, and so on. At present we decide by unstructured discussion, and the outcomes are erratic. I would like us to allocate in line with prioritarianism rather than simple cost-per-benefit aggregation: improvements in people's lives should count for more the worse off those people are, though the total amount of good done must still matter, and we should not be in the business of making anyone worse off merely to narrow a gap.

Please write a decision procedure the committee could actually run within a two-hour meeting, with no member having formal training in ethics or economics. It should specify, in order: the questions to ask about each proposal and what counts as an adequate answer; how to construct a defensible ranking of beneficiary groups by how well off they are when our data are coarse and we cannot put everyone on a single numerical scale; how to set and apply a priority weight, and how to test whether a ranking of proposals is sensitive to the exact weight chosen; what the procedure should say when the only equality-improving option available makes some people worse off and no one better off, and whether it should treat that case differently from one where resources move from better-off to worse-off groups at some loss of overall efficiency; and the points at which the procedure must stop and hand the question back to the committee because it is a matter of values rather than analysis.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a cheap intervention producing small but certain benefits for a destitute group, against a costlier one producing large benefits for a merely moderately comfortable group; a proposal to withdraw funding from a well-provided service and redirect it to a worse-off group, where the redirection loses about a third of the value in transit; and a proposal that would reduce measured inequality across our portfolio purely by cutting a service to a comparatively advantaged group, with the savings returning unspent to the endowment. At least one of these should produce a recommendation our board will find uncomfortable. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot settle and where it is liable to misfire. Please write the whole answer in British English.
