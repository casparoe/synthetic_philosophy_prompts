So I've been properly scooped. Two weeks before I submitted my paper, a group in Singapore published basically the same result -- same method, same finding, and as far as I can tell completely independent, no leaked draft, nothing dodgy. Just the same idea arriving at the same time, because the tools and the datasets were all sitting there for anyone to pick up.

Annoyingly, this happened right before I'm due to give our department's history-and-philosophy-of-science seminar, and I'd already pitched a talk arguing that simultaneous discovery shows most findings are roughly inevitable given the state of a field, and that our whole priority-based reward system (first-past-the-post publication credit, citation counting, the Nobel three-person rule) therefore misallocates recognition. I want to argue we should be crediting the conditions that made a discovery possible, or at the very least caring far less about who got there first. Now the talk is obviously going to look autobiographical, which I need to handle.

My argument leans heavily on Wallace's 1858 Ternate paper, "On the Tendency of Varieties to Depart Indefinitely from the Original Type". My reading of it is that Wallace doesn't present natural selection as a bolt from the blue at all -- he builds it up out of facts any competent working naturalist of the time already had in hand (the struggle for existence, variation, what breeders had long shown about domestic varieties), so that the theory reads as almost forced once you put the pieces together. The fact that he and Darwin arrived at it independently is, for me, the exhibit: the discovery was in the air, and who "made" it is closer to an accident of biography than we're comfortable admitting.

The audience: mostly historians of science, who I gather have a professional allergy to inevitabilism and think in terms of contingency; a couple of philosophers of biology; and -- the awkward bit -- a handful of working scientists, including one professor who famously won a priority dispute early in his career and whose standing partly rests on it. No one else from my field.

Prepare me for the Q&A. I want the objections I'll actually get from this lot (not from my labmates), roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way a historian of science or a philosopher would actually put them, with a note on how much damage each one does to the talk. For each: what I should just concede up front, what I should hold my ground on, a two-minute reply, and where the reply is soft. I especially want to know whether my reading of the Wallace paper will survive contact with people who've actually studied the Darwin--Wallace episode, since I'm leaning on it as my main historical anchor and I'm not a historian. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- though I suspect it's the obvious one about whether this is all just sour grapes over getting scooped, in which case I need a better answer than "no it isn't".
