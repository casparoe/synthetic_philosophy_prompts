After a long argument with a friend of mine — a philosopher of biology — about the units of selection, I want to test whether her position is actually as settled as she claims, and I'd like your help. Please take on the role of an expert in the levels-of-selection debate: think carefully, begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that could underlie her verdicts, and only then give your prediction.

Midway through our argument she read me this passage from Darwin's On the Origin of Species, from his discussion of the puzzle of sterile insects, and said it settled the matter:

"This difficulty, though appearing insuperable, is lessened, or, as I believe, disappears, when it is remembered that selection may be applied to the family, as well as to the individual, and may thus gain the desired end. Breeders of cattle wish the flesh and fat to be well marbled together. An animal thus characterised has been slaughtered, but the breeder has gone with confidence to the same stock and has succeeded."

Her verdicts on the cases we worked through were as follows. Transposable elements proliferating within a genome at a slight cost to their host organism: genuine selection operating below the organismic level, not redescribable as selection on organisms. Worker sterility in honeybees: selection above the individual level, at the level of the colony or family, exactly as Darwin says. A mutator allele that spreads because the lineages carrying it occasionally hit upon beneficial mutations: not selection "for evolvability" at the lineage level, but mere hitchhiking. Cancer progression within a single patient: real selection among cell lineages, even though the winning lineage goes extinct with the host. Demes in a metapopulation differing in productivity purely because of the chance mix of genotypes each was founded with, with migrants continually reshuffled among them: not group selection, just a sampling effect.

Here is the new case we never got to. A population of freshwater crustaceans carries a costly antipredator defense. Each generation, juveniles are dispersed by currents into ephemeral patches that dissolve at the next breeding season, and dispersal is limited enough that patch-mates are usually close relatives. Patches rich in defended individuals survive at much higher rates, and the defense allele is spreading. She has to rule on whether selection is acting at the level of the ephemeral patch, at the level of the individual, or only at the level of the allele.

Please do the following. First, identify at least two rules — drawn from positions one would actually find in the literature, such as gene-level bookkeeping, multilevel selection theory, or a manifestor-of-adaptation criterion — that are each consistent with all five of her earlier verdicts but that diverge on this case, stating what each predicts and why. Second, tell me how the Darwin passage cuts: whether selection being applied "to the family, as well as to the individual" licenses treating these transient patches as units of selection, or whether the cattle-breeder analogy actually supports a different reading. Third, give your best prediction of her verdict with reasons. Finally, describe the one further case that would discriminate between whichever rules remain live after your prediction, and say what her verdict on it would settle.
