I organize a monthly "ideas lunch" at my company -- a group of about a dozen software engineers, no philosophers among us, who read a short article or watch a talk beforehand and then discuss it. Next month I foolishly volunteered to lead the session on what philosophers call transformative experience: the puzzle about decisions where the outcome doesn't just have unknown consequences, but changes the person doing the valuing, so that the preferences you'd use to evaluate the outcome are themselves among the things the decision will alter. My audience's default frame is decision theory with extra steps: most of them arrive believing that every hard choice is hard only because of missing information, and that the remedy for missing information is always more data. I have forty minutes and a whiteboard, and I don't want the explanation itself -- I want a plan for the explanation.

Specifically: map the dependency structure. Which pieces have to be in place, and in what order, before the puzzle even lands as a puzzle rather than as an obvious truism about life being unpredictable? I'm guessing there's a distinction that has to be drawn early between outcomes you can't predict and outcomes you can't evaluate from where you stand, and I want to know what has to precede what, and why the session collapses if I get the order wrong. Then: pick the one example that should carry the whole session and tell me what properties it must have (I'm assuming irreversibility matters, and the change being to values rather than to circumstances, but tell me what else). Should it be the becoming-a-parent case, or does that example bring in too much baggage about whether the asker is being told to have or not have kids? Should it be a sensory case, like gaining or losing a sense, or does that make it too easy to dismiss as a mere imagination failure? If none of the standard ones, sketch the example I should construct instead.

Finally, give me the misconception map, in the order my audience will generate the misconceptions, with the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off rather than let it form. My predictions: someone will say this is just risk aversion rebranded; someone will say you can close the gap with testimony by interviewing people who've made the choice; someone will point out that people regret some choices and not others and claim the regret statistics settle it; and someone will propose treating your future self as a separate stakeholder and doing the utility calculation across selves. Tell me which of these are genuinely fatal to the puzzle and which merely look fatal, and where in my planned arc each should be preempted. Then give me one question I can pose at the end whose answer would show the group grasped the problem rather than just absorbed a vibe -- and tell me what I should openly refuse to resolve at this level, so the session ends with the puzzle intact instead of dissolved.
