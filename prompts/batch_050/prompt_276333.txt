So the whole "was agriculture a mistake" debate has been stuck in my head lately. You've got the Sahlins line that hunter-gatherers were the original affluent society, working maybe 15-20 hours a week with better nutrition, then Diamond calling the Neolithic revolution the worst mistake in human history, Harari repeating something similar in Sapiens, James Scott pushing a version about early states being coercive traps -- and on the other side, people who point at skeletal stress markers, population bottlenecks, seasonal starvation, infanticide in foraging societies, or who just say that 8 billion people couldn't exist any other way so the question is moot. It feels like this debate has been running for half a century and nothing ever moves. Everyone cites the same !Kung work-hour studies from the 60s, the same paleopathology from Dickson Mounds or wherever, and then they talk past each other.

Here's my hypothesis, but please push back on it: I think this is at least three different disagreements wearing the same costume. One is empirical (were foragers actually healthier and leisured than early farmers, and how much do you trust ethnographic analogies from the 20th century for the Paleolithic). One is evaluative (does "better" mean leisure, health, autonomy, total well-being, or the sheer existence of more people -- and is average vs total the real crux). And one is a counterfactual mess, since the people who exist under farming aren't the same people who would exist under foraging, so maybe there's no coherent comparison at all. Am I right that the sides are mostly answering different questions, or is there a real factual core that would settle it if resolved?

I'd also like your take on whether the "it couldn't have been otherwise / population lock-in" move actually dissolves the question or just dodges it, because it shows up alot in these arguments.
