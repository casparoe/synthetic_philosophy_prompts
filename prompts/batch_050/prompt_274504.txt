so i'm drafting a novel set in a little coastal commune that governs itself entirely by Habermasian discourse ethics -- every dispute, from fence lines to bedtimes, gets settled in open dialogue where only the forceless force of the better argument is allowed to count. For one chapter I need an in-world artifact: the commune newspaper's advice column, maybe 600 words, two or three letters with answers. The columnist should be a total true believer, completely sincere, and that's where the comedy has to live -- the advice should follow the theory faithfully (every affected perspective gets heard, nobody can be excluded, keep talking until genuine uncoerced consensus, bracket status and power at the door) while the letters quietly show the cracks: a woman who's been "in dialogue" about her husband's snoring for four years, a teenager who's learned to strategically perform sincerity, someone asking when they're allowed to stop deliberating and just act already, maybe a note from the poor guy who always ends up moderating. The satire should land the standard critiques -- who decides whether consensus is real or just everyone being exhausted, whether bracketing power just makes it invisible, whether demanding rational justifiability flattens things like grief or taste -- without ever stating them outright or turning the columnist into a hypocrite. deadpan the whole way through, no winking.
