I am taking a philosophy of biology course this term, and we have just spent three weeks on attempts to define life. I am struck by how completely the debate seems to have stalled. The working definition used by NASA -- roughly, a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution -- has been around for decades, its critics have been around just as long, and as far as I can tell neither side has moved. Critics point to the usual counterexamples: a mule is alive but cannot evolve by itself, a virus seems alive on some criteria and not others, and anything we define by reproduction or evolution might exclude the first living thing or the last surviving member of a species. Defenders reply that the definition is a tool for detection, not a census of individual organisms, and then the critics say that this concedes the counterexamples, and around it goes.

My hypothesis is that the two sides are not actually disagreeing about the facts of biology at all. Everyone agrees about viruses, mules, prions, fire, and frozen embryos. I suspect the disagreement is about what a definition of life is supposed to do -- whether it is a detection criterion for astrobiology, a theoretical carving of nature for biology, or an explication of our ordinary concept -- and that the sides keep scoring each other's proposals against purposes the proposer never had. But I am not confident in this. It could instead be that there is a genuine substantive disagreement underneath, about whether life is really a natural kind at all or just a convenient cluster concept, and that the definitional bickering is a symptom of that deeper issue. Or it could be that the disagreement is about standards: about whether a single counterexample to a proposed definition is a refutation or just a reminder that biological categories have fuzzy edges.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is -- verbal, substantive, a difference over which cases count as data, or a difference over the purpose of the definition -- using what each side actually argues, and assess my hypothesis against the alternatives. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical discovery (say, about the origins of life or about genuinely borderline systems), a formal criterion, or an agreement to split the question into several. If you think no progress should be expected because the question itself is ill-posed, argue for that instead, and explain why the debate feels so much like a real one if it is not.
