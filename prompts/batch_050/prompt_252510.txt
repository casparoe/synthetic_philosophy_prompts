Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about what disease is, and I want to stress-test the position I ended up defending. My view is roughly the naturalist one in the philosophy of medicine: a condition is a disease just in case it involves the failure of some part or process of the organism to perform a function that is statistically normal for members of its reference class -- its species, age group, and sex. On my view, this is an empirical matter through and through; judgments about what is bad, undesirable, or worth treating enter only afterward, when we decide what to do about diseases, not when we identify them. My friend, a normativist, kept insisting that every purported case of dysfunction smuggles in a value judgment, and that naturalism only looks neutral because it draws its reference classes and its notion of function in ways already shaped by what we care about.

I want to know what would actually count against my position. Not just the familiar parade of hard cases -- senescence, presbyopia, dental caries, dyslexia, sickle-cell trait in malarial regions, beneficial departures from the statistical norm -- though I want those too, with a verdict on whether each one is a genuine counterexample or merely a case my view classifies counterintuitively but defensibly. I also want the deeper threats: the charge that statistical normality cannot fix the reference class without circularity, that the notion of biological function cannot be cashed out without values, that naturalism has been insulated by ever more refined idealization clauses until it risks nothing at all. Midway through the argument I proposed a test case of my own: imagine a heritable variant that reliably lowers one internal capacity while extending healthy lifespan, which everyone agrees medicine should neither prevent nor treat. I claimed my view handles this cleanly by separating classification from treatment; my friend claimed it shows the classification is doing no work. Tell me which side that case actually cuts for.

Please sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what critics say against the view from what a committed naturalist would herself concede as counting against it, and say plainly if you think the position survives. Write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
