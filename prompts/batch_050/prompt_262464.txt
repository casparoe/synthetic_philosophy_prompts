I'm a retired molecular biologist (forty years in the lab, mostly protein folding stuff) and about a year ago I fell into a philosophy reading group that meets at the public library on Thursdays. Right now we're on the Epicurean arguement that death can't be bad for you, because when you exist death doesn't, and when death exists you don't, so there's no subject around to be harmed. I volunteered to play devil's advocate against it at next week's meeting, so I've been collecting the standard replies. Here's what I have so far: (1) the deprivation account -- death is bad because it cuts your life short compared to the life you would have had, and badness is comparative, so no experience of the harm is needed; (2) the point that death needn't be bad at any particular time, so the whole "when is it bad?" timing puzzle isn't fatal -- the badness attaches to your life as a whole rather than to some moment during it; (3) the reply to Lucretius's symmetry argument that our asymmetrical attitudes toward prenatal nonexistence and posthumous nonexistence track something real, since rational concern is biased toward the future, so the fact that the nothingness before my birth doesn't bother me doesn't show the nothingness after my death shouldn't; (4) the point that people can apparently be harmed by things they never experience at all -- slander behind your back, a promise broken after you're gone -- so the no-experiencer-no-harm premise is shaky on independent grounds.

What I'd like from you is a fifth reply that isn't on this list and isn't just a rewording, special case, or mashup of these four. I'm fairly sure I've got all the standard moves covered, so you may need to get a bit creative -- that's fine, even encouraged, as long as the argument is honestly stated and not smoke and mirrors. Explain how it works, how it differs structurally from (1) through (4), and whether you actually think it's any good -- I'd rather show up to the group with one decent novel argument than three flimsy ones. And since I learned the hard way in science that you should know your own weak points before the audience finds them: please also tell me what would change your mind -- which consideration or counterargument would make you withdraw the reply, or where your reasoning for it is most likely to break down.
