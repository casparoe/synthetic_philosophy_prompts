So I've been reading Montaigne's essay on cannibals and rewatching the story of the 1972 Andes plane crash (the Uruguayan rugby team -- 45 people aboard, crashed in the mountains in October, 16 came down off the mountain in late December after 72 days, and they survived by eating the bodies of passengers who'd died, most of whom were their friends and family). I want you to analyze the Andes episode through the lens of that essay. Not a book report on either one -- I know both stories. The analysis.

Some things I specifically want mapped: Montaigne's move isn't just "everyone calls foreign things barbaric," it's a comparative ranking -- he grants cannibalism is barbaric but argues Europeans torture living people while the Tupinambá only mutilate the dead, and he thinks the first is worse. Apply that ranking to the Andes case: the survivors killed no one and ate only the already-dead, so where do they land on Montaigne's scale, and does his frame even have a slot for cannibalism done for survival rather than as war ritual? Second: the public reaction went from horror (press frenzy when it came out) to broad sympathy pretty fast, and the Catholic authorities essentially absolved them. Is that shift Montaigne's mechanism in action -- custom deciding what counts as barbarous -- or is something else going on, like the consent the survivors say they gave each other in advance, which is a concept I don't think Montaigne's essay has anywhere?

And run it in the other direction too: what does the episode do to Montaigne's relativism? Does a case where the "barbaric" practice happens inside our own culture, to people we know, under conditions we can imagine ourselves in, support his view (judgments of barbarism are parochial and melt on contact with understanding) or break it (there's a non-relative distinction doing the work here -- killing vs. eating the dead, coercion vs. consent -- and Montaigne was actually gesturing at it rather than at relativism)? Where the essay is just silent about the case, say so instead of making it talk.
