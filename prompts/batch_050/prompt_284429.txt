I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a feature about whether institutions should trust large groups over small panels of experts — in forecasting, in citizen assemblies, in grant review, and in corporate decision-making. At every stage of my reporting, sources keep reaching for the same phrase: "the wisdom of crowds." One editor used it to justify replacing a five-person expert committee with a survey of two thousand laypeople; a political scientist used it to defend majority rule as such; a tech executive used it to argue that averaging thousands of independent guesses about the future outperforms any individual analyst, even when most of the guessers know very little.

I strongly suspect these three people are invoking three different claims, only some of which are defensible, and that the Condorcet jury theorem is doing silent work under at least one of them. What I would like is a careful disambiguation I can use as the skeleton of the piece. Please set out the distinct precise theses that "the wisdom of crowds" might be expressing — ranging from the near-trivial (e.g., that averaging cancels idiosyncratic error) to the strong and contentious (e.g., that a large group of mostly ill-informed individuals will systematically outjudge a few well-informed ones, or that majority rule tracks the truth regardless of what the majority is like). For each reading, please say: whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; what mathematical or empirical result, if any, actually underwrites it; and whether it would license the specific journalistic uses described above.

Where the Condorcet jury theorem enters, please be exact about its working parts rather than gesturing at it: the binary-choice structure, the requirement that each voter be more likely right than wrong, the independence assumption, the sincerity assumption, and what happens to the conclusion when each of these is relaxed — in particular when voters' errors are correlated by shared information sources, when the choice is among more than two options, when competence differs across voters, and when people vote strategically rather than sincerely. I would also like you to flag any reading of the slogan that conflates two things my sources seem to run together: the claim that aggregating many judgments cancels noise, and the claim that aggregation reveals signal. Finally, please end by stating which single reading of the slogan you think most of my sources probably mean, and whether the feature's headline claim — that crowds are wise — survives the disambiguation.
