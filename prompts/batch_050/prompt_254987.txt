So my civics teacher Mr. Delgado does this thing where every friday he puts a weird voting-rights case on the board and we debate it, and now he's offering extra credit to anyone who can predict his answer to next weeks case by figuring out what rule he's been secretly using all semester. Here's how he's ruled so far, as best as I wrote it down: American citizens who've lived abroad for twenty years -- still get to vote, he said yes. Immigrants with green cards who've lived here for years but aren't citizens -- yes, at least in local elections. Tourists visiting for two weeks -- no. Sixteen year olds -- yes, because laws apply to them and they pay taxes on their part-time jobs. Little kids -- no. People currently in prison -- yes, and he was weirdly emphatic about that one. And then there was a case about a woman who lives one street outside the city limits but drives in every single day for work -- he said no, she doesn't get a vote in city elections.

Next week's case, which the extra credit rides on: our county is holding a referendum on whether to approve a big factory, and most of the waste from it would wash downriver into the next state over. Do the people in that state get a vote in our referendum? Me and my friends argued about this at lunch for like forty minutes. Half of us think his rule is obviously "anyone the decision seriously affects should get a say," in which case the downriver people definately get a vote. The other half think it's more like "anyone who has to live under a place's laws gets a say in making them," and since the downriver people don't live under our county's laws, they're out of luck no matter how bad the pollution gets. The woman-outside-the-city case seems to point the second way, but honestly she was only missing out on, like, road maintenance decisions, while the downriver people might get actually poisoned -- so maybe the size of the stakes matters to him and neither of our rules is exactly it.

So what do you think he'll say about the downriver case, and which rule (or mix of rules) is he most likely running, given everything so far? If his past verdicts genuinely can't settle it, tell me what follow-up case I should ask him about in class that would split the candidate rules apart. And if there's a third rule I haven't even considered that fits his whole track record, I want to hear that too, because I really need this extra credit.
