I'm a second-year PhD student in metaethics, and I've spent much of this year working through the Frege–Geach literature on expressivism for my qualifying paper. After several months I find myself less sure what the disagreement is than when I started, and I want a diagnosis rather than another verdict.

Here's my rough map. Non-cognitivists from Stevenson and Hare onward held that moral utterances express attitudes rather than beliefs; Geach and Searle objected that moral sentences embed in unasserted contexts — conditionals, negations, disjunctions, attitude reports — and preserve their meaning and inferential behavior there, which an attitude-expression account seemingly can't explain, since "If stealing is wrong, then..." doesn't express disapproval of anything. The expressivist responses I know — Blackburn's projectivism and quasi-realism, Gibbard's hyperplan states, Schroeder's "being for" constructions — all try to recover the embeddings compositionally, and critics like Schueler, Unwin, and Dreier keep producing recalcitrant cases (mixed inferences, embedding under "believes," the negation-asymmetry problem). Meanwhile a second front opened up: deflationists about truth argue that the cognitivist/non-cognitivist contrast collapses once truth and belief are deflated, so the whole debate needs reformulating; and hybrid theorists just concede part of the field and keep what they want. My seminar can't even agree on whether Blackburn "solved" the problem or merely changed the subject.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like tested, is that this debate stalled because it has quietly fragmented into three different disputes wearing one name: a formal dispute about whether a compositional semantics for normative language can be built on attitudinal primitives (which seems in-principle answerable), a metasemantic dispute about what fixes the content of moral thought and talk (where the two sides may accept the same formal results but disagree about what they're results of), and a methodological dispute about what counts as the data — surface logical form and inference patterns, or functional and psychological facts about moral judgment. If that's right, a lot of the literature is ships passing, and "progress" looks different in each fragment. But I may be rationalizing a mess I've failed to master.

So: what kind of disagreement is the Frege–Geach debate, actually? Diagnose it using what the main parties actually argue, not textbook summaries of them. Is there a hidden formal question doing the work, or a bedrock difference about what semantics is for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a specific theorem or construction, a conceded case, a reframing à la deflationism — and for which fragment? If you think my fragmentation hypothesis is wrong, or that I've misdescribed any position, or that the debate is less stalled than I assume, say so plainly rather than working around my framing.
