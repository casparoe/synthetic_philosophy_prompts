Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about whether a sentence like "Stealing is wrong" states a fact that can be straightforwardly true or false, or instead expresses an attitude — disapproval, an aversion, acceptance of a norm. We made little progress, and I suspect part of the problem was that we never settled what an expressivist account of moral language would even have to accomplish in order to count as adequate. Before I read further into the literature, including the Frege–Geach problem, write the desiderata. The list should cover the phenomena an expressivist theory has to explain — how moral sentences embed under negation and in the antecedents of conditionals, why arguments mixing moral and non-moral premises appear valid, why moral discourse wears the surface grammar of fact-stating talk if it isn't fact-stating — as well as the distinctions it must not collapse (for instance, between endorsing a claim oneself and merely reporting that someone else does), with the consideration or example motivating each requirement. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Then, briefly, indicate how a crude "wrong = boo to that" view, a plan-expressing view, and a sophisticated quasi-realist view fare against the list — but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard.
