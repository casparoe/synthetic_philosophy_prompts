I am a retired molecular biologist who, over the past two years, has been making his way through the philosophy of medicine, and our small reading group has asked each member to circulate a statement of their commitments on the concept of disease so that the others can probe it for inconsistencies. I would be grateful for a stress-test before I embarrass myself. My position, as best I can currently articulate it, is the following. First, I hold that disease is a value-free, purely biological notion: a disease is a statistically subnormal functioning of some part or process of the organism, measured against the appropriate reference class of the species (same age group, same sex, and so on), roughly along the lines of Boorse's biostatistical theory. Second, I hold that aging is not itself a disease, and nor are the declines that are normal for one's age, such as presbyopia; these are the background conditions in which diseases occur, not pathologies. Third, I hold that the removal of homosexuality from psychiatric classification was the correction of a scientific error rather than a political concession: it simply never met the criteria for being a disease, and psychiatry belatedly recognized this. Fourth, I hold that not every statistical abnormality qualifies as a disease, because a condition must be harmful to the individual who bears it; harmless deviations, such as left-handedness or unusually dense bones, are mere anomalies, and I take this to be plain common sense rather than a philosophical addition. Fifth, I hold that whether something is a disease cannot vary from culture to culture — a condition is a disease everywhere or nowhere — while I freely concede that which diseases are worth treating, and how urgently, depends heavily on a society's resources, environment, and priorities.

What I would like from you is an audit rather than a lecture on the naturalism–normativism debate in general. Please go through my five commitments and identify which pairs, if any, are in genuine tension — including tensions that arise only in combination, where one commitment quietly does work that another forbids — and which pairs merely appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection. For each genuine tension, I would like to know how severe it is, what precisely generates it, and what the cheapest available fix would be, together with what that fix costs me elsewhere in the position. Where an apparent tension dissolves, please show why it dissolves rather than simply asserting that it does. Finally, if you judge that several tensions exist, tell me which single revision to my statement would restore the most coherence, bearing in mind that I would rather abandon a peripheral commitment than rebuild the whole edifice. I should mention that I do not myself know where the weak points are; the fourth commitment in particular strikes me as the sort of thing any sensible person would say, and the third as a settled matter of historical record, so if the trouble lies there I will be surprised but not unwilling to hear it.
