So I got into a dumb argument with my roommate last week. He's selling his old bike, listed it for $250, and someone immediately offered $300 because these bikes go for more than he thought. He turned it down because it "felt like losing" the bike now that he'd decided to keep it or whatever. I told him that's textbook irrational, it's just the endowment effect, and I said the thing everyone says: "losses loom larger than gains." He got annoying about it and asked me what that even means, precisely, and honestly I couldn't say much more than "people hate losing stuff more than they like getting stuff, like 2-to-1, I read that somewhere."

Now I'm worried he has a point. So: what are the different precise claims that slogan could actually be making? I can think of candidates like -- a claim about how bad losses feel vs how good equivalent gains feel, a claim about the actual shape of people's valuation function around a reference point, a claim about a measured behavioral ratio, a claim that loss-framing makes people worse at decisions, maybe others I haven't thought of. Lay them out as a list, and for each one say: is it basically trivially true, is it a substantive empirical claim that's held up, is it contested or has it fallen apart under replication pressure, and -- this is the part I actually need -- does it even support calling my roommate's bike preference a *bias*, as opposed to just a preference that's allowed to exist? I have a sneaking suspicion that "losses loom larger than gains" is a descriptive claim about average responses in gambles and it does zero work in telling anyone whether their attachment to a specific thing is legitimate.

Two requests on how to pitch the answer: please clearly separate what's basically settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this stuff (I get the sense the replication situation around some of these effects is messier than pop books let on, but I don't know the details), and don't present a contested take as the consensus. And write it in American English please.
