I am developing the ethics module for a certification course for financial advisers, and the unit on client communication turns on the lying–misleading distinction. Standard presentations treat this as a two-party dispute -- "lying is worse per se" versus "no intrinsic moral difference" -- but I believe the real space of positions is richer, and I want the course to sort trainees by what they actually believe rather than by which label they find comfortable.

Please construct a decision tree that sorts a respondent into a position on whether lying and misleading differ morally. Follow these specifications:

1. The tree should be built from the logical space, not from a list of names. Begin by identifying the genuinely independent questions on which positions can come apart -- for example: whether what is morally relevant is what was said or what the hearer was made to believe; whether the wrong of deception consists in a breach of a linguistic convention, in an abuse of trust, or simply in causing false belief; whether the speaker's intent matters independently of the means; and whether conventions of a particular professional context can change the verdict. Then locate actual positions within the space those questions generate.

2. Each branching question must be posed as a concrete case a respondent can answer without knowing any terminology. At least one case must involve technically true but misleading statements about fees or past performance; at least one must involve a lie and a misleading statement that produce the identical false belief in the same hearer; and at least one must involve a statement that is literally false but which everyone in the conversation understands non-literally (as with certain ceremonial or diplomatic formulas).

3. The tree must contain between six and eight diagnostic questions and must yield, at minimum, leaves corresponding to: (a) lying is intrinsically worse than misleading because it violates a convention of assertion; (b) there is no intrinsic difference and apparent differences reduce to context; (c) misleading can be worse because it recruits the hearer's own inference and so corrupts their agency more deeply; and (d) the distinction matters only where a practice of trust makes assertion costlier, so the verdict varies by institutional setting. Other leaves should be added wherever the logical space supports a coherent position.

4. For each leaf: name the position (or mark it "unnamed" if no one defends it), state its most awkward commitment in one sentence, and give one client-communication case on which it issues a verdict most advisers would find counterintuitive.

5. For every combination of answers that yields an unoccupied leaf, state briefly whether the gap reflects incoherence, mere unattractiveness, or simple neglect.

6. If any two of your leaves cannot be separated by any admissible question, say so explicitly and explain whether the failure is a limitation of the instrument or evidence that the positions were never distinct.

Conclude with a short note -- no more than a paragraph -- stating what the tree takes for granted (for instance, that the deception succeeded, that stakes are held fixed across cases) and what would change if those assumptions were relaxed.
