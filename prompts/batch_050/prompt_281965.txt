Is there a name for this move? I've been reading cosmology-adjacent stuff on and off for years (the usual blogs, the usual forums) and I keep running into the same argumentative move, and I genuinely can't tell if it's a recognized thing with a literature or just a habit physicists have.

The pattern: someone proposes a cosmological model -- say, the universe is old and big enough that random fluctuations will occasionally assemble, out of dust or vacuum or whatever, a lone conscious mind complete with false memories of an entire life and an entire scientific tradition. In such a universe these fluke observers vastly outnumber evolved ones like us. Fine so far. But then the response to the model isn't "here's where the physics breaks" or "here's a measurement that rules it out." The response is: if this model were true, then statistically *you* would almost certainly be one of the flukes, your memories would be fabricated, the experiments you think you ran never happened -- so the model has to go. The model gets rejected because of what it says about *you*, not because anything in a telescope disagreed with it.

Another instance, maybe: people dismiss the simulation hypothesis partly on the grounds that if it were true, all our physics would be the physics of a fake world and couldn't be trusted -- as though that were a point against the hypothesis rather than just an awkward consequence of it.

And I think it shows up outside physics too: radical skepticism gets waved off as self-defeating -- if you believed you were deceived about everything you'd have no grounds for believing that either -- even though the skeptic's scenario is perfectly consistent, just unwinable.

So my questions:

1. Is there an established name for rejecting a theory on the grounds that it predicts your own evidence is unreliable? It's not self-contradiction, exactly -- the theory doesn't contradict itself, it contradicts *your grounds for trusting the theory*. It's not unfalsifiability either; if anything the problem is that the theory predicts too much (too many observers). It's adjacent to self-refutation but I don't think it quite is that, since nothing stops the theory from being *true* -- it just apparently can't be rationally believed.

2. Is the move even valid? Sometimes it feels like a legitimately devastating objection: a theory that tells you your data is noise has eaten its own evidence. Other times it smells like pure motivated reasoning -- the universe doesn't owe you believability, and you don't get to rule out a true theory just because believing it would be self-undermining. Where's the line, and does anyone have a principled way of drawing it?

If there's an established concept here, tell me what it is and how the described thing differs from the nearby things it isn't. If there isn't, coin me a term with a real definition and some boundary cases: when is "this theory makes me a statistical fluke" a knockout objection, and when is it cope?
