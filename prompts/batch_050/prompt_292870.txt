So I TA for an intro continental philosophy course (mostly second-years, about 22 students, one 75-minute seminar slot) and we're about to start the tool analysis in Being and Time. Last year I just lectured on readiness-to-hand vs presence-at-hand and it was a disaster — they nodded along, memorized the hammer example, and I could tell nothing landed. This year I want them to have the experience first and get the vocabulary second, so no lecture upfront and nobody should hear Heidegger's name until the very end.

You're an experienced philosophy instructor who has run hands-on phenomenology exercises before, so think carefully about what actually works in a room of 22 fidgety undergrads, not what sounds nice on paper. Maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate activities first and then commit to the best one. What I want is an activity using only cheap stuff I can get at a dollar store (pens, paper, scissors, tape, string, that kind of thing) where students do some absorbing hands-on task with a tool, and then you engineer a breakdown — actually several distinct kinds of breakdown, because Heidegger distinguishes ways equipment can fail (the tool that's just broken, the tool that's missing, the tool that's in the way) and I want them to hit all three without knowing it. The point is they should catch themselves shifting from fluidly using the thing to staring at it as a mere object, and notice that shift as something that happened TO them, not something they decided.

Structure your response with these sections, in this order: "Brainstorm" (3-4 sentences on the options you considered and why you picked this one), "Materials and setup" (short, a list is fine), "The activity, step by step" (the meat of it, maybe 400-500 words, with rough timings and exactly what I say and do at each stage, including how to fake the equipment failures without it feeling staged or me looking like I sabotaged them), "The debrief" (roughly 250 words: the questions I ask to get them to describe the shift in their own words before I name it, and how to map their moments onto ready-to-hand, present-at-hand, and the three breakdown modes), "What will predictably go wrong" (at least three failure modes and how to recover), and "What this can't reproduce" (a short honest paragraph on which feature of Heidegger's account the activity flattens or fakes and that I'll just have to tell them about). One more constraint: students shouldn't be able to guess the point in advance, so nothing philosophical in the task instructions themselves.
