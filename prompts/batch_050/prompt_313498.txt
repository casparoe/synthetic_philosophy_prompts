I’ve been thinking about this ever since I saw a documentary where a painting experts thought was an old master turned out to be fake. The actual paint on the wall hadn’t changed, but suddenly everyone acted like the painting was less impressive. I don’t have an art or philosophy background, but my view is that an original’s special value comes from what I call its causal biography: this exact object was made by that person, was present at particular events, survived particular choices, and is the endpoint of a real chain of actions. A perfect copy can preserve the visual experience, and maybe even be more useful for studying the artist’s technique, but it doesn’t have the same biography. At the same time, I don’t think this makes originals infinitely valuable or means people who enjoy replicas are being irrational.

Next week I’m giving a short talk defending this view to a local museum fundraising committee. The audience will be about half private collectors, plus one skeptical conservator who keeps saying that museums are there to preserve information, not sacred relics, and a digital-art enthusiast who thinks scarcity of physical objects is mostly a scam. Prepare me for the objections they are actually likely to raise, ordered from most likely and damaging to least. Phrase each objection the way that particular audience member would phrase it, then give me a reply I could deliver in under two minutes. For each reply, say what I should concede, what I should refuse to concede, and where my position is weakest.

Please include practical complications rather than treating “original” as perfectly clear: restoration, paintings made partly by assistants, sculptures cast from an artist’s mold, prints made in numbered editions, and cases where almost none of the original material survives. I also want one objection that sounds devastating but is easy to answer, one that sounds easy but is actually dangerous, and the question I’m secretly hoping nobody asks. Please don’t turn the prep into a bibliography or rely on famous philosophical jargon; I need something I can use while standing in a room of people who care about auctions, preservation, donors, and whether alot of this is just snobbery.
