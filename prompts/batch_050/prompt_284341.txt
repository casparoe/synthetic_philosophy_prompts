My friend Jonah thinks he's dissolved the whole "moral deference is weird" puzzle and his argument seems to prove way too much. Setup: most people feel like there's something second-rate about believing something's wrong purely because someone told you so ("eating meat is wrong because my ethics TA says it is"), even when the person is smart and trustworthy. Jonah says that's just a prejudice. His argument:

(1) You're justified in believing p on someone's say-so only if you have reason to think they're reliable about p's subject matter.
(2) For ordinary factual stuff we meet this condition all the time: track records, spot-checks, knowing that someone got vetted by an institution, seeing their past calls pan out.
(3) But we meet it for moral stuff too. I can check someone's moral judgment against cases where I already know the answer (everyone agrees you don't torture cats for fun), check whether their views hang together, see whether they're honest, impartial, not just rationalizing what benefits them. That's a track record same as any other.
(4) If I have reason to think someone is morally reliable, then believing them about moral questions I haven't settled myself is just as justified as believing a historian about the War of 1812.
(5) So pure moral deference is epistemically perfectly fine, and the nagging feeling that it's not is just status quo bias about who gets to have moral authority.

What bugs me: if this goes through, it doesn't just show deference is *allowed*, it seems to show a community where everyone outsources all their moral opinions to a handful of trusted gurus loses nothing at all compared to one where everyone thinks for themselves. And that can't be right. Also, the same machinery would apparently license deferring about literally anything to anyone you find trustworthy-sounding, since on his view checking reliability never requires being able to answer the questions yourself. So I think it proves too much, but I can't find the step that's doing it, and honestly (1) and (2) both look undeniable to me, and (3) seems like just... true, that's really how I'd vet someone.

Tell me which step you think I should reject, why it looked fine to me anyway, and what rejecting it costs. Also: is there a way to keep the conclusion that moral testimony can justify belief while still explaining what's deflating about "it's wrong because she said so," or do I have to pick one? I'm hoping the answer involves a distinction he's smuggling past me, but maybe that's cope. Answer in American English please.
