I cover science and ideas for a general audience, and I am drafting a column on hard choices: leaving a secure research post for a riskier one, choosing between two partners, prioritising a parent’s care over a child’s opportunity. I want to avoid the usual mush about following your heart, but I also do not want a bibliography or a tour of named positions. Treat the concepts below as common philosophical currency and reason from first principles.

Please give your actual credence in this proposition, stated as precisely as I can manage: there are real human choices in which, even after all relevant empirical facts are known and all relevant values are frankly acknowledged, neither option is better, worse, or exactly equal overall; the options remain related by some positive normative relation rather than mere indifference; and selecting one of them can still be rational rather than an arbitrary spasm. Call the proposition H.

Decompose the reasoning rather than giving a bare number. Use at least these terms, adding any you think I have missed: P1, the probability that practical value is genuinely plural, so that no single scale captures welfare, autonomy, loyalty, excellence and the rest; P2, the probability that some plural values are not merely hard to weigh but fail full comparability, so more of one does not always compensate for less of another; P3, the probability that failure of comparability does not collapse into exact equality or a vague zone where anything goes; P4, the probability that rational agency can make a warranted selection among options related only in this way; P5, the probability that the idealisation in H is coherent, namely complete information plus acknowledged plural values without the choice becoming easy. For each term, give a number, one sentence of justification, and a note on dependence: which earlier term, if false, would most damage it. Then state how the terms combine; if a simple product is misleading because of overlap, say so and give your adjusted rule.

To keep this usable for the column, include two test cases. First, a reader choosing between a modest job near an ailing parent and a brilliant distant job that would probably accelerate an irreversible decline in contact; second, a funder choosing between two equally competent grant proposals, one safer and one more transformative. In each case, say whether H helps, misleads, or changes nothing.

Mark your weakest premise explicitly, name the kind of evidence or argument that would move it most, and distinguish that from mere pressure to sound decisive. If you think the demand for a number is itself corrupting, say so after giving the number anyway.

End with the machine-readable line \answer{0.xx}, where the entry is your final credence in H to two decimal places.
