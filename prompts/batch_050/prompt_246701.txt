So I've been going down a rabbit hole on Gödel's rotating universe stuff and his argument from it that time isn't objectively real -- the rough idea being that GR allows physically possible cosmologies with closed timelike curves, in those worlds there can't be a genuine lapse of time, and since those worlds obey the same laws as ours and differ only in contingent facts about how matter is arranged, the lapse of time can't be a real objective feature in our world either.

Here are the objections to that argument I already know, so don't repeat these or thinly disguise them:

1. The inference doesn't transfer. Even if time is "ideal" in a Gödel universe, why should that tell us anything about worlds like ours that have a global time order? Gödel's answer is that something as fundamental as the lapse of time shouldn't hinge on contingent matter distribution, but that just restates the controversial premise.

2. The models may be mathematically fine but physically bogus -- e.g., quantum back-reaction near would-be closed timelike curves (Hawking's chronology protection idea) suggests the laws themselves conspire to prevent CTCs, so the "physically possible" premise fails.

3. Consistency constraints (Novikov-style) defuse the paradox worries Gödel leans on: time travel along CTCs doesn't let you change the past, only be part of it, so the alleged absurdity of such worlds is weaker than he needs.

4. The premise that real temporal lapse can't depend on contingent facts is just question-begging against the relationalist, who is perfectly happy for temporal structure to be contingent.

5. Observationally our universe doesn't seem to rotate, and Gödel's solution doesn't even expand, so it's a lousy model of actual time anyway.

What I want: one more objection that is genuinely different from all five -- not a rewording, not a special case of #1 or #2, and not just "combine 1 and 4." If you can only come up with something that collapses into one of these on inspection, say so instead of pretending. And then give me an honest verdict on whether your new objection actually lands, or whether Gödel's argument survives it.
