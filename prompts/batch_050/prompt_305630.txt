My team is entering a pricing tournament run by a large e-commerce platform, and we are deadlocked over strategy. The rules are as follows. Roughly a dozen competing firms each submit a small program that sets the firm's price for a single round of competition; there are no repeats, and firms cannot communicate except through their submitted code. The twist is that before prices are set, every program is granted read access to the complete source code of every other program in the field. The economics are simple: if every firm holds at the standard markup, each earns a comfortable profit; a firm that undercuts while the others hold captures most of the market and earns far more; and if several firms undercut, everyone earns close to nothing.

A junior engineer on our team has proposed writing our program so that it inspects rivals' code for a reciprocity clause — something of the form "hold price if the other program provably holds price against programs like mine, and undercut otherwise" — and then holds price exactly when it finds such a clause. Her argument is that a rational rival, upon reading our source code, will realize that undercutting gets it nothing while matching the clause earns it the comfortable profit, and so transparency itself makes mutual restraint the rational outcome. Our CFO considers this hopelessly naive: once our code is locked in, she says, a rival's best move is to undercut no matter what our code promises, so the clause gains us nothing and only exposes us.

Please adjudicate. Is the junior engineer's reasoning coherent in a strictly one-shot interaction, and if so, what work is the source-code transparency doing that ordinary rational choice cannot? What assumptions about the rivals' rationality and about the provability of program behavior does her proposal require, and are those assumptions realistic here? Then consider the complications: only some rivals' code is legible enough to verify; a rival submits obfuscated code; a rival writes code that mimics the reciprocity pattern to fool our test while undercutting anyway. Finally, give concrete advice on what we should actually submit — an unconditional undercutter, a conditional cooperator of the kind proposed, or something more elaborate — and identify the failure modes we should guard against. Please write your answer in American English.
