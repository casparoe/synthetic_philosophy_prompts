My friend and I had a long argument last night about Machiavelli's treatment of fortune and virtù in The Prince, and I want to know whether the position he defended is actually Machiavelli's. His summary ran like this: Machiavelli holds that fortune and virtù divide human affairs between them, roughly half and half. Fortune is like a river in flood: when the weather is calm it seems harmless, but when it rises it sweeps everything away unless dikes and dams were built in advance. Virtù, therefore, is essentially foresight -- the prudent ruler prepares his defenses in quiet times, and whoever prepares properly masters fortune. When Machiavelli says fortune favors the impetuous, he means that bold, aggressive action always beats caution, because fortune rewards audacity. The upshot is that political success is half luck and half preparation-plus-boldness, and failure simply means you failed to prepare. I pushed back mainly on the word "always": I seem to remember Machiavelli saying that men prosper when their natural temperament fits the times and are ruined when the times change, which sounds like it undercuts the idea that preparation and boldness suffice. But my friend is better read than I am. Which parts of his summary are accurate, which are defensible simplifications, and which misrepresent Machiavelli? In particular: is virtù really a matter of foresight and advance preparation, does Machiavelli think impetuosity reliably wins, and does the claim that fortune governs half of our actions support my friend's tidy picture of success as half luck?
