So I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy training, and I've been reading Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus in an old public-domain translation. This passage is giving me trouble:

"Accustom thyself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply sentience, and death is the privation of all sentience; therefore a right understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life an illimitable time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality. [...] Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not. It is nothing, then, either to the living or to the dead, for with the living it is not and the dead exist no longer."

Two things I'm stuck on. First: the step from "good and evil imply sentience" to the conclusion seems to smuggle in a premise like "nothing can be bad for you unless you consciously experience it as bad." Is that really the crux of the arguement, or am I misreading? Because if that premise is doing the work, it seems to prove too much -- e.g. someone trashing my reputation after I die couldn't harm me either, which feels wrong.

Second: does "when death is come, we are not" actually address what people are afraid of? Sure, the *state* of being dead can't hurt, there's nobody there to hurt. But what people dread is that their life gets cut short, that they lose future experiences they'd otherwise have. That deprivation seems to be located at a real moment in time, affecting someone who exists right up until then. So is Epicurus just answering a different question than the one most people are asking -- reassuring us about being dead when the complaint was about losing life? Walk me through whether the argument survives that objection, and where an Epicurean would push back.
