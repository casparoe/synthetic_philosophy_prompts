In an online debate about mind uploading, a skeptic argues that a scan-and-copy of your brain would no more be you than a high-resolution photocopy of a painting is the painting: the original stays where it is, the copy is a new object that merely resembles it, and so deleting an upload's backup file harms no one. An enthusiast replies that a person is a pattern rather than a stuff — a second performance of a song is the song, a second instantiation of the pattern is a second instance of you — and that switching off an upload is therefore closer to killing a sleeping person than to shredding a photocopy. Audit both analogies as arguments about digital minds. Lay out the mapping each one relies on (original and copy, type and token, matter and pattern), and distinguish the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the conclusions actually drawn from the ones that are merely available to a pedant. Then say what each analogy quietly presupposes about persistence over time: the photocopy side assumes identity tracks continuous material constitution, the song side assumes it tracks organizational invariance, and neither argues for its assumption so much as embeds it. One thing that bothers me is that both sides slide between two distinct questions — is the upload me? and is the upload someone, a subject with moral standing of its own? Assess whether either analogy can support a verdict on the second question at all, or only on the first, and what follows for the deletion case if the answers come apart. If neither analogy can carry the weight it is being made to carry, propose a better one and say precisely what it gets right.
