I'm doing a course in decision theory and we've just covered Good's theorem on the value of information. As I understand it, the theorem proves that the expected value of any cost-free piece of information is strictly positive before you make a decision — free information can only ever make you better off, never worse off. I've been using this in an argument with my flatmate and I want to check I'm on solid ground before I push it further.

She's decided not to take a genetic test for an untreatable condition that runs in her family. Her reasons are the usual ones: she'd rather not know, the anxiety of a positive result would make her miserable, and she's vaguely worried about insurance. But surely Good's theorem settles this? The test result is (let's assume) free information, and the theorem says free information always improves your position in expectation, so her refusal is straightforwardly irrational — a case of letting feelings override the maths. So, first question: what exactly is the error in her reasoning, in decision-theoretic terms? And can I generalise the argument the way I want to? If free information can never hurt, it seems to follow that full transparency is always a good thing — insurers should be allowed to demand customers' genetic data, companies should share all their internal metrics with all their employees, and so on, since more information can only ever help. Is there any principled stopping point, or does the theorem really commit us to maximal transparency everywhere?

That seems to flatly contradict the theorem's claim that the value is always positive. Where did my calculation go wrong? Please answer in British English.
