I'm writing a survey of arguments against the doxastic conception of delusions — the view that delusions such as the Capgras or Cotard delusion are genuine beliefs, not imaginings, acceptances, or attitudes of some other kind. So far I have four objections. First, the integration objection: delusions are not inferentially connected with the subject's other beliefs in the way genuine beliefs are — the patient who believes her husband has been replaced by an impostor does not draw the conclusions one would expect, such as asking what happened to the real husband. Second, the action objection: subjects often fail to act on their delusions in circumstances where a sincere belief would reliably issue in action, the phenomenon sometimes called double bookkeeping. Third, the evidence objection: delusions are not responsive to counterevidence and counterargument in the way that beliefs, even very irrational ones, characteristically are. Fourth, the interpretability objection: attributing belief requires enough overall rational coherence for the subject to be interpretable, and delusional content is too bizarre and disconnected to pass that threshold. Give me one more objection that is not a restatement, special case, or conjunction of these four — ideally one resting on a different mark of belief than inferential integration, action-guidance, evidence-sensitivity, or interpretability. Then say briefly whether your objection survives the standard reply available to the doxastic theorist, namely that belief is a tolerant and possibly disunified kind that can accommodate irrationality of any of these degrees.
