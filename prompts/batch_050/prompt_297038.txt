I am a journalist preparing a long-form feature on how decentralized scientific teams, activist networks, and human–AI laboratories manage to act together without a commander. Draft the joint statement that might result from an adversarial collaboration between the following two positions:

- Plan-First Individualists hold that every genuine case of joint action can, in principle, be fully explained through interlocking individual intentions, beliefs, expectations, dispositions, and personal commitments. Talk of what a group intends or decides is legitimate only as compressed bookkeeping for those member-level states and their organizational effects.
- Distributed-Agency Pluralists hold that some organized groups possess intentions, reasons, and normative commitments that are realized through their members but cannot be reduced to a collection of separately held member attitudes. They deny that this requires a mysterious collective consciousness or an additional person hovering above the members.

The statement should concern the following fictional research collective. Six epidemiologists, two community organizations, a statistician, and a software system coordinate a public-health investigation. No participant understands the entire sampling plan. A shared protocol distributes tasks, while the software system, which has no beliefs or desires, reallocates work in response to new data. Proposed changes become binding after twenty-four hours unless a registered objection appears. One researcher privately objects to a change but misses the objection deadline because of a family emergency; the collective proceeds. Later, the decision procedure produces a publication strategy that every human participant personally opposes, although each continues to follow it because each had accepted the procedure.

Produce a document in six sections:

1. Scope and terminology: define coordination, joint action, group intention, group agency, and responsibility without presupposing either camp’s theory. Keep this section accessible to an intellectually curious general reader.
2. Claims both camps can sign: provide eight to twelve numbered claims that are substantive rather than merely diplomatic.
3. Residual disagreements: present a table with the disputed issue, the Plan-First formulation, the Distributed-Agency formulation, and the precise point at which the two explanations diverge. Phrase each position so that its defenders would recognize it as their own rather than as a caricature.
4. Cruxes: identify three sub-questions such that settling any one of them would substantially weaken at least one side. For each, state what observation, argument, or counterexample would count against that side.
5. Application to the fictional collective: analyze four moments—the initial formation of the protocol, the missed objection, the software system’s reallocation of work, and the implementation of the unanimously disliked publication strategy. For each moment, record both camps’ interpretations and indicate whether they make different predictions or merely offer different descriptions.
6. Publishable summary: conclude with a 150-to-200-word joint statement suitable for magazine readers.

Do not adjudicate which camp is ultimately correct. Where part of the dispute is verbal, identify the ambiguous term and show how it generates the appearance of disagreement. Where the dispute is genuine, avoid dissolving it into a difference of emphasis. Do not supply citations, bibliographies, or invented quotations; the value of the document should lie in the precision and neutrality of its formulations.
