So I'm in a grad seminar on concepts and categorization, and a friend of mine put together this argument that she thinks shows the whole "what are concepts" debate in psych and philosophy of mind is broken. It feels like it proves way too much but we can't figure out where it slips, if it does. Can you play the role of a careful philosopher of cognitive science and walk me through it? I'd like you to think step by step, maybe brainstorm the candidate weak points first before committing to a diagnosis.

Here's the argument, numbered the way she wrote it. (1) Concepts are compositional: anyone who has the concepts PET and FISH, and grasps the relevant mode of combination, thereby has the concept PET FISH, and what they think about pet fish is fixed by what they think about pets and about fish. (2) If concepts were prototypes, the prototype of PET FISH would have to be computable from the prototypes of PET and FISH. (3) But that computation is impossible: the prototypical pet fish (something like a goldfish) is not similar to the prototypical pet (a dog or cat) nor to the prototypical fish (a trout or salmon); typicality structure of complex concepts just isn't inherited from the parts in any rule-governed way. This is the famous pet fish case everyone cites. (4) So concepts aren't prototypes. (5) The exemplar view -- your concept is just your stored memory of particular instances you've encountred -- fares no better: I can have a perfectly good concept of PET FISH or even NOCTURNAL PENGUIN while having stored zero exemplars of the combination, and no recipe for pooling or intersecting exemplar sets predicts the typicality judgments people actually give for novel compounds. (6) So concepts aren't exemplar stores either. (7) The remaining option is the theory theory, where a concept is its role in a little mental theory. But theories are holistic: my concept FISH includes whatever I believe about fish, so when I learn one new fact about gills my FISH concept changes, which means you and I have never shared a single concept, which makes successful communication and genuine disagreement literally impossible. Since we plainly do communicate and disagree, (8) the theory theory is out too. (9) Every account of what concepts are has been ruled out, so concepts must be unstructured atoms with no internal analysis. (10) But if concepts are atoms, they can't be learned by composing or analyzing anything, so every concept anyone has ever had -- including CARBURETOR and SMARTPHONE -- must have been in the mind all along. (11) That's absurd. (12) Therefore something is deeply wrong with... something. Her conclusion is basically just that gesture.

Now, where does this go wrong? My friend wants to keep (1) at almost any cost -- she thinks compositionality is non-negotiable for explaining productivity -- and she'd also hate to give up (3), since the empirical typicality data is real. I personally suspect the move somewhere between (7) and (9) is doing sneaky work, but I can't pin it down. Please identify the weakest step or steps, explain why each one *looked* fine (because they mostly do look fine), and price the alternatives: what does it cost to defend prototypes against (2)-(3), to save exemplars against (5), or to defend a non-holistic version of the theory theory against the communication argument in (7)? If the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and why. I'd rather get a real diagnosis than a diplomatic "everyone's partly right."
