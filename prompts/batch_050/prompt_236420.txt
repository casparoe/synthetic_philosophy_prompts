I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in decision theory, and I'm grading homework responses to the following question:

"Good's theorem (on the value of information) says, roughly, that for an agent who will act optimally on whatever evidence arrives, the expected value of receiving costless information before deciding is never negative. (a) State the theorem carefully, making its key assumptions explicit. (b) Give an informal explanation of why it holds. (c) Does the theorem establish that a rational agent should always prefer to gather more evidence before making a decision? Why or why not?"

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

(a) Good's theorem states that in any decision problem, the expected utility of first receiving a free observation and then choosing an act optimally in light of it is strictly greater than the expected utility of choosing an act immediately, without the observation. The theorem assumes the agent has precise prior probabilities and utilities, that she updates her beliefs by conditionalizing on the observation, and that the observation itself costs nothing. As I understand it, this result is essentially an application of Blackwell's comparison of experiments: a more informative signal structure is always weakly preferred, and adding a free observation amounts to moving to a more informative experiment.

(b) The reasoning is straightforward. Once the evidence arrives, the agent retains every option she had before: she can always simply ignore the observation and take whichever act she would have chosen without it. Since she can guarantee herself at least the no-information payoff, and may be able to do better by conditioning her choice on what she learns, acquiring the information cannot hurt her and will in general help.

(c) The theorem therefore shows that a rational agent should always gather more evidence before deciding. Since the expected value of information is positive, leaving evidence ungathered is leaving expected utility on the table. For example, a doctor reasoning according to the theorem should order every available diagnostic test before prescribing a treatment, and an investor should commission every feasible study of a market before entering it. The only qualification is that the agent must actually update and act optimally on the evidence she receives.
