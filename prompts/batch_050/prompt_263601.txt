A philosophy of mind research group contains two members who have spent a year arguing past each other. One is a committed enactivist: she holds that cognition is constituted by an organism's dynamic, skillful coupling with its environment, that perception is a form of active engagement rather than the construction of an inner world-model, and that internal states deserve to be called representations only in a thin and explanatorily marginal sense. The other is a committed representationalist: he holds that perception and action are mediated by internal states that stand in for distal states of affairs, carry information about them, and can be decoupled from the current stimulus, as in planning, imagery, and prediction. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration and have asked you to draft the joint statement that both will sign.

The statement should contain the following sections, in this order:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of propositions both parties can endorse without reservation: what the standard experimental record (studies of skilled action such as catching a fly ball or driving, of change blindness and inattentional blindness, and of double dissociations in visual processing) does and does not establish; what any adequate theory of mind must explain; and which arguments each side now concedes were weaker than they first appeared. Avoid vacuous claims of the form both sides believe cognition involves the brain and the environment.

2. Residual disagreements. A table with two columns, giving the enactivist's own formulation of her position and the representationalist's own formulation of his, row by row, so that each would accept the wording of their own side. Include the definitional dispute over what deserves to be called a representation: must a representation be capable of standing in for something absent, or does a state whose entire role is exhausted by ongoing sensorimotor coupling already qualify?

3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Include at least: whether smooth, real-time skilled action requires internal predictive models or can be fully explained by prospective control and attunement to ambient information; whether neuroscientific evidence can in principle decide the issue or systematically underdetermines it; and whether the word representation is doing explanatory work for the representationalist or is a label the enactivist could adopt without changing any prediction.

4. Registered predictions. For each crux, a concrete experiment or case both parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Address, at minimum, this agreed case: expert table-tennis players whose view of the ball is interrupted for variable intervals mid-rally. State precisely which pattern of tracking accuracy and recovery behavior would count as evidence for an internal forward model, and which pattern would count as evidence for continuous informational coupling; state what result would count as a genuine loss for each side. Then propose one further case of the collaboration's own design, with predictions registered in the same way.

5. Verbal residue. Identify which parts of the dispute, if any, are terminological rather than substantive, and propose neutral replacement vocabulary for them.

Write in American English.
