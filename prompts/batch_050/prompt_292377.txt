A Substack post I read last week (I'd rather not name the author) built an argument about utilitarianism's demandingness around this passage from Chapter II of Mill's Utilitarianism:

"The happiness which forms the utilitarian standard of what is right in conduct, is not the agent's own happiness, but that of all concerned. As between his own happiness and that of others, utilitarianism requires him to be as strictly impartial as a disinterested and benevolent spectator."

The author's gloss, roughly, was this: Mill's own words settle the modern debate about whether demandingness objections rest on a caricature. The disinterested-spectator requirement means your own projects and attachments get exactly zero special weight in determining what you ought to do, so the demand for total impartiality in conduct is not a 20th-century overreading (as some apologists claim when they say Mill was really a rule-utilitarian with roomy permissions) — it is the stated theory. And Mill's well-known insistence that motives are irrelevant to the morality of an action only makes it worse: you needn't *feel* impartial benevolence, but you must *act* exactly as the spectator would, with no category of the supererogatory to shelter in. The author concluded that anyone who finds that implication absurd should stop calling themselves a Millian and admit the theory is false.

My instinct, from years of reading EA-adjacent arguments in both directions, is that this is mostly right about the letter of the passage but smuggles in a step somewhere between "standard of right conduct" and "what you must actually do, moment to moment." But I can't locate the sleight of hand precisely. Which parts of the gloss would a careful reader of Mill accept, which conflate things Mill keeps apart (in this chapter or elsewhere in the book), and does the author's concluding dismissal follow from the accurate remainder?
