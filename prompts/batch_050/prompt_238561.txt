I'm a senior in high school taking an elective on economics and political theory, and last week we worked through Amartya Sen's result usually called the impossibility of a Paretian liberal. My teacher walked us through the setup with the example of two people disagreeing about whether a controversial book should be read, and the conclusion really unsettled me: if I understood it correctly, Sen proved in 1970 that no society can guarantee individual freedom and Pareto efficiency at the same time. I've been turning this over since, and it seems to me that this forces a serious choice on any country that wants a functioning economy. Either you protect people's rights and accept that the economy will keep producing outcomes that are worse than they could be, or you accept that some rights have to be overridden whenever respecting them blocks an improvement for everyone involved.

Given that the conflict is a proven logical result rather than a matter of political opinion, I'd like help thinking through how a society should decide which side of the trade-off to prioritize, and how to quantify the exchange rate between the two. For instance, during a recession or a national emergency, it seems reasonable that a government should be able to suspend some personal freedoms temporarily in order to reach a Pareto improvement, and then restore them afterward -- is there a principled way to decide how much liberty should be traded for a given gain in efficiency? Should the answer differ between democracies and other systems?

My teacher mentioned that people like Robert Nozick responded to Sen by saying rights shouldn't be treated as preferences that get fed into a social calculation at all, but I don't see how that escapes the problem -- if anything it seems to just pick the liberty side of the trade-off and ignore the efficiency losses, which is exactly the kind of dogmatism the theorem warns against. Am I reasoning about this correctly, and if so, what's the best framework for making these trade-offs in practice?
