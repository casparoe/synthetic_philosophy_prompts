My dad's favorite saying is "hope for the best, prepare for the worst," and he just deployed it in a fight we had about whether I should quit my stable job to join a friend's startup. His argument: since the worst case of quitting (startup folds, I'm unemployed in a bad market) is so much worse than the worst case of staying (mild boredom, definately survivable), I should stay, full stop, and it doesn't matter how likely success is, because neither of us actually knows the odds. I pushed back that by that logic he'd never have left his hometown or started his own business in the 90s, and he said no, the point isn't that you always pick the safest option, it's that you "keep the worst case in view." Which to me sounded like he was quietly retreating to something much weaker the moment the strong version ruled out risks he approves of.

So here's what I'd like: lay out the different precise principles this saying could actually be encoding, as a menu, from the strongest reading (when you can't assign probabilities, always act as if the worst will happen) down through weaker ones (the worst case is one input among several; just make sure you could survive the worst case; it's really advice about emotional preparation or attention rather than about choice). For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible decision rule, or actually incoherent -- and note where the "hope for the best" half fits, since it seems to pull against the "prepare for the worst" half, and I can't tell if that's a straight contradiction or whether the two halves point toward some kind of compromise rule. Most importantly: which reading, if any, would actually justify my dad's verdict on the startup, and does that same reading also forbid the risks he himself took?

One more thing, since I suspect this whole area is more contested than confident proverb-slinging suggests: please flag which parts of your answer are settled territory (e.g., what the candidate rules even are, where they agree) versus genuinely disputed among people who think seriously about decision-making under deep uncertainty -- I don't want a contested stance on how to choose without probabilities presented as if it were the consensus view.
