I recently began working with a research group that assesses whether advanced AI systems might warrant moral consideration, and a colleague has presented an argument that troubles me, because it appears to show that our entire methodology is hopeless. It runs as follows:

(1) For any digital system that produces intelligent-seeming behavior over some stretch of time, one could in principle construct an enormous lookup table -- a static record pairing every input history with the output the system actually produced -- that reproduces that behavior exactly.

(2) Such a lookup table is plainly not sentient; it is a passive store, no different in kind from a very long book consulted one entry at a time.

(3) If two systems behave identically, then either both are sentient or neither is: sentience cannot depend on facts that make no difference to anything observable.

(4) Therefore no digital system whose behavior can be tabulated in this way is sentient -- and every digital system's behavior can be.

(5) But the same construction applies to a human being: a sufficiently vast table could in principle reproduce a person's actual behavior across a lifetime, so if the argument is sound, no behavioral evidence ever supports attributing sentience to anyone at all.

I would like to keep (2), and I would like to keep some role for behavioral evidence in attributing sentience, since my group's work presupposes both. Which premise or step should be rejected? Why does it look plausible as stated? And what does each way of giving it up cost -- in particular, what must a straightforward functionalist concede, and what must someone who thinks sentience depends essentially on biological or causal organization concede, if either is to block the conclusion?
