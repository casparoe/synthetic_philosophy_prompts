I'm one of three volunteers running the hardship fund for a big housing co-op (around 600 units). Members hit by bad stuff — lost jobs, medical bills, flooded storage, fleeing an abusive partner — can apply for small grants, and we get maybe 30-40 applications a year. We have to be consistent because members see who got paid and who didn't, and lately we've been going on vibes and it's starting to cause real fights at the monthly meetings, where decisions get ratified and appealed.

A friend who's into political philosophy told me what we're groping toward is basically luck egalitarianism: compensate people for bad brute luck (things that just happen to you), not for bad option luck (the downside of gambles you chose). That sounds exactly right as a slogan and totally useless as a procedure, because every real case is a mix.

So write us the procedure. Something a non-philosopher volunteer can run on an application in under an hour, that:

- starts from the brute/option luck distinction but doesn't stay stuck there — e.g. build in something like Dworkin's hypothetical insurance test (would a reasonable person, not knowing how their own luck would turn out, have insured against this loss?) as an actual working step, and tell us what to do when the answer to that question is itself unclear or contested
- handles the regress problem: every choice an applicant made is partly a product of the circumstances, temperament and upbringing they were born into, so "they chose it" never cleanly settles anything. Give us a principled stopping point so the review doesn't turn into a psychoanalysis of the applicant's whole life
- handles the expensive-tastes problem — if someone's loss is large because the life they were living was expensive, is that option luck, or irrelevant, or partly both?
- has explicit points where the procedure stops with a no, and explicit points where it kicks the decision back to the committee as a values call, and makes clear which is which
- says what to do about the harshness problem: the applicant whose need is enormous and whose fault is clear. If the procedure says no to the person who skipped insurance and then got flooded, say so, and tell us whether we should add a floor of no-questions-asked relief and what that does to the coherence of the whole scheme

Then run it step by step on these three applications:

1. A member who quit a stable job to open a bakery, which failed in year two when a supermarket opened next door. She wants compensation for the lost income.
2. A member with a congenital condition whose medication costs just jumped when the drug was dropped from the national reimbursement list. Full documentation, no choices anywhere in sight.
3. A member who stored his uninsured motorbike in a garage he knew was in a flood zone because it was the cheapest option, and lost it in the spring flood. He has no other vehicle and needs it to get to work.

I want at least one of these to come out at an answer that will be unpopular in the co-op meeting, and I want the procedure to own that rather than weasel out of it.

Last thing: be honest about where this procedure will misfire — what kinds of applications will it get wrong, and is there anything the whole brute/option luck framing is blind to that we'd be baking into the fund without noticing?
