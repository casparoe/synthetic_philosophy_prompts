I volunteer as a docent at a small regional museum, and this year the curators have let me sit in on meetings about two thorny items: a conservation proposal for a landscape whose varnish has darkened badly, and a loan request for a painting that a rival institution now insists is a period copy rather than the original our label describes. Preparing for those meetings forced me to write down my working assumptions about originals, copies, and restoration, and I would like them audited for coherence before I embarrass myself in front of the curators. Here is what I find myself committed to. First, I think an original painting is aesthetically more valuable than even a flawless duplicate of it, and I mean genuinely aesthetically, not as a matter of market price or snobbery: when van Meegeren's supposed Vermeers were exposed as his own work they rightly lost standing, and they would not deserve that standing back even if the deception had never been discovered. Second, I hold that aesthetic value supervenes on what is perceptible in the work, on how it looks to an attentive viewer; facts about who made it, when, and why are art-historical background, not part of the work's aesthetic character. Third, I take the character and motives of the maker to be aesthetically irrelevant: a forgery painted out of devotion to the master would be no better or worse, aesthetically, than one painted for money or to humiliate the experts. Fourth, I believe conservation can genuinely recover aesthetic value rather than merely trade one appearance for another: stripping discolored varnish or removing a later hand's overpainting reveals value that was in the work all along, and the cleaned painting is better because it is closer to how the work was meant to be seen. Fifth, I think none of this privileging of the original applies to multiples: every properly struck impression of an etching, every bronze cast from the artist's mold, every competent performance of a score is fully the work, and there is no privileged first instance whose aesthetic rank the others can only approach.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture on the aesthetics of forgery. Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what exactly would have to give on each side; rank the tensions rather than treating them all as equally urgent. Some of these I suspect are actually fine together, and part of what I am testing is whether you agree, so please do not flag every pair out of caution -- explain why the ones you acquit are acquitted. Then tell me which single revision, if I could only make one, would clean up the most, and what it would cost me in terms of the cases I care about. One constraint: where you think a tension can be dissolved by drawing a distinction (between kinds of value, or kinds of artworks, or kinds of fact about a work), I want you to say whether that distinction is one I can actually afford given my other commitments, or whether accepting it quietly abandons one of them.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
