I’m a retired field ecologist who has lately been reading philosophy of science, mostly badly and with too many exclamation marks in the margins. A friend has asked me to run one meeting of her evening seminar for adults, about 14 people and 80 minutes, and I’d like to design an activity around the “old evidence” problem for Bayesian confirmation. As I understand it, if some evidence E was already known, then its probability is 1, so simply learning E cannot raise the probability of a hypothesis H. Yet it seems obvious that a scientist can suddenly become more confident in H upon realizing that H explains or entails some familiar old fact. The new discovery seems to be a relation between the theory and the evidence, not the evidence itself. I want the group to feel that distinction rather than just hear me say it.

Could you design a runnable classroom game or structured simulation for this? Please use a concrete fictional scientific episode, preferably not coins, cards, or colored balls. I had imagined something like participants receiving an old, slightly messy research dossier—field notes, measurements, failed predictions, instrument quirks—and several competing hypotheses, with everyone knowing from the beginning that all the observations in the dossier are old. During the game they should discover or construct previously unnoticed connections between hypotheses and observations, and then have to decide whether and how much confidence should change. Ideally the rules should also create some temptation toward cheating intellectually: quietly modifying a hypothesis after seeing the data, treating an auxiliary excuse as harmless, or acting as though accommodation were just as good as prediction. Please include the setup, materials, roles or teams, exact rules, a plausible scoring or reward system that does not use money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and guidance for how much Bayes notation to introduce. The mathematics should be available for people who want it, but the game should still work for people who freeze at the sight of a formula.

The debrief matters as much as the game. I want it to map particular moments onto ideas such as conditionalization, logical learning, prediction versus accommodation, auxiliary assumptions, and the worry that a theory can be gerrymandered to fit whatever already happened. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for instance, people pretending they did not know evidence that was public all along—and how to prevent or handle those problems. Finally, be honest about which feature of the real philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce, especially anything involving ideal logical omniscience or precise numerical credences. And since I’m still learning the territory, please distinguish clearly between what is basically standard Bayesian machinery, what most philosophers would accept as a fair description of the old-evidence puzzle, and which proposed solutions remain genuinely contested; please don’t present one disputed resolution as if it were the settled consensus.
