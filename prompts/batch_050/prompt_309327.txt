I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I’m putting together a sidebar on a fight I keep seeing underneath arguments about referendums. It isn’t simply pro-democracy versus anti-democracy. It’s two different pictures of what a popular vote is for.

Picture an adversarial collaboration between these two camps:

The citizen-lawmaking camp thinks initiatives and referendums should be a normal way to make policy, not just a last resort. On this view, representative bodies systematically ignore some widely shared concerns because of party discipline, donor influence, agenda control, or simple inertia. Switzerland is the rough inspiration here: repeated popular votes can let citizens put issues on the table and discipline lawmakers between elections.

The emergency-brake camp thinks direct votes are most defensible when they prevent or reverse a clear failure of representation. Complex statutes, coordinated policy packages, and long-term budgeting should normally be drafted by accountable institutions. A referendum can be a crucial veto or a demand for reconsideration, but treating it as an ordinary legislature invites oversimplified choices, manipulation by whoever frames the question, and policy without an identifiable owner. California’s ballot-initiative morass and the Brexit vote are the obvious cautionary tales.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could plausibly sign after a serious collaboration. Don’t adjudicate between them and don’t turn either into a caricature. Structure it like this:

1. Shared ground. Give 6–10 claims both camps could endorse. Make them substantive but genuinely bipartisan: for example, claims about agenda control, preference intensity, political accountability, the difference between choosing ends and designing means, or the dangers of treating one vote as a permanent mandate. Avoid empirical claims that would require a citation.

2. The remaining fault lines. State each disagreement in two parts: how the citizen-lawmaking side would describe its position, and how the emergency-brake side would describe its own position. Seperate normative disagreements from empirical and institutional ones. Flag any dispute that is mostly verbal, such as both sides using legitimacy or the will of the people differently.

3. Cruxes. Identify the questions that would actually move one side or the other. I’d expect some to concern whether voters can reasonably assess complex proposals, whether initiative campaigns mainly counteract elite agenda control or create a new kind of agenda control, how direct votes affect politically weak minorities, and whether repeated participation improves or degrades judgment. Don’t feel limited to those. For each crux, name an argument, institutional comparison, or imaginable evidence that both sides would accept as relevant, and record what each predicts it would show.

4. Apply the statement to this invented proposal, the Citizen Agenda and Veto Act:

Citizens who gather signatures from 6 percent of registered voters, with a minimum in at least three-quarters of the country’s regions, may place a drafted statute before the legislature. Within nine months the legislature must pass it unchanged, pass a compatible amendment, or put both versions on the ballot. A plain-language explanation, projected budget effects, and public hearings are required. If both ballot versions receive majority approval, the one with more yes votes takes effect after eighteen months. The legislature may later amend it only by a three-fifths vote, except for technical corrections or urgent spending changes. Constitutional amendments require both a national majority and majorities in most regions.

Which parts would each camp accept, resist, or redesign, and why? Where would their reasons be the same even though their recommendations differ?

5. End with a short section called What would change our minds. For each camp, specify one finding or argument that would weaken its position and one that would not, even though opponents often assume it should. Where relevant, use Switzerland, Brexit, and California as types of case rather than pretending that contested historical details settle the argument.

Make it sound like a careful public document, not a transcript and not a magazine op-ed. No bibliography or citations, please.
