ok so I work at a small democracy-reform org (won't say which country, but think somewhere in Europe with a big non-citizen resident population) and we've got funding to run ONE serious multi-year campaign, maybe 4 years, with real money behind it. Our board has narrowed it down to three candidate reforms:

1. Compulsory voting, roughly Australia-style (modest fine, easy conscientious-objection style exemptions, no enforcement theatrics)
2. Lowering the voting age to 16 for national elections
3. Full national-election voting rights for long-term resident non-citizens (say 5+ years of legal residency)

We have to pick one and commit. My question: which should it be, and in what order would you rank the other two as backups? But I'm way less interested in your final answer than in the reasoning behind the ranking. Specifically I want you to think through:

- Which reform actually changes WHO the electorate is the most, per person enfranchised or mobilized, versus which one mostly shuffles turnout among people already eligible
- Feasibility: which of these could plausibly pass in a real European-ish political climate in the next decade, and which is dead on arrival no matter how good the arguments are
- Durability and backlash risk — e.g. if you win the non-citizen vote, does the next government just strip it back, and does a failed fight make future expansion harder
- The downstream stuff people don't talk about: does compulsory voting change party strategy in ways that matter more than the raw turnout numbers? Does enfranchising 16-year-olds mostly just import parental partisanship, and if so does that undercut the main argument for it?

Also, pick one of the three that you think is most OVERRATED relative to its actual likely effects, and say why — like, which one has the biggest gap between how it's argued about (as a matter of democratic principle, all-affected-interests vs. all-subjected-to-coercion and so on) and what it would actually do to electoral outcomes or policy. We keep getting pulled toward whichever reform sounds most principled in a seminar room and I suspect that's the wrong selection criterion for a campaign that has to survive contact with real politics.

Defend the ordering with actual criteria, not just vibes. If two of them are close, say what evidence would flip them.
