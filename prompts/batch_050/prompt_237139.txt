I'm prepping a unit on the rule of law for my jurisprudence course and I want to stress-test my own intuitions before I teach Fuller's eight desiderata as if they were gospel. So here are my verdicts on some cases, roughly in the order I'd present them, and I'd like to know what principle (if any) is holding them together.

1. A regime passes a statute that is technically public but written so obscurely that only specialists can parse it. Bad for rule-of-law reasons, I think, even though nothing was kept secret.
2. A regime keeps its criminal code secret but in practice only ever punishes things that are obviously wrong anyway (murder, theft). Still bad, and it feels worse than case 1, though I'm not sure I can say why.
3. A retroactive tax adjustment that claws back a loophole people had been exploiting in good faith. This bothers me, but only mildly.
4. Retroactive criminal punishment for conduct that was legal when done but clearly immoral (say, a newly democratic state prosecuting officials who tortured under color of the old regime's explicit authorization). This bothers me less than case 3, even though the retroactivity here is criminal and the stakes for the defendant are higher. I'm aware this is the awkward one.
5. A legal system like the UK's, with no single codified constitution and lots of convention. Seems basically fine, rule-of-law-wise, despite failing promulgation in a loose sense.
6. A legal system where every statute is clear, public, prospective, and stable, but judges routinely ignore the statutes and decide cases by flipping a coin -- and everyone knows it. This strikes me as a total rule-of-law failure, worse than 1-4, even though all of Fuller's formal criteria about the laws themselves are satisfied. So I guess I don't actually think legality is only about the properties of the laws.
7. A system where courts apply the laws faithfully but the laws themselves are monstrous (apartheid-style). I want to say the rule of law is substantially present but the system is still evil -- i.e. I lean against the Fuller-ish thought that legality has inherent moral content. Though case 6 makes me wonder if I'm smuggling moral content back in.

So: is there a single principle of legality that delivers verdicts 1-7, or even 1-6 plus my lean in 7? Fuller alone clearly can't handle 6. Raz's instrumental account might, but I suspect it gives the wrong verdict on 4 or forces me to treat 4 and 3 identically, which I resist. Waldron's emphasis on courts and argumentation helps with 6 but I'm not sure it explains why 2 is worse than 1.

Give me your best candidate principle (or two rival ones, if you think the verdicts genuinely fork), stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing work in which case. Tell me which of my verdicts each candidate has to sacrifice -- especially whether the 3/4 asymmetry is defensible or whether I'm just being squeamish in a pattern-matching way. And then tell me what your favored principle says about a case I havent considered: a legal system where the laws are clear and prospective but change so frequently that no one can plan around them.
