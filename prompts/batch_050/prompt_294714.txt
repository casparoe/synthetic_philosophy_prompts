In my accuracy-first epistemology seminar, two students got into a surprisingly heated fight over this case. Priya, an otherwise ideal forecaster, assigns 0.7 to rain today based on all her meteorological evidence. She then receives a trustworthy calibration audit: on a large number of past occasions when she had exactly this same evidence, it rained 80% of the time. Assume conditions are stable and the audit was not cherry-picked.

Their exchange went like this:

1. Ada: Accuracy-first considerations require Priya to move to 0.8. That forecast would have earned a better Brier score in the relevant reference class and would be calibrated.
2. Ben: This conflates a good ex post track record with expected accuracy. Given Priya’s current credence of 0.7, a strictly proper scoring rule makes 0.7 immodest: by her own lights, no other credence has higher expected epistemic utility. So she cannot have a purely epistemic reason to change without new evidence.
3. Ada: But the audit is new evidence. Refusing to revise treats information about her reliability as epistemically irrelevant.
4. Ben: If the audit really supports 0.8, then Priya should already have that credence once she possesses the audit. Saying “her credence is 0.7, and accuracy tells her to adopt 0.8” describes irrationality; it does not identify a further non-evidential reason for revision.
5. Ada: That is just the familiar phenomenon of higher-order evidence. Someone can learn that her judgment is systematically off without yet knowing which first-order consideration she misweighted.
6. Ben: Still, calibration is not the fundamental goal. Once Priya has the audit, the question is whether 0.8 minimizes expected Brier loss relative to her updated total evidence, not whether “being calibrated” has independent value.
