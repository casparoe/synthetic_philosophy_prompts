In the economics course I teach, I introduce money with the standard comparison that money is a measure of value, just as a ruler measures length and a balance measures weight: prices tell you how much things are worth the way inches tell you how long things are. The analogy does a lot of quiet work for me later in the syllabus, because it supports the idea that money is neutral -- a transparent measuring device that records values determined elsewhere, in the real economy of goods and wants, without affecting them. I have never been fully comfortable leaning on it, and my discomfort sharpened when I reread the passage in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (Book V, chapter 5) that seems to be the ancestor of the whole comparison. In Rackham's translation, Aristotle writes:

"money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food."

So far, so good for the ruler analogy. But a few lines later in the same discussion he writes:

"And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

Audit the ruler analogy for me, using this passage as the test case. First give me the correspondence table: what, in the analogy as teachers like me actually use it, plays the role of the ruler, the measured lengths, the act of measuring, and the unit. Then say where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for my neutrality conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, take seriously the tension internal to Aristotle's own text: he calls money a measure of all things, but then locates the real standard in demand -- something that varies with human wants and could shift or vanish -- and insists that money's existence is conventional throughout. No ruler shrinks because people stop caring about length, and the meter convention, while conventional, is not subject to revision in the way Aristotle says nomisma is. Does the second half of the passage refute the picture the first half invites, or can a sophisticated version of the measure analogy accommodate both halves at once? Finally: if the neutrality conclusion I want for my syllabus depends on the measure picture, does it survive your audit, and if the ruler is the wrong comparison, what would be a better analogy for money that stays faithful to both halves of Aristotle's account? Aim for around 700 words.
