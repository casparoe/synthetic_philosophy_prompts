So here's the situation. I spent about 35 years as an experimental physicist (condensed matter, lots of spectroscopy), retired a while back, and in retirement I've gotten into philosophy of science and epistemology. I can hold my own with the concepts now, but my native vocabulary is still the one I did science in: likelihoods, estimators, consistency, identifiability, confidence intervals, systematics, sensitivity analyses. When I read probability theory I instinctively translate everything into that.

Here's what I keep bumping into. In the Bayesian literature there's a family of results people gesture at with the phrase "priors wash out" -- the idea that agents who start with different prior beliefs, if they update on the same stream of evidence, will end up in agreement, and that this is supposed to answer the objection that Bayesianism just builds subjectivity in at the foundation. I've read enough to know the theorems have teeth but also real assumptions (something about giving the truth a nonzero prior, something about the likelihoods eventually dominating, and I gather there are fiddly measure-theoretic conditions I only dimly follow).

What I'd like you to do: restate this whole debate -- the convergence results, their assumptions, and the dispute about what they actually establish for rationality -- in the vocabulary I actually think in. Consistency of estimators, asymptotics, identifiability conditions, likelihood dominating the prior the way a big dataset dominates a small one, sensitivity analysis over starting assumptions. Do it carefully, theorem by theorem if you can, and don't sneak Bayesian talk back in and call it translation.

Then the part I care most about: a residue report. What doesn't survive the translation? Some specific things I'm suspicious about, but don't limit yourself to these:

- The frequentist/estimation vocabulary talks about *procedures* and their long-run properties. The Bayesian theorems seem to be about *agents* and what they're rationally required to believe. Is there anything in estimation talk that corresponds to that, or does it evaporate?
- The condition that your prior assign nonzero probability to the truth smells to me like an identifiability condition. Is that right, or is something lost in saying it that way?
- Bayesians seem to worry about cases where convergence fails or takes absurdly long -- priors that are dogmatic, or that smear probability in the wrong places. My vocabulary has analogues (misspecified models, pathological estimators) but I can't tell if they capture the same worry.
- Is there any counterpart in estimation theory to the claim that convergence *justifies* the framework -- that the theorems are doing normative work, not just descriptive work? My instincts say "a consistent estimator is just a good estimator, full stop," and I suspect the Bayesians are reading more into the math than is there. But maybe it's the other way round and my vocabulary just can't say the thing they're saying.

End with your honest verdict: is the residue a genuine gap in the estimation framework (the theorems really do say something about rational belief that estimation talk can't express), or is it a sign that philosophers are making more of these results than the math supports? If it's somewhere in between, say where.
