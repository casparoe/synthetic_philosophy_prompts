I teach an upper-division seminar on the ethics of war, and this term I assigned, alongside the primary readings, a chapter from a recent trade book written for general readers. A student came to office hours last week confused, because the chapter's presentation did not match what she took from the seminar readings, and after rereading it I suspect she is right to be confused. Here is the chapter's summary of the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants and of Jeff McMahan's critique of it:

"Classical just war theory holds that soldiers on opposing sides of a war are moral equals. A soldier who fights in an unjust war does nothing wrong by fighting, so long as he observes the rules governing conduct in battle, because responsibility for the war rests entirely with political leaders and ordinary soldiers are simply in no position to judge the justice of their country's cause. Jeff McMahan has argued that this comfortable doctrine is a mistake. On his view, whether it is permissible to kill in war depends on the justice of one's cause, just as in ordinary life it depends on who is the wrongdoer. Soldiers who fight for an unjust cause are morally responsible for posing wrongful threats, and for that reason they deserve to be killed; they may permissibly be targeted even when they are not doing anything immediately dangerous. Just combatants, by contrast, have done nothing wrong, and so they may never permissibly be attacked. McMahan concludes from this that the laws of war should be brought into line with the underlying morality, so that soldiers who participate in unjust wars can be prosecuted simply for having fought."

My inclination is that the opening characterization of the traditional view is broadly right, and that the attribution of the basic critical idea to McMahan is right, but that the chapter goes astray in at least two places in how it states his positive view and his practical conclusion. I do not fully trust my recollection, however, and I want to be careful before I tell my student that a published book misrepresents the literature. Could you audit the paragraph sentence by sentence? For each claim, tell me whether a defender of the relevant position would accept it, regard it as a tolerable simplification for a general audience, or reject it as a distortion, and explain why, with particular attention to how McMahan actually grounds the asymmetric status of unjust combatants and to what he says about the relation between the deep morality of war and the laws of war. Finally, the chapter presents its last sentence as the natural practical upshot of the critique: if McMahan's premises are granted, does that upshot follow, or has the author conflated two questions McMahan himself takes pains to keep apart?
