I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on the principle of indifference, and before committing my objections to print I want to rehearse them against a capable defender rather than a strawman. Please play that defender. Your position: the principle of indifference, properly applied, is a legitimate rule for assigning probabilities under ignorance, and Bertrand-style paradoxes diagnose ill-posed problems rather than any defect in the principle itself. You hold that when a problem possesses a genuine symmetry, there is a uniquely correct indifferent distribution, found by requiring invariance under whatever transformations leave the problem statement unchanged, and that the familiar contradictory assignments arise only because people thoughtlessly apply indifference to whatever variable happens to appear in the wording. Play this position with real resources: you may concede that naive indifference fails, that the principle has a restricted domain of application, and various small points along the way, but you never concede that the paradoxes refute the principle as such. Stay in character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and if one of my challenges is genuinely ambiguous, you may ask one clarifying question before answering.

Here is my opening move. Consider a case deliberately constructed to have no symmetry for your method to exploit. I tell you only that a certain machine produces cubes whose side length lies somewhere between 1 cm and 2 cm, and I ask for the probability that the next cube's side exceeds 1.5 cm. Indifference over side length gives 1/2. Indifference over volume gives (8 − 3.375)/7 ≈ 0.66. Your invariance machinery is silent here: nothing in my statement exhibits a scaling group or marks any variable as the natural one — there is only a bare interval and total ignorance. So you face a dilemma. Either you admit that in such cases the principle yields no answer at all, which concedes that ignorance alone never determines a distribution and that every successful application of the principle was secretly relying on extra structure supplied by the problem; or you produce a rule for privileging side length over volume, and whatever rule you produce will itself be a substantive commitment I never gave you. On either horn, the principle is not a logic of pure ignorance but a device for importing assumptions while concealing that it does so. What is your reply?
