I'm a novelist, and at my writers' group last Thursday two of the others got into a proper row over something and neither of them would drop it, and I've been turning it over since because it actually bears on a book I'm drafting (one of my characters is surveilled without knowing it, and I need to decide whether the reader is meant to feel she's been wronged or not). The argument went roughly like this. (1) Martin claimed that privacy is only ever instrumentally valuable: a breach of privacy harms you through its consequences, like embarrassment, blackmail, losing a job, and so if a peeping tom watches someone undress and she never finds out and he never tells a soul, he has done something creepy and wrong in general, but he hasn't wronged *her privacy*, because she's none the wiser and nothing follows from it. (2) Priya shot back that on that view a surgeon who secretly reads a patient's diary while she's under anaesthetic, memorises nothing actionable and tells nobody, has done nothing wrong to her either, and that our revulsion at voyeurism shows the wrong is located in the *access itself* -- someone got into your private sphere -- not in whatever happens downstream. (3) Martin said fine, but if access is what matters, then the spam filter that scans all her email, or CCTV footage that gets recorded and automatically deleted unwatched, violates her privacy every bit as much as the voyeur does, which is clearly daft, so consequences have to come back into the picture somewhere. (4) Priya admitted she couldn't cleanly say where mere "processing" ends and "access" begins, but suggested the difference is the standing possibility of a conscious mind actually taking you in: the deleted footage could never have been watched by anyone, whereas the voyeur really was watching. Martin just replied that this makes the wrongness of voyeurism depend on some spooky fact about *could have beens*, and at that point the pub quiz started and they never resolved it.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the four moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a sentence or two of justification for each, and flag anything important both of them seem to be missing. I don't mind a bit of hedging where it's genuinely unclear, but I do want actual verdicts rather than "philosophers disagree". Finish with a single last line in exactly this form: \answer{whoever is closer to right overall, plus a very short reason}.
