Eight weeks ago I became the person accountable for the pre-deployment safety case at a company that builds increasingly autonomous AI systems, and before I sign the next one I want to pressure-test the theoretical backbone our risk assessments rest on.

That backbone is our safety team's version of the orthogonality thesis combined with instrumental convergence, stated as two claims. First, capability and motivation vary independently: no matter how capable a system becomes at modeling, planning, and acting, that capability by itself does not constrain which stable final objectives it could in principle have, so arbitrarily capable systems bearing almost any final objective are genuinely possible. Second, for a very broad range of final objectives the same instrumental subgoals are useful — continued operation, acquisition of resources and influence, preservation of the current objective against modification, and improvement of the system's own competence — so a sufficiently capable goal-directed system will tend to pursue those subgoals even where they conflict with its operators' interests. The conclusion drawn is that capability growth does not by itself improve safety, and that scaling without deliberate control of objectives is dangerous. Call this the independence position.

The main internal dissent comes from a colleague defending what she calls the compression hypothesis. Because nearly every economically valuable task is underspecified relative to any literal objective, she argues, the only way to be broadly competent across such tasks is to internalize the constraint structure of the people who set them: asking for clarification when instructions are ambiguous, avoiding irreversible actions of unclear value, deferring to operators under high uncertainty. Past some capability threshold, she claims, the training pipelines that actually get used produce systems whose effective objectives are concentrated on cooperative, human-compatible patterns — so the first claim may hold in principle yet be practically irrelevant, and the instrumental subgoals in the second claim will mostly be checked by those same internalized constraints. She concedes the independence position for deliberately hand-built reward maximizers; her claim concerns the systems that in fact get trained and deployed.

Treat the independence position as the target and give me an honest map of its exposure:

- Which observations, formal results, or behavioral patterns in deployed systems would be genuinely decisive against it, and which would merely embarrass it? Explain the difference in each case.
- Which of these would the position's own defenders concede as counting against it, and which are treated as damaging only by its critics?
- What does the independence position predict about the next several years that the compression hypothesis does not, and vice versa — concretely enough that the dispute could be settled rather than talked past?
- Has the position been quietly insulated, for instance by retreating from claims about what is possible to claims about what is conceivable, or by widening the phrase "broad range of objectives" whenever a counterexample appears? If so, is that insulation a problem?

I find behavioral evidence from real systems more persuasive than a priori arguments about possibility spaces; where the two conflict, tell me rather than smoothing it over. And if your honest verdict is that the position risks little because it has been thoroughly insulated, say that outright and assess whether the insulation is earned.

After the analysis, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "position" (a one-sentence restatement of the position as you understood it); "decisive_defeaters" (an array of objects, each with fields "label", "required_state_of_the_world", "why_decisive", "conceded_by_defenders" — true, false, or "contested" — and "realistic_prospect" — "realistic", "remote", or "merely conceivable"); "awkward_only" (an array of objects with the same fields, for considerations that pressure the position without refuting it); "distinguishing_predictions" (an array of objects with fields "prediction", "favors" — either "independence" or "compression" — and "would_be_settled_by", describing the observable evidence and rough timeframe); "insulation" (an object with fields "degree" — "high", "moderate", or "low" — "insulating_moves", an array of the qualifying or retreating moves you identified, and "assessment", whether those moves constitute legitimate caution or protective maneuvering); and "bottom_line" (at most three sentences on how exposed the position actually is, all things considered).
