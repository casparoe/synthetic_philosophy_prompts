When physicists count the microstates of a system of particles, they use a different bookkeeping for particles of the same kind than for distinguishable ones. Exchange two electrons and no new microstate results: the swapped and unswapped arrangements are counted as the very same state of affairs. This single convention is what delivers Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac statistics, and its classical counterpart — dividing by N! to acknowledge that permutations of identical molecules generate nothing new — is what resolves the Gibbs paradox about the entropy of mixing. Yet the same physicists elsewhere speak of particles as if they were trackable individuals: an experimenter follows a single particle's track through a detector, and no one finds this talk confused. My question is what metaphysics of identity the counting practice presupposes. Does it require denying that particles possess any primitive thisness — some fact about which electron is which that goes beyond their properties? Or is it compatible with particles being genuine individuals, so long as permuting them does not produce a physically distinct state? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of identity and individuation behind the practice, and be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with: could a metaphysics on which electrons are full-blooded individuals nonetheless license the same counting? Then assess whether the practice is internally consistent, given that individuating talk about particular particles is tolerated in the laboratory but excluded from the state-counting. Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change — in the counting rules, and hence in the empirical predictions — if particles turned out to be individuals in the classical sense, each bearing a thisness that made every permutation a genuinely distinct arrangement.
