I've been reading a popular book on the philosophy of mathematics — I'd rather not name it, since my question is about the content, not the author — and there's a paragraph I'm tempted to use as the hook for a blog post about what we can claim to know and how. But I only want to build on it if it holds up, and after years of reading rationalist forums I've learned that this is exactly the kind of paragraph that feels rigorous while quietly smuggling in a conclusion. The paragraph runs as follows.

"When Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken announced their proof of the four-colour theorem in 1976, they divided the labor in the obvious way: a traditional, human-checkable argument showing that any counterexample to the theorem would have to contain one of a few thousand configurations, and a brute-force computer check that each of those configurations is reducible. Since no human being could ever survey the computer's part of the proof, the proof is not genuinely verifiable, and we believe the theorem the way we believe a result in chemistry — on the strength of a massive experiment rather than on the strength of a proof. Thomas Tymoczko drew the natural conclusion that mathematics is a quasi-empirical science whose results are known a posteriori, and the episode stands as the first time mathematicians were forced to accept a theorem on faith. When Georges Gonthier produced a fully machine-checked formalization of the proof in Coq in 2005, the philosophical problem evaporated, because the proof could at last be verified line by line."

The broad shape of this matches what I remember from old discussions about unsurveyable proofs, and the author is a working mathematician, so I'm inclined to trust it. Still, the move from "no human can survey the computation" to "we believe this the way we believe chemistry" strikes me as suspiciously fast, and I can't tell whether my unease is tracking a real error or just my own attachment to proofs being a priori.

So: is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification that a specialist would let pass in a popular treatment, and what is actually wrong or misattributed. In particular, I want to know whether unsurveyability and unverifiability are being run together, whether Tymoczko's actual conclusion is as sweeping as the paragraph makes it sound, whether the "first theorem accepted on faith" framing survives scrutiny, and whether a machine-checked formalization like Gonthier's answers the original worry or merely a different one — I've seen people on the forums conflate "we're now certain it's correct" with "we now understand why it's true," and I suspect something similar is happening here.
