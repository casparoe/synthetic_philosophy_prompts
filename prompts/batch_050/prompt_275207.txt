So I'm writing a thing about whether "generations" (Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, etc.) are real kinds or just marketing labels slapped on arbitrary birth-year ranges. I've been collecting mechanisms by which a cohort *could* become a genuinely real kind -- i.e. processes that would make the members of a generation actually similar to each other and different from adjacent cohorts, rather than the label being an empty fiction. My list so far:

1. Shared formative events -- big public events (a war, 9/11, the 2008 crash, covid) hit people at impressionable ages and leave a lasting mark on everyone who was, say, 15-25 at the time, in a way that genuinely separates them from people who were 40.
2. Economic cohort effects -- the state of the labor market when you first enter it (graduating into a recession vs. a boom) has durable effects on earnings, risk attitudes, trust in institutions, etc. This one is at least measurable.
3. Institutional cohorting -- schools, conscription, licensing ages etc. process people in age-batches, so a cohort literally shares experiences and networks in a synchronized way.
4. Looping effects of the label itself -- once "Millennial" exists as a category, people start identifying with it, behaving as Millennials are supposed to, and institutions start treating them as a group (HR policies, targeted ads), so the classification feeds back and makes the kind realer than it was.
5. Demographic reality -- at least one boundary (the post-WWII baby boom) tracks an actual bulge in birth rates, so there's a non-arbitrary population fact underneath that particular label.

Here's my question: what's a *sixth* mechanism that isn't on this list? I don't want a rewording, special case, or combination of the five above (e.g. don't just give me "formative events but for technology" -- that would be a special case of #1). It should be a genuinely different causal process by which a birth cohort could come to share real, robust properties as a group. Also tell me whether your mechanism would make generations "real" in a strong sense (like homeostatic property clusters) or only in a weaker, statistical sense.

Also: please be clear about what's basically settled here (e.g. that the marketing-style boundaries are arbitrary, that *some* cohort effects exist in the data) versus what's genuinely contested among sociologists/demographers/philosophers, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus. American English please.
