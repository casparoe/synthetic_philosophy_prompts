DRAFT: "Three Theories Walk Into a Bar"

Section 1. The philosophy and psychology of humour has three classic theories and one newer contender. The superiority theory (Hobbes and friends) says laughter is a sudden glory at others' expense -- we laugh at the stumble, the loser, the butt of the joke. The incongruity theory (Kant, Schopenhauer) says humour is the perception of an expectation violated, a script that suddenly doesn't fit. The relief theory (Freud) says laughter discharges pent-up psychic energy, which is why taboo topics are comedy gold. And the recent benign violation theory (McGraw and Warren) tries to unify things: humour requires a violation that is simultaneously appraised as benign.

Section 2. Here's my claim: the century-long debate between these is largely a non-dispute, because the theories are answering different questions. Superiority answers "who is humour about and what does it do socially?" Incongruity answers "what is the formal structure of a humorous stimulus?" Relief answers "why does laughter feel the way it feel's?" You could consistently hold all three, and benign violation theory is just what you get when you notice this and glue incongruity (violation) to a relief-ish appraisal condition (benign). The only genuine disagreement is whether the social/aggressive element is essential or incidental.

Section 3. My own proposal: humour is playful norm-probing. Jokes let a group test which of its norms are load-bearing by violating them in a low-stakes key, and laughter is a public signal meaning roughly: violation registered, reclassified as safe, we can keep going. This differs from benign violation theory in one way I think matters -- BVT is individualistic (it's about one person's appraisal), whereas mine is essentially about audiences and coordination. Predictions: humour should be weaker and weirder alone; laughter should be more socially policed exactly where norms are contested (which is why comedy about currently-fought-over topics generates the most heat); and failed jokes should be informative rather than merely neutral, because a joke that lands badly tells you where the norm boundaries actually are.

Section 4 (sketchy). Worries I haven't dealt with: puns (where's the norm?), absurdist humour, laughing from tickling, why some norm violations are horrifying rather than funny even when "safe."
