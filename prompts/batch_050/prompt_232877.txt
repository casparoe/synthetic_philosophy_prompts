I'm giving a talk at an APA-adjacent workshop on comparative ethics in about six weeks, defending what I know is a spicy thesis: that the standard framing of the Mozi-vs-Confucian dispute (impartial care on one side, graded love on the other) is mostly a dispute about moral *psychology*, not about moral requirements. My claim is that if you actually build out Confucian graded love with the extension mechanism (the Mengzi move where the king who can't watch the ox being slaughtered gets that feeling stretched outward), and you build out Mohist impartial care with its own constraints (the Mohists still need filial piety and special relationships to make the whole thing motivationally stable, they just refuse to let those ground obligations), the two positions converge on almost identical prescriptions in almost all realistic cases. What actually differs is what they say you're feeling and why you should trust that feeling. So the "debate" is a debate about which moral education regime produces reliable agents, and I think the Mohists win that one.

The audience: roughly half Chinese philosophy specialists, split between two real Mohist partisans (the kind who think Mozi has been unfairly treated by the whole tradition and have the three standards memorized) and some Confucian virtue ethics people, plus a handful of utilitarians and care ethicists who will keep trying to translate everything into their own vocabulary and probably misunderstand both sides in the process.
