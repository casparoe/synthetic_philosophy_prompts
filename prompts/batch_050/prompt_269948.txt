I've been messing around with toy models of self-modifying agents for a side project, and I keep bumping into the Löbian obstacle, which my friend who's way too into the whole AI alignment scene keeps telling me is the deep reason agents can't just rubber-stamp their successors. My rough understanding: a proof system strong enough to do basic arithmetic can't consistently prove the blanket rule "if my system proves P, then P," because by Löb's theorem, proving that conditional for any particular P already requires being able to prove P itself. So full self-trust buys you nothing, and if you tack it on as an axiom anyway you've smuggled in something unearned. Fine. But my gut verdicts on concrete cases don't line up into any coherent pattern, and I want to know what principle (if any) I'm actually running.

So here are my verdicts. (a) A big theorem prover that only accepts proofs a tiny separate checker can verify line-by-line: trusting the checker is totally unproblematic. (b) An agent that builds a successor identical to itself -- same axioms, same rules -- just with a much bigger search budget: feels completely fine, it's basically just me with more time. (c) An agent that builds a successor which is itself plus one new axiom saying everything the old system proves is true: feels fishy as hell, like pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps. (d) Me trusting future-me-with-a-week over current-me on a math claim I could in principle check myself: obviously fine, I do this constantly. (e) An agent deferring to a successor so much smarter that the agent can't even in principle follow the successor's reasoning -- like me accepting a superintelligence's proof of a conjecture where I can't check any of the steps: my gut says this is rational in practice, but I notice it seems structurally identical to (c), which I rejected. (f) An agent that trusts its successor only for proofs under some fixed length bound, or only for statements below some complexity ceiling: feels fine but weirdly ad hoc, like I'm just dodging.

The pair I can't reconcile is (b) versus (c) -- in both cases the successor is "me, plus more confidence in me" in some loose sense, so why does one feel safe and the other feel like a scam? And (d) versus (e) bugs me too, since whatever makes deferring to smarter future-me okay for a human seems like it should either work for the formal agent too or I should be way more suspicious of myself than I am. So: is there one principle that gets all or most of these? My tentative candidate is something like "trust is legitimate exactly when the truster could in principle re-derive or verify the trustee's outputs itself, given enough resources" -- which handles (a), (b), (d), maybe (f), and kills (c) and (e) -- but I'm not sure it survives contact with the actual theorem, and I suspect it might wrongly bless (c) depending on how you cash out "in principle." If you think a different principle does better, give me that instead, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and say what it predicts for cases I didn't list, like trusting a successor that's merely very probably sound, or a chain of successors where each one only trusts the next.
