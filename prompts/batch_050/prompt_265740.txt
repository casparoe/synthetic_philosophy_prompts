In a discussion at a dinner party about moral responsibility, someone cut things short with the line "your brain decides before you do," citing Libet-style neuroscience as proof that free will is an illusion. I've since seen the same one-liner used to argue everything from "you can't be blamed for your actions" to "conscious deliberation is a post-hoc story the brain tells itself." I'd like you to treat the slogan itself as the object of analysis. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing -- for example, a claim merely about the timing of conscious intentions relative to measurable brain activity; a claim that the readiness potential is itself the decision; a claim that conscious will is causally inert; a claim that all decision-making is unconscious; a claim that experimental setups like Libet's generalize to weighty real-life choices -- and for each, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or actually false given what's known about the experiments and their later replications and reinterpretations (including the "free won't" veto idea and worries about what the readiness potential even measures). Then tell me which reading the slogan's users most plausibly intend, and which readings, if any, would actually support the dinner-party use of ending a debate about moral responsibility. Finally, state what evidence or argument would change your verdict on the strongest reading -- where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
