I am drafting the framing chapter of a dissertation on the metaphysics of time travel, and before circulating it to my committee I want an audit of whether my working position is jointly tenable. Here is the package as it currently stands:

(1) Backward time travel is logically and metaphysically possible. The grandfather paradox shows only that travel to the past is subject to consistency constraints; it does not show that such travel cannot occur.

(2) There is exactly one timeline. A traveler who journeys to the past changes nothing: everything she does at her destination time has always been part of the history of that time, including her arrival.

(3) No special mechanism enforces consistency. There is no force, law, or guardian of the past that prevents a traveler from killing her grandfather. Every failed attempt fails for reasons that would fully satisfy an onlooker who knew nothing of time travel: a jammed pistol, a sudden cramp, a passerby's intervention, a change of heart.

(4) Travelers in the past retain their abilities in the ordinary sense. A trained, armed, well-positioned assassin standing before her grandfather can kill him in exactly the sense in which any competent assassin can kill a target; nothing about her situation constitutes an incapacity, and verdicts about what she is able to do are to be reached by the same standards we apply to anyone else.

(5) Rational deliberation is insensitive to the temporal location of the outcome. A traveler who knows the historical record — who knows her grandfather lived to sire her parent — can nonetheless rationally deliberate about whether to kill him, form the intention, and make the attempt, and her attempt is not thereby less rational than an identical attempt on a stranger whose future is unknown to her.

(6) Consistency constraints generate no explanatory debt. Even in a world where time travel is commonplace and attempts on ancestors are made daily, the pattern of universal failure would call for no explanation beyond the ordinary explanations of the individual failures; there is no coincidence problem for the single-timeline picture, at any scale.

Some of these I have held for years and others are recent additions, and I have lost track of what commits me to what. Please audit the set: identify each pair or larger subset that stands in genuine tension, ordered from most to least severe, and for each one state precisely what the tension consists in, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs. Distinguish these from pairs that merely appear incompatible — where a tension dissolves once the commitments are stated carefully, show the dissolution rather than flagging it. I would rather abandon one thesis cheaply than defend the whole package at high cost, so conclude by naming the single revision that purchases the most consistency for the least sacrifice. Finally, state what would change your verdict: which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be sound, would reorder your ranking, and where in your audit your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
