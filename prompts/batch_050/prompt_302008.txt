I work at a small nonprofit that gives free legal help to people seeking asylum in my country, and we're drowning. We get maybe 200 requests a month and can take on maybe 30 cases, so the intake team basically triages, and right now triage means whoever reads the file goes with their gut. I've been trying to build a proper screening procedure around the classic refugee-law definition -- you know, a well-founded fear of persecution on grounds like race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership of a particular social group -- but the definition keeps dissolving on me when I try to apply it. Half our cases are people fleeing gang recruitment in Central America, or domestic violence where the police laughed at them, or a region where the state has basically stopped existing and a famine is underway. None of those fit the textbook persecution-by-a-government picture, but refusing them feels monstrous.

Act as someone who's spent years both studying refugee/forced-migration ethics and working in asylum adjudication, and think carefully about this before writing -- maybe start by brainstorming what distinctions actually do the work in triage decisions, then turn it into a procedure. Here's a half-formed idea I want baked in if it survives your scrutiny: I suspect our intake decisions are secretly tracking three different variables that my team treats as one -- (a) how well the person's story maps onto the legal definition, (b) how bad it would be for the person if they're sent back, and (c) how much our lawyers can actually change the outcome. I want a triage procedure that makes those three explicit, scores or sorts each in some usable way, and tells us in what order to weigh them when they conflict (e.g., a legally airtight case we can't lose vs. a legally hopeless case where the person faces death if returned). It needs to be runnable by a smart volunteer in about 20 minutes per file, with no law degree. Tell me which distinctions from the theory absolutely have to survive the simplification and which we can drop, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire or where it's really just moral judgment wearing a procedure costume.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases: (1) a journalist threatened with imprisonment for criticizing her government -- easy mode, but I want to see it work; (2) a teenage boy fleeing forced recruitment by a cartel his town's police are paid by; (3) a farming family displaced by a multi-year drought that their own government's corruption made far worse than it had to be. Include at least one case where the procedure spits out a priority ranking you suspect I won't like, and tell me so. Also say clearly what the procedure can't do, so I can warn our board before we adopt it.
