Retired organic chemist here, about five months into a philosophy-of-everything reading habit that my wife finds alarming. Here's a thing I keep tripping over and I want to know if it has a name — probably two names, actually.

In the lab we had a mass spectrometer that ran for thirty-odd years. Over that time we replaced the source, the analyzer, the detector, the pumps, the boards, eventually even the frame it sat on. On the inventory it's still the same instrument, serial number and all, and nobody batted an eye. Fine. But when I sat down to write up old methods for a review, I noticed I kept sliding between two completely different ways of talking about it, and I can't tell which one I actually believe.

Way one: the instrument is fully there at every moment — Tuesday's spectrometer is the whole spectrometer, and "lasting thirty years" means that one and the same object kept showing up, day after day, while parts swapped around inside it.

Way two: the instrument is spread out in time the way it's spread out in space. Tuesday's spectrometer is a temporal slice of a much bigger thing, the same way the left ion pump is a spatial slice. "Lasting thirty years" means the four-dimensional worm is thirty years long, and nothing is ever wholly present at an instant.

Here's the kicker: I catch people switching between these mid-conversation depending on which is convenient, like when we argue about whether a calibration "still holds" for the same instrument. Is there an established name for each picture, is one of them the default among philosophers, and — the part I really can't get a grip on — is there any experiment or argument that could decide between them, or are they just two vocabularies for the same facts? And please don't just say "ship of Theseus," because that's about whether it survives the repairs; I'm asking what it even means for anything to survive anything.
