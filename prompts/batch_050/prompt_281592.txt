I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and in retirement I have been working my way through the philosophy of science I once dismissed as a luxury. Popper has been my main companion this year. One claim of his has nagged at me: his contention, at least at one stage of his career, that Darwin's theory of natural selection is not a properly testable scientific theory but something closer to a metaphysical research programme -- valuable as a framework, but not falsifiable in the way a law of physics is. As I understand it, part of his worry was that the theory risks circularity, since fitness tends to be defined in terms of survival and survival is then explained in terms of fitness, and that almost any outcome can be accommodated after the fact by some story about selection pressures. I gather he softened or partly retracted this view late in life, which interests me too.

Here is what I would like. My working vocabulary is that of statistics and experimental inference: hypotheses, likelihoods, fitted parameters, overfitting, identifiability, null models, out-of-sample prediction. Please restate Popper's critique of Darwinism in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. For instance, I have a hunch that his tautology worry translates into something like a complaint about a parameter that is defined by the very data it purports to explain, and that his unfalsifiability charge resembles the complaint one levels at a model flexible enough to fit any dataset. But I am not confident in either mapping, and I would like it done carefully rather than impressionistically. Where Popper draws on specifically Darwinian details, feel free to reconstruct them, but keep the translation disciplined.

Then give me what I think of as the residue report. Which parts of Popper's position translate cleanly into statistical language, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? I am particularly curious about his insistence on falsification as a line between science and non-science, since in my world theories are rarely killed by a single observation; they lose ground gradually to better-fitting rivals. If that aspect of Popper cannot be expressed in my vocabulary, is that a deficiency of the statistical framework -- does it genuinely lack the resources for his demarcation question -- or is it evidence that the demarcation question itself, at least as Popper posed it, is less substantive than it appears? I would also welcome a judgment on whether his late-career softening looks, from the statistical point of view, like a correction of an original error or like a retreat from a defensible position.
