in my cog-sci program the standard pitch for massive modularity is the swiss army knife analogy: the mind isn't one general-purpose blade, it's a bundle of special-purpose tools, each shaped by selection for some recurrent ancestral problem (cheater detection, face recognition, folk physics, language), and my prof leans on it pretty hard to conclude that domain-general reasoning is basically a myth -- so courses that promise to teach transferable critical thinking are, in his words, trying to sharpen the whole knife at once. fodor's older picture keeps the modules small (input systems) and leaves a big unmodular central processor doing the actual thinking, which is roughly the one-blade picture the analogy is arguing against. audit the analogy for me. give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the blade, the little scissors, the corkscrew nobody uses, the person deciding which tool to pull out, the sharpening -- then where it breaks: the knife had a designer who foresaw the tasks and selection doesn't foresee anything; tools on a knife don't call each other but cognitive systems plainly interact; someone has to decide which tool fits the situation and the knife has no tool for that; and humans get fluent at reading and spreadsheets and chess despite there being no plausible module for any of them. tell me which of these disanalogies actually threaten my prof's conclusion and which are just pedantic points about knives, whether the analogy is doing real argumentative work or just illustrating, and if it dies, what's a better analogy for whatever's actually true in the vicinity (a workshop? an ecosystem? software with plugins?). i mostly want to know whether the "critical thinking is a scam" conclusion survives once the analogy stops carrying it.
