I run a monthly seminar on philosophy of mind and animal ethics, and this term we got stuck on what one participant calls the descent problem. The starting point was William Kingdon Clifford's 1878 essay On the Nature of Things-in-Themselves, where he argues roughly as follows (paraphrasing, not quoting): since we infer consciousness in other humans from their outward behavior and resemblance to ourselves, and since there is no evident line on the scale of organized beings where this inference ceases to be licensed, the uniformity of nature pushes us to attribute some elementary form of feeling all the way down, until matter itself is conceived as built from faint atoms of experience. The seminar split cleanly in two. One camp accepts something like Clifford's inference and draws the ethical consequence: if elementary experience permeates matter, then the moral calculus of suffering cannot stop at animals with nervous systems, and effective altruist reasoning that prioritizes vertebrates may be missing an enormous, possibly dominant, class of morally relevant micro-suffering. The other camp holds that Clifford's continuity argument equivocates on what the uniformity of nature licenses, that experience plausibly requires integrated organization of a kind absent below some threshold of complexity, and that talk of suffering in fundamental physics is a category error that would paralyze ethics rather than extend it.

Write the joint statement these two camps should be able to produce after a serious adversarial collaboration, framed explicitly as a response to Clifford's descent argument. I want: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, including what both sides accept about the evidential status of behavioral inference to consciousness, about the absence of a sharp biological cutoff, and about what would follow for ethics if micro-experience were real; (2) a disagreements table where each residual dispute is stated in one sentence that the camp holding the view would itself sign, distinguishing genuine empirical or metaphysical disputes from ones that are merely about the meaning of words like feeling or subject; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side -- I suspect one crux is whether there is a principled, non-arbitrary threshold of organization for experience, and another is whether aggregating hypothetical micro-sufferings into a total is coherent at all; and (4) for each crux, a piece of evidence, an argument, or a thought experiment that both camps agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how it would be resolved written down in advance.

Please keep the Clifford material as the anchor: state each side's official reading of the descent argument and where, exactly, each thinks the inference first goes wrong or first becomes irresistible. Do not adjudicate the disagreement; the deliverable is a document both camps could put their names to.
