In a graduate seminar on value theory, we have spent several weeks on the fitting-attitude (or "buck-passing") analysis of value: the proposal that for something to be good or admirable just is for there to be reason to respond to it with a favorable attitude — to admire it, desire it, or choose it. We have now reached the standard stumbling block, the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I find myself unable to state precisely why it is a problem rather than a mild oddity. The textbook case is the demon who credibly threatens severe punishment unless you genuinely admire some intrinsically worthless object, say a saucer of mud. Intuitively you have excellent reason to admire the mud — your well-being depends on it — yet the mud is obviously not admirable. If being admirable just is being such that there is reason to admire it, the analysis seems to yield the wrong verdict. But I want the problem explained more carefully than this: what exactly goes wrong, and why is it not enough for the proponent of the analysis simply to bite the bullet?

I devised a variant to test the standard replies, and I would like it treated as the central case. Suppose the demon cannot be fooled by outward displays and demands the genuine attitude; suppose further that an enterprising pharmacist sells a pill that reliably induces real, felt admiration for whatever you are looking at when you take it. Now distinguish three claims: (a) you have reason to take the pill while looking at the mud; (b) you have reason to want to admire the mud; (c) you have reason to admire the mud. Please explain the wrong kind of reasons problem by working through these three claims: which of them the demon case actually supports, which of them the fitting-attitude analysis needs for its verdicts about value, and why the case puts pressure on the analysis only if some of these claims stand or fall together. In doing so, lay out the main diagnostic strategies in the literature in your own terms: the distinction between object-given and state-given reasons (reasons that bear on the attitude's object versus reasons that bear merely on the desirability of having the attitude), the proposal that the analysis should quantify only over reasons of the "right kind" (and the circularity worry this invites), and any other way of splitting the notions of reason or of value that you think does real work.

Finally, say which strategy best handles the pill case and whether you think the problem generalizes: does something structurally identical arise for the fittingness of belief (where a bribe to believe seems to give a reason of the wrong kind for belief), and if so, is that a point in favor of the diagnosis you prefer or an embarrassment for it? Lead your response with a one-sentence bottom line stating your diagnosis of the problem, and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
