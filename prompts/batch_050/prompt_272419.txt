I TA a reading group on philosophy and economics and last week we were on discounting in climate cost-benefit analysis, and I walked everyone into an argument that now seems to prove way too much. Going in I thought the no-pure-time-preference line was the safe boring premise everyone grants -- I remembered Ramsey's old point that caring less about wellbeing just because it arrives later is indefensible -- and that the real fights were all about growth rates and what the social discount rate should be. But if you push the arbitrariness thought one step further it seems to eat everything, and I can't find the exit.

Here's the argument as I wrote it on the whiteboard. (1) A mere difference in *when* a good or bad thing happens is not, by itself, a reason to care more or less about it; caring more about a pain just because it's sooner is as arbitrary as caring more because it falls on a Tuesday. (2) So within my own life I should be temporally neutral: no preferring the root canal to be past rather than future, no discounting the misery of my 80-year-old self relative to my present self. (3) But the reasoning in (1) never used anything special about time -- it works for any merely *positional* difference, including where a pain happens and, crucially, whose pain it is. (4) That a pain is mine rather than a stranger's is a merely positional fact in the same way that its being Friday's rather than Tuesday's is. (5) So I have no reason, considered in itself, to prefer that a pain befall me rather than a stranger, or my kid rather than a stranger's kid. (6) So agent-relative partiality -- special duties to my children, the permission to spend on my own small comforts while strangers are hungry -- is a prejudice on a par with pure time preference, and rationality demands perfect impartiality about everything. Now (6) is absurd, or anyway I refuse to accept it, but each step felt fine when I said it out loud. I know the Parfit-flavored move is to bite the bullet and say the seperateness of persons is shallower than we think, and I have a vague feeling that whatever makes prudential concern rational *within* a life (continuity, same subject, something) has no analogue across lives and so breaks my analogy somewhere between (2) and (5) -- but I can't make that precise, and maybe that's just me begging the question in favor of selfishness. If I have to give something up, I'd definately rather keep "don't discount future people in the climate math" than keep partiality, but I'd love to keep both and want to know what each option costs.

Give me your diagnosis as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up: first the step you reject, second why that step looked fine when it isn't, third what I'd pay for instead rejecting (1) outright, fourth what I'd pay for instead rejecting the analogy at (3)-(4), and fifth what all this implies about whether the climate discounting fight was ever really about pure time preference in the first place.
