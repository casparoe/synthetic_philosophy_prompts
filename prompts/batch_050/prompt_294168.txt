I'm a software engineer, and a few years ago a hallway debate at work got me thinking seriously about the inefficacy objection to individual consumer ethics: the idea that one person's choice not to eat meat, or to fly less, or to vote, makes no actual difference because markets and elections are coarse-grained systems that absorb individual actions without changing anything. I kept turning it over, and I've since come around to the opposite position, which I'll state as precisely as I can: individual contributions to collective harms are morally significant even when no particular harm is traceable to one's action, because purchases and votes function probabilistically — a supermarket's decision to order another crate of chicken is triggered by some purchase, and while any given purchase has only a small chance of being the threshold-crossing one, the expected harm of each purchase equals the full harm of the threshold being crossed divided by the number of purchases, so the expected disutility of my buying a chicken is roughly the disutility of one chicken being produced. In other words, I accept the expected-value reply essentially wholesale, and I extend it beyond the market case to voting and to emissions, where I'd treat imperceptible contributions as small fractions of a collectively caused harm rather than as zero.

What I want is a serious stress test of this position: what would actually count against it? I'm not interested in a survey of the debate where I pick my own winner — I want you to take a definite stand on which defeaters are fatal, which are damaging, and which are merely awkward, and to argue for that ranking. Some candidates I can already see, which you should assess and add to: the worry that expected-value reasoning breaks down when probabilities are genuinely unknown or when the threshold model doesn't fit the causal structure (e.g., elections decided by one vote are fantastically rare, and emissions seem to work by smooth aggregation rather than thresholds, which might make the objection stronger or weaker depending on the case); the worry that the view proves too much, obligating me to optimize every trivial purchase; the worry that it proves too little, licensing free-riding whenever I'm confident others will pick up the slack; and the engineering-flavored intuition that a system insensitive to individual inputs is one where the right unit of moral evaluation is the institution, not the consumer. For each defeater, say whether a committed defender of my position would themselves concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as a mischaracterization, and distinguish those two honestly rather than stacking the deck. Finally, tell me straight whether the expected-value reply has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — whether there's any real-world observation about how supply chains, elections, or carbon budgets actually respond to marginal demand that would force me to abandon it — and if you conclude the position is well insulated, take a stand on whether that's a strength or a symptom.
