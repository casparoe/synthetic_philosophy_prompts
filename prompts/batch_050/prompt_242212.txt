I'm a novelist, most of my stuff is contemporary fiction, and right now I'm working on a book about a small touring theatre company, which means I've been spending a lot of time hanging around actual theatre people. And I keep noticing this thing that I swear must be a known phenomenon but nobody I've asked can give me a proper name for it. As long as a show's run is open-ended, everyone is lovely to each other -- covering each other's cues, sharing the good costumes, staying late to fix sets. But the moment a closing date gets announced, it's like a slow poison. People start hoarding props for their portfolios, nobody covers for anyone anymore, small grudges that were burried for months suddenly come out. And here's the part that really interests me: it happens even when everyone knows they'll all be rehired for the company's next production in the autumn. So it's not simply "we'll never see each other again so why bother."

I've seen the same shape elsewhere. Roommates in the last month of a shared lease stop doing dishes. A friend who co-writes TV scripts says the final draft of anything is where partnerships go to die. My brother-in-law is an economist and when I described this he waved his hand and said game theory solved this decades ago, something about prisoners, but when I actually looked that up it was about two guys in seperate cells making one decision each, which is... not really what I'm describing? What I'm describing is a long stretch of cooperation that rots specifically from the end backwards, and the rot seems to start even before anyone has reason to think the others have stopped cooperating -- sometimes just one person anticipating the end seems to trigger it.

So: does this configuration have an established name? I suspect I'm circling a few different concepts and I'd like them disentangled -- whatever covers the end-of-run collapse specifically, versus the more general idea that cooperation needs an open-ended future to survive, versus that pre-emptive unraveling thing where everyone defects early because they expect everyone else to. Tell me what the nearest named concepts are, where my theatre case fits and where it doesn't (especially the bit where the same people WILL work together again -- does that break the standard account or is there a version that handles it?), and if the exact thing I'm describing has no name, say so and propose one with a definition sharp enough that I could tell whether it applies to, say, a marriage where both people have privately decided to divorce but haven't said it yet. I'm not after a label to drop in dialogue, I want the actual carving, because the novel kind of hinges on getting this right.
