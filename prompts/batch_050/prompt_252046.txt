Having retired last year from forty years in experimental chemistry, I have taken to attending a philosophy reading group at my local library, and I have rashly agreed to present a short paper at our next meeting on the distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. Rather than present a finished essay, I intend to circulate a page of notes, and I should like your comments on those notes before I do. The core of my argument runs as follows. The standard way of drawing the line, as I understand it, is that legitimate persuasion addresses a person's capacity for reasons whereas manipulation bypasses or subverts that capacity — the rhetoric of the courtroom versus the flattery of the salesman. My contention is that this distinction does far less work than advertised. A great deal of what we cheerfully call rational persuasion works by routes that bypass deliberation just as thoroughly as any salesman's trick: the ordering of options on a menu, the vivid anecdote that outruns the base rate, the confident tone that smuggles in assent, the framing of a risk as a 90 per cent survival rate rather than a 10 per cent mortality rate. As an experimentalist I spent my career knowing that the design of an instrument quietly decides what can be observed, and I cannot see that the architecture of a conversation is any different. My tentative conclusion is that there is no clean distinction in the mechanism, only in the upshot: persuasion becomes manipulation when it moves a person away from what they would endorse on calm reflection, whatever route it takes, and stays legitimate when it moves them towards it. I note, but do not resolve, two difficulties: first, that this makes the legitimacy of persuasion depend on an outcome the persuader cannot know in advance; and second, that it seems to license quite aggressive 'nudges' provided the persuader is right about what the other person would reflectively endorse.

What I would like from you is not a verdict on whether my position is fashionable but comments of three kinds. First, accuracy: is the standard picture I attack — reasons-addressed versus reasons-bypassed — actually held in anything like the form I state it, or have I built a straw man from half-remembered seminar chatter? Second, the weak joints in the argument: where does the move from 'mechanisms overlap' to 'only the upshot matters' smuggle in more than I have earned, and does my calm-reflection test collapse into circularity (what one endorses on reflection presumably depending on how one was persuaded to reflect)? Third, whether the analogy from instrument design, which feels illuminating to me, is in fact doing any work or merely reassuring a retired chemist that his old instincts still apply. I would also be grateful if you could flag any obvious objection I have failed to notice altogether, since the reading group contains one former barrister who will certainly find it.

Finally, two requests about the manner of your answer. Please write in British English throughout. And please be candid about your own epistemic standing: state how confident you are in the comments you make, and single out the single point — whether about the accuracy of my target, the soundness of my argument, or anything else — on which you are least certain, with a sentence or two on why.
