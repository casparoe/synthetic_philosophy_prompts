I am trying to determine what follows when someone possesses complete third-person knowledge of color vision without ever having had a chromatic experience. Consider Amara, a scientist raised in an entirely grayscale environment. She knows every physical, causal, and functional fact that completed physics and neuroscience would state about wavelengths, objects, retinas, neural processing, behavior, and verbal reports. Leave open whether facts about phenomenal character are already included among those facts; I do not want the setup to answer the main question by definition.

My verdicts on a series of variations are as follows:

1. Amara is released and sees a ripe tomato for the first time. She says that she now understands what seeing red is like. I think she has genuinely learned something.

2. Before her release, Amara can truly state every proposition about the neural state produced in normal observers by red objects, including that people call the associated experience red, that they can recognize and compare it, and that it differs from the experience produced by green objects. Nevertheless, I think there remains a sense in which she does not yet grasp the experience from the inside.

3. A device directly stimulates Amara’s visual cortex so that, while she is looking at a gray wall, she undergoes exactly the phenomenal state normally produced by seeing red. I think she thereby learns what seeing red is like even though no red object is present. So the relevant learning does not essentially concern the external color.

4. The device instead produces the phenomenal state normally produced by green objects, while a technician incorrectly tells Amara that this is what red looks like. When Amara later sees an actual red tomato, she thinks that its appearance is not the appearance of red. I think she then gains phenomenal acquaintance with red while retaining a false belief about how that appearance is conventionally labeled.

5. A different treatment enables Amara reliably to sort red objects from green ones, but leaves her unable to remember, imagine, visualize, or describe the relevant phenomenal difference when no colored object is present. I think she has acquired a genuine recognitional competence but not the full kind of experiential knowledge possessed by someone who can recall or imagine red.

6. Amara’s twin has had vivid experiences of every color in dreams since childhood, although she too has lived in a grayscale environment and does not know which dreamt quality is connected with which public color term. When she first sees a tomato, I think she does not acquire a new phenomenal quality, but does acquire a new relational fact: this already familiar quality is the one red objects normally produce in observers like her.

7. Amara’s reaction upon first seeing the tomato—including her surprise—is exactly the reaction her completed neuroscience predicted. I think the surprise is real, but I do not think its occurrence by itself shows that anything physically unpredictable has happened.

8. If a physicalist says that the supposedly complete scientific description already entailed every phenomenal truth, I am unsure whether Amara’s pre-release knowledge would have to include what red is like, or whether knowing the entailment and its physical relata could still leave her without the relevant phenomenal concept.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? Please state the account precisely rather than merely naming familiar positions. In particular, distinguish among learning a new fact, acquiring a new concept or mode of presentation, gaining recognitional or imaginative abilities, becoming directly acquainted with a phenomenal quality, and learning how that quality is publicly labeled. Identify which cases motivate each distinction and which pair of my verdicts is hardest to reconcile.

If no simple principle preserves every verdict, describe the most plausible reflective equilibrium and state clearly which verdict I should revise. Also say what the resulting account predicts for two further cases: an experience that is immediately forgotten but leaves an implicit capacity for recognition, and a person who has all of Amara’s scientific knowledge but lacks only the concept red while possessing an equivalent phenomenal concept under another name.

Finally, separate what is broadly agreed among people familiar with this debate from what remains genuinely contested among physicalists, dualists, ability theorists, acquaintance theorists, and defenders of phenomenal-concept strategies. Do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus merely because it best organizes my cases.
