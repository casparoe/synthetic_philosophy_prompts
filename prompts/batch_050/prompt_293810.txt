So I'm in the third year of a politics degree and our reading group just did the distributive justice chapter of Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, and I want to stress-test where I've landed because I suspect I'm trying to have things both ways somewhere. The bit that properly convinced me was this passage from the "Patterning" discussion, just after the Wilt Chamberlain example (p. 160 of the edition we used): "Whoever makes something having bought or contracted for all other held resources used in the process (transferring some of his holdings for these cooperating factors), is entitled to it. The situation is not one of something's getting made, and there being an open question of who is to get it. Things come into the world already attached to people having entitlements over them." My supervisor raised an eyebrow at the rest of my views, so here they are in full:

(1) The Wilt Chamberlain argument is decisive against patterned and end-state principles of distributive justice -- if the fans freely hand over their 25 cents each, there's no injustice in Chamberlain ending up rich, and keeping any pattern in place would mean constant meddling in people's free choices. (2) I accept the entitlement theory wholesale: justice in holdings is purely historical, just acquisition plus just transfer, and there is no further question about whether the resulting distribution "looks fair". (3) That said, I think the state can legitimately levy income tax to fund genuine public goods like basic scientific research, vaccination programmes and flood defences, because everyone benefits from these, so it's not really redistribution, more like billing people for services they use. (4) I don't think present-day people owe reparations for historical injustices like colonial land seizures -- the victims are long dead and you can't wrong someone by what your great-grandparents did. (5) I believe in self-ownership: I own my body and my labour, which is why taking part of my earnings by force strikes me as deeply wrong. (6) I also think rich people have a serious moral duty to give a large chunk of their wealth to effective charities, and a society where the rich hoarded everything would be morally rotten even if every single holding in it had been justly acquired.

Does this package hang together? Please audit it rather than lecture me: identify the genuine tensions, roughly in order of severity, say precisely which commitments pull against which and why, and for each one tell me the cheapest way to restore consistency and what that costs. I'd also like to know if any apparent tensions actually dissolve on closer inspection, since I have a feeling at least one of them does. Please don't just flag everything -- I want to know where the real problems are. Oh, and could you write the answer in British English? Cheers.
