I run the engineering org at a mid-size software company and I keep watching us walk into the same trap: we keep pouring time and money into something because we've already poured so much in. Classic sunk cost stuff, and someone pointed me at the dollar auction -- the game where the two top bidders both pay, so second place has every reason to bid $1.01 on a dollar, then $1.02, and so on -- plus the broader escalation-of-commitment literature. I get the concepts, but knowing the trap exists has not once stopped us from walking into it, because by the time it's obvious we're already committed.

So: write me a decision procedure I can run, as part of our quarterly review, on any project, hire, vendor relationship, or competitive fight we're already invested in, to tell whether we should keep going, restructure, or exit. Ground it in what the dollar auction actually shows about why escalation happens (competition, face-saving, the cost of quitting being visible while the cost of continuing is diffuse), not just a generic "ignore sunk costs" lecture. I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and forces an exit or escalation decision, and -- important -- the spots where judgment can't be eliminated, flagged as such. I have maybe 20 minutes per item per quarter, and the output has to be something a project lead and I can fill out together without either of us having read the literature.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a migration to a new infra stack that's 14 months into what was scoped as 6, with the old stack half-deprecated; (2) a talent bidding war with a rival company over a candidate we interviewed first and have spent months courting; (3) pick a third case yourself where the procedure gives an answer I'd hate (e.g. it says kill the migration) and show why that's the procedure working, not failing.

Also tell me what would change your mind about the design -- what finding or argument about escalation would make you restructure the procedure, and where you think it's most likely to misfire in practice.
