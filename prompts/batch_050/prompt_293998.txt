I'm a retired experimental physicist (particle stuff, so I spent forty years living inside likelihood functions) and in retirement I've started reading philosophy of law on my kindle, more or less at random. Two courtroom hypotheticals keep coming up and they're driving me a bit nuts. First: a woman gets sideswiped by a bus at night, no witnesses, no camera, but everyone agrees 80% of the buses on that route are run by the Blue Bus Company. Second: a thousand people attend a rodeo, the organisers prove only 499 tickets were sold, and they pick one attendee more or less at random and sue him for the price of admission. In both cases the number you get out of Bayes' theorem clears the more-likely-than-not bar that civil courts claim to apply, and yet judges and law professors tie themselves in knots refusing to find liability on the statistics alone. So here's what I want. Restate this whole debate in the vocabulary I actually think in: priors, likelihood ratios, posteriors, reference classes, calibration, loss functions, decision thresholds. Is "preponderance of the evidence" really just posterior > 0.5? Is "beyond reasonable doubt" just the same thing with a higher threshold, or is something else going on? And when people say naked statistics can establish facts about the group but not pin it on this particular guy, does that distinction survive in my vocabulary or evaporate? Then give me the residue report: what in the lawyers' fuss refuses to translate cleanly? My prejudice is that half of it is muddle dressed up in Latin, but I also have a nagging feeling that a verdict isn't just a credence with a wig on -- there's something about the court doing something to a specific defendant rather than reporting a number -- and I can't say what the extra bit is. Be honest about whether the untranslatable residue points to something my framework is missing or to something the lawyers are confused about.
