I'm in a research ethics seminar and we just did the Milgram obedience experiments (early 60s at Yale, the ones where participants thought they were delivering escalating electric shocks to a learner in the next room, most people went all the way up the board, the shocks weren't real, everyone got debriefed afterwards). Our group got into an argument that we couldn't settle, so I want a real analysis: run the Milgram setup through the standard framework for when consent is valid -- the stuff about deception, coercion, and competence. The usual move in class was just to say "they were deceived, so the consent was invalid," but that seems way too fast, and that's where I want the weeds.

Here's the specific thing I want pushed on: the participants consented to do exactly the thing they did -- sit at a board, press switches, read word pairs -- and nothing about that physical act was misdescribed. What they were deceived about was the state of the world around the act (that a man was being hurt, that this was a study of memory rather than of obedience). So does deception invalidate consent only when it misdescribes the act itself, or also when it misdescribes the consequences and context? Give me the best case each way, and say what follows for Milgram specifically. Then do the coercion side: the experimenter's prods were verbal, no threats, you could in principle walk out (and some people did) -- does social pressure from an authority figure in a lab coat rise to the level of undermining consent, and where's the line between that and mere persuasion? And what does the debriefing do -- can you retroactively fix invalid consent by disclosing afterwards, or is that just an apology dressed up as a remedy?

Finally, run it in the other direction: what does the episode show about the consent framework itself? If we conclude the participants' consent was invalid, we seem committed to saying that huge swaths of ordinary psychology research (and arguably some everyday stuff, like not disclosing your real reasons for a breakup) involve invalid consent, which feels like a reductio -- but if we conclude it was valid, we seem to be saying you can validly consent to being made an unwitting torturer, which also feels wrong. Tell me which bullet the framework should bite, or whether Milgram is a case the standard deception/coercion/competence toolkit just wasn't built for.
