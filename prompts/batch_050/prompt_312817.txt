I'm in a philosophy of probability course this semester and we just covered the reference class problem, and I realized that if I just go by my gut on cases, my verdicts dont add up to anything I can state. So here are my verdicts -- tell me what principle (if any) is behind them.

1. Car insurance. A 28 year old nonsmoker with a clean record should have her premium set by the stats for 28-year-old nonsmokers with clean records, not by drivers in general. The narrower class just seems obviously right here.

2. Weather app says 80% chance of rain. As far as I can tell the "reference class" there is something like days where the atmospheric readings matched this pattern, which is a weird gerrymandered class nobody could even list the members of. But I still think its a perfectly fine basis for the probability.

3. Coin flip. If you knew the exact force, angle, air currents etc. of a toss, the outcome would be fixed, so the "class" of tosses with exactly those conditions has frequency 1 or 0. But I really don't think that means the probability of heads is 1 or 0 once you've got that info. The right class stays something like flips of a roughly fair coin, even though more specific info is available in principle.

4. Hit and run case from my roommate's law class. A bus company owns 80% of the buses that use the street where the accident happened, and that's literally all the evidence. My gut says you cannot find the company liable on that alone. But the statistics here are better than in the weather case, where I was totally happy.

5. My grandma's doctor. There's a narrow class of women over 75 with her exact condition, but only a handful of known cases, so the doctor reasons from the broader class of older patients with heart conditions generally. That seems right to me too -- at some point the narrow class is too small to tell you anything.

So 1 and 3 pull against each other (go narrow vs. don't go maximally narrow), 2 and 4 pull against each other (good stats are enough vs. the same stats aren't enough), and 5 seems to allow going *wider* when narrow fails, which 1 doesn't seem to allow.

Is there one principle that gets all five, or close? If not, tell me which verdict I should give up and why. And then tell me what your principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: I want to know the probability that *I* pass this course, and I can't decide if the right class is all students who take it, students with my attendance record and midterm grade, or just me, in which case I have no idea what a frequency would even mean.
