I'm a retired chemist and over the last year or so I've been reading my way through moral philosophy the way you'd work through a new literature, and I just finished Appiah's book on honor and moral revolutions. His claim, as I understand it, is that practices like dueling in Britain and footbinding in China didn't end because people were finally persuaded by the moral arguments against them -- the arguments had been around for ages and were widely recieved and agreed with. They ended when the practice stopped being a source of honor and started being one of shame or ridicule. I also know, at least in outline, the older Williams thesis about the slave trade: that abolition in Britain tracks the decline of slavery's economic usefulness to British capital more than it tracks any humanitarian awakening, and that the humanitarian story is largely retrospective self-congratulation.

So here's what I want analyzed. Take two real episodes: the disappearance of dueling among the British upper classes across the early-to-mid 19th century, and British abolition of the slave trade and then slavery itself. For each one, work out what each of the three candidate mechanisms -- moral argument, economic interest, and honor -- would predict about how the practice should have ended: who resists longest, what the holdouts say in their own defense, whether the practice dies from the top of the social ladder down or the bottom up, whether people at the time framed the change as learning something new or as finally acting on something they already believed. Then tell me which mechanism actually fits each episode's shape, and be honest about the mismatches -- for instance, Appiah himself treats abolition as a partial case, and the Williams thesis has been battered by later economic historians, so don't just assign one mechanism per episode like a matching exercise.

The thing I actually want to get to: if honor is the engine rather than argument, does that mean moral philosophy was mostly decorative in these episodes -- a story the winners told afterward -- or does argument have some upstream role, like slowly building the stock of agreed-upon reasons that an honor shift later cashes in? As a scientist I keep wanting a causal diagram here and I suspect the honest answer is that no single-variable account works, but I'd like to see the best version of each mechanism drawn out sharply enough that the two episodes can adjudicate between them, rather than a mushy "all three played a role."
