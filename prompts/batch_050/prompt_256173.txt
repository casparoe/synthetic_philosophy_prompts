I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of cognitive science course, and I've volunteered to run one session of the student reading group attached to it. We've just finished the unit on predictive processing and the free energy principle: roughly, the idea that the brain is constantly issuing top-down predictions about incoming sensory signals, that perception is the brain's best current hypothesis about what's out there, and that prediction errors only travel upward and force revisions when they can't be quietly explained away. The problem is that everyone in the group (about 12 people, 75 minutes, a seminar room with a whiteboard and movable chairs) keeps treating this as a loose metaphor. Several of them said it boils down to "we sometimes see what we expect to see," which defangs it completely. I want a session that makes the view feel like a claim about all perception, all the time — and that also gets at the two ideas they find most slippery: first, that attention, on this picture, is the brain adjusting the weight or "precision" it assigns to different sources of prediction error, so that the same error can be ignored in one context and model-shattering in another; and second, that a system can minimize prediction error not only by updating its model but by acting on the world so that the world comes to match the model ("active inference"), which they keep dismissing as a tacked-on extra rather than half the theory.

Design a structured activity for this session in which the theory shows up in what participants do and feel, not in what I tell them. Concretely: at some point they should catch themselves confidently perceiving something that was never there (or failing to register something that plainly was) because of a prior the activity installed in them; at some point they should feel the same anomalous observation go from dismissible noise to impossible-to-ignore as its reliability stacks up; and at some point they should find themselves in a situation where the cheapest, most natural way to make their expectations come true is to rearrange the situation rather than revise their beliefs — and only afterwards realize that this is what they did. Constraints: no equipment beyond paper, pens, a deck of cards, and audio played from a phone; no lecture up front; and nobody should hear the phrase "predictive processing" until the debrief, since half the attendees haven't done the reading and the activity needs to land for them too. Real but small stakes are welcome if they help (e.g., a point pool or a modest prize), as long as the stakes serve the illusion rather than just the competition.

Please take on the persona of an experienced instructor of philosophy of mind who has run many such sessions and who thinks carefully, before writing anything, about what participants will literally see, hear, guess, and wrongly remember at each stage — not about what the theory says in the abstract. Structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order: "Session goals" (about 100 words, what participants should come away seeing); "Setup and materials" (about 150 words, including any preparation I must do in advance and anything I should deliberately conceal); "The activity, phase by phase" (about 500 words, with a timeline totaling 50 minutes, rules, roles if any, and the exact instructions I'd give at each transition); "The debrief" (about 250 words, mapping specific moments onto top-down prediction, precision weighting, and active inference, with the questions I'd pose rather than the answers I'd hand out); "Predictable failure modes" (about 150 words, what will realistically go wrong and how to recover mid-session); and "What the activity can't reproduce" (about 100 words, being honest about which features of the view — say, the formal variational machinery, or the claim that this is the brain's basic mode of operation rather than an occasional glitch — a staged classroom exercise cannot make felt, and what I must simply supply in words).
