I am a third-year PhD student in political theory applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and I need a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea is as follows. The most influential case for steep inheritance taxation argues from equal opportunity: inherited wealth is an unearned windfall that lets some people start life far ahead of others through no doing of their own, so taxing it heavily and redistributing the proceeds levels the starting line. But the very same reasoning seems to condemn a great deal of ordinary parenting -- tutoring, cultivating useful social networks, paying for enrichment, even differential investments of parental time -- and almost nobody is willing to follow it there. The standard replies on behalf of the family appeal to the special value of intimate care and to gift-giving as an expression of a relationship. My suspicion is that these replies protect too little in one respect and too much in another: they may justify some substantial wealth transfers (a parental gift at a wedding, say) while failing to justify others that look structurally identical to the transfers the equal-opportunity argument targets (a trust fund whose point is precisely to secure social position). I want to test whether a distinction between transfers that express a relationship and transfers that transmit advantage can do the work the existing debate needs -- drawing a principled line between permissible and impermissible intergenerational transfers without either licensing all of familial partiality or abolishing the family as we know it.

Turn this into a proposal. It should state the central question precisely; explain why the main existing positions in this debate -- luck-egalitarian defenses of inheritance taxation, sufficientarian alternatives, and family-values objections to both -- leave the question open rather than answering it; lay out the method (what I would actually do, roughly month by month, including how I would test the relationship-expression versus advantage-transmission distinction against hard cases); say what the possible conclusions are and give my honest prediction, with reasons, of which one the project will reach; specify what a successful result looks like and what would count as the project failing; and explain briefly why a philosopher rather than an economist or legal scholar is the right person to do this work. Do not include citations or a bibliography -- I will add those myself -- but you may refer to positions and arguments by description.

Two further constraints. First, keep the scope realistic for one year: if part of the idea as I have stated it is too big for a single dissertation, say so plainly and propose the cut. Second, do not hedge. The proposal should take definite positions -- on how the central question is best formulated, on which of the existing answers comes closest and why it still falls short, and on the hypothesis the project is built around -- and argue for those choices rather than presenting me with a menu of options to choose among.
