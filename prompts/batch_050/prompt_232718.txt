In my seminar on biography and moral education, my students have absorbed the line that Plutarch wrote Lives, not histories, and they now deploy it reflexively: any question about whether an anecdote in the Alexander or the Cato actually occurred is waved away as a category error. Yet Plutarch's own account of his project sits uneasily with this use. In the preface to his Life of Demetrius, explaining why he includes vicious men among his subjects, he writes: "So, I think, we also shall be more eager to observe and imitate the better lives if we are not left without narratives of the blameworthy and the bad." Lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims that Lives, not histories might be making — from the merely classificatory (biography is a different genre from history) to the epistemically radical (factual accuracy is irrelevant to a life's value) — and mark which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are probably false. Then say which of these readings would actually license my students' dismissal of factual questions, and whether those readings survive the Demetrius passage: if the moral force of a life, for Plutarch, lies in its power to provoke imitation or aversion in the reader, does it matter that the example be real, or only that it be plausible and vividly drawn? I would rather keep the slogan than lose my students' interest in moral exemplars, but I want to know what keeping it costs.
