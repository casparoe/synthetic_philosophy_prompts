I run a small forecasting group, and at our last session two members — Dana and Victor, both experienced and both people I respect — had an extended argument I couldn't settle. The setup was straightforward: we were estimating the probability of a specific future event, and Dana and Victor spent an hour pooling every piece of evidence either of them had. They then wrote down their credences: Dana said 70%, Victor said 40%. Both numbers survived several rounds of questions and challenges; neither would move. That touched off the following exchange, which I've reconstructed as accurately as I can:

1. Dana argued that, given a well-known result in the economics of information to the effect that rational agents with shared starting assumptions cannot have common knowledge of disagreeing posteriors, their situation is impossible as described. They have exchanged all their evidence, they each know the other's stated credence, and each regards the other as honest and competent. So at least one of them must be reasoning badly, and since neither can point to a specific flaw in the other, the only defensible response is for both to move toward the middle. Standing pat is itself the irrationality.

2. Victor replied that the result Dana cited requires identical prior beliefs, and no two actual people have those. His priors were shaped by decades of experience Dana doesn't share, and forming different starting assumptions is not a reasoning error — it's just having a different past. So the theorem's conditions aren't met, the impossibility result doesn't apply, and persistent honest disagreement between competent people is perfectly coherent.

3. Dana answered that the differing-priors defense proves too much and explains nothing. If invoking priors could always license permanent disagreement, then no one would ever have reason to update on anyone else's judgment, forecasting as a practice would be pointless, and markets could never aggregate information. Worse, the move is unfalsifiable: any disagreement whatsoever can be retroactively attributed to hidden priors, which makes it a permanent excuse rather than an argument. And since priors are ultimately just the residue of earlier evidence and reflection — all of which they'd now shared — the appeal to priors collapses into the very disagreement it was supposed to explain.

4. Victor's response had two parts. First, even granting identical priors, the theorem requires that each person's posterior be common knowledge, and announcing a number out loud doesn't achieve that: a stated credence is a report, not a fact about one's epistemic state, and common knowledge — as opposed to merely both of them knowing the number — requires an infinite hierarchy of mutual awareness that one utterance cannot establish. Second, the theorem idealizes agents as perfect Bayesian reasoners, which neither of them is; a result proved for ideal agents simply does not bind actual humans, any more than theorems about frictionless planes bind carpenters.

5. Dana closed by saying the idealization objection cuts both ways. If Victor insists they are fallible reasoners outside the theorem's scope, then Victor equally cannot claim any principled guarantee that his stubbornness is rational rather than just overconfidence. And whatever the theorem's precise assumptions, the practical finding that averaging two informed estimates typically beats either one alone suggests the spirit of the result survives even where its letter fails.

I stayed out of it and genuinely cannot tell who won. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but flawed, or clearly right, with a brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both parties missed. Please write your answer in American English.
