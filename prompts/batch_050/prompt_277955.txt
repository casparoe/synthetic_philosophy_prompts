I’m applying for a small one-year postdoc grant and need a three-page research proposal on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. I was talking to a friend who reviews grant applications late at night: sometimes she becomes convinced that her current reasoning is unreliable, but she also thinks the application in front of her is clearly strong and doesn’t want to suspend judgment entirely. My half-formed idea is that we should distinguish retaining a credence from licensing that credence as a basis for planning, testimony, or public action. I’m calling the middle state credence escrow: the agent still believes that p, perhaps with high confidence, but temporarily refuses to rely on p until she can recalibrate. The main question I want the project to answer is whether this makes room for a genuinely rational form of epistemic akrasia—believing p while rationally believing that one’s belief that p is irrational—or whether it collapses into ordinary suspension, lowered confidence, or a familiar conciliatory position under another name.

Write the proposal with a precise statement of that question, an explanation of why existing debates about misleading higher-order evidence, peer disagreement, calibration, and level-bridging principles leave it unresolved, and a plausible method for answering it rather than just cataloguing examples. I’d like the method to include a small taxonomy of cases, toy formal models of first-order and higher-order updating, and imagined applications where acting and merely believing come apart, such as medical triage, hiring, or forecasting. Say what conclusions the project might reach, what would count as discovering that credence escrow is not coherent, and what would count as a successful positive account. Include a realistic month-by-month plan for twelve months and an honest note about the biggest conceptual risk. Please don’t include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review by author; I’ll add those later. Write the whole proposal as continuous prose, like a polished letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or separate numbered sections.
