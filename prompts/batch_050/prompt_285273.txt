I keep circling back to the claim that the agricultural revolution was, on balance, a mistake for most humans: not merely that farming had costs, but that the shift from foraging to cultivation traded away health, leisure, autonomy, and rough equality in exchange for surplus, hierarchy, and later civilization. A friend objects that this is romantic primitivism dressed up as anthropology; another says it is obviously true once you compare bones, workloads, and the birth of taxation. Treat the stronger version as my position and play it straight: what would count against it? Please separate evidence about average welfare from evidence about distribution, resilience, and option value, since a society can be worse for most people yet better at absorbing shocks, supporting specialists, or generating later goods that no forager band could have produced.

I want a map of the view’s exposure rather than a verdict. Which findings would be fatal: for example, would it matter if early farmers were healthier than we think, if forager affluence depends on unusually benign environments, if apparent forager leisure is really seasonal desperation, or if pre-state violence was common enough to make hierarchy look like protection? Which results would merely be awkward: that agriculture enabled cities, writing, science, and moral circle expansion, but only after millennia of domination? Say what defenders of the mistake thesis should themselves concede as counting against them, not only what critics would throw at them, and rank the defeaters from decisive to inconvenient. End with an honest assessment of whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability by appeals to long-run payoffs, and if so, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind or simply the price of asking a counterfactual historical question.
