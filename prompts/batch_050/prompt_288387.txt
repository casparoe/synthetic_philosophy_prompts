I produce a small podcast on philosophy and the sciences of human nature, and our upcoming episode is devoted to the question of whether an evolutionary account of conscience debunks morality, vindicates it, or simply changes the subject. The topic has become pressing for me personally: I was raised in a religious household, have drifted from that faith over the past two years, and my father recently told me over dinner that without God there is no reason to think my sense of duty tracks anything real. Our guest is a historian of science who has spent two decades studying Charles Darwin's writings on morality and religion, and I want to press her on a passage from Chapter IV of The Descent of Man (1871) that I keep turning over. Darwin writes:

"It may be well first to premise that I do not wish to maintain that any strictly social animal, if its intellectual faculties were to become as active and as highly developed as in man, would acquire exactly the same moral sense as ours. In the same manner as various animals have some sense of beauty, though they admire widely different objects, so they might have a sense of right and wrong, though led by it to follow widely different lines of conduct. If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering."

Draft eight to ten interview questions that make this passage do real work. I do not want general questions about Darwin's life, the reception of evolution, or whether science and religion can coexist. Instead, the questions should probe at least the following: whether the hive-bee thought experiment commits Darwin to moral relativism or only to the contingency of which duties a conscience happens to contain, and whether those two things can really be kept apart; what his deliberate use of the phrase "sacred duty" implies about the independence of morality from religion -- is he suggesting that reverence is just the felt authority of whatever instincts selection installed, or does he leave room for duty to be grounded in something that would survive the discovery of its origins; and whether the analogy with the sense of beauty strengthens or quietly undermines his case, given that most of us treat aesthetic disagreement as merely a matter of taste. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply my guest is most likely to give -- for instance, if she answers that Darwin distinguishes the faculty of conscience from its contents, the follow-up should ask whether that distinction can bear the weight of my father's worry. Please number the questions, with the follow-up indented beneath each, and end with a single-sentence summary, as the last line, of what the interview should establish about Darwin's position.
