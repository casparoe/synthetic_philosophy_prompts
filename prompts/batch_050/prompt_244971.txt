"Williams's decline thesis is that by the early 1800s slavery had stopped being profitable. The slave trade had been hugely profitable in the eighteenth century and those profits helped finance Britain's industrial revolution, but by 1807 running the trade cost more than it returned, so ending it didn't really threaten anyone's economic interests. That's why Williams thinks the standard story -- that abolition happened because campaigners like Wilberforce won the moral argument -- gets things backwards. Abolition was cheap, so Britain could finally afford its conscience.

Appiah's account of moral revolutions is that practices like dueling in England and footbinding in China ended when new moral arguments against them finally persuaded people, and he thinks slavery ended the same way once abolitionist arguments convinced the public it was wrong.

So the two views are direct rivals: Williams says moral arguments were window dressing on economic change, Appiah says moral arguments did the real work. Since they can't both be right about what caused abolition, you have to pick. Williams has the better case because he explains the timing -- abolition arrived when slavery stopped paying -- while Appiah can't explain why the arguments suddenly worked when they did."
