last meeting of my ancient philosophy seminar is next week, and we're wrapping up the unit on the epicurean argument that death cant be bad for the person who dies. i want to end the session by reading a poem aloud, so write me a sestina about the argument -- but the form has to earn its keep, not just be decoration.

here's what i mean. a sestina recycles the same six end-words in a fixed pattern, and lucretius's symmetry point is basically that the nothingness before you were born and the nothingness after you die are the same nothing, and nobody loses sleep over the first one. so pick six end-words that can do double duty: the very same words should have to describe both the prenatal blank and the post-mortem blank as the poem cycles through its stanzas, so that by the third or fourth round the class can feel the mirror closing -- the language literally cant tell the two abysses apart. choose your own six, but stuff like before/after, nothing, me, dark, time, fear is the right neighborhood.

then the envoi, those last three cramped lines where all six words have to show up, is where you let the deprivation objection speak: the thought that death is bad not as a state youre in but because of what it takes from you, the dinners uneaten and the books unwritten and the people unmet. dont resolve it. my students spent all week fighting about whether the symmetry argument survives that move, and i want the poem to land exactly where they landed, which is nowhere in particular.

two constraints. it has to be decent enough as verse that i wouldnt be embarassed to read it with the classicist who sits in sometimes, and accurate enough that nobody walks away thinking epicurus thought death was a treat. paraphrase the philosophy, no direct quotes from the texts.
