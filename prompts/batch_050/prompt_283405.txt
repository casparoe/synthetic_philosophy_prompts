I am a novelist, and next month I am to deliver the first annual memorial lecture at the small literary festival that has been renamed in honor of my oldest friend, a fellow novelist who died last spring. She was unusual among us in having read philosophy before turning to fiction, and Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind was the one philosophical book she pressed on people for forty years. She held that his diagnosis of the ghost in the machine -- the category mistake of treating the mind as a hidden inner occupant steering the body from within -- applied with equal force to how novelists talk about their characters. She would object, politely but immovably, whenever an interviewer said that one of her creations had a life of its own or lived in her head; a character, she insisted, is not a small ghost housed in the machinery of a book, and a writer is not a landlord to invisible tenants.

The audience will be mixed: her family and her general readers in most of the seats, and a contingent of philosophers from her old department in the front row, who will wince audibly at any sloppy gloss of Ryle. Please write the lecture, about twelve minutes when read aloud. Its subject is the question every memorial lecture is obliged to ask -- what survives of a writer? -- and I want her Ryleanism to do real structural work rather than serve as decoration. It should rule things out: I cannot say, even as a figure of speech she might have tolerated, that she lives on in her characters, or that her voice is still with us, if those are merely the ghost in the machine in funeral dress. But it should also rule things in: the lecture should find, in Ryle's picture of the mind as something displayed in what people do rather than concealed behind what they do, something truthful and consoling to say about what a novelist actually leaves -- about characters as exercises of a person's capacities rather than vessels containing a person, and about what readers genuinely perform when they take up her books. The philosophers should feel that the Ryle is right, including any gestures toward his famous cases of category mistakes, such as the visitor who tours the colleges and asks to be shown the university; the family should feel they have been given something to hold; and no one should feel lectured at. I do not need quotations or citations, but the philosophy must be accurate throughout.
