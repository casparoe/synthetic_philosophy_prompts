I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've run into a pattern I can't find a term for. It's adjacent to the Epicurean idea that death can't harm you because once it arrives there's no one left to be harmed — but my puzzle is about a different asymmetry. When a 30-year-old dies, everyone agrees she's been deprived of the fifty years she would otherwise have had; that's supposed to be what makes it tragic. Yet nobody thinks a 30-year-old has been deprived of anything by having been born when she was rather than fifty years earlier, even though living from 1945 instead of 1995 would be just as much extra life, measured in years. When I've pressed people on this, the reply is always that she couldn't have been born earlier — that an earlier-conceived person would be someone else entirely, since different gametes would be involved — whereas she genuinely could have lived longer. But that seems to make the badness of death hinge on a fact about reproductive biology rather than on anything about her or her losses, and it feels unstable: her exact birth time seems every bit as contingent as her death time. Is there an established name for this differential treatment of nonexistence before birth versus nonexistence after death? Is the "you couldn't have been born earlier" reply a recognized position with its own defenders and critics, or just a folk move? And please separate what's actually settled here — say, that ordinary attitudes really are asymmetric in this way — from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers, rather than presenting any one resolution as the consensus.
