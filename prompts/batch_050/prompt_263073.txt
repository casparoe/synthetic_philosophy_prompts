I help run a small volunteer mapping project — about 40 of us survey footpaths, benches, drinking fountains and so on around the county and put the data online for free. Right now we're having a licensing fight: some people want everything released with no strings at all, others want share-alike terms so that any company that builds a product on our data has to give its improvements back to the commons. While chewing on this I was rereading Locke and got stuck on a bit of chapter V ("Of Property") of the Second Treatise of Government, sec. 29:

"Though the water running in the fountain be every one's, yet who can doubt, but that in the pitcher is his only who drew it out? His labour hath taken it out of the hands of nature, where it was common, and belonged equally to all her children, and hath thereby appropriated it to himself."

Tell me the most important implications of Locke's labour theory of appropriation — as it shows up in this passage specifically, not just the greatest-hits version — for how our licensing argument should go. In particular, take a stand on these:

1. The passage seems to say labour alone, with no one's consent, is enough to make something yours. Does that mean each volunteer owns the records they personally surveyed, so the project can't change the license without tracking down all 40 of us (including the ones who've gone quiet)? Or does contributing to a shared dataset under known terms defeat the analogy before it starts?

2. Locke's proviso — that appropriation is only fine where there's enough and as good left in common for others — seems trivially satisfied for data, since copying a dataset leaves the original sitting there. Does the proviso do any real work for non-rival stuff like this, or does it reappear in disguise (e.g. share-alike as a way of keeping "as good" available to later comers once companies start enclosing what we made)?

3. The pitcher example draws a line between the person who draws the water and whatever labour went into the fountain itself. Is there an analogue of the fountain-builders in our case — the people who maintain the servers, wrote the survey app, verify submissions — and does Locke's account give them a different kind of claim than the individual surveyors, or none at all?

No need for citations or a bibliography, just argue from the passage and the general structure of Locke's account; I'll dig up page numbers myself if I need them. Where the theory genuinely doesn't settle one of these questions, say so rather than forcing a verdict.

End your reply with a last line starting "Bottom line:" that states in one sentence what, on your reading, Locke's account implies our license should actually do.
