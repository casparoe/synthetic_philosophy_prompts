I'm a software engineer who has been following debates about AI risk, and I've run into two arguments that share a name. I'd like to know how much they really share.

The first is the strategy-stealing argument from combinatorial game theory. In a game like Hex, where placing an extra stone of your color on the board can never hurt your position, the first player can co-opt any winning strategy the second player might have: make an arbitrary opening move, then play the second player's supposed strategy from there, treating the extra stone as irrelevant. Since the game has no draws, this shows the first player must have a winning strategy — without exhibiting one.

The second is the strategy-stealing assumption I see invoked in AI safety discussions: roughly, that for any plan an unaligned advanced AI system could execute to acquire power or resources, an aligned system (or human institutions assisted by aligned systems) could execute something sufficiently similar to compete — "stealing" the strategy — so that unaligned systems gain no decisive advantage merely from being unaligned.

Please compare the two, addressing the following. (1) What exactly does the game-theoretic argument establish, and which structural features of Hex does it depend on? (2) Which of those features have plausible analogues in the AI case — is there anything corresponding to "extra stones never hurt," to the absence of draws, to taking turns — and which features clearly fail to transfer? (3) The game-theoretic argument is nonconstructive: it proves a winning strategy exists without providing it. Does the AI version need to be constructive to do the work it's asked to do, and is it? (4) Given your answers, is the AI strategy-stealing assumption best understood as a theorem-style claim, an empirical conjecture, or a research desideratum — and does borrowing the game-theory name lend it more authority than it earns?
