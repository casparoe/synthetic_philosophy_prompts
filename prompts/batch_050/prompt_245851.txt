I sit on the policy committee of a regional hospital network, where we have spent months deadlocked over an end-of-life care protocol; the impasse is not about facts but about genuinely conflicting values among the members. At the last meeting a senior colleague pressed an analogy he clearly considers decisive: moral compromise, he said, is simply trade. Each party concedes something it values less in order to secure something it values more, and just as voluntary exchange leaves both traders better off, a well-constructed compromise is a mutual gain, so our agonizing is irrational -- we should identify the efficient bargain and sign it. He is now using this picture to argue that anyone who keeps objecting after a "fair split" is on the table is being self-indulgent. Please audit the analogy for me. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the goods, the prices, the gains from trade, and the voluntary consent of the parties when the commodities are moral commitments rather than possessions. Then identify where the mapping breaks -- I suspect it matters that what one "trades away" in a moral compromise is not a holding but a principle one remains committed to even while acting against it, and that consenting to a violation may itself express endorsement in a way selling a bicycle does not -- and say which of these disanalogies are fatal to my colleague's intended use and which are merely cosmetic. Finally: once the disanalogies are priced in, does his conclusion that a fair split is a Pareto improvement no reasonable person refuses survive, and if the trade picture is the wrong frame, what analogy would better capture what is actually happening when people of conflicting values settle?
