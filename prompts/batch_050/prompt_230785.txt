I coordinate the peer-review program for a mid-size research institute -- basically I oversee about 40 review panels a year that score grant proposals and internal project reports, and each panel has 5-8 reviewers from different departments. Lately I've had three seperate complaints that boil down to the same thing: reviewers from certain groups (junior staff, one visiting researcher with a heavy accent, a technician rather than a PhD) made objections in panel meetings that got brushed off in the room, and then a senior person raised nearly the same point twenty minutes later and it got discussed and credited to them. I can believe this is happening but I also don't think every ignored objection is a bias problem -- sometimes an objection is just wrong, or badly explained, or the panel is out of time.

Write me a procedure I can hand to panel chairs (and train them on in a 90-minute session, so it can't assume they've read any philosophy) for deciding, in the moment or right after, whether a dismissed contribution is a credibility-judgment failure of the biased kind or a legitimate epistemic call. It should be usable as a checklist or set of ordered tests, grounded in whatever philosophers actually know about testimonial injustice and credibility deficits/excesses, and it has to handle at least these wrinkles:

- how to tell an identity-prejudice discount apart from a reasonable discount (e.g., the objector is speaking outside their expertise, or their last three objections were wrong)
- the case where the objection was inarticulate or used non-standard vocabulary but might be tracking something real -- is that the chair's problem to fix, and how much effort is enough
- the reverse case, where someone gets believed too much because of their status and the chair should be actively discounting
- what the chair should do when they can't tell, given the meeting has to end on time

Work the procedure through two or three realistic meeting vignettes, including at least one where it outputs "the dismissal was fine, no injustice here" even though the complainant will hate that answer. Also tell me explicitly what the procedure can't settle -- where the chair just has to use judgment -- and what I'd need to collect over a year to tell whether the training is working, since I have to report something to the director.
