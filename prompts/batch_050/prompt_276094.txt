I'm on the organising committee of a small philosophy-of-language workshop, and this year we did something a bit gimmicky: we paired a die-hard traditional conceptual analyst (the kind who thinks the philosopher's job is to lay bare the concepts we actually have -- what we mean by KNOWLEDGE or CAUSE, tested against intuitions about cases) with an ameliorative analyst in roughly Haslanger's mold (the kind who thinks the interesting question is what concepts we should have for our legitimate purposes, and who'll happily replace our everyday notion of WOMAN or RACE with something that serves those purposes, intuitions be damned). We locked them in a room, metaphorically, and told them to produce a joint statement instead of another talk-past-each-other panel. They gave up and went to the pub. So now I'm writing the document myself for the workshop website, and I need help drafting it.

Write the joint statement these two could plausibly both sign. I want: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims -- things like whether both can accept that words' meanings are partly fixed by use and social facts rather than by what speakers introspect, whether both can accept that our actual concepts can be defective in ways worth noticing, and whether "what does X mean?" and "what should X mean?" are really the rival answers to one question or answers to two different questions; (2) a disagreements section where each remaining dispute is stated in wording that each side would accept as a fair description of its own position -- I'm especially suspicious that some of their fights are verbal, e.g. whether "changing the subject" is an objection or just a redescription of what ameliorative analysis proudly does, so please diagnose which disputes you think collapse into terminology and which are real; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one side -- and for each crux, a case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance (e.g., does a case where a re-engineered concept clearly does better theoretical work but fails to match anyone's intuitions count for the ameliorativist, or does the analyst have a reply that both would recognise as legitimate?). Format: agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes.

One more thing that matters to me: please be careful to mark what's actually common ground in the real literature versus what's your plausible reconstruction of what reasonable versions of each position would say, and don't present a contested claim (on either side) as if it were the settled consensus -- the workshop attendees will know the difference and I don't want egg on my face.
