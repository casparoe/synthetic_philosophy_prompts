I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one thing I've noticed is how often Kuhn gets invoked as a rhetorical trump card: whenever someone dislikes the state of a field -- nutrition science, macro, parts of psychology, AI research -- the move is to declare that what's needed is a paradigm shift rather than more normal science, and that resistance from the establishment is exactly what incommensurability predicts. I finally read The Structure of Scientific Revolutions properly, and now I'm in an awkward position: I can see that the paradigm-shift framing is doing real work in some historical cases, but I also suspect it's unfalsifiable as commonly deployed, since any failure of a purported revolution can be redescribed as the old paradigm defending itself. Before I commit to a view on this, I want to step back and ask what I should even be asking of a theory of scientific change.

So: write the desiderata. What must any adequate theory of how science changes -- Kuhnian, Lakatosian, Popperian, Bayesian, or otherwise -- actually deliver? Which phenomena does it have to explain: the long stretches of stable, cumulative, puzzle-solving work; the occasional genuine ruptures; the would-be revolutions that fizzled; the cases where the old guard's resistance turned out to be rational rather than dogmatic; the apparent convergence of mature sciences on stable results? Which historical episodes must it get right, and which distinctions must it respect (translation failure versus substantive disagreement, discovery versus justification, change of method versus change of theory)? For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and be honest about which demands are genuine constraints and which are just one camp's prejudices dressed up as neutral criteria. I especially want to know whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable -- my suspicion is that a strong reading of incommensurability conflicts with the claim that paradigm choice can be rational, and that cumulativity conflicts with the possibility of genuine loss in revolutions -- because if so, the list itself is a result and every contender is just picking which requirement to abandon.

Then, briefly and without making it the main event, score the usual suspects against the list: Kuhn, Lakatos's research programmes, and a roughly Bayesian or error-statistical picture. One more constraint on the exercise: fix the purpose as practical. The theory I want is one good enough to adjudicate actual disputes -- when someone claims a struggling field needs a paradigm shift, I want principled resources for asking whether that's a diagnosis or an excuse, and whether a mature field's stability is evidence of truth-tracking or just of paradigm entrenchment. Flag which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidates.
