After a long argument with a friend about whether knowing history mostly steadies judgment or mostly becomes an excuse for vanity, nostalgia, and score-settling, I realized we were really disagreeing about Nietzsche’s three historical senses—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—and about how each can serve life while also becoming poisonous in excess. Next month I have to explain this to a reading group of educated non-philosophers in one ninety-minute session. Do not give me an explanation of the essay or a reading list; design the teaching plan I should use.

Plan the sequence as a dependency structure: what must be established first about history as something in the service of present action rather than a warehouse of facts; when to introduce the three modes so that they appear as answers to one problem rather than as three independent tastes; where to place the claim that each mode has a proper dose and a characteristic pathology; and only after that, how to show that the interesting difficulty is not choosing one mode but knowing when each becomes falsification, suffocation, or vindictiveness.

Choose one carrying example to run through the whole session and tell me exactly what it must contain and must avoid. I am inclined to use a concrete civic case—a town arguing over whether to preserve, reinterpret, or remove a celebrated but compromised founder’s monument—because it can elicit all three responses without requiring specialist knowledge. Say why this is better than using an obvious villain, a saintly hero, or a major event everyone already has opinions about, and specify the features that prevent the example from collapsing into a debate about that monument alone.

Map the misconceptions I should expect and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off: that monumental history is just propaganda for greatness; that antiquarian history is mere nostalgia or conservatism; that critical history licenses present-moral superiority and indiscriminate demolition; that the three modes are personality types or political camps; that Nietzsche is recommending more history rather than the right amount and kind; and that “life” means whatever the strongest person currently wants. For each misconception, give the earliest sentence or exercise that would prevent it without prematurely resolving the real tensions.

Finish with two checks for understanding I can run in the last fifteen minutes: one that tests whether participants can diagnose which mode is speaking in an unfamiliar statement, and one that tests whether they can state the healthy use, the excess, and the missing corrective for a single case. Also tell me what to leave out explicitly at this level—textual minutiae, later influence, scholarly disputes, and any verdict on which mode is best—and how to say that omission without making the material feel evasive.
