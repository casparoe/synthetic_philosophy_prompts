The student's answer reads as follows:

"Spinoza argues that democracy is the most natural form of the state because it departs least from the natural condition in which every individual's right extends exactly as far as their power. In the state of nature, right is coextensive with power: whatever a person can do, they have the right to do. When people form a commonwealth, they pool their power into a sovereign. In a democracy — which Spinoza calls an absolute imperium — the multitude as a whole holds sovereignty, so no one transfers their right to another person; each person subjects themselves only to a power of which they are a constituent part. Democracy therefore preserves natural right more fully than monarchy or aristocracy, and it is the form of state in which citizens remain most free.

Spinoza's argument for democracy is ultimately epistemic as well as political. Because decisions in a democracy are made by the many rather than the few, they reflect a kind of collective reason: Spinoza holds that the multitude, taken together, is less likely to act irrationally than a single ruler driven by appetite, and its deliberations therefore tend toward what is genuinely useful for everyone. Democratic decisions are thus not merely the most powerful but also, in general, the most rational available.

Finally, Spinoza infers from the identity of right and power that the sovereign's right is limited only by its power, so subjects retain no rights against the state. However, because no one can actually surrender their power of thinking and judging, every citizen retains an inalienable freedom of thought and speech, which the state cannot rightfully restrict. Spinoza concludes that a democracy, combining the greatest concentration of power with the greatest preservation of individual right, is both the most stable and the freest regime."
