So here's some background: I've taught the PII unit in my upper-division metaphysics seminar more times than I can count, and every year I watch students tie themselves in knots over Black's two-sphere universe. The weird thing is, I moonlight in logic -- I've taught model theory for years too -- and I keep noticing that my logic students find a structurally similar set of issues almost boring, because their formalism just handles it without complaint. So I want to press on that contrast properly.

Here's the exercise. Restate the debate between the friends of the identity of indiscernibles and Black's two-sphere counterexample in the vocabulary of introductory model theory: structures and their domains, relations, isomorphisms, automorphisms, elementary equivalence, complete types, indiscernible sequences. Assume total fluency in that vocabulary -- don't explain the terms, use them. Do it in stages: first translate the principle itself (what does "if x and y share all their properties, they're identical" become when 'property' is regimented as definability-without-parameters? what changes if you allow parameters?), then translate Black's symmetrical universe as a structure, then translate the two positions in the dispute as claims about that structure.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which moves in the metaphysical debate map cleanly onto something a model theorist would say (I'm guessing the symmetry of Black's universe has a nice rendering in terms of automorphisms, and the "but how could God or anyone pick one sphere rather than the other" intuition looks like something about definability), which moves only go through with distortion, and which have no expression at all. In particular I want you to be honest about the following worry: model theory is happy to treat isomorphic structures as the same for most purposes, and it never has to answer "but are there one sphere or two?" in the metaphysician's sense of the question -- the domain has whatever cardinality it has, and asking which element is which beyond that isn't well-formed. Does that mean the target vocabulary simply declines to ask the question the metaphysicians were arguing about? If so, is that a limitation of model theory, or evidence that the original debate was running on a notion of numerical difference that doesn't survive regimentation?

End with your verdict: does what fails to translate point to something real that the formal framework can't say (haecceity, primitive thisness, whatever you think it is), or does it suggest the metaphysical debate was trading on conflating "indiscernible" with "identical" in a way the formalism usefully exposes? I have my own leanings but I'd rather you argue for yours. Keep it rigorous but informal -- this is for my own thinking, not for publication.
