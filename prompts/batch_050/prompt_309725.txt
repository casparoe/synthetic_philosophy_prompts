In my undergraduate seminar on virtue ethics, we spent a session on Benjamin Franklin's plan for attaining moral perfection, and we got stuck on a moment near the end of that section of the Autobiography, just after he describes how a Quaker friend's rebuke led him to add Humility as a thirteenth virtue:

"I cannot boast of much success in acquiring the reality of this virtue, but I had a good deal with regard to the appearance of it. I made it a rule to forbear all direct contradiction to the sentiments of others, and all positive assertion of my own. I even forbid myself, agreeably to the old laws of our Junto, the use of every word or expression in the language that imported a fixed opinion, such as certainly, undoubtedly, etc., and I adopted, instead of them, I conceive, I apprehend, or I imagine a thing to be so or so, or it so appears to me at present."

What puzzled us is that Franklin reports this as a success story: he immediately catalogues the benefits (pleasanter conversations, a readier reception for his proposals, influence in public councils) and never circles back to the missing reality. Construct a thought experiment that puts pressure on exactly this gap, under the following constraints. Two characters in a realistic present-day setting (an office, a hospital ward, a neighborhood association — not an eighteenth-century pastiche) both adopt a Franklin-style regimen: the little book, one virtue per week, deliberate rehearsal of the virtue's outward forms, targeting the same virtue. Both must be wholly sincere; neither may be cynically gaming the system or consciously pursuing a reputation. At the end of the exercise their ledgers must be identical, because the method as Franklin designed it cannot register the difference between them — and yet one must plausibly have acquired something like the reality of the virtue while the other has only its appearance. The divergence has to fall out of a single structural difference between their situations or practices (what the daily tracking makes salient, whether the performances have an audience, what counts as a "fault" mark), not from luck, talent, or effort. State the scenario itself in one paragraph; the passage above does not count toward that length.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications: show why the two ledgers come out identical; then walk through three readings of the case — Franklin's own apparent view (that acquiring the appearance is genuine progress, or at least no failure), an Aristotelian habituation reading (on which rehearsing the outward act should develop the inner state, so the divergence requires explanation), and a situationist or character-skeptical reading (on which both characters may have less than Franklin thinks) — saying what verdict each gives and which of them the case confirms or embarrasses. Identify which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed, and give the strongest attack a defender of Franklin could make on the case: would she deny that appearance and reality actually come apart in my scenario, or deny that Franklin ever needed the reality? Finally, answer the interpretive question the experiment is built to probe — whether the "appearance" concession is a reductio of the method, a quiet redefinition of virtue as social performance, or a joke at Franklin's own expense — and in doing so, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this text; in particular, do not present one reading of Franklin's tone (sincere self-help manual versus wry self-parody) as if it were the consensus.
