In three weeks I am presenting to my city's community safety and licensing committee a proposal that the council formally advocate for replacing our current enforcement-led approach to prostitution with full decriminalisation along New Zealand lines: neither selling nor buying sex would be an offence, sex work would fall under ordinary labour, health-and-safety and planning law, small owner-operated brothels would be permitted without burdensome licensing, and money now spent on policing would be redirected to health outreach and properly funded support for anyone wanting to leave the industry. My core claim will be that the 2003 New Zealand reform improved workers' safety, their access to health services, and their willingness to report violence, without the growth in the overall size of the industry that critics predicted.

The committee has nine members. Three councillors are committed supporters of the Swedish approach of criminalising buyers, and one of them was elected partly on that platform. A senior police officer attends in an advisory capacity and is, I believe, sceptical of anything that reduces his leverage over brothel-keepers and landlords. A public health consultant is broadly sympathetic to me but visibly anxious about anything that could be read as condoning the exploitation of migrant women. And the chair of the residents' association for the one neighbourhood with visible street-based work will be in the public gallery and may well be invited to speak.

Prepare my briefing for the question period. List the objections I will actually face from this particular audience, ordered by how much damage each could do to the proposal, phrased in the vocabulary each person would naturally use. For each: what I should concede without hesitation, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a frank note on where that reply is weakest. Distinguish the objections that have genuine force from the ones that are merely loud. Tell me what to do if the Swedish-model councillors succeed in framing the whole question as men's violence against women rather than as a matter of labour and public-health policy. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks, and my best available answer if it comes.
