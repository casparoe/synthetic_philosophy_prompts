I host a monthly philosophy podcast aimed at graduate students, and next month's guest is a Kant scholar whose new book argues that the framework of Perpetual Peace — three definitive articles built around republican constitutions, a voluntary federation of free states, and a cosmopolitan right of hospitality — is not merely of historical interest but remains the correct blueprint for international order, superior to both world-state proposals and pure realist pessimism.

The episode will be organized around one passage that the book leans on heavily: the opening of the Second Definitive Article, where Kant claims that a state of peace among neighboring peoples is not the natural condition — the natural condition is war, in the sense of a standing threat of it even where open hostilities are absent — and that peace must therefore be instituted, that is, founded and secured by right rather than merely enjoyed by luck. (I am paraphrasing rather than quoting; I will hand him the passage on air.) My guest reads this as the essay's argumentative pivot: the move from peace as an absence of fighting to peace as an artifact that must be constructed, and hence as something institutions, not goodwill, must deliver.

Draft nine or ten interview questions for this guest, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I want the questions to turn on the actual machinery of his reading rather than invite him to restate it. Concretely, I want the sequence to do the following, roughly in this order: press the analogy between individuals exiting the state of nature and states doing the same, and ask why that analogy does not commit him to the world state Kant himself rejects; probe the voluntariness of the federation — whether a league states may enter and leave can genuinely secure anything, or merely redescribes the natural condition it was meant to end; ask how his institutional reading of the passage handles Kant's own striking concession elsewhere in the essay that nature, working through human inclinations and even through war itself, pushes states toward peace, and whether that providential machinery sits awkwardly with the claim that peace must be instituted by right; test the empirical side of the blueprint — the democratic-peace literature reads Kant as predicting that republics rarely fight one another, and I want to know what the view says about cases of democracies at war with each other and about authoritarian states that have been durably peaceful; and ask him about the right of hospitality and whether his reading of it is thick enough to matter for current debates about asylum. End with a question about what evidence — historical or institutional — would count against the book's central claim, and what the Kantian framework actually risks.

For each question, give me the follow-up that cuts off his most plausible escape route. He is polished and will, if allowed, answer every hard question by noting that Kant wrote Perpetual Peace as a regulative ideal rather than a prediction; I want at least two of your follow-ups designed specifically to make that move cost him something.

One request about how you answer: if you think any of my premises is confused — for instance, if the passage I have described cannot bear the weight my guest (or I) want to put on it, or if my summary misrepresents the Second Definitive Article — say so plainly and correct it rather than building questions on a flawed reading. I would rather have that before the recording than discover it on air.
