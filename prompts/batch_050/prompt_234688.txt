Okay so I'm building a handout for my intro epistemology course on the value problem — the Meno thing, i.e. why knowledge is supposed to be worth more than merely true belief when both get you the same right answer. Instead of lecturing, I want to give students a "find your position" decision tree: you start at the top with the puzzle and each fork is a question that splits the space until you land on a named stance. Then we discuss in section who ended up where and whether they can live with it.

Act as an epistemologist who's genuinely deep in the value-of-knowledge literature (the tethering/stability answer, the swamping problem, credit-and-achievement views, knowledge-first approaches, pragmatic answers) but who's also good at translating it for sharp 19-year-olds. Think carefully and take your time. Before building the tree, do a brainstorm pass: list 10-12 candidate fork questions, then pick the 7-9 that actually carve the space cleanly and say in a sentence why you dropped each of the others (e.g. "redundant with Q3", "everyone answers the same way", "smuggles in the label").

Hard constraints:

- Required leaves (you can add more if the space demands it): (1) the stability/tethering answer in the spirit of the Meno — knowledge sticks around because it's tied down by an account; (2) the credit/achievement answer — knowledge is valuable because the truth is to the believer's credit, like a goal you scored rather than one that bounced in off a defender; (3) the swamping verdict — from a purely truth-loving standpoint knowledge is NOT more valuable than true belief, and the intuition that it is rests on a mistake; (4) the knowledge-first refusal — the question assumes you can understand knowledge as true-belief-plus-stuff and then ask what the stuff adds, and that assumption itself is wrong; (5) a practical-guidance answer — knowledge matters because of what it licenses you to do, assert, or plan on.
- Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names. Work out which questions generate the possible combinations of answers, then locate the actual positions in that space. If some combination is unoccupied, mark it and diagnose briefly: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored?
- Every fork must be answerable by someone who doesn't know the jargon, so phrase each one through a concrete mini-case, and your tree must use at least three of these somewhere: (a) two people who both correctly direct a traveler to the next town, one who has walked the route and one who picked the turn at random and got lucky; (b) a stopped clock that shows the right time versus a working clock, where you need to leave for the station; (c) a cook whose dish works because of a lucky ingredient substitution versus one who understands the chemistry and could fix it when the pantry changes; (d) a trivia player whose winning answer was a pure hunch versus one who reconstructed it from things she actually knew; (e) a belief that has to keep guiding you for ten years, like a doctor's judgment about a treatment.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and say what extra question you'd need to split them (and why you didn't just include it).
- Each leaf gets: the position's name, one sentence a student could say out loud to identify with it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a smart opponent immediately pokes at.
- Finish with a worked example: invent a student ("Devon") whose gut reactions you stipulate at each fork, walk him down the tree node by node, name his leaf, and then say whether the awkward commitment there would actually bother someone with his gut reactions or not.

One last thing: the whole point is to sort students by what they think about the cases, not by which label sounds smart, so no fork should be phrased like "do you think beliefs can be valuable as achievements?" — that just asks them to pick a team. If you catch one of your forks doing that, rewrite it.
