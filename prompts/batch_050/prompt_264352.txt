So I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a draft, and my main character quits her job at a hedge fund to start a nonprofit for insect welfare, and honestly the whole third act depends on whether the reader comes away thinking she's a hero or thinking she's talked herself into something absurd. I gave a chapter to two friends and they ended up in a shouting match at my kitchen table, and now I need to know what they actually disagree about so I know which scenes to leave ambiguous and which to commit on.

Friend one is the expected value guy. His line: even a small chance, say one or two percent, that insects can suffer, multiplied by the sheer number of insects alive at any moment, means insect welfare outweighs basically everything else humans worry about, and my character is the only sane person in the book. Friend two thinks this is a trick arithmetic plays on you: multiply a tiny probability by a big enough number and you can justify anything, and at some point a low enough credence stops being a reason to act and starts being a rounding error with delusions of grandeur. Though to be fair she also muttered something about insects just being little clockwork, so maybe she has two arguments and is mixing them.

Write the joint statement these two could plausibly produce if I locked them in a room for a weekend and made them do an adversarial collaboration. I want:

1. A numbered list of claims they'd both sign. Don't make these vacuous -- actually figure out where they overlap. I suspect they agree on more than they realize (the numbers really are staggering, sentience is genuinely uncertain, moral weight shouldn't require certainty, etc.), but check me on that.

2. The disagreements, each stated in wording both would accept for their own side. Seperate the fights that are really about words from the ones that are about the world. For instance, I genuinely can't tell if "insects probably aren't sentient" and "even if they might be, you shouldn't act on it" are one position or two.

3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions where, if one of them got a clear answer, at least one of them would have to update. For each crux, name a concrete piece of evidence, an experiment, or an argument that both would agree bears on it, and write down what each side predicts about it in advance, so neither can move the goalposts later.

4. One short paragraph each where they say what would change their mind. If either of them can't name anything, flag that, because that's a character detail I can use.

Keep it casual enough that I can mine it for dialogue, and don't give me a reading list, I can find the sources myself.
