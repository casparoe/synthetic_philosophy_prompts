I host a small interview podcast on moral questions, and my next guest is a philosopher who defends the view that victims can be morally required to forgive, and that genuine forgiveness does not depend on the wrongdoer's repentance, apology, or even acknowledgment that a wrong occurred. I have no academic training but have been turning this over on my own, and I want questions that force her past restating the position. Please draft seven or eight interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The set should cover at least the following pressure points: whether an obligation to forgive is in tension with the idea that resentment is sometimes the only way a person takes their own mistreatment seriously, so that requiring forgiveness can look like requiring a victim to endorse their own demotion; whether forgiving someone who has never apologized quietly collapses into saying the wrong did not matter, or into simply deciding to move on, which anyone can do for prudential reasons and which seems to need no moral praise; the thought that if forgiving what is easy to forgive is no achievement, then forgiveness that costs nothing and demands nothing of the wrongdoer may have stopped being forgiveness at all; who besides the direct victim, if anyone, has the standing to forgive, and what happens to her view when the victim is dead or unreachable; and how a moral requirement can bind us to relinquish a feeling we do not seem to choose in the first place, given that we cannot simply decide to stop resenting someone the way we decide to repay a debt. Where possible, push her to work through concrete cases rather than trade in abstractions. Write the questions and their follow-ups as continuous prose, with no headings, numbered lists, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would lay them out in an email I could read aloud from.
