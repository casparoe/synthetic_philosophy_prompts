I teach a philosophy of mind seminar for undergrads, around 18 students, one 75-minute session. I want to use the session to set up the question of whether a system made of mindless parts can itself have a mind -- the Aunt Hillary setup from Gödel, Escher, Bach, where the ant colony seems to understand things and have conversations even though no individual ant understands anything at all.

Design an activity where the class itself becomes such a system. Each student gets a simple local rule on an index card and follows it mechanically, with no idea what the group as a whole is doing. But the class collectively ends up doing something at least minimally cognitive -- storing a bit of information across time, classifying an input, producing an answer to a question -- that no individual student did, knew, or can reconstruct from their own card. The reveal (this is what "you" computed) should only come after they've run it, and ideally some students should at first refuse to believe the class really did it, or insist "nobody actually understood anything, it was just card-flipping" -- that reaction is exactly what I want to harvest in the debrief.

- the exact local rules. This is the hard part: simple enough that a student can execute theirs in a couple seconds under noisy classroom conditions, but opaque enough that nobody reverse-engineers the global function from their own card. It needs to actually work, not just work in principle, so walk me through a concrete example input and what should happen step by step.
- materials, setup, and a timeline with rough minuts
- a second round or variation so it's not a one-trick demo -- maybe swapping out some students mid-run, or running the same function with a different group of people, since I want them to wrestle with what stays "the same system" across those changes
- a debrief that maps what happened onto the levels-of-description point (signals vs symbols, ants vs colony, neurons vs thoughts) and anticipates the obvious student objections: this is just like the Chinese room, the students understood their cards so it's not really mindless parts, the TA running the clock was the real mind, etc.
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (timing failures, students peeking at each other's cards, someone figuring out the global pattern and shouting it out) and how to recover
- an honest note about which feature of the real question the activity can't reproduce. I suspect the fact that my students are themselves conscious agents who can peek at the global level makes the analogy misleading in at least one direction, and I'd rather have that spelled out than discover it mid-class.

Constraints: no computers, no projector, just index cards, tape, and people standing up and moving around. And no lecture before the activity -- they shouldn't hear the words "emergence" or "functionalism" until the debrief.
