I am preparing the central essay assignment for my upper-level seminar on the ethics of torture, and I want the question to force students to do philosophical reconstruction rather than rehearse the standard ticking-time-bomb debate. Here is the task I intend to set; please answer it as if you were a strong student in the course, in an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, in American English. (The quoted passage does not count toward the length.)

The international legal prohibition on torture is usually presented as absolute and exceptionless: the Convention Against Torture, in force since 1987, forbids torture without allowing any emergency, war, or public-safety justification. But the same treaty defines its object very carefully. Article 1 provides:

For the purpose of this Convention, the term "torture" means any act by which severe pain or suffering, whether physical or mental, is intentionally inflicted on a person for such purposes as obtaining from him, or a third person, information or a confession, punishing him for an act he or a third person has committed or is suspected of having committed, or intimidating or coercing him or a third person, or for any reason based on discrimination of any kind, when such pain or suffering is inflicted by or at the instigation of or with the consent or acquiescence of a public official or other person acting in an official capacity. It does not include pain or suffering arising only from, inherent in, or incidental to, lawful sanctions.

Note three features of this definition. First, whether severe suffering counts as torture depends on the purpose for which it is inflicted: the same act, producing the same pain, is torture if aimed at extracting information but apparently not if inflicted for no listed purpose. Second, the act must be connected to the state; a private kidnapper who inflicts identical suffering falls outside the definition. Third, suffering inherent in or incidental to lawful sanctions is excluded wholesale, however severe.

Your task: reconstruct the theory of torture's wrongness that this definition presupposes. It plainly cannot rest on a purely welfare-centered view, on which what matters is the victim's suffering simpliciter — for then the purpose-clauses, the state-connection requirement, and the lawful-sanctions exclusion would be unintelligible. What alternative view of the wrong — one centered on the exploitation of a person's agency, on the specific relation between state and detainee, or something else — makes sense of all three features at once? Then test whether the treaty is internally coherent: does an absolutist prohibition sit comfortably with a definition this sensitive to purpose and legal status, or does the absolutism presuppose a different theory of the wrong than the definition does? Distinguish carefully between what the text strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with; identify where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, apply your reconstruction: if the definition were redrafted on a purely suffering-centered theory, what would change (would lawful sanctions, private violence, or non-purposive cruelty come within the ban?); and does the purpose-sensitive structure of the definition illuminate the ticking-time-bomb case — in which the interrogational aim is the whole point — or does it simply evade it? Defend a verdict on whether the treaty needs the theory you attribute to it.
