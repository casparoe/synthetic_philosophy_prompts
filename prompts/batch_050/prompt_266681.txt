For several years I have followed the debate over Kant's universalizability tests, and I have come to suspect it is stalled rather than merely difficult, but I cannot tell whether my suspicion is warranted. On one side stand the heirs of Hegel's emptiness charge: a purely formal test of whether a maxim can be universalized either rules in too much, because a maxim specified with enough detail will universalize anything, or rules out too much, because harmless maxims fail it while some genuinely appalling ones, stated at the right level of generality, sail through. On the other side stand defenders who distinguish contradiction in conception from contradiction in the will, insist the test applies to the maxims an agent actually acts on rather than to descriptions constructed after the fact, and deny that Kant ever offered it as a mechanical decision procedure that outputs verdicts for spectators. What strikes me is that each side's replies never seem to land. The critics produce another counterexample; the defenders respond that the counterexample misdescribes the maxim or misunderstands the test's role; the critics answer that a test requiring this much stage-setting about maxim individuation has smuggled in the substantive moral knowledge it was supposed to generate. And around it goes.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are disagreeing about what the test is for: the critics treat it as a candidate decision procedure and judge it by its outputs, while the defenders treat it as a discipline of reflection for agents checking their own reasons, where counterexamples engineered by third parties simply do not engage it. If that is right, the debate is largely two answers to different questions under one name. But I can also see an alternative diagnosis: that there is a real, deep disagreement about whether any test built only from the structure of willing can yield determinate content without importing the evaluator's own moral views, and that the verbal appearance is a symptom of that disagreement rather than a substitute for it.

What I want is your diagnosis, with reasons grounded in what the two sides actually argue, not a survey of positions and not a verdict on Kant. Is this a verbal dispute, a disguised substantive one, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a worked derivation of some duty that both camps accept as correctly derived, a formal result about what purely formal procedures can and cannot determine, an explicit split into two separate debates, or is there a case that no progress should be expected because the impasse reflects something about what moral justification can be? Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the diagnosis you give, and single out the one point in it you are least sure of and why.
