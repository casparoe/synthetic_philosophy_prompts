Next semester I am teaching an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of applied mathematics, and our final unit covers indispensability arguments and the more recent debate over genuinely mathematical explanations in science. The centerpiece of that unit is the periodical cicada case: the species that emerge only once every thirteen or seventeen years, with the claim -- pressed by Alan Baker and contested at length since -- that the primeness of these numbers does real explanatory work, since prime periods minimize how often a cicada brood coincides with predators whose own cycles are shorter. My students reliably get stuck on whether this is mathematics explaining nature or merely mathematics redescribing what the evolutionary story already explains, and I want to close the unit in a way that keeps that question alive rather than lecturing at it.

To that end, I would like a poem I can read aloud at the end of the last session. It should be a sonnet: fourteen lines of iambic pentameter, either Petrarchan or Shakespearean in rhyme scheme, whichever serves the argument better. I want the octave (or the first three quatrains) to present the cicada case with real fidelity -- the underground years, the massed emergence, the predators' shorter cycles, and the sense in which a fact about the integers seems to reach down into the forest floor and do something. The volta should then pivot to the skeptical worry: perhaps the primeness is only bookkeeping for a causal history that natural selection wrote on its own, and perhaps the honeycomb's hexagons, which you may gesture at in a line or two, show that optimality results in mathematics prove nothing about what is doing the explaining.

Two further requests. First, the poem should not resolve the dispute; it should end in genuine suspension, because that is where I want the discussion to begin. Second, please get the science and the philosophy right enough that a philosopher of mathematics reading it would nod rather than wince -- a poem that merely name-drops prime numbers would be useless to me. Give the poem a title, and feel free to add two or three sentences afterward explaining the choices you made at the volta, since I may share those notes with the class.
