I would like your help thinking through a situation from my working life. Please take the role of a professional philosopher — rigorous, but attentive to how things actually matter to practitioners — and reason carefully, weighing rival principles against each other before settling on a view.

I am a novelist. Seven years ago I began a book about a lighthouse keeper who murders his wife. I completed a draft, set it aside, and have since rewritten it from scratch four times. The lighthouse keeper is now a woman who runs a ferry; the murder became a disappearance, then an accusation that may be false; the setting moved from the Hebrides to the Adriatic; the wife became a brother. For years I preserved one scene — a rowboat found drifting with a picnic laid out in it — but last month I cut that too. Not a single sentence of the original draft survives. And yet, when people ask what I am working on, I still say: the same book I have been writing for seven years.

Two questions. First, am I right to say that? If I am not — if at some point in the revisions this ceased to be that novel and became another — when did the change occur, and does anything of consequence actually turn on where the line is drawn? Second, a complication. Last year I learned that a friend to whom I once showed the third draft has published a novel that keeps that draft's plot and its ferry captain almost intact, though every sentence is hers. I feel she has published my book; she maintains she has published hers. I notice my two intuitions may not sit comfortably together — in my own case I claim continuity across total change of content, while in hers I deny that shared content suffices. A judge could settle the copyright question; I want it settled on the merits, and I want to know whether I must give one of the two intuitions up.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my situation.
