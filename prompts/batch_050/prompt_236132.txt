For my graduate seminar on mental causation, I want the standard of evaluation fixed before we open the literature. Write the desiderata that a theory of mental causation must satisfy to count as adequate, where adequacy means this: the theory must vindicate both the explanatory practice of psychology — that people's reasons genuinely explain their actions — and our practice of moral responsibility, in which agents are answerable for what their reasons led them to do. Give the requirements as a list, with the case or argument motivating each, and mark each as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. The list should cover at least the following: consistency with a plausible causal closure principle for the physical, together with a precise statement of which closure principle the theory can actually afford; the causal efficacy of mental properties and not merely of mental events; the causal relevance of content — that an action be explained by what the agent believed, and not only by the neural syntax realizing the belief; a treatment of apparently systematic overdetermination, saying either why it is tolerable or why it is not and how the theory avoids it; and an account of psychopathological cases in which behavior occurs without the apparently relevant reason — confabulation, action under delusion — where the theory must explain why the mental cause fails to be efficacious there if it is efficacious in normal cases. I also want one desideratum the literature does not standardly demand: state whether the theory's verdicts survive translation into an interventionist framework, and if the theory rests on a notion of causation that resists such translation — production, generation, metaphysical determination — say what work that notion is doing that intervention-talk cannot. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect that closure, the non-identity of mental and physical properties, genuine mental efficacy, and the ban on systematic overdetermination form such a set — identify the set explicitly and say which member you would sacrifice; the list's display of that tension is the result I most care about. Finally, score the main contenders briefly against the list: type-identity theory, functionalist non-reductive physicalism, epiphenomenalism, an interventionist account, and the family of views that grant mental properties causal relevance while denying them causal production. Note which single requirement eliminates the most candidates. A table is acceptable for the scoring; the prose should carry the requirements.
