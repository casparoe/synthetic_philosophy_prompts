I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of ethics and aesthetics, and I've become preoccupied with a cluster of conventions in historical drama that seem, on reflection, to pull against one another. Biographical films and prestige television series about real events routinely open with a title card announcing that what follows is based on a true story, and they often go further, recreating documented episodes with great care -- period-accurate rooms, verbatim-adjacent renderings of famous speeches, actors made up to resemble their subjects. Yet the very same works compress timelines, invent confidants and antagonists, merge several real people into a single composite character, and stage private conversations between the dead that no one could possibly have witnessed or recorded. Strangest of all, many of these productions close with a standard legal disclaimer insisting that the work is fictional and that any resemblance to real persons is coincidental -- a claim that sits in flat contradiction to the opening card. Audiences and critics, for their part, seem to tolerate all of this, but only up to a point: a film that invents dialogue for a deceased statesman is praised as illuminating, while one that attributes to the same statesman a crime he did not commit, or whitewashes one he did, provokes outrage and sometimes litigation threats from estates and survivors.

What I want to understand is what this body of practice presupposes. What conception of truth does it take to govern historical dramatization -- is it working with a distinction between factual and emotional truth, or between the letter and the spirit of the record, and are those distinctions coherent enough to bear the weight the industry puts on them? What view of our obligations to the dead does the selective outrage presuppose: does the practice treat the dead as having surviving interests in their reputation, or does it instead treat misrepresentation as a wrong to the living audience, or to history itself -- and can those be disentangled by looking at which inventions provoke scandal and which pass without comment? And what does the simultaneous assertion and disavowal of truth -- the opening card and the closing disclaimer -- presuppose about how audiences actually understand fiction: does it make sense only on a picture of viewers as sophisticated readers of genre conventions, or is it better explained as an institutional compromise that presupposes nothing philosophically at all?

I'm equally interested in what the practice would look like under rival commitments. If we took seriously the idea that the dead have interests that survive them, would that license the current pattern of permissiveness about private scenes combined with strictness about public acts, or would it demand something quite different -- consent from estates, perhaps, or an accuracy standard? Conversely, if the audience's epistemic interests are what matter, does the composite-character convention survive scrutiny, given that viewers plausibly walk away believing more of the dramatized record than critics assume? Finally, where is the practice simply underdetermined -- which of its conventions could be licensed by several competing theories of truth and posthumous obligation, so that no amount of scrutiny of the conventions themselves will tell us which theory the culture actually holds?
