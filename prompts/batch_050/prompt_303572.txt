The question was:

"State the dogmatism paradox (the one associated with Kripke's dogmatism puzzle about future evidence). What exactly is the allegedly absurd conclusion, and which assumptions are needed to generate it? Then assess one possible response to the paradox: does it succeed, and why or why not?"

Here's the student's answer:

The dogmatism paradox, due to Gilbert Harman, goes like this. Suppose I know that p. Since I know it, p is true. But if p is true, then any evidence against p is evidence that supports a falsehood, which is just to say it's misleading evidence. Since I know that p, I can figure all of this out a priori, so by the closure of knowledge under known implication, I know in advance that any evidence I might encounter against p is misleading. But if I know it's misleading, it seems like I can rationally just ignore it without even looking at it. And that's the absurd conclusion: knowledge seems to license me to dismiss all future counterevidence sight unseen, which is just closed-mindedness dressed up as logic.

The argument needs a few assumptions. First, closure: if I know p and I know p entails q, I'm in a position to know q. Second, the definition of misleading evidence as evidence supporting a falsehood. Third, the idea that knowing evidence is misleading makes it rational to disregard it.

My response: I think the paradox dissolves once we're careful about what "evidence" means. If I genuinely know p, then there can't really be any genuine evidence against p — only apparent evidence, like forged documents or lying witnesses. Genuine evidence by definition points toward the truth. So the conclusion isn't absurd at all: ignoring non-genuine evidence is perfectly rational, and the paradox only looks scary because it trades on calling misleading stuff "evidence" in the first place. The real lesson is that we should restrict "evidence" to things that reliably indicate truth.
