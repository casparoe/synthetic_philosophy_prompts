A colleague who works on AI capabilities — not safety — recently sent me a blog post about AI alignment that he said "finally explained HCH in a way that made sense." The post summarizes Paul Christiano's HCH ("Humans Consulting HCH"), an idealized construction from the iterated amplification line of alignment research, as follows:

'HCH is a proposal for turning an ordinary human into a superintelligence through recursion. A human is posed a question together with a budget of subcalls; whenever she cannot answer directly, she may consult fresh copies of herself, delegating to each a strictly simpler subquestion, and then aggregate their answers into her final output. Since every copy enjoys the same privilege, a single human with sufficient recursive depth can in principle match any desired level of cognitive performance — the scheme is, in the author's words, a human bootstrapped into a god. Because every node in the resulting tree is an actual human reasoning one step at a time, the system's outputs are aligned by construction: there is nowhere for an alien optimization process to hide, and any distortion of the human's values would have to pass through a person who could simply refuse to pass it along. The load-bearing premise is the factored cognition hypothesis, the claim that hard questions can always be factored into easier ones, and small-scale experiments on question decomposition have by now confirmed this hypothesis for practical purposes. The upshot is that the remaining problem is economic rather than conceptual: HCH as literally described is far too slow and expensive to run, so the real research agenda consists in finding machine-learning shortcuts that approximate the same computation cheaply enough to be competitive.'

Something about this summary bothers me — particularly the phrase "aligned by construction," which seems to be doing far more work than the author admits — but I don't know this literature well enough to say precisely where the summary overreaches. I would like an accuracy audit I can act on before deciding whether to cite the post in a grant proposal. Specifically:

1. Go through the summary claim by claim and sort each into one of three categories: accurate, a defensible simplification, or a misrepresentation. Where a claim conflates two things the underlying view keeps apart, or overstates a conditional thesis as an unconditional one, please identify exactly what has been collapsed or inflated.
2. In particular, assess the treatment of the factored cognition hypothesis. Is the summary's statement of the hypothesis faithful? Does the epistemic status the summary assigns to it match how proponents and critics actually regard it? And does the summary omit any structural constraint of the HCH construction — for instance, on how much information can pass between a human and her subcalls — that bears on whether the hypothesis is even plausible?
3. Evaluate the transparency argument: does the fact that each individual node is a human reasoning step by step entail that the tree as a whole is inspectable, or that value distortions cannot survive aggregation? What would a careful proponent of iterated amplification concede here, and what would they push back on?
4. Assess the concluding claim that the remaining problems are "economic rather than conceptual." Which conceptual open problems does this framing suppress, and does the conclusion survive once the misrepresentations identified earlier are corrected?

Finally, a meta-question: the post is written by someone clearly sympathetic to this research direction, and its errors, if they are errors, mostly flatter the view rather than caricature it. Are there places where the summary is unfair to critics of HCH — for instance, by presenting contested empirical questions as settled — and how should I weigh that kind of sympathetic distortion differently from an opponent's strawman?
