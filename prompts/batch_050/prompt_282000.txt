In coverage of China's social credit system, I keep running into the same deflationary move, made both by sympathetic commentators and by people shrugging off the criticism: a social credit score is just a credit score. We already let FICO and its equivalents assign us a number from our behavior and open or close doors accordingly — housing, loans, insurance, sometimes employment. China's system, on this line, is the same instrument extended a bit wider, and Western alarm about it is either hypocrisy or Orientalism; Germans who object are just projecting their particular data-protection anxieties onto a mundane piece of administrative infrastructure.

I'd like a careful audit of that analogy. Please give me, first, the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the scoring agency, the input data, the aggregated number, the decision-makers who consume the score, the scored individual, and the mechanisms of dispute and correction, on each side of the comparison. Second, the disanalogies — and here I'd like you to do more than list them. A FICO score is issued by competing private firms for a particular commercial purpose, regulated by statute, confined to financial behavior, and avoidable in principle by someone willing to live outside credit markets; a state-run trustworthiness score has a single operator who is also the police, the courts, and the legislator, draws on conduct far beyond repayment, and feeds consequences like travel restrictions and school admissions. Which of these differences actually matter for the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that there is nothing categorically new here, only a difference of degree — and which are superficial? Does 'difference of degree versus difference of kind' even survive scrutiny as a framing once the operator of the score is also the sovereign?

Third, use the audit to illuminate the transatlantic contrast. Americans treat credit scoring as unremarkable and extend scoring logic generously — background checks, risk-assessment algorithms in sentencing, insurance telematics — while Germany, with the same liberal-democratic commitments, treats even private credit bureaus as suspect, restricts data retention aggressively, and grounds all of this in a constitutional right to informational self-determination rather than in property or market-fairness terms. If the analogy from social credit to FICO is weaker than its users claim, does the German position turn out to have diagnosed something the American position misses — or is the German stance itself parasitic on a different, equally questionable analogy, say between one's data and one's home or one's sealed correspondence, where the state's reading of my file is treated like a break-in or an intercepted letter? Audit that second analogy briefly as well: what does the trespass/interception picture get right about data protection, and where does it mislead (data is non-rival, often voluntarily disclosed, frequently inferable rather than taken)?

Finally: whatever survives of the first analogy, tell me what the better comparison case for a social credit system would be, if any — probation regimes, driver's-license point systems, professional licensing, school discipline records — and whether substituting the better analogy changes the verdict on whether the system's critics or its deflationists have the stronger position. I want the conclusion stated plainly: how much of the deflationary move survives the disanalogies, and what, if anything, remains genuinely novel about a state that scores the trustworthiness of its own citizens.
