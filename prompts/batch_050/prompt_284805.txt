I'm a PhD student (philosophy of religion, mostly working on the divine attributes) and I moonlight as the external expert on our national Advertising Review Panel, the body that hears complaints about misleading marketing claims. I need help drafting the decision memo for our weirdest case yet, and I'd like you to play the role of a philosopher who has thought hard about omnipotence but also understands that institutions need an answer, not a seminar. Think carefully before you write; brainstorm a bit first if it helps, but the final memo must take a definite position, because that's the whole point of it.

The case. A studio sells a god-game called OMNIA. Its selling point is a free-text command box: you can order your deity to do anything and the engine renders it. The marketing says, in large letters, that your god "can do absolutely anything." During launch week, players started typing the obvious: "create a boulder so heavy that you cannot lift it." The game freezes for about ten seconds, then shows the god creating the boulder, straining, and lifting it anyway while a banner flashes PARADOX RESOLVED. Some players were delighted; a sizeable group has now filed a complaint saying the advert is misleading, because (a) the god demonstrably fails to create a boulder he can't lift -- he just lifts it -- and (b) the engine also refuses "draw me a square circle," which just produces an error message. The studio's response: no reasonable consumer reads an advert literally; the game in fact responds to every single input with something, which no competitor manages; and the complaint proves too much anyway, since the same logic would condemn adverts for "unlimited" data plans that throttle you after 50GB.

There's one more wrinkle the panel keeps arguing about. The game has a Vow Mode in which your god can make a genuinely irrevocable vow -- the engine hard-codes it -- e.g. "I vow never to lift any boulder heavier than ten tons." Players have shown that a god who takes this vow and then creates an eleven-ton boulder cannot lift it: the game makes him fail, permanently. The complainants say this proves the "can do anything" claim was false from the start, since the god can bring about a state in which there is something he can't do. The studio says a being that can bind itself is more powerful, not less, so the vow case actually supports them.

Draft the memo I'll put before the panel next Tuesday, around 800-1,000 words, recommending one of our three standard rulings: claim upheld (advert stands), claim misleading (advert must be withdrawn or reworded), or claim permissible only with qualification (they must run it as, say, "can do anything logically possible"). Whichever you recommend, commit to it -- the panel alrady has two fence-sitters and my job is to hand them something they can vote for or against. The memo needs to: state the bottom-line ruling in the first paragraph; explain why the boulder complaint does or doesn't show the god can't do everything, taking seriously the standard philosophical move that "a stone an omnipotent being can't lift" isn't a genuine task at all, any more than a square circle is; deal honestly with the PARADOX RESOLVED display (does depicting the god doing both sides of the contradiction make the claim true, or does it just paper over incoherence with animation?); settle the Vow Mode dispute; and address the "no one reads adverts literally" defence, including whether the unlimited-data comparison is actually fair. I want the strongest version of the studio's case answered, not a strawman -- the studio reads our memos and appeals the sloppy ones.

One last constraint: don't just gesture at "philosophers disagree about omnipotence." Where the philosophy genuinely leaves room, say what a reasonable decision-maker should do anyway, because we have to publish something on Thursday.
