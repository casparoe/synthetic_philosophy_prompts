I work as a software engineer, mostly on product analytics and A/B testing infrastructure, and I've run into a pattern in my data work that I can't find a settled name for. I'd like you to act as a statistician-philosopher: someone equally at home with the formal properties of contingency tables and with the philosophical literature on causation and evidence. Please think carefully, begin by brainstorming the candidate concepts and literatures this might belong to before committing to an answer, and write in British English throughout.

Here is the pattern, with two real instances from my work (numbers altered).

Instance one. We tested a new recommendation algorithm. In the aggregate data, variant B won clearly: higher click-through overall. But when I split users into "new accounts" and "established accounts", variant A won in both segments. My first instinct was that the split must be right and the aggregate misleading — the mix of new versus established users differed between the two arms, and established accounts click more under any variant, so the aggregate comparison was really comparing different populations. Fine. I now reflexively distrust aggregates.

Instance two, which broke that reflex. A different team measured whether a feature increased long-term retention. The aggregate said yes. They split users by "engaged in week one" versus "not engaged in week one", and within each segment the effect shrank to nothing. They concluded the feature doesn't work. But here's the thing: engagement in week one is downstream of the feature — the feature causes week-one engagement, which in turn causes retention. Conditioning on week-one engagement blocks exactly the path through which the feature works. In this case, splitting the data destroyed the true effect rather than revealing it.

So I have two cases with the same surface shape — an aggregate table and a segmented table that tell different stories — where in one case you should trust the segments and in the other you should trust the aggregate, and the difference seems to depend entirely on whether the splitting variable sits causally before or after the treatment. Meanwhile, people in my industry use the same slogan for both: they call any aggregate-versus-segment reversal "Simpson's paradox" and treat "always disaggregate" as the moral.

I then read a summary of an old paper — G. Udny Yule's "Notes on the Theory of Association of Attributes in Statistics" (Biometrika, 1903) — which apparently discussed how pooling heterogeneous material can manufacture an association between attributes that have none in any of the sub-populations, or mask one that's present in all of them. If that's an accurate description, Yule had the first half of my pattern over a century ago. What I can't tell from secondary chatter is whether anyone has named, or even clearly discussed, the second half: the error of automatically preferring the disaggregated table, when the conditioning variable is a mediator, a collider, or otherwise downstream of the thing you're measuring.
