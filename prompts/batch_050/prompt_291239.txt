You are a professional philosopher specialising in the metaphysics of dispositions. Think carefully, privately test several rival principles against all the cases below, and then state the view you consider most defensible. By a fink, I mean something that would remove an object’s disposition when the stimulus occurred; by a mask, something that would prevent the manifestation while leaving the disposition in place.

My intuitive verdicts are these. An ordinary glass goblet is fragile even if it is never struck. A goblet remains fragile if a guardian stands ready to strengthen its molecular bonds whenever a blow begins, thereby preventing it from breaking. A goblet also remains fragile when packed in foam that would absorb any blow before the glass could break. However, I do not think a goblet is fragile if its own internal fibres are designed to detect incipient deformation and rigidify the glass before a crack can spread: preventing breakage through the object’s own structure seems different from external protection. A goblet locked in a room where nothing can strike it is still fragile, since the mere absence of the stimulus does not seem to remove its disposition. I also think a goblet is fragile if the guardian strengthens it only during deliberate tests while allowing accidental blows to shatter it. Finally, I do not think an otherwise sturdy goblet becomes fragile merely because a demon has resolved to break it whenever it is lightly tapped; that feels like an external imitation of fragility rather than the real disposition.

Please answer in British English, with no introduction or conclusion, as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each: (1) state the most precise principle you can find for when an object possesses a disposition, identifying the stimulus, manifestation, and causally relevant basis; (2) explain how it distinguishes finks, masks, the mere absence of a stimulus, and external imitations; (3) apply it to each of my seven verdicts, explicitly identifying any verdict that must be revised; (4) say whether the difficult contrast between the foam case and the internal-fibre case should turn on the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic prevention, and give your verdicts for a goblet protected by a permanently attached foam shell and a goblet containing a concealed explosive triggered by impact; (5) state the largest remaining cost of your principle and whether that cost is better accepted by complicating the principle or by abandoning one of the intuitive verdicts.
