Context: in our section meetings the students mostly got hung up on the aggregation problem, i.e. whether contractualism can explain why you should save the five instead of the one, and whether it goes too far the other way and lets one person's death trump saving a thousand people from paralysis. So this was the essay question:

"Scanlon's contractualism is often said to have trouble explaining why the numbers should count. Explain the structure of Scanlon's account of wrongness and how it handles the comparison of complaints in rescue cases. Does the view permit the numbers to count, and if so, how? In your answer, discuss at least one case where the view appears to give the wrong verdict, and say how a contractualist might respond."

Here's the student's answer (retyped, so any typos are mine):

"Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. The test is contractual: we imagine everyone motivated to find principles they can justify to one another, and ask which principles would survive. Because the parties deliberate behind a veil of ignorance about which position they will occupy, each party gives equal weight to every standpoint, which is what makes the procedure impartial rather than a modus vivendi between people with unequal bargaining power.

The key mechanism in choice cases is the comparison of complaints. When principles conflict, we compare the complaint each person would have against a principle permitting the act with the complaints others would have against a principle forbidding it. Because each person's complaint is assessed individually — the individualist restriction — the view does not sum harms across persons. This is Scanlon's answer to utilitarian aggregation: any number of headaches cannot outweigh one death, because complaints are not commensurable in that additive way.

Applied to the standard rescue case — one person trapped on one side of the bay, five on the other, time to reach only one side — the view says to save the five, but not because five deaths sum to more badness than one. Rather, where the complaints on each side are equally strong (each person stands to lose her life), the complaint of the one is matched by the complaint of one of the five, and the remaining four then break the tie. So numbers do count, but only as tie-breakers among equally strong complaints.

This generates a well-known difficulty in mixed cases. If I can save one person from death or a thousand from paralysis, the individualist restriction seems to require saving the one: the dying person's complaint is the loss of her life, no paralyzed person has a complaint anywhere near that strong, and aggregation across the thousand is forbidden. Many find this result implausible. Scanlon's own response is to bite the bullet: since aggregation is never permitted, the one must be saved, and the contrary intuition is a residue of consequentialist thinking we should learn to distrust. A rival contractualist proposal instead allows sufficiently similar complaints to be pooled, so that the numbers can win when each individual's stake is grave enough.

A second problem is that of irrelevant utilities: the strongest-complaint procedure appears to let trivial considerations tip outcomes. If, in the five-versus-one case, saving the one would also satisfy some bystander's minor preference, that bystander's complaint seems able to break the tie toward the one, which looks wrong. Scanlon responds by restricting which complaints are relevant to the comparison, though critics argue the restriction is ad hoc.

Overall, contractualism replaces aggregation of welfare with comparison of individual complaints. The justifiability of the act to each person is what matters, not the total."
