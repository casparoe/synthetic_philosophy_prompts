I'm in a medieval philosophy seminar, and for a paper I've been reconstructing an argument from Ibn Rushd's Decisive Treatise. The problem is that my reconstruction seems to prove something I'm sure he didn't believe, and I can't locate the failure. Here it is:

(1) Demonstrative reasoning, when valid, yields truth, and scripture is truth, so scripture cannot genuinely contradict any demonstrated conclusion.

(2) When the apparent sense of a scriptural passage appears to conflict with a demonstrated conclusion, the passage is to be given a non-apparent interpretation, and there is always some admissible interpretation available.

(3) Such interpretation is to be undertaken only by those trained in demonstration, and its results are not to be communicated to the general public.

(4) Therefore no apparent scriptural statement can ever function as evidence against a demonstrated philosophical thesis, since any apparent conflict simply triggers reinterpretation of the statement.

(5) Therefore scripture is compatible with every demonstrable proposition whatsoever.

(6) But a text compatible with every proposition rules nothing out, and a text that rules nothing out conveys no information.

(7) So, for the very people permitted to interpret it, scripture has no content -- which makes Ibn Rushd's insistence that philosophers are obligated to interpret it, and that it is true throughout, either empty or dishonest.

The conclusion can't be right as a reading of him: he plainly thinks some scriptural commitments are fixed and not up for reinterpretation, and he criticizes the Ash'arites and al-Ghazali precisely for mishandling which passages admit of interpretation. But I can't see which numbered step does the damage. (4) looks like it just restates (1) and (2), and (6) looks like a platitude about information. If I had to keep something, I'd keep (1) and (3); I'd most willingly sacrifice (5) or (6).

Please identify which step or premise you reject, explain why it looked innocent, and say what rejecting it costs the rest of the argument. Where the scholarly terrain matters -- for instance, what Ibn Rushd actually held versus what Straussian readings attribute to him -- please separate what is broadly agreed among specialists from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
