So I work at a consumer protection agency and my team reviews complaints about subscription pricing — telecoms, gyms, software, that kind of thing. Lately we've been seeing a pattern that bugs me and I want a rigorous way to classify it. Roughly: a customer gets walked through a *sequence* of changes where each individual step looks fine to them at the time (add this feature for $3, swap that plan for this one plus $2, downgrade here, upgrade back there) but if you trace the whole history they've ended up basically where they started, minus a pile of money. My econ-adjacent coworkers call this money pumping and say it only works because the customer's preferences are intransitive or because they can't see the whole trajectory. But then our industry contacts say no, people's tastes just genuinely change over time and every swap was voluntary, and honestly sometimes that's true — someone adds international calling before a trip and drops it after, that's not exploitation.

I want you to turn the philosophical/economic idea behind money pump arguments into an actual procedure my analysts can run on a case file. Constraints: analysts have about 30 minutes per case, they are not philosophers or economists, whatever you give me has to produce a classification a lawyer can act on, and our legal team signs off before anything gets called a violation.

What I need:

1. A decision procedure — an ordered set of tests or a checklist, in words, that takes a customer's transaction history plus the company's offer terms and outputs one of: clear money-pump-style extraction, benign preference change / normal upselling, or can't-tell-needs-judgment. Be careful about what the theory actually requires: each step being individually acceptable by the customer's own lights, the sequence looping back to roughly the starting position, the agent not being able to foresee or resist the trajectory, and so on. Tell me which of those conditions can be checked from records and which can't.
2. The hard distinctions have to survive the simplification — especially genuine taste change vs. engineered cycles, and "customer made a dumb choice" vs. "customer was pumped." Tell me if any distinction I'd want in there actually has to be dropped because it's untestable in 30 minutes with paperwork.
3. Worked applications: run the procedure on (a) the classic case where a telecom cycles a customer through plan swaps that each cost a couple dollars and lands them on their original plan, (b) the travel-calling-feature case, and (c) one case where the procedure spits out an answer I won't like — either calling something exploitation that looks innocent, or clearing something that feels predatory — and explain why that's the right output anyway.
4. An honest list of what the procedure can't settle, i.e. the cases where an analyst has to just make a call, and what we should do procedurally with those (escalate? default to no violation?).

Last line should be a one-sentence summary of the whole thing starting with "Bottom line:".
