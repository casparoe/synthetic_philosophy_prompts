I am the designated physician for a small county cycling league, and next month the board will vote on whether to adopt a rule I drafted. A new prescription nasal spray helps some riders recover concentration after repeated minor head knocks; in those riders it reliably brings reaction time and error rates back to roughly where they were before the crashes. It also, however, improves the same measures in many uninjured riders by about as much, and a few parents now want healthy juniors to use it before time trials because everyone else seems to be doing it. Our insurer will reimburse only if I code it as treatment of an injury-related deficit, never as making a normal rider better, but the diagnostic threshold is a moving target: I can define normal relative to each rider’s own pre-crash baseline, relative to the league’s current distribution, relative to age-matched non-riders, or relative to last year’s juniors, who were slower before the spray spread. If I choose the rider’s own baseline, a naturally gifted rider who declines after concussion is owed restoration to a level most people could never reach, while a less gifted rider with identical symptoms gets less help. If I choose the league distribution, widespread voluntary use quietly redefines normal upward, so yesterday’s enhancement becomes tomorrow’s required treatment and nonusers become patients by comparison. If I choose non-riders, riders complain that their sport-specific capacities are real parts of their health; if I choose last year’s juniors, the rule seems to freeze an arbitrary moment just because it preceded the technology. My draft says the spray is permitted and covered when it repairs a documented loss caused by riding, prohibited during competition when it only lifts a rider above their own stable baseline, and reevaluated whenever more than a third of starters are using it. The board asks whether this line is principled or merely a reimbursement fiction dressed up as ethics. Advise me. Please identify the philosophical structure of the problem rather than giving generic sports-medicine cautions, say which parts of my rule track defensible distinctions and which parts smuggle in contestable norms about normal functioning, desert, fairness, positional advantage, authenticity, and consent under pressure, and recommend a rule I can defend in plain language to parents, insurers, and riders who feel coerced either way. Where the territory is genuinely disputed among careful people, mark it as disputed rather than presenting one side as settled consensus. Do not provide citations or a reading list. Write the answer as continuous prose only, like a considered email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
