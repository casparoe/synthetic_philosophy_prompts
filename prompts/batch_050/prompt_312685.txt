Last Saturday, over dinner that ran well past midnight, a close friend of mine — she trained as an economist, I as a philosopher — argued with me about sunk costs, and we parted without resolution. Her position was the orthodoxy she teaches: once a cost is sunk it is sunk, and letting it bear on any subsequent choice is a fallacy, full stop. I found myself defending the opposite thesis, which she dismissively called the Concorde fallacy with a philosophy degree. I want to know how exposed my position actually is.

My view, which I will call the commitment view, holds that honoring a sunk cost is instrumentally rational in a recognizable class of cases: (i) when the prior expenditure functions as a self-binding device against a temptation the agent knows her future self will face (finishing the difficult book one has paid for, because abandoning every difficult book would become a habit); (ii) when abandoning the course of action would transmit a costly signal to counterparties whose future cooperation the agent needs, so that persistence protects a reputation that mere forward-looking calculation would erode; and (iii) when the expenditure, though unrecoverable, has generated information or options whose value can only be realized by continuing. On my view, "ignore sunk costs" is a sound heuristic that has been overgeneralized into a theorem.

My friend's counter, pressed with some force, was that each of my cases can be redescribed as ordinary forward-looking reasoning — the signal, the habit, the information are all future benefits — so nothing genuinely honors the sunk cost, and my view collapses into the orthodoxy it claims to oppose. I replied that this redescription is always available, which makes the orthodox view unfalsifiable by stipulation rather than true. She replied, not unfairly, that my view is the unfalsifiable one: any stubborn continued investment can be redescribed post hoc as self-binding or signaling, so I have built a rationale that can rationalize anything.

Tell me what would count against the commitment view. Which empirical findings about escalation of commitment, which possible cases, which formal or decision-theoretic results would defeat it — and what does the view predict, in concrete advice, that the orthodox position does not? Distinguish defeaters that would be fatal from ones that would merely be awkward, say which of them a sincere proponent of the view would concede as counting against it (as opposed to what only critics would press), and address directly the charge that it is insulated by post-hoc redescription: if that charge sticks, is it a defect, and what observation, if any, does my view actually rule out? Please state how confident you are in your overall assessment, and single out the one point in it — including your severity rankings — that you are least sure of.

After your prose analysis, give me a structured summary as a single table with these columns, ordered from most to least threatening to the view: Defeater (a one-line name); Kind (empirical, conceptual, formal, or methodological); What would have to be true (the observation or result required); Severity (fatal, serious, or merely awkward); Defender's concession (whether a proponent would grant it counts, with a few words on why); Confidence (your confidence in that row's severity rating, as low, medium, or high).
