A colleague of mine took a course on the logic of cooperation years ago, read Hofstadter, and has described herself as a superrationalist ever since. I used to think I knew what she meant by that, but her verdicts on cases have made me unsure, and we now have a small bet riding on how she will judge two new ones. Throughout, the standard prisoner's dilemma payoffs are: 3 to each player if both cooperate, 1 to each if both defect, and if one defects while the other cooperates, 5 to the defector and 0 to the cooperator. Her verdicts so far:

1. A one-shot, anonymous, symmetric prisoner's dilemma against a stranger, with no communication: cooperate.
2. The same game against an exact copy of her own decision process: cooperate. She calls this the easiest case.
3. The same game against a man she knows for certain defects in every such game, as a matter of settled policy: defect.
4. The same game against a co-worker whose way of thinking she has observed for years and judges very similar to her own: cooperate.
5. A series of one-shot prisoner's dilemmas against a hundred different strangers, where no player learns of any other round and results are never disclosed: cooperate every time.
6. A stag hunt against a stranger: hunt the stag.
7. Newcomb's problem with a demonstrably reliable predictor: take one box.
8. Zero-sum games: no special reasoning at all; just play to win.

The two new cases:

Case A. A one-shot game against the co-worker from verdict 4, with asymmetric payoffs, where each knows the other well and knows that the other knows it. If both cooperate she gets 3 and he gets 30; if both defect she gets 2 and he gets 2; if she defects while he cooperates she gets 4 and he gets 1; if she cooperates while he defects she gets 1 and he gets 31. Defection strictly dominates for each player, but her choice swings his payoff by nearly thirty points while costing her at most one.

Case B. The standard symmetric prisoner's dilemma against a stranger who is famous for cooperating in every one-shot dilemma, who adopted that policy long ago for reasons having nothing to do with her, and who knows nothing about whom he is playing. His cooperation is, as she likes to say in other contexts, already in the ledger.

Please do the following. First, lay out the rules she might implicitly be running. I can think of at least four candidates — reasoning keyed to the symmetry of the situation; reasoning keyed to the correlation between her choice and the other player's; reciprocity keyed to the other player's cooperative disposition; and maximizing the combined payoff — but she may be running none of these cleanly. For each candidate you take seriously, show it is consistent with all eight verdicts, or identify the verdict that rules it out. Second, for each surviving rule, state what it predicts in Case A and in Case B; where a rule is genuinely silent on a case rather than merely awkward, say so rather than forcing a prediction. Third, give your best guess as to which rule is actually hers, predict her verdicts on both cases, and defend the guess from the verdicts themselves, including her remark that the copy is the easiest case and anything else you find probative. Finally, describe the single further case that would best distinguish your favored rule from its closest rival, and state what each rule would predict about it.
