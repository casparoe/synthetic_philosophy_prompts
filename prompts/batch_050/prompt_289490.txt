I'm prepping my grad seminar on the evolution of cognition and once again the centerpiece is Dennett's little tower of generate-and-test -- Darwinian creatures, Skinnerian creatures, Popperian creatures, Gregorian creatures -- mostly because in twenty-five years nobody has handed me a better taxonomy to teach, which is starting to feel like an embarassment for the field. So before seminar 3 I want to commission, from you, the spec: what would ANY taxonomy of kinds of minds have to do to be worth replacing Dennett's with? Give me the full desiderata list, with the case or consideration behind each item. Some things I think it has to handle, but correct me or add to these: it must get the easy cases right (E. coli chemotaxis, the Sphex wasp's stereotyped loop, Skinner's pigeons, an animal that lets its hypotheses die in its stead, a human sharing tools via culture) without the interesting cases breaking it (a crow bending wire, an octopus, an LLM -- is an LLM Skinnerian, Gregorian, or does it show the categories were never natural kinds?). It must respect distinctions I care about: phylogenetic sequence vs. position in design space, behavioral flexibility vs. the mechanism producing it, having an inner model vs. merely being well-described by us as having one. Flag any desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable -- that's the part I'll grade hardest -- and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most taxonomies people have actually proposed. One half-formed hunch you can engage or ignore: I've never been convinced Popperian and Gregorian form rungs on a ladder rather than independent axes, since tool culture without inner simulation and inner simulation without culture both seem instantiated in nature, which would make the tower a cube or something messier. Briefly score Dennett's ladder and one alternative (the independent-axes reading or any rival you like) against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
