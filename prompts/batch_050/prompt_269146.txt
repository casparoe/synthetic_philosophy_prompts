For a paper I am writing, I need to take a stand on whether forgiveness can ever be morally obligatory, and I want the reasoning forced into the open rather than a verdict followed by a gesture at the literature. Here is the target proposition, stated precisely: there are genuinely possible circumstances in which a victim owes forgiveness to a wrongdoer — circumstances where withholding forgiveness would be wrong of the victim (not merely ungenerous), and where blame for withholding it would be fitting. Call this proposition O. Give me your credence in O, but the number matters less to me than its derivation, so please structure the answer as follows.

First, decompose O into the sub-questions on which it depends, assigning each a rough probability of its own. At minimum, address these four:

1. Controllability. If forgiveness essentially involves relinquishing resentment, and emotions are not under direct voluntary control, then the principle that ought implies can threatens O. If forgiveness is instead, or also, an act or commitment — a pledge to stop treating the past wrong as a reason for continued hostility — obligation looks far more coherent. How likely is it that the best account of what forgiveness is makes it sufficiently action-like to be the kind of thing that can be owed at all?

2. The electiveness intuition. Many people feel that owed forgiveness is as confused as owed gratitude or owed love: forgiveness is a gift, and a gift demanded is spoiled. Is this a genuine datum that any adequate theory must accommodate, or a prejudice produced by conflating forgiveness with the warm feelings that sometimes accompany it? How likely is the intuition to survive scrutiny?

3. Conditionalism and reduction. The most plausible obligation cases seem to be ones where the wrongdoer has repented and apologized — but then perhaps what is actually owed is only the acceptance of a sincere apology, or the cessation of punishment and cold treatment, and calling it forgiveness adds nothing. How likely is it that every apparent obligation to forgive reduces to some other, less controversial duty?

4. Unconditionality. If O is true, must there also be cases in which forgiveness is owed to the unrepentant, or does O stand or fall with the conditional cases? Separately, assess whether unconditional forgiveness is even coherent, or whether forgiving someone who has neither repented nor apologized collapses into condonation — treating the wrong as not so bad after all.

Then state how these sub-probabilities combine into your credence for O. They will not be independent, so say where the dependencies are (for instance, whether your answer to 1 drives your answers to 2 and 4). Identify the single sub-question to which the final number is most sensitive, and name the one consideration or argument that would move your credence the most in either direction. For calibration: my own credence in O is about 0.25, and a coauthor's is about 0.75; given your decomposition, say where our disagreement most likely lives.

Commit throughout: I do not want a survey of positions or a menu to choose from. Where philosophers disagree, pick a side and defend the pick, including on the contested points above. End your answer with your final credence in O as \answer{...} containing a single number between 0 and 1.
