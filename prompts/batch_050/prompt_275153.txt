I recently learned about Good's theorem -- roughly, that for an expected utility maximizer, gathering free information before making a decision can never hurt you in expectation, and strictly helps whenever the info could change what you end up doing. I feel like I've argued myself into a corner with it:

(1) If information is free and might bear on some decision you'll face, taking it never lowers your expected utility. (this is just the theorem)
(2) If the information turns out to be useless, you can always just ignore it, so the worst case is that you're exactly where you started.
(3) So it's always at least as good to take free information as to refuse it.
(4) So, monetary costs aside, you should always take the free genetic test, always read the spoiler before watching, always find out whether the mole is malignant the moment testing is cheap, and never pay even a dollar to avoid learning something.
(5) But (4) is nuts. Spoiling every movie you ever watch isn't a requirement of rationality, and people who decline Huntington's testing aren't all making a mistake. A descision theory that condemns every one of them has gone wrong, not them.
(6) So either the theorem is false or some step between (1) and (4) doesn't follow.

I doubt it's (1), since that's the actual math. My hunch is that (2) is smuggling something, but I can't pin down what. Which step do you reject, why does it look fine at first, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to keep two things it's (1) and (5) -- tell me what I'm paying for them.
