I write a science-and-ideas column for a general interest magazine, and I keep running into the same move at the forecasting and rationality-adjacent events I cover. Two people will be deep in an argument -- last month it was AI researchers disagreeing about whether scaling alone gets you to AGI -- and a third person jumps in with "rational agents can't agree to disagree," as if that settles it. Sometimes the addendum is "so one of you is being irrational," sometimes it's "so just average your credences and move on." There's apparently an actual theorem behind this (Aumann? seventies?), but the way it gets used feels way stronger than any theorem could be.

Can you unpackage the slogan for me? I want a menu of the distinct precise claims it could be making, ordered from platitude to radical, with a verdict on each -- trivially true, substantive and defensible, or just false. Some readings I can already half-see: (a) persistent public disagreement is evidence that at least one person is updating badly; (b) two perfect Bayesians who share a prior and whose posteriors are common knowledge literally cannot end up with different numbers; (c) whenever you disagree with an epistemic peer you ought to shift some way toward their view; (d) any disagreement that survives full honest discussion proves someone is irrational or lying. Carve it differently if my carve-up is wrong.

Then the part I actually need for the column: which of those readings is the real theorem, and which reading, if any, supports the conversational uses I described -- ending the AI argument, the "someone's irrational" jab, the "average and move on" advice. If the theorem needs shared priors and common knowledge of posteriors and neither debater has either, does the slogan do any work at all in real disputes, or is it just a way of calling the other guy irrational with math-flavored authority? Should I let sources get away with it in print?
