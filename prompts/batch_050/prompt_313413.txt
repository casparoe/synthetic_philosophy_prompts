I am a novelist, and for the past two years I have also run a small subscription scheme that sends members one book and one film each month, chosen by me. The scheme sells itself, somewhat loftily, on offering work that resists rather than merely entertains, and I have come to realise that I make these choices by instinct dressed up as principle. Around the same time I finally read the culture industry chapter of Adorno and Horkheimer's Dialectic of Enlightenment, and while I am wary of turning a dense piece of critical theory into a managerial tool, I think their central distinctions could genuinely discipline my selections: the difference between art that confronts the audience with something the world as it is cannot accommodate, and product that only appears to do so; standardisation concealed beneath surface novelty (what they call pseudo-individuality); amusement that functions as a continuation of work rather than an escape from it; and the way critique itself gets absorbed and sold back as a style. What I want is not a summary of the chapter, which I have read, but help translating it into a procedure I can actually run.

Concretely: I choose twelve books and twelve films a year, I spend perhaps three evenings with each candidate before deciding, and the stakes are modest but real, since members pay and occasionally complain. I would like a decision procedure in words -- a sequence of tests or questions I can work through with a novel or film in front of me -- that keeps the distinctions that matter and discards what cannot survive contact with a Tuesday evening. It should tell me what to look at first and why that has to come first, which of the theory's contrasts (standardisation versus genuine form, pseudo-individuality versus real particularity, reconciliation versus negation) can actually be operationalised by one reader and which cannot, and where the honest answer is that I am making a judgement call rather than applying a test. It must handle the hard cases rather than the easy ones: a formally daring work that is emotionally hollow; a thoroughly conventional genre piece that nonetheless leaves the reader changed; a work that attacks contemporary society in exactly the terms its target audience already enjoys hearing; and something I simply loved, where I cannot tell whether I loved it because it resisted me or because it flattered me.

Please apply the procedure to two or three hypothetical candidates, one of which should produce a verdict I will dislike -- a case where the procedure condemns a book I would instinctively have chosen, or clears one I would have scorned -- and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle at all. Two failure modes I want guarded against from the outset: a checklist that quietly reduces to "difficult good, pleasurable bad", which is a vulgarisation of the theory and not what Adorno and Horkheimer say; and a checklist so hedged that it ratifies whatever I already wanted to pick. If the procedure is any good, it should occasionally overrule me, and I would like to know in advance how I will recognise those occasions.
