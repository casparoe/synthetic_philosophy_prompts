I run the standards-and-practices desk at a mid-size streaming service, and roughly once a month I have to rule on a docudrama or biopic script at the greenlight stage: a writer wants to dramatize the life of a real, deceased person, and my job is to say which departures from the record are acceptable and which are not. We have no staff historian, legal review happens after my ruling and only covers defamation of the living, and I typically have a few days per script. I want to stop improvising.

Please write me a decision procedure I can apply to any script of this kind. It should be grounded in the philosophical question of what, if anything, is owed to the dead as opposed to the living, and it should build on -- and, where you see fit, refine or replace -- the following working distinctions I have been using:

1. Decorative compression versus load-bearing invention. Some changes merely make the story tellable (merging two minor characters, reordering events everyone agrees happened); others do argumentative work -- the invented scene is evidence for the interpretation of the person the film is selling. My intuition is that the second kind needs far more justification, but I have no test for telling them apart in borderline cases.
2. Three different victims. A portrayal might wrong the dead person themselves (if posthumous harm is even coherent), wrong living survivors and descendants, and wrong the audience by implanting false beliefs that will outlive the closing disclaimer. These pull apart: a film can be kind to its subject and cruel to the truth, or scrupulously accurate and devastating to a surviving family. I currently have no rule for which of these takes priority when they conflict.
3. The evidence asymmetry problem. Audiences can fact-check a portrayal of a famous statesman; almost no one will check a portrayal of an obscure private citizen destroyed by one film. It seems perverse that obscurity should license greater invention, yet that is what my current practice amounts to.

The procedure should specify: the questions to ask about a script, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; at which points the procedure simply returns "no" without further deliberation; and at which points it must hand the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than of the record. Where my distinctions above break down, say so and repair them rather than preserving them for my sake.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

- A limited series about a recently deceased, much-loved musician includes an invented scene implying a hidden addiction, which the estate denies and no source supports; the scene is what makes the finale's tragedy land.
- A film about a monarch dead for four centuries attributes to them a specific crime that some serious historians have argued for but most reject, because the screenwriter believes it is probably true.
- A biopic of a 20th-century scientist compresses two real collaborators into one, in the process erasing a woman whose contribution is documented but little known.

Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do: which of my monthly rulings will remain judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and at least one case type where following it faithfully will produce an answer I will not like -- either clearing a portrayal my viewers will find repugnant, or blocking one my executives have already sold.
