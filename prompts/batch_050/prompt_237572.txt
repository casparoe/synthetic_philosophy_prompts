Next month I'm presenting to our cognitive science reading group a thesis I've been circling for years, ever since I first encountered Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project: that what makes every 'a' an 'a' is not shape but role. My claim, precisely: a glyph is an 'a' if and only if it occupies the 'a'-slot of a functioning alphabetic system, where the slot is fixed jointly by the letter's orthographic function and by its stylistic coherence with the font's other glyphs. A font, on my view, is a coherent set of answers to a fixed set of stylistic questions, and a font's 'a' is constrained by every other glyph in it — the other letters of a strange font predict its 'a' better than the 'a's of other fonts do. Shape-similarity to stored exemplars is therefore neither necessary nor sufficient: a single-story 'a' and a double-story 'a' share less geometry than a long s (ſ) shares with an f, yet the first pair is one letter and the second is two; Latin H and Greek capital eta are one shape and two letters. Letters are like chess pieces — a knight carved as an elephant is still a knight, because knight-hood is constituted by the moves.

The audience: about a dozen people in three factions. Two philosophers — one a thoroughgoing Wittgensteinian about concepts who thinks family resemblance dissolves all essence-talk, one an empiricist who thinks prototypes plus context handle every categorization phenomenon. Two vision-lab machine learning researchers who believe a trained classifier settles the matter and that talk of roles and letter-spirits is pre-computational nostalgia. And one practicing type designer, quiet but lethal, who has drawn hundreds of 'a's and is allergic to theories of her craft. The group skews heavily toward longtime readers of GEB and the rationalist blogs, so strawmen get punished on sight; I need the objections at full strength, voiced the way each faction would actually voice them, not the encyclopedia versions.

Prepare me with these sections, in this order:

1. Who's in the room (~120 words) — what each faction cares about and which of them is actually dangerous to this thesis.
2. The objections, in the order they'll come (~1,200 words) — six to eight of them, each with: the objection phrased as that faction would phrase it, the real force behind it, my two-minute reply, and where the reply is soft.
3. Concede early and cheap (~150 words) — what I should give away up front.
4. Lines I must hold (~150 words) — the commitments the thesis cannot survive losing, including its weakest load-bearing point.
5. The question I'm hoping nobody asks (~250 words) — name it, explain why it's the one, and give me the best available damage control.
