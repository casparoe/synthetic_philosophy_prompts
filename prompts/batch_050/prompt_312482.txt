I design tabletop games, and last month one of my designs was disqualified from an annual competition on the grounds that it was, in the judges' words, not really a game. It is a cooperative, turnless activity with no scoring and no win condition: players collectively maintain a fictional ecosystem and the exercise simply ends when the group agrees to stop. I appealed, arguing that the competition rules nowhere define "game," and the judges replied that no definition is needed because everyone knows what a game is and mine is not one. In reading around the dispute I came across section 68 of Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations, where he considers the suggestion that the concept of a game might be treated as the logical sum of a set of sharply defined sub-concepts, rejects it, asks how the concept of a game is bounded and what still counts as a game and what no longer does, and answers that no boundary can be given, even though we nonetheless use the concept without one. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers, Helena and Marcus, who take opposing sides on my appeal. Helena defends a broadly Wittgensteinian position: the judges' ruling fails because the concept of a game has no essence or boundary to which my design could be shown not to conform, and competent use of the word requires neither. Marcus opposes her without caricaturing Wittgenstein: he argues that the absence of a statable definition does not preclude principled boundary-drawing, that resemblance to paradigm games is a matter of degree on which rational judgment is possible, and that a competition, like a court or a customs authority, may legitimately impose a working boundary even on a family-resemblance concept. I want them to engage each other's actual arguments rather than restate their positions: Helena should press Marcus on whether his imposed boundary is anything more than convention dressed as discovery, and Marcus should press Helena on whether her view leaves any room at all for the judges' ruling to be wrong rather than merely disagreeable, and hence whether it can actually help my appeal. They should also disagree explicitly about what the passage from section 68 establishes: whether it merely reports how we happen to use the word or licenses the stronger conclusion that boundary claims about "game" are confused. Have them apply the dispute to my specific design rather than to chess or football, and let each concede at least one genuine point. Write the dialogue as continuous prose, each turn beginning with the speaker's name followed by a colon, roughly twelve hundred words in total, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables anywhere in the response.
