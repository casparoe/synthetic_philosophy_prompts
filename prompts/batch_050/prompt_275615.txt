Last weekend I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about the history of ordinary people. My position was that a real history from below is impossible: since nearly everything that survives on paper was produced by states, churches, courts, and tax collectors, the common people of the past appear in the record only at the moments they collide with those institutions, and any account built from such traces is deformed at its root. My friend held that microhistory answers this — that one anomalously dense document, a trial record or a long deposition, can crack open an entire mental world precisely because the interrogation forced the voiceless to speak. We went home unpersuaded. Next month I teach an undergraduate historiography seminar — 18 students, one 75-minute session — and I want to stage the argument rather than recount it.

Design an in-class activity that makes students feel both sides of this dispute before either side is named. The shape I have in mind is a source-based reconstruction exercise: teams receive different packets of fabricated documents about the same small community and the same event, and must write up what happened and what life there was like. I will fabricate the sources myself, so tell me exactly what each packet should contain and how it should be rigged — which packets should consist of elite institutional records (rolls, decrees, inspections) in which ordinary people figure as numbers or trouble, and which should contain one rich but compromised document in which a common person's voice comes through only under pressure, filtered through a clerk and a questioner. Specify the sequence of phases, the instructions each team gets, and how the groups should be made to compare their reconstructions so that the asymmetry of the archive becomes visible to them on its own: students should catch themselves mistaking silence for absence, or mistaking a coerced confession for transparent testimony, before I ever say so. Include a debrief that maps what happened onto the dispute between the history of elites and states on one side and microhistory on the other, including the point that the documents were not lost at random but were produced and kept for reasons. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. And be frank about which feature of the real historiographical problem — for instance, that the actual past cannot talk back and correct a misreading — a classroom simulation cannot reproduce, so that I know what I must supply in discussion.
