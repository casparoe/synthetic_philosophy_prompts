I teach a first-year module on minds, machines and transhumanism, and one of my colleagues in computer science puts together a little handout for the students each year. This year's version has a paragraph on Nick Bostrom's simulation argument that reads like this: "Bostrom proved that we are almost certainly living inside a computer simulation. His reasoning is simple: any civilization that survives long enough will inevitably want to run huge numbers of ancestor-simulations, so simulated minds will vastly outnumber flesh-and-blood ones, and plain statistics then says any given mind, yours included, is far more likely to be simulated than real. The argument rests on the obvious fact that consciousness is just computation, so a simulated brain is conscious in exactly the way ours is. Bostrom himself concludes that we are simulated beings created by our own descendants."

Now, I've actually read the original paper ("Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?", 2003), or at least I think I have, and something about the handout grates on me. Here's Bostrom in his own words, from the introductory section: "Suppose that these simulated people are conscious (as they would be if the simulations were sufficiently fine-grained and if a certain quite widely accepted position in the philosophy of mind is correct). Then it could be the case that the vast majority of minds like ours do not belong to the original race but rather to people simulated by the advanced descendants of an original race." The hedging there — "could be the case," and the whole thing explicitly dangling off an assumption imported from philosophy of mind — seems to sit uneasily with the handout's tone of "proof" and "obvious fact."

So: go through the handout's summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts are accurate, which parts flatten something Bostrom is careful about, and which parts he would flatly object to. In particular, does the passage I quoted support the handout's reading or undermine it — what exactly is the structure of the conclusion he argues for, and how much weight does the substrate-independence assumption bear? Also tell me whether my instinct that something is off is well-placed or whether I'm being a pedant about a harmless simplification.
