In the third chapter of On Liberty (1859), "Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being," John Stuart Mill writes:

"Where, not the person's own character, but the traditions or customs of other people are the rule of conduct, there is wanting one of the principal ingredients of human happiness, and quite the chief ingredient of individual and social progress."

Later in the same chapter Mill makes the stakes concrete by holding up China as his cautionary case: a civilization he takes to have perfected the machinery for pressing every mind into the same maxims, and to have been frozen in place ever since. I want you to take that Millian framework — custom as the enemy of spontaneity, uniformity as the enemy of progress, and the harm principle as the boundary between what society may and may not police — and use it to analyze the China of our own century: the social credit system. Take these as the salient facts, and flag any you think I have wrong: a 2014 State Council planning outline proposed a national system for assessing "trustworthiness"; what actually exists is not a single unified score but a patchwork of blacklists and "red lists," most prominently the Supreme People's Court list of judgment defaulters, linked through inter-agency "joint punishment" agreements to consequences such as exclusion from air travel and high-speed rail; much of it aggregates and enforces obligations that were already on the books, and Western reporting has often exaggerated the omniscient single-score version.

Do four things. First, interpret the passage: what exactly does Mill claim is lost when custom rather than one's own character supplies the rule of conduct — happiness, progress, or both — and by what mechanism does the loss occur? Second, run the mapping in detail. Mill's despotism of custom is organic, bottom-up, and aimed at making everyone alike; the social credit system is engineered, top-down, largely enforces legal duties (debts, court orders) rather than moral opinion, and works by differentiating citizens rather than homogenizing them. Which of these features places the system squarely within Mill's target, and which push it outside? Third, using Mill's own distinctions — self-regarding conduct, conduct harmful to others, opinion, and his proviso that expression may be restricted when it amounts to instigation — sort the system's components (defaulter blacklists, travel restrictions, red lists, proposals to score everyday behavior) into what his principle would permit, what it would condemn, and what it leaves silent on; a Millian case that blacklisting defaulters is legitimate harm-prevention is available, and I want to know whether it survives scrutiny. Fourth, reverse the direction: does the episode confirm Mill's warning, or does it expose what the passage cannot see — for instance, that the modern danger is not a people made all alike but a state that governs each person through a personalized dossier? And if Mill, whose framework polices coercion rather than knowledge, cannot say what is wrong with the dossier itself, does condemning the system require going beyond him to something like the German constitutional doctrine of informational self-determination? Tell me, in the end, whether the passage diagnoses the social credit system, misdiagnoses it, or reveals that Mill's worry was always about a different pathology.
