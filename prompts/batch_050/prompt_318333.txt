I teach a section of an introductory genetics-and-society course, and at some point every semester a student deploys the line "genes load the gun, but the environment pulls the trigger" — usually to wrap up a discussion of heritability estimates for something like IQ, depression, or body weight, as though the slogan itself resolved whatever question was on the table. Last week a student used it to argue that since a trait is highly heritable but also obviously environment-sensitive, twin-study heritability numbers "don't really tell us anything." I'm fairly sure that conclusion doesn't follow, but I also suspect the slogan is doing at least three different jobs depending on who's saying it, and I want to be able to show my students the seams.

Please take the slogan apart. Lay out the distinct, precise claims it could be making about the relationship between heritability and environmental malleability — for instance, readings about variance decomposition within a population versus claims about what would happen under interventions, readings about where causal "blame" for an outcome sits, and readings that conflate high heritability with fixedness (or carefully avoid doing so). For each reading, say whether it's a truism, a substantive and defensible empirical claim, a claim that's true only relative to a particular population and range of environments, or something that's actually false, and give a concrete example trait where the reading succeeds or breaks down.

Then do the part I most need: assess whether any of these readings actually supports my student's conclusion that a high heritability estimate is uninformative once you grant that environments matter, and tell me which reading the slogan's users most often have in mind when they deploy it in public arguments about education or health policy. I'd like the output as an annotated menu of readings I can adapt for my students, not a single exegesis of the one true meaning of the metaphor.
