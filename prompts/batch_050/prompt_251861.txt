Over dinner last week I watched two colleagues — Dev, a philosopher of language, and Lena, a logician — argue past midnight about how to think about borderline cases. It started with a trivial trigger (a brewery's offer of a free pint to anyone "tall," and a patron nobody could classify), but it quickly became a general fight about vagueness, and I'd like a careful verdict.

Dev's opening position was unabashedly epistemicist: vague predicates have exact boundaries, and borderline cases are simply cases where we are ignorant of which side of the boundary the thing falls on. Calling the borderline patron "neither tall nor not tall," he said, is just refusing to accept instances of classical logic we have no good reason to refuse. Lena replied that nothing so drastic is needed: on her view, a vague claim is true if it comes out true on every legitimate sharpening of the language and false if it comes out false on every one, and the borderline claims are simply those that do neither. Classical logic survives intact, she insisted, since "either the patron is tall or he isn't" holds on every sharpening; what lapses is only bivalence for the particular borderline sentence. Her charge against Dev was that nothing in our linguistic practice could possibly fix a boundary at exactly one height rather than a millimetre over, so his view makes meaning answer to facts no speaker could ever have access to.

Dev answered that this argument assumes meaning must be scrutable to its users, which we already know is false: most of us successfully refer to elms without being able to tell an elm from a beech. He then pressed two points he clearly thought decisive. First, on Lena's account the claim that there exists a precise height at which tallness begins comes out true — true on every sharpening — even though no particular height makes it true, and he found a truth with no true witness intolerable. Second, her framework merely relocates the boundary, since "definitely tall" has borderline cases of its own. Lena conceded the first was a genuine cost but called it the price of treating vagueness as semantic indecision rather than ignorance, and on the second she said higher-order vagueness embarrasses everyone equally, including Dev, who owes us an account of why our ignorance of the boundary is so peculiarly incurable — a fact about our own words that no conceivable inquiry could reveal. Dev's last word was that an extravagant posit beats a contradiction; hers was that there is no contradiction, only an unfamiliar semantics.

Neither convinced the other, and I couldn't settle it either. Please adjudicate: tell me who is right about each specific move — the excluded middle point, the scrutability-of-meaning argument, the truth-without-a-true-instance objection, and the higher-order vagueness standoff — and whether both are missing anything, such as assumptions they share that a third party might reject. Where the literature on vagueness has a genuinely settled verdict on one of these moves, say so, but where philosophers who work on this remain divided, please flag the point as contested rather than presenting either my friend's position as the consensus.
