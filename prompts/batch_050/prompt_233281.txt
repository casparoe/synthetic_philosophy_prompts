Two classmates of mine had an argument after our history teacher cancelled the whole year group's end-of-term trip because three unidentified students had vandalised the common room, and I'd like an outside verdict. Maya defended the decision: she said a year group is a real community, not just a list of individuals, that shared punishment creates the peer pressure that actually stops vandalism, and that anyone who enjoys the group's privileges can fairly share its costs. Tom replied that punishment is only just if it lands on the person who chose the act, that pressuring innocents to inform on their friends treats people as instruments of the group rather than members of it, and that no court in this country would convict someone for their classmates' crime. Maya answered that Tom was smuggling in a courtroom standard that even the West doesn't live by, since families, teams and whole nations are routinely held together by shared blame and shared honour, and that his 'choose the act' principle would dissolve armies and families overnight. Tom said those cases rest on voluntary membership in a way being born into a year group doesn't, but conceded that he'd still feel personally ashamed if his own family behaved disgracefully, which Maya called his position collapsing in on itself. Can you go through their exchange round by round and say who is right about each specific point, including whether Tom's concession actually damages his case as much as Maya claimed, and whether either of them is missing something about what a person owes to a community they never chose? Please answer in British English.
