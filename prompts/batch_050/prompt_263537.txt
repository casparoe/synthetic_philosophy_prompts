Here's a decision theory construction exercise. Build me a thought experiment in which two agents manage to pull off an acausal trade — each does something costly that benefits the other, with nothing causal coming back in return — but under these constraints:

- They're ordinary humans (or small human-run outfits), not AIs, not copies of each other, and not simulations. Neither can inspect the other's source code or anything remotely like it.
- No communication, no trusted third party, no reputation or repeat play: it's one-shot, anonymous, and each knows the other will never learn for certain what they did.
- Each agent's grip on the other's psychology is no better than what a perceptive human could manage — some evidence about the other's circumstances and general cast of mind, but nothing like a predictor.
- The stakes should be mundane enough that a normal person could plausibly face them. No galaxy-scale payoffs, no utilons, no exotic physics.

The hard part: the case has to be genuine acausal trade and not just a symmetric coordination problem cracked by salience or by superrationality among near-identical reasoners. So include a diagnostic — describe one modification of your scenario under which the coordination/salience solution breaks but the acausal trade should still go through, or, if you can't find one, admit that your case collapses into a coordination game and say why.

After the scenario: walk through each agent's reasoning step by step, name the structural feature doing the work (logical correlation, shared decision procedure, something else?), and say what breaks if you strip that feature out. Then, as a final twist, give the smallest change to your setup that turns the exact same pattern of reasoning into acausal extortion rather than trade — and tell me wether there's a principled line between the two, or wether "extortion" is just trade we happen to disapprove of.
