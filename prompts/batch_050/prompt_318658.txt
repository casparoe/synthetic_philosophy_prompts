I am a PhD candidate in the history of modern philosophy, and next month I am presenting a work-in-progress paper to my department's early modern reading group. I would like help preparing for the roughly forty minutes of discussion that will follow the talk. The paper defends a deflationary reading of Hume's two definitions of cause in Section 7 of the first Enquiry. My thesis is that the two definitions are not rival accounts of a single concept but answer different questions: the first treats cause as a philosophical relation, fit for reasoning and inference; the second treats it as a natural relation, describing the habitual transition of the mind that actually constitutes our causal thinking. Once this division of labor is recognized, I argue, the New Hume debate largely dissolves. Regularity theorists and skeptical realists have been arguing about what Hume believed concerning objective necessary connection, whereas the definitions themselves settle only what we can legitimately mean by "cause" and how we in fact come to think in causal terms. On my reading, Hume can consistently suppose that nature has ultimate principles unknown and perhaps unknowable to us, while denying that any such supposition enters into, or is needed for, the content of our causal judgments.

The audience will be about a dozen people, and three of them concern me. First, a senior graduate student committed to a skeptical realist reading along Strawsonian lines, who has thought carefully about whether appeal to relative ideas can reconcile talk of unknown causal powers with Hume's theory of ideas, and who knows the relevant passages in the Treatise and the Enquiry at least as well as I do. Second, a postdoc working on expressivism and quasi-realism who reads Hume's account of the internal impression of the mind's determination as an ancestor of projectivism about modality, and who is inclined to argue that the realism question is one Hume's own machinery renders unaskable — a conclusion that would cut against my paper from the opposite direction. Third, a historically minded faculty member whose expertise is seventeenth-century Cartesianism, particularly Malebranche and occasionalism, and who is likely to press me on whether my reading makes Hume's position on necessary connection either derivative of his occasionalist predecessors or needlessly adversarial toward them. The remainder of the room will be sympathetic generalists without a settled position.

Please prepare me as follows. Give me the objections in the order they are most likely to arise in the discussion, ordered by a combination of how probable each is and how damaging it would be to my thesis if left unanswered. Phrase each objection as that particular audience member would actually phrase it, including the textual or historical hooks they would reach for. For each objection, tell me: what I should concede outright and why conceding it costs me little; what I should hold and how to hold it convincingly in under a minute; and the weakest point in my own reply — the place where a follow-up question would do the most damage. Finally, identify the single objection I should be most afraid of: the one that exposes a genuine tension in my position rather than a mere gap in my preparation, and sketch the most honest concession I could offer in response without abandoning the thesis altogether.
