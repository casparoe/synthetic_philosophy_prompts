Alright, here's something that's been nagging at me. I'm a retired materials chemist who's spent the last couple of years working my way through moral philosophy, and I've just finished a term reading about the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism with a small reading group (three of us, all amateurs). What strikes me is that this debate seems genuinely stuck, and has been for decades. Rawls made the complaint, Nozick sharpened it, and since then it feels like both sides just keep restating their opening position in slightly fancier dress. The utilitarians say: of course we aggregate, it's the same mathematical operation whether it's across one life or many, and refusing to trade one person's small harm against another's great benefit is irrational. The objectors say: you've missed the point entirely, there's no subject who enjoys the sum, so the sum isn't good for anyone, and aggregation across persons is a category error. Round and round.

Here's my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement isn't really about aggregation at all. I suspect it's two groups answering different questions under one heading. The utilitarians are asking what an impartial decision procedure should output; the objectors are asking whether "the overall good" names anything real in the first place. So every utilitarian argument about rationality slides past every objection about metaphysics, and vice versa. A colleague of mine (a physicist, also retired) thinks it's simpler than that — that the objectors are just refusing to accept that some quantities are additive when the underlying things aren't, like confusing intensive and extensive properties, if you'll forgive the chemistry. Maybe he's right and the whole thing is a muddle about what "adding up welfare" even means.

So: diagnose this debate for me. What kind of disagreement is it actually — verbal, metaphysical, a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or a hidden formal question about what aggregation commits you to? Use what each side actually says rather than what they'd like to be saying.
