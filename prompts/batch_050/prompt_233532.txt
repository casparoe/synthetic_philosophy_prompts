So I'm in a modal metaphysics seminar and we just finished Lewis's counterpart theory vs. Kripke-style transworld identity, and my prof keeps saying the two views "mostly agree on cases" -- like for ordinary de re modal claims (I could have been a dentist, Humphrey could have won the election) both frameworks deliver the same verdict, just with different machinery doing the work. I want to stress-test that claim for my final paper.

Construct a thought experiment where the two views genuinely come apart on a verdict about what some particular person could have been or done, and where counterpart theory is the one that gives the less intuitive verdict. Constraints:

- The scenario has to be everyday-ish: no mad scientists, no teleporters, no body-swapping machines. Any weirdness should come from the modal machinery, not from the setup.
- One paragraph max for the scenario itself.
- The divergence has to fall out of the actual mechanics -- the resemblance-based counterpart relation on one side vs. stipulative cross-world identity on the other -- not from one side being caricatured. If a fair-minded Lewisian could reasonably say "that's not what the theory says," the case fails.
- Bonus points if you can work in a role-swap element (two people whose whole lives could have been exchanged between them), because that's where I suspect the trouble lives, but only if it isn't cheating.

Then do the checks:

1. State the modal claim at issue precisely, run it through each theory seperately, and give each one's verdict with the reasoning.
2. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to both verdicts if you remove it.
3. Steelman the counterpart theorist's best reply (I'm guessing something about flexible or multiple counterpart relations) and say what, if anything, that reply costs the theory.
4. If some nearby position (haecceitism, or a primitive-identity view, or whatever you think belongs) also passes or fails on your case, note it in a sentence or two.

I'm using this to argue that the "mostly agrees" line is only true for a certain class of cases, so please be honest about whether your thought experiment generalizes or is a one-off. Finish with a last line in the form \answer{...} containing the name of whichever theory you think the case damages more.
