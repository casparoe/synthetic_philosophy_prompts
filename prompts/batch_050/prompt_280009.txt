I have been following debates about asylum for a while and I keep noticing the same odd arrangement, and I would like to know whether it has a name. Governments seem to accept that once a person fleeing persecution has actually reached their territory or border, they must not send that person back into danger. Yet the same governments insist they owe nothing to people still abroad: no duty to admit them, no duty to take some share of those stranded in camps in neighboring countries, and often real effort spent preventing them from ever arriving, through visa rules, fines on airlines and shipping companies, patrols at sea, and deals with transit states. The result is that two people with identical claims to protection can be treated completely differently depending only on who managed the journey, which in practice favors people who are nearby, young, healthy, or able to pay smugglers, while a vetted family flown in through a resettlement program is welcomed and an identical family arriving by boat is turned away. The duty seems to attach to the arrival rather than to the need. Is there an established term for this arrangement, or for the gap between the two duties it rests on? If several nearby concepts exist, I would like to know how they differ and which fits best, rather than just being given a label. And please be careful to separate what is actually settled in international law and among scholars from what is genuinely disputed — for instance, whether this arrangement is widely regarded as a coherent division of responsibilities or criticized as an arbitrary loophole is something I would want flagged as contested if it is.
