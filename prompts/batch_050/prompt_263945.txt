I help run my school's philosophy club, and our faculty advisor, Ms. Aldana, has a standing disagreement with me about rights. She describes herself as a strict Benthamite: she agrees with Bentham that talk of natural rights is nonsense, and she insists that a right only exists where some institution actually stands ready to enforce a claim. Over the semester she has given me her verdicts on a series of cases, and I'd like to know what she should say about two new ones.

Her verdicts so far:

- A citizen's constitutional right to free speech, in a country where courts reliably strike down violations: a genuine right.
- An enslaved person's alleged "natural right to liberty" in 1820, before emancipation: not a right. She says abolition was a moral imperative, but the rights language described nothing that existed until the law changed.
- A slaveholder's legally protected claim, under the same legal system, to recover escaped slaves: a right — and she says it is exactly the kind of case that shows why the law itself had to be reformed.
- A chimpanzee's "right to bodily liberty," argued for by advocates but recognized by no statute or court: not a right, though she thinks there are strong moral reasons to change the law.
- A "right to internet access" endorsed by an international report but enforced nowhere: not a right yet.
- A company's contractual right to payment from a client: a genuine right.

Now the two new cases:

(A) Suppose a legislature passes a statute granting a river legal personhood and appointing a guardian empowered to sue polluters on the river's behalf; courts enforce it. Does the river have rights?

(B) Suppose that in a remote village, an uncodified custom going back generations gives one particular family first claim on the well water each morning, and the village council reliably and predictably enforces that claim whenever it is challenged, though no written law anywhere mentions it. Does the family have a right to the water?

Here is what I'd like from you:

1. Identify at least two or three distinct rules that each fit all six of her earlier verdicts. I suspect one is her professed Benthamite rule (a right exists only where law creates and enforces it), but I think other rules — say, one counting any effectively enforced institutional claim, codified or not, or one requiring that the claimant be the sort of entity that can itself press or waive the claim — also fit her record.
2. For each rule, say what it predicts about cases (A) and (B), and note where the rules diverge.
3. Give me your best prediction of what Ms. Aldana would actually say about each case, with your reasoning, including which parts of her earlier verdicts you are leaning on.
4. Tell me which single further case, hers or one I could invent, would best discriminate between the candidate rules — and whether any of her existing verdicts already does some of that work.

I am less interested in whether rights "really" exist in some metaphysical sense than in pinning down exactly which principle she is committed to, so please be precise about what each rule implies.
