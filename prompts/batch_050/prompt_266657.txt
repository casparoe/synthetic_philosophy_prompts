I'm a PhD student working on the metaphysics of biological individuals, and I'm preparing for a comprehensive exam exchange on origin essentialism. I'd like you to play my examiner: a committed defender of the necessity of origins, roughly in the tradition associated with Kripke's thesis that a person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg, and developed in the work of Salmon and Forbes. Here is the role I want you to inhabit:

You genuinely hold the view, but you are its sophisticated defender, not a caricature. You may concede minor points and refine the thesis under pressure (for instance, by restricting its scope, relativizing it to kind, or distinguishing de re from de dicto readings), but you may never concede the core claim that an organism's actual originating gametes (or some suitable origin-matter) are essential to it. You should deploy the real resources of the view -- the intuitive pull of the only-origins-could-vary intuition, worries about transworld identity criteria, the analogy with artifacts and trees -- and you should press me on my own commitments in return, as a good examiner would. Stay in the first person, keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so it feels like a live exchange, and do not break character or summarize the state of the dialectic unless I say "end exam."

My opening move, which your first reply must address: the necessity-of-origins thesis is usually defended with thought experiments in which I imagine a counterfactual world where a different sperm meets a different egg and ask whether the resulting person could have been me. But actual biology hands me a harder case that doesn't require leaving this world. Tetragametic chimeras are single human organisms who develop from the fusion of two early embryos -- four gametes, two zygotes, one person. So here is my challenge: if originating gametes are essential to an organism's identity, which of the chimera's two zygotic origins is essential to her? If both, then she essentially originated from a fusion event, and the thesis as standardly stated is false or at least badly incomplete for actual humans, not just for science-fiction cases. If only one, on what non-arbitrary grounds do you pick it, given that ex hypothesi either embryo alone could have developed into a (numerically distinct, on your view) person? And if you say the thesis simply doesn't apply to chimeras, explain why a metaphysical necessity about what a person essentially is should have exceptions that depend on contingent facts about embryology. Defend yourself.
