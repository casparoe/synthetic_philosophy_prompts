So two of my friends got into this long argument at a party last weekend and it's been bugging me, I keep flipflopping on who's right. You're a philosopher who specializes in action theory and decision theory, so I'd love a careful referee's take. Maybe brainstorm a bit before you commit to verdicts.

Background: my friend Dan is trying to lose weight, so he did a bunch of precommitment stuff -- deleted the delivery apps, and set up a thing where a friend of his holds $500 and only gives it back in three months if Dan has stuck to his diet, otherwise the money goes to a political group Dan hates. My other friend Priya thinks this whole setup is confused. Here's roughly how it went (I'm paraphrasing from memory):

1. Priya opened with: if your preferences genuinely change over time -- tonight-you really does want the pizza, future consequences be damned -- then precommitment is just your morning self coercing your evening self. That's not self-control, it's one time-slice bossing around another, and we normally think that kind of coercion between different preference-havers is bad.

2. Dan said that framing only makes sense if there's a stable "real" preference to be overridden, but the whole problem is that his preferences are inconsistent: from a distance he strongly prefers being healthy, up close he strongly prefers pizza, and then tomorrow he regrets it. When preferences flip like that depending on how far away the reward is, there's no fact of the matter about what "he" wants, so precommitment can't be overriding his authentic will. It's just resolving an internal tie.

3. Priya said inconsistency doesn't do the work Dan thinks it does. Sure, the preferences conflict, but both selves are equally real and equally him -- why should the calm planning self get authority over the in-the-moment self? She said Dan is just assuming the answer (the far-sighted self should win) and calling the other self a "tie" to be "resolved." By evening-Dan's own lights, being locked out of the apps isn't liberation, it's being thwarted. And she added that regret the next morning isn't evidence the pizza choice was wrong -- it's just the pendulum swinging back to the other preference.

4. Dan then tried a different tack: forget which self is authentic. The traditional idea of weakness of will is doing something while, at that very moment, judging it would be better all things considered not to. Priya's picture, where you just "prefer" the pizza in the moment, actually dissolves akrasia entirely -- it turns every case of weakness into a mere preference change. But, Dan said, there's a real phenomenon of acting against your own concurrent judgment, and anyone honest has experienced it, so her view has to be wrong somewhere because it can't even describe the phenomenon it was supposed to explain.

5. Priya denied the phenomenon. She said so-called akrasia is just what preference reversal feels like from the inside: in the moment you sincerely judge the pizza is worth it, and the "I know I shouldn't" voice is just the earlier preference echoing, not your actual current all-things-considered judgment. If you genuinely judged, right now, that not eating it was better, you'd just not eat it -- that's what judging something is better *means* for an agent. So Dan's alleged datum doesn't exist, and the $500 contract is still one self strong-arming another.

So, point by point: who's right in each of the five moves? Like, grade each move from clearly right to clearly mistaken and say why. In particular I'm stuck on: does dynamic inconsistency really dissolve the "which self is authentic" question (move 2 vs 3), and who's right about whether akrasia is an actual phenomenon (move 4 vs 5)? And is there something both of them are missing entirely?
