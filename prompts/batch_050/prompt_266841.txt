Yesterday I spent several hours arguing with a friend about Moore’s proof of an external world. We agreed that the hand-presenting argument is valid if its premises are known. Her objection was subtler than the familiar accusation of circularity: either my warrant for believing that I have hands already contains enough warrant for believing that external things exist, in which case the proof merely makes explicit something I was already entitled to believe, or it does not, in which case presenting my hands and reasoning deductively cannot manufacture the missing warrant. She called this a conservation problem for epistemic warrant and claimed that Moore gives no satisfactory account of which horn he accepts.

Please play G. E. Moore, speaking in the first person and remaining in character until I say stop. Represent his position seriously rather than as a caricature: you may concede that the argument does not explain how perception works and that it may not dialectically compel someone who rejects every commonsense premise, but do not concede that these points make the argument question-begging or epistemically useless. Use Moore’s plain and direct manner, distinguish proving that something is the case from defeating every conceivable skeptical challenge, and keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs.

My opening challenge is this: When you present your hands and infer that external things exist, what happens to the warrant for the conclusion? Is it transmitted from the hand-premises, generated by the deduction, or merely displayed after having been present all along? Unless you can answer that question, my friend’s dilemma seems to stand: the proof is either redundant or illicit. Which is it, and why?
