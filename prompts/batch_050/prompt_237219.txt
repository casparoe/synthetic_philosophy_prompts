The ancient Chinese thinker Gongsun Long, associated with the School of Names, defended the thesis that a white horse is not a horse. The thesis survives on an ambiguity: it is plainly true if it asserts that the class of white horses is not identical to the class of horses, and plainly false if it denies that an individual white horse is an instance of horse. Construct a pair of thought experiments, each at most one paragraph and set in ordinary modern life (a customs office, a housing association's pet policy, a library catalogue, an insurance claim, or similar), engineered to meet the following specifications.

In the first scenario, reasoning with the same surface form as Gongsun Long's — an X-marked Y is not a Y — must be not merely defensible but the correct inference for the character in the story to draw: the conclusion should be one a reasonable official or administrator would actually act on, and refusing to draw it should look like a mistake. In the second scenario, structurally similar reasoning must be an obvious sophism, such that a character who deploys it (to evade a fee, a regulation, or an obligation) is plainly gaming the wording rather than making a legitimate point. The two scenarios should differ in only one relevant respect, so that comparing them isolates the feature that flips the verdict — for instance, whether the operative rule quantifies over kinds or categories rather than individuals, or whether the verb is doing class-identity work rather than predication work. Do not secure the contrast by stipulation: the difference in verdict must fall out of how the rules in each scenario are actually formulated, not from one character being flagged as honest and the other as dishonest.

After the scenarios: (a) state, for each case, what conclusion the Gongsun-Long-style reasoning licenses there and why; (b) name the single feature doing the work, and explain what happens to each case if that feature is removed — in particular, whether removing it collapses the first case back into a bare equivocation; (c) construct the strongest defence the sophist in the second scenario could offer on Gongsun Long's behalf, i.e., a reconstruction of the original argument on which it is a substantive claim about names, kinds, and attributes rather than a pun, and say whether that reconstruction is available to the modern character or only to the ancient one.

Finally, be explicit about what is settled and what is genuinely contested. It is uncontroversial that the paradox trades on some equivocation between readings of the copula or between reference to kinds and reference to individuals. But interpreters disagree about what Gongsun Long himself was doing: whether he was perpetrating a deliberate sophism for dialectical sport, advancing a serious doctrine about the distinction between names for kinds and names for attributes, or reasoning in a way made more natural by features of classical Chinese grammar (on one influential hypothesis, its nouns behave more like mass nouns than count nouns). Do not present any one of these readings as the scholarly consensus; where your scenarios or your reconstruction in (c) lean on a particular interpretation, say so.
