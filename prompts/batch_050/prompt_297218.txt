Here it is:

Everyone knows the wrong kind of reasons problem for buck-passing accounts of value. If being admirable just means there are reasons to admire something, then a demon who will torture everyone unless you admire him seems to make himself admirable, which he clearly isn't. The usual responses either distinguish object-given from state-given reasons, or restrict the relevant reasons to ones related to the object's own features. I think both moves are epicycles. My proposal: so-called wrong kind of reasons are perfectly good reasons, they're just reasons about a different object than we assumed. When the demon threatens you, the reason counts in favor of the *state of affairs* in which you admire him -- that's a genuinely valuable state of affairs, since it prevents torture -- and says nothing about the demon himself. Admiration is like a tool here, and reasons to use a tool aren't reasons to think the tool is beautiful. So the problem dissapears once we're careful about what the reason is a reason *for*. I then test this on two cases: first, a pill that makes you admire your friend's terrible poetry (I say taking the pill can be justified while the poetry stays bad); second, a more novel case -- an AI assistant that flatters you constantly, where you know the flattery is engineered to keep you engaged, so you have pragmatic reasons to enjoy it but, I claim, no reason whatsoever to take the enjoyment as tracking anything about you. If my view is right, the AI case should feel exactly like the demon case, and I think it does. I conclude that buck-passing survives, and that the whole wrong-kind-of-reasons literature rests on conflating reasons for attitudes with reasons for the outcomes of having them.

One thing I already suspect is weak: in the pill case I'm not sure I've shown the poetry stays unadmirable rather than just shown we have no reason to *form* the admiration, and maybe those come apart in a way that hurts me.
