Okay so I've been reading up on intuitionistic logic, the constructivist flavor of math where they reject the law of the excluded middle. As I understand it, the way this works is that intuitionism is a three-valued logic: a statement can be true, false, or "undetermined" (nobody has proved or refuted it yet). That's why excluded middle fails — if p is undetermined, then "p or not-p" comes out undetermined rather than true. So far so good.

But then I got stuck on a few things:

1. If Goldbach's conjecture currently sits in the middle truth value, what happens when somebody proves it in 2031? Does its truth value change? That makes mathematical truth time-dependent, which feels super weird — 2+2=4 presumably stays put, but conjectures apparently migrate between values.

2. If truth values can change, how does a proof stay valid over time? Couldn't some lemma it relies on flip out of "true" later and take the whole proof down with it?

3. Worse: once "undetermined" is an official truth value, shouldn't excluded middle fail again at that level? Like, couldn't a statement be undetermined between being true and being undetermined? Then we need a fourth value, and presumably a fifth, and so on. Does the hierarchy bottom out anywhere or is it turtles all the way down?

So my actual question: how do intuitionists stop their three truth values from spiraling into an infinite tower of values, and how do they keep proved theorems stable as time passes? I assume there's a standard fix for both, since people do constructive mathematics all day without the whole ediface collapsing — I just can't see what it is.
