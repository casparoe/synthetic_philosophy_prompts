Exam question: "Grice distinguishes what is said from what is conversationally implicated. Explain this distinction using the example of an utterance of 'Some of the students passed the exam,' indicating which maxim generates the implicature that not all of the students passed. Then say whether Grice would count this as a generalized or particularized conversational implicature, and why. Finally, explain why Grice treats irony as a case of conversational implicature rather than as part of what is said, identifying the maxim involved."

Student's answer: "What is said is the literal semantic content of the sentence uttered, which is fixed entirely by the conventional meanings of the words and the syntax. So what is said by 'Some of the students passed the exam' is just that at least one student passed, since that is the standard logical meaning of 'some.' But the speaker conversationally implicates that not all of the students passed. This works through the maxim of Relation: the hearer reasons that if the speaker knew that all the students passed, saying 'some' would not be relevant to the topic at hand, so the speaker must not know or must believe it is not the case that all passed. Grice would count this as a generalized conversational implicature, because the implicature is carried by utterances of 'some' across a wide range of contexts and does not depend on special features of the particular occasion; it can, however, still be cancelled, as in 'Some of the students passed -- in fact, all of them did.' Irony is conversational rather than part of what is said because the speaker says something they do not believe and expects the hearer to work out the intended opposite meaning; this involves a deliberate flouting of the maxim of Quantity, since the speaker says more than is warranted, and the hearer calculates the intended meaning from the recognition that the utterance could not be meant literally."
