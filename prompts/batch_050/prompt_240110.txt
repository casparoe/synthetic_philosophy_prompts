I'm a high school senior, and my philosophy club has spent this term on collective responsibility, including a unit built around Jaspers' postwar lectures on German guilt. At our last meeting, one member presented the argument below as a proof that nations cannot be guilty of anything. None of us broke it during the meeting, but I'm convinced it has to fail somewhere, because its conclusion would make nonsense of things I take seriously — for instance, that a state can owe reparations, and that an official apology can be a genuine moral act rather than theater. The argument, as she wrote it up for our shared notes:

(1) A nation is a continuing agent: it acts through institutions that deliberate, decide, sign treaties, and declare wars, and it persists even as its individual members are entirely replaced over time.
(2) If an agent knowingly does a grave wrong and is still around afterward, then that agent — and no one else — bears the guilt for what it did.
(3) Germany, as a corporate agent, waged aggressive war and carried out genocide in the middle of the last century.
(4) The German state that exists today presents itself as continuous with the one that did those things: it has paid the old state's debts, taken on its obligations, and officially accepted responsibility for the crimes of that period.
(5) Therefore the guilt for those crimes belongs to today's German state.
(6) Guilt is, in the end, a property of persons: to say a group is guilty can only mean that its members are guilty.
(7) Therefore present-day Germans are guilty of crimes committed before they were born.
(8) But a person can only be guilty of what they themselves did or failed to do; no one is guilty of anything that happened before they existed.
(9) So if nations were guilty, their members would be guilty of things they never did, which is impossible. Nations are therefore never guilty — only individuals are.

She concludes that talk of national guilt, inherited guilt, or a state owing anything for its past is at best a loose metaphor, and she dared us to name the premise we reject. Taken one at a time, each premise sounded like something I had already granted in earlier meetings. If pressed, my hunch is that the sleight of hand happens in the move from the state's guilt to the guilt of individual Germans, but when I floated that she answered with an analogy I couldn't defeat: when a gang commits a robbery, the gang's guilt just is the members' guilt — each one is on the hook — so steps (5) through (7) are exactly how group guilt works in ordinary life.

Here is what I'd like from you. First, name the premise or step you think should be rejected, and explain why it looked plausible enough that a room of careful readers let it through. Second, tell me what rejecting it costs: which other commitments — about gangs, teams, family pride, corporate fines — one would then have to abandon or reinterpret, and whether that price is acceptable. Third, tell me whether your diagnosis leaves me what I most want to keep: that reparations and official apologies can be genuinely owed, not merely generous. Fourth, since Jaspers is our unit text, relate your diagnosis to his distinction among the kinds of guilt — does the bad step amount to running two of his kinds together, or is the real problem elsewhere, such as in the picture of what a nation is? Finally, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the one point in your answer where you are least sure you have it right.
