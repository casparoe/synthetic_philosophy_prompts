I got summoned for jury duty last month and while sitting in the waiting room it struck me how weird the whole thing is. Nobody interviewed me about my reasoning skills, nobody checked whether I know anything about evidence or bias; they literally picked my name out of a hat (well, a database), and that randomness is treated as the *virtue* of the system, not a bug. Same with the green card lottery, or those citizens assemblies that countries like Ireland keep convening for constitutional questions, or oversubscribed schools assigning spots by lot. But then turn around: nobody, and I mean nobody, seriously proposes picking the president, or even a city council, by lottery, and the idea of randomly assigning surgery patients to random adults off the street is obviously insane. We seem to think random selection is the fairest possible method in exactly the domains where we also say the stakes are highest (a jury can send someone to prison for life!), while in politics, where the ancient Athenians happily used sortition for most offices, we've collectively decided that elections are the only legitimate way to pick rulers.

So here's my question: what philosophical commitments does this mixed practice presuppose? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory that would make it rational to use the lot for jurors but the ballot for legislators. Candidate ingredients might be: a view about whether political judgment is a skill that's unequally distributed or a generic capacity of any adult; a distinction between *judging* (applying shared standards to a case) and *ruling* (setting the agenda, exercising power); an idea that legitimacy flows from consent/accountability rather than from representativeness; or maybe just a bet about which errors are worse where. Tell me whether the actual practice strictly presupposes one coherent theory here or is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. whether several rival theories (say, an epistemic one about competence and a procedural one about consent) would each license the same pattern, and where the pattern is just a historical accident dressed up as principle. Also, stress-test it both directions: what would have to change in our theory of citizenship if we extended the jury logic to the legislature (lottocracy-style), and conversely, if we take seriously the anti-lottery intuition we apply to politics, shouldn't we also want professionally trained jurors -- and what does it say that most people recoil from *that* too?

Please write this as a structured analysis, maybe 600-900 words, and as the very last line give me a one-sentence bottom line starting with "Verdict:" stating whether the current lot-for-juries, election-for-legislators pattern rests on one coherent principle or papers over a genuine tension.
