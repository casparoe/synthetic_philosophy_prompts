I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on AI alignment and political philosophy, and one session is devoted to the question of whose values an aligned AI system should serve in the first place. I've collected what I take to be the standard answers in the literature, and I'd like you to help me find a gap. My list so far: (1) the individualist answer -- each system should serve the values and preferences of its own user or operator, much as a tool or employee serves its principal; (2) the democratic answer -- the values should be set by some aggregation of everyone's input, via voting, deliberative polling, or a similar procedure; (3) the moral-realist answer -- the system should be aligned to whatever the correct moral theory actually requires, with popular input mattering at most as evidence about what that is; (4) the liberal-neutralist answer -- the system should avoid taking sides among contested conceptions of the good and confine itself to enforcing harm constraints, rights, and the law, leaving users free within those bounds; (5) the contractualist or overlapping-consensus answer -- the system should act on principles that no reasonable person could reject, or that adherents of very different moral doctrines could all endorse for their own reasons; (6) the welfarist-aggregation answer -- the system should weigh the interests or preferences of all affected parties, including people who never interact with it directly, and maximize accordingly.

Here's what I'd like from you: propose a seventh answer that is genuinely different from all six of these -- not a hybrid of two of them, not one of them restricted to a special case, and not a mere implementation detail of one of them (e.g., 'use a citizens' assembly instead of a referendum' is just a variant of (2)). It's fine if the answer you propose has few or no prominent defenders in the existing debate; I'd actually prefer that, since the seminar's point is to test whether the usual taxonomy is exhaustive. Explain what the proposal is, who or what fixes the values on this view, and why it doesn't collapse into any of the six.

Finally, give me your honest assessment: is the proposal actually defensible, or does it fail in an instructive way? If it fails, I'd like to know whether the failure is a contingent one (hard to implement, politically impossible) or a conceptual one (incoherent, or secretly just one of the six in disguise).
