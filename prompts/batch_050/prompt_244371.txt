Yesterday evening I spent nearly four hours arguing with a close friend, a devoted reader of Habermas, about whether the ordinary friction of office life constitutes systematically distorted communication or is simply what coordination among adults looks like. She maintained that any organization could adopt the conditions of the ideal speech situation and that rational consensus would eventually follow; I maintained that the first agenda item would never be reached. We parted unconvinced, and rather than resume the dispute by email I would like to send her a piece of satire that takes her position seriously enough to sting.

Write a parody internal memorandum, 550 to 700 words, from the Human Resources department of a mid-sized corporation, announcing that, effective immediately, all organizational communication will be conducted in accordance with the ideal speech situation. The humor must arise from fidelity to the theory rather than from caricature of it. In particular, the memo should:

1. Operationalize, in flat bureaucratic prose, the requirement that the only permissible force within the firm be the unforced force of the better argument, including disciplinary procedures for employees caught engaging in strategic action;
2. Treat the three validity claims (truth, normative rightness, and sincerity) as three distinct complaint-filing procedures, each with its own form, review timeline, and appeals process;
3. Guarantee every employee the equal right to question any assertion, introduce any proposal, and express any need, and then calmly work through what this does to the meeting schedule and the deadline for quarterly reports;
4. Classify PowerPoint, the performance review, and the org chart as steering media colonizing the corporate lifeworld, with salary payments suspended pending rationally motivated consensus on their legitimacy;
5. Close with an FAQ section in which at least one answer inadvertently exposes a genuine difficulty for the theory, for instance what employees should do when a colleague raises a sincerity objection during a fire drill, or how the firm should proceed against a participant who simply will not stop arguing and insists that this is precisely the point.

The memo must remain entirely deadpan throughout: no winking, no acknowledgment that the policy is impractical, and no asides to the reader. The target is not Habermas himself but the assumption, which my friend defended well past midnight, that communicative rationality is a policy an institution could simply adopt on a Monday morning. A reader familiar with The Theory of Communicative Action should feel that every joke is earned and that the satire is, in its way, fair.
