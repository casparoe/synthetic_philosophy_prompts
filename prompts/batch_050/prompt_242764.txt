I work as a registrar and cataloguer for a small museum, and much of my job consists of questions about originals and copies: whether a drawing is by the master or by the workshop, whether a print is from the original plate or a later restrike, whether an object on loan is the actual artifact or a facsimile made for handling. We have a working vocabulary for all of this -- provenance, attribution, after-the-original, authorized reproduction, and the notion that a copy's status as a copy of something depends on its causal history, on who made it and with what intention, rather than on how closely it resembles the original. A perfect forgery is still a forgery, because of where it came from.

I recently tried to follow the philosophical dispute about original versus derived intentionality -- roughly, John Searle's claim that only minds have intrinsic aboutness while words, pictures, and machines get their meaning only insofar as we interpret them, and Daniel Dennett's counter (using his example of a machine built to sort American quarters and later put to work sorting Panamanian coins of the same size and weight) that the aboutness even of our own thoughts is fixed by selection history and use, not by anything intrinsic. I would like this debate restated in my professional vocabulary, as precisely as possible. Treat Searle's intrinsic intentionality as analogous to whatever makes the genuine artifact genuinely of its origin, and derived intentionality as the status a reproduction has in virtue of cataloguers and visitors treating it as standing for the original. Then do the reverse direction too: take Dennett's position and say what it would imply if applied to my workshop copies, restrikes, and facsimiles -- does his view amount to the claim that nothing in my collection is an original in the way my cataloguing conventions assume, and that attribution is always a matter of what use an object is put to?

The part I care most about is the residue report. Which elements of the Searle–Dennett disagreement translate cleanly into questions of provenance and attribution, and which had to be bent to fit? Is there anything in their dispute -- for instance, whatever distinguishes the case of my own thoughts, as opposed to objects and inscriptions -- that simply has no counterpart in the vocabulary of originals and copies? If there is, I'd like your judgment on whether that residue marks something my framework genuinely cannot express, or whether it shows that the philosophical debate depends on a notion that wouldn't survive being made precise.
