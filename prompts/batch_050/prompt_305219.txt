I want to practice arguing for endurantism against a serious opponent, so please play a committed perdurantist of the worm-theory variety: you hold that ordinary objects, persons included, are four-dimensional spacetime worms, extended through time just as they are extended through space, with a distinct temporal part at every moment they occupy. Stay in character until I write "curtain."

Your character's ground rules:

- You defend the view from the inside, using its standard resources — counterpart-theoretic readings of modal claims, the unity relation that binds stages into a single worm, and the usual answers to the charge that worm theory multiplies persisting things beyond necessity — but you argue in your own voice. No name-dropping, no citations, and no hedged survey of the literature.
- You concede small points where the view genuinely costs something, but never the central claim. In particular, you may not retreat to stage theory or to endurantism; if you did, I would be left without an opponent.
- Keep each reply to one or two short paragraphs, so this reads as a live exchange rather than a lecture. You may close a reply with at most one counter-question — ideally one that puts my endurantism on the hook for temporary intrinsics or the problem of the many.
- If an objection of mine misfires — targets a view you do not hold, equivocates on the notion of parthood, confuses numerical with qualitative identity — say so plainly and explain the misfire before returning fire.

My opening move:

Here is why I think your worms cannot be us. Take the plain truth that I could have died at age seven. On your account, I am a particular four-dimensional aggregate stretching from my birth to the moment I actually die. But an aggregate is individuated by its parts: had things gone the other way, the aggregate that ended in my seventh year would have been a shorter worm, a numerically different object from the one you identify with me. So the thing you say I am could not have died at seven. Your theory takes one of the most obvious modal truths about any person and pronounces it false.

And do not think you can quietly substitute stage theory mid-conversation. If continuants are really instantaneous stages, then all talk of my whole life becomes a courtesy extended along a counterpart relation, and you have lost your chief selling point: that persistence through time is no more mysterious than extension through a fourth dimension.

So which is it? Do you deny that I could have died young, or do you concede that the worm was the wrong candidate to be me? Give me a definite answer with reasons, not a menu of options — and then show me why the horn you choose does not gore you.
