I work at a small animal welfare nonprofit, and in three weeks I'm presenting to the board of a mid-sized shrimp aquaculture company. The board is four ex-farmers who grew up in the industry, one vet who mostly thinks about disease outbreaks, and a finance director. I'm asking them to commit to two things across their supply chain: phasing out eyestalk ablation of broodstock within three years, and requiring electrical stunning of all shrimp before slaughter instead of the current practice of killing by asphyxiation on air or in ice slurry. My pitch rests on the claim that decapod crustaceans are probably sentient, so these practices plausibly cause serious suffering at enormous scale (billions of animals, trivial per-animal costs to fix). The UK has already written decapods into its sentience legislation, which I want to use as a this-is-coming-anyway argument.

Prepare my Q&A. Give me roughly the eight objections I'll actually get from this audience -- not the ones a philosophy seminar would raise -- ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumble it. Phrase each one the way a shrimp farmer or a finance director would actually put it (they'll talk about cost per kilo, throughput on the harvest line, "they're basically water insects", whether ice slurry isn't already a kind of stunning, whether a stunned shrimp keeps quality or goes mushy, and why they should move before regulation forces them to). For each objection: what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and a two-minute reply, plus a note on where the reply is soft. I want honesty about which objections have real force -- I suspect the ice slurry one does, since industry defends it as thermal stunning and the evidence there is messier than I'd like.

Also flag the question I'm hoping nobody asks: I think it's some version of "if shrimp are sentient enough that we have to stun them, why is it fine to farm and eat them at all -- and what about the fish that go into their feed?" Tell me whether that's actually my biggest vulnerability or whether I've got the wrong one, and give me a fallback answer for it that doesn't blow up the whole proposal. One more wrinkle to factor in: the company sells to buyers in countries with no welfare rules at all, so "the law is coming" doesn't apply to half their volume.
