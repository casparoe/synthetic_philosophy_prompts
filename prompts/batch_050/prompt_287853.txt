After forty years in experimental biochemistry I have retired, and in my retirement I have been reading philosophy of biology, where I keep encountering rival definitions of life — metabolic, thermodynamic, Darwinian, autopoietic — each defended chiefly by exhibiting one case it handles well. Before I commit to any of them, I should like the specification first. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of what it is to be alive must satisfy: which cases it has to get right and why (viruses, dormant spores, a mule, a single isolated cell, mitochondria, a self-replicating machine, the biosphere taken as a whole, fire as the classical decoy), which distinctions it must not collapse (being alive versus having once been alive, life as a property of individuals versus of lineages or systems, necessary versus sufficient criteria), and which findings from biology and chemistry it cannot afford to contradict. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list itself turns out to be inconsistent, that is the result I most want to see. Then, briefly, how the leading contenders fare against it. A table is acceptable.
