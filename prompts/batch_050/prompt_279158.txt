I co-teach an upper-level PPE seminar called Markets, Mandates, and Morals, and I am revising the final exam after last year's price-controls question disappointed me. I asked a plain question about whether minimum wages and rent control are defensible, and nearly every student produced the same essay: draw the textbook supply-and-demand diagram, note the surplus or shortage, gesture at Card and Krueger for the minimum wage and at "housing economists broadly agree" for rent control, then conclude that the ethics is a matter of weighing efficiency against fairness. The answers were interchangeable, and I could not tell who actually understood anything. For this year's exam I want a question built around a fictional policy package -- I'll call it the Valesburg Fair Housing and Wages Act -- that makes the standard diagram the *starting point* rather than the answer. The Act has three provisions: (1) annual rent increases on existing tenancies are capped at inflation minus one percent, but rents reset to market whenever a unit turns over; (2) a municipal minimum wage set at sixty percent of the local median, applying only to firms with more than twenty employees; (3) a tax credit for landlords who voluntarily keep units at the capped rate for sitting tenants for ten or more years. What I want students to wrestle with is what I'm calling the incumbency problem: the package systematically advantages people who already have a lease or a job at the moment it passes, and shifts costs onto the marginal renter searching for an apartment, the marginal worker not yet hired, and the small employer just under the firm-size threshold. The interesting ethical question, which I want them to take seriously in both directions, is whether protecting incumbents can be justified by something like a reliance or tenure-based interest -- the thought that long-standing tenants and workers have built lives around arrangements the state encouraged -- or whether that is just a moralized defense of insiders at outsiders' expense.

Please write the exam question itself (students will have ninety minutes), followed by a model answer of roughly 600 words, and then a marking scheme. The model answer should engage both the economics and the ethics rather than treating them as separate compartments: on the economics side, it should distinguish statutory incidence from economic incidence, explain why provision (1) predicts different effects for sitting tenants versus apartment-seekers and why the vacancy-decontrol feature changes the long-run supply picture, and at least consider the monopsony caveat under which provision (2) could raise rather than reduce employment for covered firms while creating distortion at the firm-size margin; on the ethics side, it should give the strongest version of the reliance-interest defense of incumbency privilege and the strongest version of the objection that this licenses a transfer from the least-established to the well-established, and it should not pretend the answer is obvious. For the marking scheme, identify five specific moves a top answer must make, assign marks to each totaling 20, and for each move state in one line what separates an A answer from a B answer on that point -- that is, name the specific error or omission (for example, treating the wage floor's burden as falling on "employers" full stop, or analyzing the rent cap as if turnover did not exist) that should cost a student the marks.
