I am a novelist, and my working fluency is in the vocabulary of narrative craft: point of view, free indirect style, psychic distance, the reliable and unreliable narrator, dramatic irony, and the retrospective revelation that reclassifies an earlier scene — the chapter the reader must go back and reread once the story discloses that what it showed was a dream, a delusion, or a misperception. I want you to restate, as faithfully as you can, the debate in the philosophy of perception between common-factor accounts of experience and disjunctivism, including the argument from illusion that motivates the common-factor side, in the vocabulary I have just described. If I have already garbled the debate in the way I have framed this request, correct my version before translating it.

The translation should proceed through the moves of the debate, not just its conclusion. The argument from illusion seems to me to have a natural craft counterpart: I can write two passages, one in which a character genuinely sees the figure at the window and one in which she hallucinates it, that are identical sentence by sentence and therefore indistinguishable from within her perspective, and the inference from that indistinguishability to a shared experiential nature looks like an inference I make professionally all the time. The disjunctivist denial of that inference is the harder case for translation, and I want it rendered honestly rather than flattened: what does the claim that veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of state become, in a world where an author fixes which one occurred and could have fixed it otherwise?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which elements of the debate map cleanly onto craft phenomena, which survive only with distortion — I suspect the philosopher's indistinguishability is not the same relation as a reader's inability to tell a dream sequence from a real one, since I engineer the one and no one engineers the other — and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? In particular, does craft vocabulary even have the resources to express a claim about the intrinsic nature of an experience, as opposed to claims about what characters and readers can or cannot tell? Where there is residue, say whether you take it to be a defect of my framework or a sign that the philosophical dispute is less substantive than it appears.

Finally, a verdict on my own instinct. My professional intuition is that the veridical scene and the hallucinatory scene are the same passage with different surrounding facts, and that this is all the common-factor theorist could mean. Tell me whether that is the common-factor view faithfully restated or a confusion of the text with the experience it depicts — and, as a test of whether the translation has succeeded, answer this: when I write an illusory scene and never reveal it as such, does my craft practice commit me to the disjunctivist's claim that what occurred on the page was all along a different kind of thing than the reader experienced, or to the opposite?
