Write a dialogue between two philosophers who agree that some choices are genuinely hard in a way that has nothing to do with ignorance or time pressure, but who disagree about what makes them hard. Set the exchange over a concrete case they are both stuck on: one of them must choose between two careers — a secure academic post in the city where her aging parents live, and a riskier, more creatively promising position abroad — and she has already done all the homework there is to do. Neither option is better overall, and yet, she insists, they do not feel equal either.

The first interlocutor defends the view that the two options are "on a par": related by a fourth positive value relation beyond better than, worse than, and equally good, and that in such cases the chooser can rationally commit to one option by willing herself to be the kind of person for whom it matters most — that reasons in hard choices can be created, not merely discovered. Have her explain what the will adds that a coin flip does not.

The second interlocutor resists the whole apparatus. He argues that the appearance of a fourth relation is better explained by vagueness or indeterminacy in the betterness relation itself: the small-improvement argument — that if the careers were equally good, a slightly sweetened version of one would have to be strictly better, which it plainly isn't — shows only that the comparison is indeterminate, not that some sui generis relation holds. And he presses the claim that "creating reasons by commitment" is either just deciding (which needs no exotic metaphysics of value) or a confusion about what reasons are: you can make yourself care more about staying near your parents, but you cannot thereby make it true that you now have more reason to stay.

Both speakers should be equally sharp and equally charitable. They must respond to each other's actual arguments rather than restate their opening positions: have the first interlocutor answer the charge that willing cannot manufacture value, and have the second explain what is lost — phenomenologically and practically — if the indeterminacy view is right and there is nothing to do but pick arbitrarily. By the end, they should converge on a precise statement of what they still disagree about (for instance, whether "on a par" is doing explanatory work that indeterminacy cannot, or whether rational agency requires a gap between reasons and choice), without either one conceding. Aim for roughly 800–1200 words, in a polished but natural conversational register.
