I've been arguing about this with a friend from my behavioral economics reading group, and we keep coming back to an observation Adam Smith makes in The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759): that people suffer considerably more when they fall from a better situation into a worse one than they ever enjoy when they rise from a worse situation into a better one. Smith doesn't present this asymmetry as a defect of judgment; he treats it as a settled feature of human nature and builds his account of ambition partly on top of it. My friend says this shows that what economists now call loss aversion was recognized, long before the experimental literature, as a legitimate structure of preference rather than a bias. I want to test that claim against my own gut verdicts on cases, because mine don't line up cleanly, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

Here are my verdicts. Someone who inherits a stock portfolio and keeps it for decades seems to me perfectly rational; someone holding the same amount in cash who refuses to buy that identical portfolio also seems rational, yet these two can't both be expressing the same underlying preference, and that bothers me. Declining to sell one's grandmother's ring at triple its market price, when one could never afford to buy it back, strikes me as a wholly legitimate preference, not a bias. Declining to swap one's concert ticket for a seat two rows away plus a hundred dollars in cash strikes me as a bias, pure and simple, even though the formal structure looks identical to the ring case. A homeowner who refuses to sell below the purchase price in a down market, even when moving is clearly best for the family, seems to be in the grip of a bias. An investor who holds losing stocks year after year to avoid realizing the loss is definitely biased, but I can't say exactly how this differs from the homeowner. A person who refuses a coin flip that would gain them one hundred and fifty dollars or lose them one hundred seems to me neither biased nor fully rational, and I keep changing my mind about it. And on Smith's own kind of case — a wealthy person who would be genuinely devastated by losing half their fortune — I want to say the anticipated suffering is real and therefore rationally weighty, not an error to be corrected.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these? I'd like it stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it — for instance, whether the distinguishing mark is hedonic (losses genuinely hurt more, so weighting them more is accurate self-prediction), or attachment-based (losses of identity-constituting goods are real losses, losses of fungible ones are not), or something about the reference point itself (which baseline is legitimately mine). Please also say what the principle predicts for two cases I haven't settled: a country that refuses to sell a piece of territory no citizen ever visits, and a person who would pay five hundred dollars to avoid a week's headache but wouldn't accept five hundred dollars to undergo one. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which one you think I should drop if forced to choose.
