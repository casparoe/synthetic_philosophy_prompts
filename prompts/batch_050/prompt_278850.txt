I want a principle for when a major life decision can be rationally weighed — its possible outcomes compared by value — and when the structure of the decision rules that out, in something like the way the literature on transformative experience (L.A. Paul and her critics) suggests. Here are my gut verdicts:

1. Having a first child: not weighable. I cannot know what the life I would be choosing is like, and it predictably rewrites my values, so deliberation feels like a coin flip in disguise.
2. Trying durian for the first time: weighable on a whim, even though I equally cannot know what it is like in advance.
3. Receiving a cochlear implant after lifelong deafness: weighable. Others can tell me what the change did for them, and my reasons for wanting hearing would survive it.
4. Joining a monastery because I want to become someone who values that life: weighable, perhaps even admirable — the transformation is the point of choosing.
5. Having a second child: weighable, since I have lived the transformation — though this seems to concede that the first child could not have been decided on rationally.
6. Consenting to the implant on behalf of my deaf infant: here my instinct flips. That the transformation would not be mine makes me want to defer the decision until the child can choose.

Cases 1 and 3 sit badly together, since testimony and enduring reasons are available for both, and 4 treats as a reason exactly what 1 treats as disqualifying. My tentative principle is that a decision is weighable iff its outcome can be evaluated in concepts and values I already possess — but that fails case 2. Repair it or replace it: state your best principle precisely, give its verdicts on two cases I have not listed (enlisting for a deployment one expects to be traumatizing; taking a drug that permanently shifts one's personality), and say honestly which of my six verdicts it overturns.
