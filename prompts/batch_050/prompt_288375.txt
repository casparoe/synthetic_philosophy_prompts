I am a novelist, and my revision process fails in a particular way: I render my characters' minds inconsistently. In one chapter a woman acts from drilled habit and reflex; three chapters later, under no greater pressure, she conducts an elaborate inner cost-benefit analysis in metaphors she never had the schooling to formulate. Readers rarely notice consciously, but they stop trusting the character, and they are right to.

I would like you to turn Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of kinds of minds into a working procedure I can run, scene by scene, at the desk during revision. The taxonomy, as I understand it: Darwinian creatures act on inherited, hard-wired dispositions, the environment simply triggering the response. Skinnerian creatures learn within a lifetime by trial and error in the world, their behaviors stamped in or out by consequences. Popperian creatures run the trials in an inner simulator instead, letting candidate actions be tested and rejected in the head before any real risk is taken. Gregorian creatures import tools built by others — language, number, writing, diagrams, practices — and those tools reorganize what their thinking can even be. Every adult human is presumably all four at once; the craft question is which register a given scene is actually in, and what that register licenses in the prose.

Concretely, I want:

1. An ordered set of questions or tests I can apply to any scene to determine which register the character is operating in — or should be, given who they are, what they have been taught, and what the scene demands of them.
2. For each register, what the prose may and may not do: what kinds of perception, deliberation, and error are available to that mind. Each register should fail differently, and I want the characteristic mistakes spelled out, since a character's errors are often what convinces a reader. In particular, address interiority: whether free indirect style or interior monologue is even available at a given level, or whether it smuggles in Gregorian tools the character does not possess.
3. Rules for movement between registers: how to render regression under stress, fatigue, or intoxication, and growth through education or the acquisition of a new tool, so that a shift reads as earned rather than as the author changing her mind about who the character is.
4. Guidance for ensemble scenes in which different characters occupy different registers at once.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a soldier in combat whose training is doing the work; a character quietly planning a betrayal over weeks, aided by a notebook; and a third case of your choosing, constructed so that the procedure delivers a verdict I will not like — one that forbids a move novelists make constantly — where you tell me plainly whether that move is defensible craft or simply cheating.

Finally, mark the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to me: where the question is no longer cognitive plausibility but artistic license, which no taxonomy can settle. My constraints are these: the procedure must be usable without expertise in cognitive science, it will be applied dozens of times per manuscript, the stakes are the reader's trust, and the only person who signs off on its verdicts is me.
