So I'm in an upper-year poli sci seminar on nuclear strategy and we've been doing Schelling and the whole rationality-of-irrationality thing, and I keep getting stuck on a pattern that I can't find a clean label for, or maybe it has one and I just havent hit it yet. The pattern is this: a state deliberately makes itself *less* in control of escalation, and does this openly, and this loss of control is precisely what makes its deterrent work. Like putting missiles on hair-trigger alert so that no leader could possibly call them back in time, or delegating launch authority down to local commanders so the other side knows that decapitating the capital doesn't end the threat, or putting a small tripwire force somewhere so that if the enemy advances, their own soldiers' deaths drag the bigger country in whether or not anyone in the capital decides it should. In each case the state is safer *because* the situation might spiral into something literally nobody wants and nobody chose. It's not bluffing, it's the opposite of bluffing.

Now, I know this is adjacent to brinkmanship, but brinkmanship as I understand it is about the maneuvering itself, rocking the boat, taking steps toward the edge. What I'm describing is more the prior move of sawing off your own steering wheel and showing the other driver. And it's adjacent to commitment devices and burning bridges, but those are usually about removing your own *options*, whereas here it's more like removing everyone's option to keep things under control once something goes wrong. I've also seen people mention that Schelling had a specific phrase for threats whose fulfillment is left partly to chance, but I'm not sure whether that covers the institutional version, where the "chance" is built into your own command structure rather than into the situation.

So: does this exact configuration have an established name of its own, distinct from brinkmanship, precommitment, and tripwires, or is it just treated as a species of one of those? If it's a species, which one, and where's the boundary? And if the literature never really carved this out as its own thing, what would you call it and how would you define it so it doesn't collapse into any of the neighbours?
