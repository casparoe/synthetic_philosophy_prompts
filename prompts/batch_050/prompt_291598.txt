Next month I'm giving the farewell toast for my old boss, a political campaign manager who's retiring after thirty-five years, and I've decided -- possibly a terrible idea, but it's too late, I told people -- to structure the whole thing around Machiavelli's contrast between virtù and fortuna. The room will be a genuine mix: a few political-science academics who will notice immediately if I get Machiavelli wrong, a lot of grizzled operatives who've never read him, and a bunch of 24-year-old staffers at the start of their careers. Here's why the theme isn't just decoration: her career is awkward to eulogize honestly. Her most famous win came when the opposing candidate self-destructed in a scandal two weeks before the vote -- pure luck, everyone knows it -- and her most heartbreaking loss came after a flawless campaign that got flattened by a surprise recession in the final quarter. Meanwhile the thing she's actually proudest of is invisible: decades of boring preparatory work, building local party infrastructure and donor networks in the good years, which meant her candidates kept surviving disasters that wiped out rivals. That's basically Machiavelli's point about fortune being a flooding river you can only survive if you built the dikes and levees in calm weather -- she built the dikes. So the toast has to thread a needle: it can't flatter her by pretending her wins were all skill (the operatives would laugh), and it can't console her by blaming her losses on bad luck in a way that makes her whole career sound like dice-rolling (the young staffers need to hear that preparation matters, that virtù is real even if fortuna governs its share).

Please act as a professional speechwriter who also happens to know Renaissance political thought cold, and think carefully about the balance before you write -- maybe sketch the structure to yourself first (I only want the final toast, though). The toast should be about 600-800 words, warm but not sappy, with at least one line the operatives will actually repeat to each other at the bar afterward. The Machiavelli should be load-bearing but unobtrusive: don't quote The Prince chapter and verse at them, just let the fortuna/virtù frame quietly organize what can and can't be said about luck, skill, and what a career in politics actually amounts to. It should land differently for the three audiences -- the academics should catch the architecture, the old hands should feel their lives were understood, and the 24-year-olds should leave a little less naive about what they're signing up for and a little more motivated to build dikes. Begin with the toast itself, no preface, no notes to me, no "here's your speech."
