I write about physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I'm assembling a satirical sidebar for our year-end issue on the way physicists talk about beauty. The target is a real habit of mind: the use of elegance, naturalness, and the absence of fine-tuning as evidence for or against a fundamental theory — the sensibility that treats an ugly theory as suspect even when no data yet rule it out, and that kept predicting beautiful new particles which have so far declined to appear.

Please write the piece as an official bulletin from an imaginary regulatory body — call it something like the Bureau of Theoretical Standards — which certifies fundamental theories the way an aviation authority certifies aircraft, except that its safety criteria are entirely aesthetic. Around 600 words. Deadpan bureaucratic prose throughout: numbered sections, defined terms, an incident report, a recall notice. No winking at the reader, and never state the moral outright.

Work in at least the following, naturally rather than as a checklist:

- A certification schedule whose criteria are recognizable versions of real heuristics — naturalness, symmetry, simplicity, the unification of forces — rendered with the flat specificity of a government form, so that a reader who knows the field recognizes the genuine aesthetic arguments underneath.
- An incident report on the Large Hadron Collider's failure to turn up the predicted beautiful new particles, framed as a manufacturing defect. The investigation should seriously entertain the possibility that nature itself failed quality inspection, since a universe with a light Higgs boson but no supersymmetric partners is, by the Bureau's own criteria, fine-tuned and therefore presumptively defective.
- A recall notice or safety advisory for a class of theories found to be insufficiently elegant, carrying the standard bureaucratic hedge that the theory remains empirically adequate and may continue to be operated at the owner's risk.
- The industry's standard defense against the fine-tuning complaint, along multiverse lines: the product is not defective; the customer merely received one unit from a very large and mostly unobservable product line.

The satire should function as a reductio: follow the aesthetic-evidence habit faithfully enough that a reader who endorses it has to say exactly where the parody stops being faithful. It should also be fair — let the bulletin concede, in its own idiom, that past certified theories, the ones that predicted positrons or the bending of starlight, really were beautiful by the Bureau's standards, so that the sting is that the test has stopped working, not that it never worked. Don't name real living physicists; an invented spokesperson is welcome. Close with the Bureau's founding charter: one or two sentences stating its creed in perfectly earnest administrative language.
