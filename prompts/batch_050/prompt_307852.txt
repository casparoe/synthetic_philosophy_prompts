Here's a chunk of Aristotle's Politics (Book I, around 1257a) that we didn't have time for in lecture:

"with every article of property there is a double way of using it; both uses are related to the article itself, but not related to it in the same manner—one is peculiar to the thing and the other is not peculiar to it. Take for example a shoe—there is its wear as a shoe and there is its use as an article of exchange; for both are ways of using a shoe, inasmuch as even he that barters a shoe for money or food with the customer that wants a shoe uses it as a shoe, though not for the use peculiar to a shoe, since shoes have not come into existence for the purpose of barter."

And a few lines later:

"Exchange on these lines therefore is not contrary to nature, nor is it any branch of the art of wealth-getting, for it existed for the replenishment of natural self-sufficiency; yet out of it the art of business in due course arose."

Write a dialogue between two classmates, Priya and Daniel, who disagree about what this passage commits Aristotle to. Priya reads it as drawing a line in the sand: barter that patches a surplus or shortfall is natural and fine, but the moment you exchange in order to come out ahead you've crossed into something unnatural — so on her reading ordinary profit-seeking trade already falls on the wrong side of the passage, and Aristotle only says exchange is "a use" of the shoe in a thin, technical sense. Daniel thinks Priya is reading a moral verdict into what's really a genealogy: trade grows smoothly out of natural barter (Aristotle says business "arose" out of it, not that it betrayed it), and the "double use" point shows exchange-value is a genuine use of a thing, so nothing here condemns buying and selling as such.

Don't make either of them a pushover. They should respond to each others actual arguments — Daniel should have to explain what work "not contrary to nature" is doing if Aristotle isn't grading kinds of exchange, and Priya should have to explain why Aristotle bothers insisting the barterer still uses the shoe "as a shoe" if exchange-use were simply a corruption. By the end they should at least pin down exactly where their disagreement bottoms out, even if they don't settle it. Around 400–500 words of dialogue.
