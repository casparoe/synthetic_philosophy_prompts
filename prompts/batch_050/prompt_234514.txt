So I TA for an intro ethics course and we're doing the unit on punishment, and I keep running into the same analogy from students, from op-eds, from a victim-impact statement we read, from everyone basically: a crime creates a debt, punishment is paying it, and once someone's paid their debt to society they're square. One student used it to argue the death penalty is sometimes the only full repayment -- a murderer took a life, anything less than a life leaves the debt unpaid. Another student, same seminar, used the same picture for the opposite conclusion: if the murderer's debt can never actually be repaid, since the dead person stays dead whatever we do, then execution is just adding a second unpaid debt, and only reparable harms can ground punishment at all. Meanwhile Beccaria, who's on the syllabus, argues in the chapter on the death penalty that no social contract could include anyone handing over the right to be killed, since you can't give others a power over your life that you don't even have over it yourself, and that the state's authority to punish is built out of small surrendered portions of liberty only -- which sounds like he's using the contract/accounting picture to abolish the death penalty rather than to price it.

Can you audit the debt analogy properly for me, since it's clearly doing work on both sides? I want the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the creditor (the victim? the state? 'society', and are those interchangable?), the debtor, the amount owed, the currency, default, discharge, and -- the one my students kept tripping on -- what corresponds to a creditor forgiving the debt, and whether a victim's family forgiving the murderer should cancel the state's claim the way forgiving a loan cancels repayment. Then where the mapping breaks: debts are fungible and quantified in advance, punishments aren't; you can pay someone else's debt but not serve their sentence; a debt collector doesn't get to choose the coin. Which of those disanalogies actually matter for the death penalty argument and which are just pedantic. And finally: does either student get their conclusion out of the analogy, or is the analogy just decorating conclusions reached elsewhere? If Beccaria's argument only works by quietly limiting the analogy rather than extending it, say that too -- I suspect it does but can't pin down where.
