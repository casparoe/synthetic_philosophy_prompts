Having retired three years ago from a career in experimental physics, I have lately been making up for lost time in philosophy, and I now convene a fortnightly discussion circle for fellow amateurs — eight to twelve of us, mostly educated laypeople, meeting for ninety minutes in a church hall in Oxfordshire. At our last session we stumbled onto a question that produced more heat than light: can it be rational to believe something whilst simultaneously believing that one ought not to believe it? One member insisted the combination was straightforwardly incoherent; another thought it described her state of mind most mornings. I would like to devote our next meeting to the matter, but I am determined that the group should feel the difficulty in their own person before anyone hands them vocabulary for it. A lecture on the distinction between first-order evidence and evidence about the quality of one's own evidence would ruin the thing entirely.

I should be grateful, therefore, for a fully worked-out activity or staged exercise that I can run without any philosophical preparation on the participants' part. My own sketch of an idea, which you may improve upon or discard, is that they might be set a series of problems on which they must record both an answer and a degree of confidence, and then be confronted with evidence bearing not on the problems themselves but on the reliability of the faculties they just used — a fabricated record of their past errors, say, or word that the coffee was doctored — so that they must decide what to do with a conviction whose grounds remain intact but whose provenance has been impugned. What I want is for them to discover, mid-exercise, that they are holding two judgements that sit badly together: this conclusion is the one my reasoning supports, and I have reason to think my reasoning on this occasion is not to be trusted. The design should include the rules and any materials, the sequence of stages with timings, what I should and should not reveal at each point, and instructions for keeping participants honest about their confidence levels rather than performing certainty or humility for the room.

Alongside the activity itself I need a debrief plan: a mapping from particular moments of the exercise to the philosophical distinctions I will afterwards introduce (first-order versus higher-order evidence, and the contested question whether an epistemically akratic state can ever be rational), with the mapping tied to things that actually happened rather than to my say-so. Please also tell me, candidly, what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run with a group of sceptical adults — I anticipate, for instance, that some will smell the deception early and begin gaming the facilitator, and I should like to know how to handle that — and identify which feature of the genuine philosophical problem the exercise cannot reproduce, so that I do not overclaim for it when I sum up.
