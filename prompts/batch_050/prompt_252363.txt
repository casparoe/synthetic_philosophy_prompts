I've been working through Jaynes and I want a consistency check before I post this anywhere public, because my usual interlocutors just nod along to anything with "entropy" in it. Roughly where I've landed:

1. Credences are probabilities, and updating on new evidence is conditionalization. Anything else gets you Dutch-booked, diachronically if nothing else.
2. Contra de Finetti and the subjectivists, priors aren't mere taste: given your total evidence there's a rationally required prior, at least in well-posed problems. Rationality constrains more than coherence.
3. The principle of indifference is fine where there's genuine symmetry — dice, cards. Bertrand-style paradoxes just show you have to respect the actual symmetries of the problem, i.e. transformation-group methods, or equivalently Jeffreys priors, for continuous parameters.
4. Given constraints like known expectation values, the right distribution is the maximum entropy one: it's the least informative distribution satisfying the constraints, hence the unique honest summary of your evidence.
5. Concrete case: a fresh coin I know nothing about. Uniform prior over the bias, Laplace's rule of succession, (s+1)/(n+2). This is just maxent with no constraints, so it's principled rather than a hack.
6. Solomonoff induction is the ideal that all of this approximates, and the choice of universal Turing machine doesn't worry me since it washes out with enough data anyway.
