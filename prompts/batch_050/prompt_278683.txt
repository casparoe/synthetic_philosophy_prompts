Two of my coworkers got into a running dispute after a news story broke, and I'd like an outside verdict. The story: a large shipping firm was fined after investigators found that its proprietary routing software -- built in-house and adjusted over many years by dozens of different employees -- had systematically steered vessels through a protected marine zone, because shaving those routes saved fuel and the firm's bonus structure rewarded fuel efficiency. No single engineer, manager, or executive ever decided to enter the zone; the practice emerged from the accumulated interaction of the software and the incentive scheme. Journalists uniformly wrote that the company was to blame.

My first coworker, Anna, insisted this was confused. Blame, she argued, requires beliefs, intentions, and awareness, and a corporation has none of these -- it has no mind, only employees with minds. Attributing the harm to "the company" is at best shorthand for legal liability and at worst a way for actual culpable individuals to hide behind a logo. The right response, she said, is to identify which managers and engineers failed in their duties and blame them.

My second coworker, Ben, replied that this misses what is distinctive about such cases. The firm has a decision-making structure -- policies, incentives, information channels, review procedures -- that produces outcomes no individual intended or even knew about. The relevant "intention," he claimed, existed only at the organizational level: the firm, through its reward system, in effect told its captains to minimize fuel costs. Employees merely occupy roles, he said, and the same policy would have produced the same outcome with entirely different personnel; blaming individuals for what the role-structure produced is the real category error.

Anna countered that "the firm in effect told them" is doing all the work in Ben's argument, and it remains metaphor. If a harm emerges from software and incentive structures that no one controls or fully understands, then the honest description is a tragic accident plus bad institutional design, not a blameworthy agent. Mens rea has to live somewhere, she said; punishing the firm's balance sheet is fine as deterrence, but deterrence isn't blame. She added that Ben's talk of organizational persistence proves too little: a policy's persistence across personnel changes just shows the incentives stayed fixed, and once you change the incentives, the same "agent" behaves completely differently -- which is not true of a person's settled character.

Ben made two replies. First, he said Anna was smuggling in a consciousness requirement that ordinary blame doesn't meet: we blame negligent people precisely for what they failed to notice, and we blame people for habitual vices -- greed, carelessness -- even when no single blameworthy choice can be isolated. A firm's incentive structure is a vice in exactly that sense, he claimed, and the firm can no more escape blame for it than a habitually careless person can. Second, he argued that if we explain human agency in terms of functional organization -- perception, deliberation, memory, action -- then we should at least take seriously that a sufficiently organized collective satisfies the same description, in which case Anna's insistence that blame "live somewhere" in an individual is an assumption, not an argument.

Anna's final move was to grant the negligence point but deny the analogy: when we blame a negligent person, we blame someone who could and should have noticed, which presupposes the kind of unified awareness a firm lacks. A firm's "awareness" is just documents and conversations distributed across people, each of whom may be individually blameless. So either the individuals could have noticed, in which case blame them, or they couldn't, in which case no one is blameworthy and we should fix the system rather than moralize about it. Ben answered that the dilemma is exactly what's in question: the whole debate is whether there's a third option between individual blame and no blame at all.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: who is right about each specific claim -- the alleged consciousness requirement for blame, the character analogy, the persistence-of-policy argument, the "blame must live somewhere" dilemma, and the dispute over whether corporate decision structures generate intentions that no individual holds -- and identify, if you can, what both parties are overlooking or tacitly agreeing on that they shouldn't. I'm not asking for a balanced summary of "corporate responsibility debates" in general; I want a verdict on the arguments as Anna and Ben actually made them.
