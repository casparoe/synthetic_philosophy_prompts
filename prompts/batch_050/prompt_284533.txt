So my friend in my political theory reading group walked me through this argument last week and I still can't find the exit, even though the conclusion seems obviously nuts. Here it is as best I remember it. (1) When two of your judgments conflict, you should defer to the one made with better information and less distortion. (2) Ulysses, ordering his crew to tie him to the mast, was sober, and knew in advance both that his future self would desperately want to be untied and exactly why -- the song would be doing the wanting. His later self, mid-song, lacks precisely that perspective. So the earlier judgment is the better-informed and less-distorted one, and rightly controls. (3) Nothing about that depends on ships or sirens: whenever your earlier self correctly predicts a future desire and has a specific story about why that desire will be misleading, the earlier judgment should control. That's all a Ulysses contract is, and it's the same logic as constitutional entrenchment -- the framers anticipate that future majorities will get swept up in panic or mania, so they lock the important stuff away from them. (4) But here's the move: for pretty much ANY future preference change, your present self can produce such a story in advance. Habituation, peer pressure, aging, boredom, the pull of novelty -- whoever you are right now, you can describe your future self's disagreements with you as distortions. (5) So rationality requires you to bind yourself now against all foreseeable preference change: sign the contract, entrench the clause, make it as costly as possible for your future self to defect from your current plans, values, tastes, whatever. (6) But that's absurd -- it makes changing your mind about anything irrational in principle, and it makes maximally rigid constitutions the most rational ones, which can't be right, the dead hand problem exists for a reason. (7) So my friend concludes that precommitment is never actually binding in any normative sense; Ulysses contracts and entrenched clauses are just prudential tricks we indulgently permit, and the later self is always fully within its rights to override.

The thing is, I'd really like to keep the verdict in (2) -- the mast case seems like the paradigm of rational precommitment -- and I'd also like to keep it being genuinely rational sometimes, not just permitted. I'm much less attached to the constitutional stuff in (3), that always seemed like the shakiest part. But I can't figure out which step actually breaks. If I reject (4), it feels like I'm just insisting that MY predictions about my future self are the good kind and everyone else's are the bad kind, which seems question-begging. If I reject (1), I lose the whole explanation of why the mast case works. So: which step do you think goes wrong, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? And if the argument is actually valid, which premise is cheapest to give up -- I'd rather know the price than keep pretending the conclusion isn't insane.
