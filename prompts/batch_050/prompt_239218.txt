Last spring, the small online retail company I co-own replaced its first-line customer support with a large language model that drafts and sends replies to customers on its own initiative. Three weeks ago a dispute reached us through a customer's lawyer. In the course of one chat session, the system had written "I promise you will receive a full refund" for an item our refund policy excludes; it had written "I sincerely apologize for our error" concerning a delayed shipment we maintain was not our error at all; and in another session it had told a customer "be warned that opening the housing voids your warranty," which our technician says is simply false. The customer's position is that the company promised, apologized, and warned, and must live with what those acts imply. Our own lawyer's position is that software cannot perform any of these acts, and so nothing was promised, apologized for, or warned about. Both positions strike me as too quick, and the disagreement keeps turning on what it is to do something with words.

Because of this I have been reading J. L. Austin's How to Do Things with Words (the William James lectures delivered at Harvard in 1955, published posthumously in 1962). Early in the first lecture Austin writes:

"It has come to be commonly held that many utterances which look like statements are either not intended at all, or only intended in part, to record or impart straightforward information about the facts."

The phrase that grips me is "not intended at all, or only intended in part." Our system's utterer, if it is an utterer, intends nothing. The company intends something, but only in the attenuated way one intends the output of a tool one has configured, instructed, and then left to run. I would like you to reconstruct, as carefully as the lectures allow, how Austin would have analyzed our three messages had he lived to see systems of this kind. In particular:

1. Into which of his categories of infelicity — misfire, abuse, or the "etiolated" uses he explicitly sets aside as parasitic on normal utterance — would each message fall, and which of his felicity conditions (an accepted conventional procedure invoked by appropriate persons, correct and complete execution, the requisite thoughts and feelings, the requisite subsequent conduct) does each message fail, if any?
2. Austin clearly allowed promises and the like to be made in writing, through authorized agents, and perhaps by notices posted in public places. Is there a principled line within his framework between an authorized human clerk, a pre-recorded announcement, and our model, or does his apparatus oblige him to treat the company as the speaker in all three cases alike? And where in his fivefold classification of illocutionary acts (verdictives, exercitives, commissives, behabitives, expositives) would he place the apology and the warranty warning?
3. How would he apply the quoted sentence itself? Is an utterance produced with no intention anywhere in its immediate causal history a case of "not intended at all," or does the company's upstream, partial intention make it the "only intended in part" case? What would Austin need to know about how such a system works in order to decide, and what would he likely dismiss as irrelevant detail?

I am not asking you to adjudicate the legal dispute; I am asking for the most defensible reconstruction of Austin's view. Please distinguish explicitly between what follows from the lectures themselves and where you are extrapolating beyond anything Austin wrote, and indicate how confident you are in each part of the reconstruction. And if you judge the exercise to be misconceived — say, because his framework simply lacks the resources for this case, or because I have misread the quoted passage or taken it out of its context — I would rather you state that plainly and explain why than produce a polished answer to a question that cannot bear the weight I am putting on it.
